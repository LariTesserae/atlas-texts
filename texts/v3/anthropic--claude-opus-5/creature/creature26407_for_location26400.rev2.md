## The People

There are forty-one of them. There were thirty-eight four years ago and there will be some other number soon; it moves by twos and threes, and nobody has ever counted more than sixty.

You will not see them first. The light is amber and poor and they are the color of the grit — dust on grease on dust, the same coat the leaves wear — and they do not stand up when something comes over the lip. What happens is that somewhere out in the haze a knock starts up: two stones, unhurried, a short figure repeated. Then another answers from a different quarter, a different figure. Then a third. They are not signaling to you. They are telling each other who is where, which is what the knocking is always for, and the news that has just gone around the bowl is that there is a **quiet** on the north scree.

That is the word. It does the work of *stranger*, *outsider*, *guest-who-cannot-be-spoken-to*, and, in certain weathers, *corpse*.

## Bodies

They are short and very deep. The chest is the whole of them: a barrel that starts high under the collarbone and does not taper, ribs sprung wide, the belly hard with breathing-muscle, and then rather ordinary arms and rather short legs hung off the bottom of it like afterthoughts. Their necks are thick, their hands are broad, their fingers thicken at the ends. Their skin, under the powder, is a dark congested plum, and their lips and nailbeds are always a little blue, and this is not illness. It is simply what a person looks like who has spent a lifetime at four thousand meters, underneath a lake of gas that has pushed most of the air out from underneath it.

Because that is the true shape of the place, and they learned it long before anyone told them: the deeper you go in the bowl, the less there is to breathe. The huts sit high, just under the tide-line, in the shallows. The gardens and the tallow terrace and the good grit are down on the floor, where the gas lies thick and the oxygen is thin and every breath is work, like drawing air through a folded cloth. So they go *down* to work and *up* to rest, and they come up the slope at the end of a shift breathing in great slow heaves with their hands on their knees, and the word they use for that climb is the word the valley people use for surfacing.

Nobody here runs. Running is an emergency and sounds like one. The ordinary gait of the bowl is a flat-footed unhurried plod that a stranger reads as sullenness and that is in fact economy: of breath, and of water, of which there is none.

## Water

There is no water in the bowl, ever, in any form. They do not wash. They scrape — a flake of glassbeard silica run down the forearm takes off the crust of dust-and-grease in grey curls, and then they re-grease, because bare skin at thirty-five degrees in dry gas cracks in a day. They are never clean and never dirty. Nothing here rots. There are no flies, no mold, no smell of anything turning; a haunch hung in a rack simply candies over and waits. Their horror-word is not *foul*. Their horror-word is *quiet*.

What water they have, they eat. The diet is wax — tallow-paddle rendered, midden-wax mined from under the trees, loaf-grease tapped warm — and fat burned in a body yields its own water, more than its weight of it, so that a person here drinks perhaps a cup a day, carried up in sealed gourds from a seep two hours down the mountain, and gets the rest out of the food, slowly, the way a camel does. They are therefore enormously fat-adapted and rather thin, and they think of thirst as a kind of hunger, and they have no word that distinguishes the two.

## Plugs and Voice

Every person in the bowl wears a pair of **plugs** on a cord around the neck: shaped tallow-wax, warmed in the hand, worked with the thumb, fitted to that one person's ears and to no one else's. A child is given its first pair before it is given a name.

They live with them half in. This is a skill. In at the deep end, out in the shallows; in when the pod season starts, out at dusk when the drones thin; both out only in the house, where the fleece and the packed dust swallow everything and a whisper is possible and is, for them, what candlelight is for other people. Lovers take each other's plugs out. That is the gesture; that is the whole of it. To hand someone your plugs is to say something that cannot be taken back.

Their voices are the voices the gas makes: an octave and a half under, flattened, enormous, with no top to them at all. A newborn in the bowl bellows like a bull calf. Elderly women speak in a register that would carry a war. They shout because they must, and because their ears are old before they are thirty, and the shouting has shaped the language: it is built out of long open sounds and hard consonants that survive a roar, with no sibilants worth speaking of, since an *s* in this air is a rumor.

And they talk with their hands on each other. Two people in conversation stand close, and each rests a palm flat on the other's breastbone, and what the hand reads is the buzz of the voice at its source, unmuddled by the bowl. A crowded argument in the shallows looks, from above the line, like a slow wrestling match conducted by people who are not looking at one another, because they are not; the eyes are the least of it; the eyes are for finding the door.

## Names

The spoken name is for the house and for the hand on the chest. Out in the bowl, a person is a **knock** — three to seven strokes on two stones, a rhythm, unmistakable at four hundred meters, audible through drone and clicking and the racket of the pods. You announce your knock when you come down to the floor. You knock again when you move. Silence from a knock that should be moving is the thing that empties the huts.

The knocks are held in one woman's head. She is sixty and holds about three hundred of them, most belonging to the dead, because a retired knock may not be reissued for four generations, and she is the only proof of when each was retired. She will not teach it to anyone until she starts to go, and she has not started to go, and this is discussed.

## Husbandry

Above the huts the **loaf lines** are moored: twenty or thirty bag-bodied animals tethered by a cord through the forward flange, drifting at head height, nudging and bumping and paddling gently against their strings, blind, harmless, warm. They are tapped for grease the way other people milk. Children are sent out at dusk to bring them down, and find them by breathing — a loaf's intake is a soft wet snore, the only wet sound in the bowl — and by feel, and never by looking.

The **hulders** are half-tame and half not. Each wears a cut length of pipe-frond strapped along the spine, slotted, so that the beast whistles as it walks, and every animal's pipe is tuned to its own note. A herd moves as a slow sour chord. A herder standing on the terrace at noon with her plugs out can tell you, without turning her head, that the chord is short a fourth, and which cow that is, and that she has gone up-slope again toward the north notch, where the cold comes over.

## Tuning

Nobody here gardens with water, because there is none, or with light, because it is useless. They garden with noise.

A **tuner** goes down with a hooked knife and a bag of wax and spends the day opening slots in pipe-frond tubes and plugging others, raising a stand a semitone, killing a beat that has set up between two clumps, aiming a drone across the flats at a hungry tallow tree that has been starving in a dead spot for a season. They work by hand and by the hair on the arm. They plant seedling fronds inside the belly of a standing drone the way other people plant in good soil.

And they copy. The oldest fronds carry slots cut by people four and five and eight generations back, and when a frond finally splits and dies, the tuner runs both hands down its fifteen meters, learning the cuts by fingertip, and then cuts them again into the young tube beside it. This is the entire archive of the place: a few thousand grooves, recopied by touch, in the dark, forever. The drones the bowl holds today are the drones it held four hundred years ago, plus error. They have drifted about a tone flat over that time. The focus of the Ear has drifted a meter west. It is the same drift, and they know it, and they have a word for it that means both *wear* and *inheritance*, and they use it about their children.

## The Argument

There is no chief. There is a standing argument about the west end of the Ear, which has been rebuilt badly twice, and everyone over about twenty is in it, and the positions are inherited along with the copper.

One party holds that the Ear must be rebuilt to its original focus and the terrace left where it is. The other holds that the focus has moved and the trees should be moved after it — that you plant where the sound *is*. The second party has been right for a hundred and fifty years and has never once won, because the trees under the Ear are the largest in the world, four hundred years old, crowns like cathedral fans, and you cannot move them, and everybody knows that the argument is not really about the trees. Meetings are held at dawn at the tide-line when the lake is at its lowest and its quietest, and they are conducted at a volume that would be, anywhere else, a riot.

## The Hammer

Hanging in a frame between the two largest huts is a dried **shrike-hammer**, cut from the animal, waxed, black, the size of a loaf of bread, struck with a mallet by whoever is nearest.

One stroke. It goes out flat across the bowl at a volume that knocks the clicking out of every living thing for a radius of half a kilometer — loafs stalling, hulders going dumb mid-note, the crawlers freezing in the grit — and for perhaps four seconds there is an actual silence in a place that has never had one. Then it all starts up again, ragged, from the edges inward.

They use it for the Cold Fall coming over the notch, for a person lost on the floor, and for a death. It is the only silence they make on purpose and they are frightened of it. Everyone plugs before it is struck, including the striker.

## The Divers

The bones in the sinks are sorted by size. Somebody sorts them.

Once a year, at the end of the pod season, the family sinks are worked by the **divers** — four or five of them, always young, chosen for lungs. They stand at the rim and breathe themselves silly for ten minutes and then go down on a rope into pure gas, into a place where there is no air, no clicking, no drone, and where their own voice, if they were foolish enough to spend breath on it, would come out as a groan that nobody above would even turn for. They have forty seconds. They stack three or four bones and come up.

They are the only people in this bowl who have ever chosen quiet, and they come up altered, and it is from among them that the traders are taken — the ones sent down the mountain with bales of glassbeard fleece and cakes of wax, into the valleys, where everything is unbearably shrill and thin and everyone sounds like a child, to bring back copper. They are said to be able to stand it. They are said to come back strange.

The valley people find them dull. They will not talk on the road. They hate the sound of their own voices up there.

*A river*, they say, when asked why they will not live below, *is a thing that talks all day and never answers.*

## The Guests

A person who goes deaf — and most who live long enough do — stops being addressed as a person of the bowl and becomes a **guest**, which is the same word as *quiet*, which is the same word as *stranger*. This is not cruelty; it is grammar, and the grammar has consequences. Guests are fed first. Guests are not asked to work the floor. Guests sit in the deadened houses doing the fine knapping and the plug-shaping and minding the small children, hands always on someone, and are spoken to by being touched, and are treated with an elaborate formal courtesy that has no warmth in it at all, because the warm forms of address are not available for use on a stranger.

They are honored and they are outside, permanently, in the house they built. Everyone can see this coming and nobody has ever proposed a change to it.

## Where They Were Born

Not here. None of them.

A woman carrying a child goes down in her last third — two days' walk, into air that hurts, into the valleys with their rivers — and gives birth there, in a house with a hearth and a roof and a family that has hosted her mother and her mother's mother, and stays until the infant is fat enough to be brought up and fitted with its first plugs. Babies conceived and carried and born in the bowl come out small and blue and mostly do not stay. Everyone knows this. Nobody pretends otherwise.

So every single person in the Sump began in a loud thin outside place, with a river going past the window, making its meaningless noise; and every one of them made a first cry that was high and small and stupid, a quiet's cry; and every one of them has had to be taught, over some years, how to be heard. The copper beads on the wrist are one per birth owed to a valley household, worn until repaid in wax, and never quite repaid, because they are not really for paying.

It is the great unspoken fact of the place, and the tuners have a slot-figure for it that they cut into every new frond low down on the tube where the wind never reaches it and it makes no sound at all.

## The Cold Fall

Twenty times a year the cold comes over the north notch at night and punches into the lake and the density collapses. The drones die mid-note. The loafs sink and thump into the grit. The bowl loses its voice.

They sit in the soft dark houses, and here is the thing they do not tell outsiders: for that hour, the gas is thin, and everybody's voice comes up. Up out of the bull register, up thin and high and reedy, a valley voice, a child's voice, a stranger's voice. A family in the dark discovers itself to be full of quiets.

So they do not speak. They sit close and put their hands flat on each other's chests, and breathe, and wait it out, reading each other by pulse instead, which does not change pitch; and outside the wind howls and the pipe-fronds crack like shot, and there is nothing to hear.

By morning the vents have breathed the bowl full again, warm and amber and sweet. Somebody goes out early with a hooked knife to see what is left standing, and gets a stand to sound — whoever does it first has the right to name the note, and keeps that right for a year, and there is a bead in it — and the drone comes up out of the wreckage, flat by a tone, four hundred years late, and holds.

Then the hammer, once, for whoever was lost. Then four seconds of nothing.

Then the answering.
