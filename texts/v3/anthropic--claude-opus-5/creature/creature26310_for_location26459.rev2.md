# The Tenants

## First, the difficulty

Ask who lives in the Sweating Notch and the honest first answer is a list of refusals. The crews don't: they come up on the cable and go down on it, and the regulations forbid a bed. The swifts don't: they cross it. The mountain itself is too new to have an owner — one hundred and eighty thousand years old at five centimetres a year, which is nothing, which is a lifetime of a species and not much more.

And yet the place is not empty. It is, by weight, one of the most crowded hundred and eighty paces on the continent. The crowding is simply arranged at a scale that defeats the eye, and organised — this is the part that takes people by surprise — around rivets.

## The crews

Not residents. Worth describing anyway, because they are the only inhabitants who can be interviewed.

Two of them at a time, six days on, twelve down. They arrive dry and are wet in twenty minutes and stay that way, and by the second day they have stopped noticing, and by the fourth they have stopped speaking even in the pump house where speech is technically possible. They eat standing up. They grease things. They lie on the steel rail to sleep for twenty minutes at a time, strapped, because there is nowhere else level and the moss will take a body's heat away through a coat in an hour.

The sign language has thirty-two gestures. Twenty-eight of them concern valves, pressures, the state of the header tank, and the specific ways a compressor can be wrong. The other four are:

*The cloud is failing* — flat hand lifted from the eyes, the only gesture made with the whole arm.

*The orchid is out* — two fingers opening from a fist, low, near the hip, almost private.

*A sixer, don't step* — the back of one hand tapped twice with two fingers of the other.

And one that has no agreed translation, made when the other person has been still too long: a flat palm turned slowly over, which the older crews say means *are you all right* and the younger ones say means *stay*. Both groups make it identically.

They take the quiet down with them. Families notice. There are marriages in the valley station that are conducted almost entirely in the second and fourth gestures.

## The swifts

They live in the air and the notch is a feature of the air, the way a reef is a feature of the sea. The updraught arrives over the western flank carrying everything the warm forest below has thrown away — aphids, thrips, spiderlings on their silk, the whole plankton of the lowlands lifted nine kilometres and stunned by cold — and the swifts take the top of the column where the food is concentrated and the lift is free. They have no need of the ground. Most of the dozen you see are two and three years old and have not touched a solid object since they left the nest cliff, wherever that is, and will not touch one until they breed, if they do.

They are the only creatures in the notch who are not wet. They fly through cloud and shed it. That *fp* you hear as one passes is not the wingbeat; it is the sound of a body displacing air that has nowhere to go.

They do not know the machines are there. Nothing in a swift's world is stationary long enough to be a landmark.

## The moss

This is the landlord.

A hand deep, and the top centimetre is alive and the rest is its own corpse. Every green shoot in the notch stands on a compressed column of its ancestors, ninety years of them in some places, the structure holding eight times its dry weight in water and releasing none of it. When you put a boot down and the print fills with clear water before you have finished lifting, that water has been in the moss for a decade. The notch does not drain. It *holds*, the way a sponge holds, and the mountain's weeping arrives into an organism already at capacity and is passed along.

Nothing here is separate from it. The cushion plants are rooted in it, the tree-ferns are rooted in the cushions, the orchid is rooted in the fern, and all of them are drinking, at fourth hand, water that came out of a fracture nine kilometres down and was released to them by a plant with no roots at all.

It advances a thumb's width a season toward the path, and is scoured back each winter, and has been holding that line — losing, gaining, losing — since before the concrete.

## The orchid

One plant, on the eastern turbine housing, in the warm dry-ish shadow of the casing where nothing else will grow. Pale green, six centimetres, unremarkable in every particular except the smell.

Hot iron. Sharp, metallic, faintly bloodlike. It is a common enough compound in flowers; somewhere below, in a valley, there is presumably a fly or a small beetle that goes mad for it and does the orchid's work.

There is no such insect here. And the notch, permanently, everywhere, smells of hot wet iron — the plates, the rail, the rivet-heads, the bronze sheen of the rock beginning to rust. The orchid is broadcasting on a wavelength the whole landscape occupies. It has been shouting into a room where everyone is already shouting the same word.

It has never set seed. It flowers whenever it likes, for nobody, and propagates by putting out small plantlets along its own stem, which is to say it has given up on the future and is simply continuing.

## The beetles and the fly

Four beetles. Three graze the lichen crusts and one eats the other three. All four have fused wing-cases and no wings beneath them, because in a place where the wind runs across a gap with three thousand metres of nothing on either side, the ability to fly is a way of being subtracted. They walk. They have heavy claws and they walk everywhere, slowly, and they take three years to do what a lowland beetle does in a season.

The fly is different. *Dolichopodid*, long-legged, metallic, a millimetre and a half, and it flies in clouds all summer at the condenser gutter — because behind sixty tiers of corrugated plate there is a pocket of dead air perhaps four metres long, the only still air in the notch, engineered accidentally in 1961 by a man drawing a lattice. The entire mating swarm of an entire species occurs inside that pocket. The larvae develop in the gutter sludge. If you took the tower down, the species would go with it within two summers.

Nature did not reclaim this place. Nature moved in afterwards and took the good rooms.

## The sixers

Now the ones I would call, if pressed, the people of the notch.

Forty-odd pale frogs beneath the pump house, in the warm wet dark under the brick, where the waste heat off the piston holds the moss at a steady twenty-two degrees. Translucent flanks; you can see the heart. Three centimetres. They do not swim and have never swum; they lay six large eggs in the wet heart of a cushion plant and what hatches is already a frog, having done its tadpole in private, inside the egg, growing and reabsorbing a tail it will never use.

Here is the thing about them. Everything else in the notch arrived — spores on the wind, dust-seeds, beetles blown up out of the forest and marooned. The frogs did not arrive. The frogs were *lifted*. Their lineage was a shore animal, in leaf litter, by a warm ocean, and the rock came up underneath them at four to six centimetres a year and they simply stayed where they were. A hundred and eighty thousand years, a few hundred thousand generations, riding. The salt is still in the mountain's cracks and something coastal is still in the frog: the flank transparency of a thing that expected to live in shallow bright water, the useless tail, the webbing on a foot that has never been wet in the way a foot is meant to be wet.

They are silent to us. They are not silent. The male calls at a frequency that sits inside the turbine groan and is eaten by it — the same drowning that took the orchid's smell, in a different medium, by the same tenant.

So they have moved house. They call in the gap.

Every eleven minutes the valve opens and the piston strikes six times, and after the sixth stroke there are two and a half seconds in which the notch is as close to quiet as it ever gets — the turbines still groaning but the great bell-note decaying away and taking the harmonics with it, a hole punched clean through the noise floor. That is when they call. A hundred and thirty times a day, every day, in the two and a half seconds after the hammer.

The crews call them sixers for this. There is a gesture for them, which is one of only four gestures in the whole language not about valves, and it means *don't step*.

Four years ago the pump was down nine days for a bearing. On the third night the underside of the pump house was, according to the man on shift, unbearable — not loud, he couldn't hear a thing, but crowded, thick with something, every frog in the colony calling at once into a silence none of them had ever encountered and none of them knew how to divide. They had no clock. When the piston went back in, it took about forty minutes, he said, and then they found the gap again, all of them, together, and it has been every eleven minutes since.

## The invisible majority

In the moss, per handful: something like two hundred thousand animals. Rotifers, nematodes, mites, springtails, and the tardigrades, which are the true dominant fauna of the Sweating Notch by count and by biomass and by every measure except the one where you have to be able to see a thing to admit it exists. They are in the water film on every leaf. They are in the lichen crusts. They are in the grease on the rail. When the cloud fails and the sun comes through unfiltered and everything glitters unbearably for an hour, they dry to grains and stop, entirely, chemically, and when the cloud is manufactured again overhead they resume mid-stride.

The fauna is not a rumour. The fauna is simply small, and the notch is not a landscape with animals in it but an animal-saturated fabric with some rock underneath.

## And the thing in the water

Last, and first.

The water does not arrive clean. It has come up out of fractures nobody has bottomed, warm all the way, under pressure, carrying iron and sulphur and dissolved stone, and it has been inhabited the entire distance. What comes out of the rock face is a suspension: archaea that have never encountered light and are killed by it within minutes of emergence, arriving continuously, dying continuously, in numbers that have no useful name.

Their bodies do not go anywhere. They settle on the wet faces and they are the reason the wet faces are the way they are. That varnished look on the running walls, that even glisten — that is not water. Water alone would bead and streak. It is a biofilm one cell thick, renewed every hour by the dead coming up out of the mountain, and it is slick in a way that no mineral surface is slick, and it is the actual, physical reason that a hundred and eighty paces takes an hour instead of four minutes.

The rule of the place is enforced by its oldest inhabitants, who have never seen it, who last about two hundred seconds in the open air, and who are arriving right now, in millions, out of the blade of rock, on their way up from the ocean the whole mountain is still trying to leave.
