# The Population of the Ninth Throat

## The sparlouse

Take the first one you find and hold it in the light bar, and you will see straight through it: gut, two pale ropes of muscle, a clutch of eggs if it is carrying. Eight millimetres. No eyes, no pigment, nothing that could be called an ear. It grazes the softest bloom off a growing edge in a slow zigzag, scraping and reversing, scraping and reversing, and when the rib it is working on breaks, it goes down with the shards and continues on the floor without any indication of having noticed.

The tenders call it the sparlouse and do not think of it as belonging here, and they are right. It has no business in bright water. It is a creature of the drowned country below the drain — the flooded joints and bedding-planes that the outflow feeds, dark, slow, sedimented, where things live for years. Some minority of them work upward against a fist-and-a-half of current and arrive in the Throat, which for them is an attic: lit, deafening, sterile, and lethal on a schedule of days. They cannot hear the room. That is why they can enter it. It is also why they cannot leave it, because the only reliable route back is the one the room decides to give them, and a sparlouse that lingers gets cemented to the wall by its own pasture.

So the population is a trickle of immigrants who have made a mistake, and their number on any given day is a measure of the flow. Three in a visit is ordinary. Nine means the drain has been running wide and free and the mountain is being generous. None means either that the water below is cold, or that the aperture is narrowing, and the tenders do not like counting none.

They breed in the room regardless. Eggs are laid in the black stripes, which is the only correct decision available, and which nothing in the animal is capable of making deliberately. It cannot detect the stripes. It simply grazes until the substrate stops yielding soft bloom and reverses, and the places where the substrate reliably keeps yielding are the places where nothing is setting, and those are the quiet lines. The whole navigation of its life is a matter of turning around when the food gets hard. In a room that is a diagram of its own voice, this is sufficient to keep it, most of the time, in the silence.

## The stripes

Three species, possibly four, thin as a stain, black-green, holding to the flutes where the light bar strikes. They arrive from above: snow-algae out of the red-tinged basin, coming down the intake in their millions, entombed in their millions, of which a few hundred a season land in a node and get twenty days.

They are the only inhabitants that can be said to be *doing well*, in the sense that there are more of them next month than there were last month, most months. But they are not stable and they are not a community. A stripe is a pulse. It takes a fortnight for the algae to darken a newly quiet line and another fortnight for them to die when the line goes loud again, and in between the room has retuned itself six or seven times. So the stripes are always describing a geometry that has already been abandoned. They are a map of last month's silence, drawn slowly, on a surface where silence moves in minutes.

The tenders read them anyway, because in a room where you cannot think, a dark line on a white wall is worth more than a theory. The rule taught to children is: *the black is where the quiet was.* Not *is*. The distinction is the difference between a knee you can rest and a knee you cannot get back.

## The eleven

The birds are the only permanent residents, and they are dead, and they are not finished.

The oldest is a swelling in the west flute with a suggestion of a folded neck and one place where the spar goes slightly grey, which is what a chough becomes after eleven or twelve years of being built into a wall. The fourth is the famous one: head enclosed under two centimetres, beak still black and still visible, one eye socket faintly readable as a dimple. The ninth still has a wing free at the wrist, and in the current of the fall the primaries move, individually, all the time, which is the single most unbearable thing in the chamber and which every tender learns to keep out of the corner of the eye.

They are counted on every visit. Counting is not sentiment. In the Throat you cannot think — thought is wiped every two or three seconds, so that you arrive repeatedly in the same appalling present — but you *can* count. Counting is not thinking. It is the one mental act the room permits, and the tenders have built their entire practice on this discovery: count the footholds, count the birds, count the strokes, count the breaths. A man who is counting is not a man who is going to drift.

And the birds are a clock. A bird enclosed in a known season, measured against the thickness over its beak, gives the growth rate of that patch of wall, and the growth rate of a patch of wall tells you whether the room is in a hungry phase or a slow one. Eleven birds are eleven calibrated gauges donated by accident. The tenders are aware of the joke, if it is a joke, that the only instruments in the Throat that measure growth are made of things that used to fly.

## Ienn

Eighteen centimetres, kneeling, right arm forward. The socket where the chisel handle was is still open, because every tender who goes down puts a thumb into it on the way past and scrapes out the fresh bloom, which takes perhaps two seconds and has been done, by best estimate, forty thousand times.

This is the only ritual the tenders have, and they do not describe it as one. The Throat has taught them what a ritual is for. There are two holes in this room that must be kept open by hand or they will close: the drain, which is for the village, and the socket, which is not for anything. They keep both. When asked why, an old tender will generally say that it is quicker than explaining, or that the bloom has to go somewhere, and then will change the subject in the flat overloud voice that all of them have.

The cord records that his mistake was not letting go.

The version that is not on the cord, and which is passed on at about the age of fourteen, along with the eleven signs, is that Ienn knelt in a black line. He did what he was taught. He found the quiet and put his knee in it and worked, and the line had gone quiet three weeks before, and had been loud again for nine days, and the algae had not yet finished dying. His wrist bound first. Then the handle would not come out of the wall, and then he would not come out of the handle.

There is an eleventh hand-sign. The first ten are *more*, *stop*, *up*, *down*, *the bar*, *hard*, *soft*, *I am at forty*, *I am finished*, *no*. The eleventh is made with the flat of the hand turned outward and drawn slowly across the body at the level of the chest, and it means *leave him*. It is taught to every child and has been used, on the cord's evidence, three times.

## The ones who go down

They are small. The room selects for it — the sill sits at the bottom of a narrowing and the work is done one-armed in a space you could not get a broad shoulder into, so over thirty-one generations the families that hold turns have become a population of compact, dense, slow-pulsed people with short forearms and unusual tolerance for cold water, who in the pastures look like a distinct kind of person and are.

The work is not diving so much as a long series of descents inside a room. The sill is at the low point; the air is at the high point, forty centimetres of shuddering bell with the column hammering it a hand's breadth from the face. So the pattern is: down, thirty strokes counted, up, three breaths, down, thirty strokes. Sixty or seventy times. Two hours. Nothing is spoken because nothing can be; two people working the sill together communicate in the signs, made large and made slow, because a fast sign in that light is not a sign, it is a smear.

What it costs is a notch. Not deafness in general — a notch, a band of loss centred on the Throat's own note and its immediate neighbours, deepening with every turn served, so that by forty a tender simply cannot hear certain pitches at all. Families adapt. You speak to an old tender high and singing, well above the note, and this is so universal in the village below that the whole settlement has an accent: a raised, slightly chanted register that visitors find childlike and that has been shaped, syllable by syllable, by a room four thousand metres up that most of them will never see. The mountain's voice is missing from their speech. It is the loudest absence in that valley.

Also: nosebleeds; a headache described as *cold*; and the smear, which lasts four or five hours after surfacing, during which edges of things quietly refuse to settle. And the loss of the two hours. Almost no tender can recall the working time. They come up with the count intact and the interval gone, and this is considered normal, and is the reason the cord exists.

## The one who stays above

At the shaft head, bare foot on the rock in the snow-glare, with the cord and a waxed string on a yoke: the string-holder. Always an old tender, always one with a deep notch — which is to say, the person who measures the note is always a person who cannot hear it.

This is not perversity. Inside the Throat there is no pitch. The sound has no direction, no source, and no note; it is a condition, like pressure. A pitch is a thing that only exists from outside. So the measurement is taken above, through the rock, by a woman of sixty who sets her sole flat on the spur and tightens the string until the buzz in her jaw and the tremor in her foot are the same buzz, and then ties the knot at the length that did it. Two vibrations, one skull, no hearing involved.

Thirty-one generations of knots, and the note down roughly a minor sixth across them, which means the room is getting larger, which is the good direction. At the far end of the same cord, kept there deliberately so that no measurement can be made without handling it, is one older knot, short and hard and polished by two centuries of thumbs. It is the last pitch of the Eighth Throat, taken nine days before it sealed itself, after which the mountain opened lower down and the lake did not stay in its basin.

Every reading is made against that knot. The day a string-holder ties a knot that comes close to it, she is expected to say nothing, walk down, and tell the pasture to move the herds.

## The moss

On one ledge in the air bell, permanently drenched, permanently deafened, roughly the size of two hands, there is a clump of moss.

It should not be possible and nobody has explained it. It feeds on what the shaft drops — spores, pollen, midges off the snowfield, mineral dust, and the exhaled breath of everyone who has ever surfaced there, which over thirty-one generations amounts to a considerable quantity of air delivered from a range of about forty centimetres by people in extremity.

It is not growing measurably. It is not breaking. In a world where the only two verbs are *accrete* and *snap*, it does neither. It is the single thing in the Ninth Throat that has simply gone on being the same size for longer than anyone can establish, and for this reason the rule is that you do not touch it, a rule with no stated justification and total compliance.

And it is what they look at. Three breaths, once a minute, sixty times a visit, for two hours, with the column hammering the surface beside the ear and the bell shuddering like a struck drumskin and the whole skull ringing: the face comes up, the eyes find the ledge, and there is the moss, soft, dark, wet, unbothered, twenty centimetres away. Then down again.

Nothing lives in the Ninth Throat. The sparlouse is lost, the algae are a pulse, the birds are furniture, Ienn is wall, and the tenders are visitors who count their strokes and go up.

The moss lives there.
