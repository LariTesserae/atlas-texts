## The Fauna of the Bowl

Begin with what they eat. It is the only fact here that nobody can argue with, and everything else follows from it.

The grey-green stone is not inert. Warm, wet, held under a few hundred metres of pressure for a length of time that makes the word *duration* embarrassing, it comes slowly apart at the molecular level and gives off hydrogen, and a thin sour breath of sulfide, and methane in threads. This is not a seep in the geological sense — there is no geology here, no bedrock to leak from. There is only the building. The seeps *are* the building, exhaling.

So: the wall is food.

Understand that and the whole congregation makes sense. Nothing in the Bowl is eating sunlight at second or third hand, as everything in your world does. Everything in the Bowl is eating architecture. The film on the terraces is the visible outer edge of it, the way a bruise is the visible outer edge of an injury; the real mass of living tissue is *inside* — kilometres of it, packed into the pore spaces of the dressed rubble, a warm sulfidic interior biosphere threaded through the fill of every terrace and every riser and every collapsed course of the rim wall. Grams per litre of pore water, and there are a great many litres. It is the majority of the life here by an order of magnitude, and it has no shape, no behaviour, no members, nothing an eye could resolve. It has been eating the Bowl from within since before there was an outside worth describing.

Every animal you will meet in the open water is, functionally, a way of moving that interior around.

---

Take the **tenders** first, because they are the closest thing in the Bowl to a reader.

They are small — a fingernail, at most a thumbnail — hard-shelled, shaped like a flattened seed, with mouthparts arranged for one motion, which is scraping in a line. They live in the joints of the original masonry. Not near the joints: *in* them, in that soft-soaped gap of a few millimetres where the seep-breath comes through and the mat grows richest, and they crop it there, and by cropping keep it thin, and by keeping it thin keep it productive, and by keeping the gap clear keep water flowing through the joint, which keeps the joint breathing, which keeps the joint fed. A tender's entire behavioural repertoire is: *find a line; follow it; if it forks, take either; do not leave it.* That is all. They cannot cross open stone. Two centimetres of polished shelf is a desert to a tender and it will turn back rather than attempt it.

Which means the tenders are dying out, and have been dying out for tens of millions of years, in the slowest and most specific way imaginable: their habitat is *legibility*. Every overturning wears the joints a little softer, rounds a little more of the corner, blurs a little more of the line, and where the line goes the tenders go. The upper terraces, the ones most polished, the ones that read now as slopes rather than steps — there are no tenders there, and haven't been for an unguessable while. They persist in the deep, in the shaded lee of retaining walls, in the corbelled sluice roofs, in the places where the fitting of stone to stone is still crisp enough to constitute a sentence. They are following the last legible parts of the original text and eating it as they go, and they are the only conservative force in the Bowl, and their conservation is indistinguishable from consumption.

There will be a last one. It will circle a soft place where a joint used to be, three or four times, and stop.

---

The **mound-makers** are the opposite animal in every respect, and they are why the Bowl looks the way it does.

Palm-sized, plated, with the overlapping dorsal shields that make you say *trilobite* even though the resemblance is a coincidence of engineering. They eat silt — pass it through a long gut, strip the mineral-eaters out of it, and extrude the tailings as a paste that sets hard in about an hour. They deposit that paste according to a rule so simple it took a long time to believe: *put it where the seep-smell is strongest and where your own cement already is, and put it slightly higher than last time.*

That rule builds a chimney. The chimney is not decoration and not shelter, or not primarily. Every forty minutes the whole Bowl slops — the seiche, two metres of it, in and out, in and out, forever — and a chimney standing in that slop becomes a pump. Water is drawn up through the mound's footing, through the seep beneath, through the porous cement, and out at the top, and the mound's interior is therefore a continuously irrigated volume of warm sulfidic water in an otherwise cold and starved shelf. Inside it, mats grow that could not grow outside it. In the deepest chambers the brood sit in that flow with their gills out doing nothing at all, and are fed.

They did not design this. They cannot have. Individually they are, as far as anyone has been able to establish, entirely stupid; you can lift one off its mound and set it down forty metres away and it will begin a new mound where it lands with no evidence of grievance. But the rule is old — older, perhaps, than everything else here — and the rule and the seiche together produce a working ventilation system, and have produced one continuously through some hundreds of millions of tidal cycles, and the cement they extrude is chemically indistinguishable from the packing in the terrace fill.

Nobody is going to help you with that. Note also, if you want to be uncomfortable about it, what happens where two colonies meet. The cement carries a colony scent. A mound-maker will not build onto foreign cement; it builds *against* it. Where two colonies grow into each other along a shelf, they raise between them a straight, low, double-faced wall, coursed, battered slightly on both faces, and at a scale of a metre or two it is not distinguishable from a retaining wall of the original construction. Not similar to. Not reminiscent of. The same object, made by an animal that has never had a thought, for no reason but not wanting to touch a stranger.

Their mounds are riddled with **squatters**, small soft borers that dissolve cement to make a tube and live in it, and a mound of forty generations is more hole than wall by the end, and fails, and is built over. That is the local cycle, and it turns in decades, and it is nested inside the larger cycle, which turns in decades too but without any rhythm at all.

---

Because the overturn is the year here. Not the year — there is no year in the Bowl, no season, no day; the light does not vary enough to entrain anything, and nothing in the water has ever needed to know where the sun is. There are exactly two clocks. One is the forty-minute breath. The other is the overturn, which comes when it comes, and which everything alive has arranged itself around with a thoroughness that reads, from outside, as fatalism, and from inside as nothing at all.
