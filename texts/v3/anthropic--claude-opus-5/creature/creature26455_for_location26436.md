# The Sill: its tenants

## The people

Seven hundred in Anneal, and something under nine thousand on the whole plateau, and you can pick any one of them out of a crowd anywhere in the world.

Start with the chest. Two and a half thousand meters does that: deep barrel, high shoulders, a resting breath you can watch from across a room. Then the skin, which is never dry and never chapped and never quite its own color, because everyone is oiled. Then the hands, which are ruined. The brine is alkaline enough to take the fat out of skin in a single shift; a plateau hand at thirty is smooth across the pads, pale, faintly waxy, with the whorls polished off it entirely. They cannot be fingerprinted. This causes them endless trouble at customs houses down in the dry lands and is a matter of quiet plateau pride.

They are short of stature and long of forearm and they wear dark clothes, which surprises people who expect white. White is useless here. White is the ground, the air, the light, the crust on your boots. Anneal dresses in indigo, oxblood, tarred black, imported dye at imported prices, for the sole reason that in a knee-high mist at noon a person needs to be *findable*. From a high rim the towns read as a scatter of dark commas moving on white — and moving is the point.

Because nobody on the Sill stands still.

You will see it within an hour and it will take you a week to name. They rock. Heel to toe, or a slow shift hip to hip, a constant low idling motion that goes on through conversation, through queuing, through prayer if they had any. Children are jiggled. Sleepers are turned. A man reading a gauge will tap one boot the whole time he reads it. It is not nerves. It is the rule of the shell, learned in the body before it is learned in the head: *the water deposits on everything that holds still*. Nothing serious accretes on a living person in a single day, but the habit does not distinguish between serious and not, and the habit is total. Plateau people who go down to the lowlands and stay there rock in bank queues for the rest of their lives.

Their faces are unreadable, and they know it. The shadowless light flattens everything; the permanent squint-fan at the eyes puts a false amusement on grief and rage alike; and the glare keeps everyone's eyes watering all day, so that no one on the Sill can tell whether you are crying. Locals will tell you this is a mercy. Some of them mean it.

So they gave up on faces and moved the whole expressive apparatus down into the hands and the shoulders. Kindness here is a palm laid flat on your forearm, above the wrist, for about two seconds. Sympathy is the same, held longer. Laughter is silent and lives entirely in the shoulders and the shut mouth. Fury is a single hard slap on your own thigh, and then the withdrawal of touch, which can last years.

## Hammers

Every person over the age of about six wears a hammer.

It hangs on a lanyard at the left hip: a little dull-headed thing, four or five ounces, one face flat and one face a blunted point, the handle wrapped in oiled cord. It is not a tool. There are tools, and they are better hammers, and nobody would tap with them. This one is a voice.

Every pipe on the plateau is a telegraph, and the code that runs on the lagging is not an emergency system grafted onto a talking culture. It *is* the language. It has grammar. It has a tense structure that outsiders find unteachable, because it marks whether a thing is holding, wandering, setting, or gone — the four states the Sill recognizes — and has no simple word for *is*. Fluent tapping runs at about the speed of unhurried speech, and the fluent are all of them, and they do it half-attending, one hand on the lagging while they eat, the way a man drums a tabletop.

And because fingerprints are gone, a person's identity is their rhythm. Three to five strokes, learned by the whole town in a week, unmistakable at any distance, opening every message a person sends for their whole life. That is the real name. The spoken name exists for the pay ledger and for outsiders. When two people who love each other are in the same room, one of them will sometimes tap the other's name on the other's knee, and this is not the same as saying it, and is considerably more.

Junction hands hear everything. In a system of open pipes there is no privacy at all: a message from Anneal to the westmost works passes through the knuckles of nine people, any of whom could hold it up and read it. So the plateau's single great taboo is not about sex or the dead or money, it is about repeating what came over the lagging. You relay and you forget. A junction hand who gossips is not punished; they simply stop being touched on the forearm, by anyone, and after some months of that they take the road west.

## The allowance of words

They can speak. They do speak. But the vapor eats airborne sound inside thirty meters, and worse than that, the vapor eats it *unevenly*, so that half a sentence arrives and half does not, and after a few thousand years of that nobody on the Sill can be bothered.

What survives is single words, delivered at close range, mouth near the ear. And because a spoken word costs you the trouble of crossing a room and leaning in, spoken words on the Sill are enormous. They are used for two things: emergency, and intimacy. *Through* means someone has gone through the crust. *Wind* means come off the piers. Those two you will hear shouted, and they carry no further than a stone's throw, and everyone within that throw moves instantly.

The other use is domestic and you are not likely to witness it. Lovers speak. Parents speak to small children, mouth to ear, and it is understood to be the most private thing a person does. There is a plateau expression for a marriage in trouble, which taps out as *dry-mouthed*.

Newcomers talk for their first fortnight, hopelessly, into the blanket. Everyone lets them. The day a newcomer stops is noticed by the whole shift, and someone will find an excuse to put a hand on their forearm before end of shift, and that is the whole of the citizenship ceremony.

## What they eat

No plants means no field, no orchard, no grazing but the mats. So:

They farm mat, in shallow warm pans behind the towns, sown and skimmed and pressed and dried into a grey-brown cake the size of a roof slate. It keeps for years, tastes of sulfur and warm bread, and is the floor of every meal on the plateau. Outsiders manage about a week of it.

They gather snail by the sack, off the outer bowls, scraped and boiled and served in the shell with a pin — cheap, endless, faintly sweet, and thrown to children by the handful. They net the seventy-degree shrimp with a sock on a wire hoop, at exactly the right band, at exactly the right hour, and this is delicacy work, feast work, and the good netters are known by name across four towns. They do not eat the waders, which are salt through and through and are also the plateau's pest control and are also, by common feeling, not to be touched. They do not eat the blind fish, which live in the deep blue vents that are also the drinking vents, and there is a sentence about that which taps out roughly as *not in our own throat*.

And once or twice a year, at cull, they eat oven — coarse dark meat, half a ton of it, the whole town at it for four days, the tallow rendered into the year's grease ration and the plates stacked for roofing and the great bones carried down to the mason's yard for the next tier's corners.

Everything else comes up the switchbacks: grain, tea, tobacco, dye, iron, and timber. There is no wood on the Sill. A wooden spoon is a wedding gift. A wooden door is a dowry. The rich have a chair.
