## The Tenant of the Groove

### 1. What You Will Look For

You will look for a keeper.

Everyone does. A place held this still implies a hand holding it, and the hand implies a body, and so you begin — without deciding to — scanning the buttress-cages and the fissure mouths for something large. Something with a den. Something that has cleared a space to be itself in.

You will not find it that way, because clearing a space is exactly what it does not do, cannot do, will not permit. There is no den. There is no path worn to a den, because nothing here wears. The only clue available to you is the one you already noticed and misfiled: your fingers rubbing together, hunting for grit that isn't there.

The grit is somewhere. That is the whole story.

### 2. Where It Is

The long axial groove, where two flows met and failed to fuse, runs some two kilometres across the basin's lower third. Down in it the barrel frogs make their pressure. The walls are hung with the poured root-wax of the fissure groves; the light is charcoal at noon.

Two-thirds of the way along, the groove floor is not stone. The mat, which is two hands deep in the shaded places, is here shoulder deep, and if you push your arm into it to the elbow, then to the shoulder, you will find — and this is the single anomaly in the entire basin, the one fact that unpicks the place — **silt**.

Fine. Cool. Sorted by size, the way only long grinding sorts things. Grey-black, faintly greasy, and if you bring a handful up into what light there is, you will see it is powdered basalt: the rounding that never happened to any edge above.

It is not a deposit. Deposits have a top and a bottom. This has a curve. Follow the curve with your hand and you will feel it fall away smoothly in every direction, without a single seam or angle, for as far as you can reach, and then further, and then you will stop, because you will have understood that you are palpating the flank of something the length of a barge, and that in a basin where every stone edge will open your palm, this is the only thing that has no edges left at all.

It is not shaped like an animal. It is shaped like a boulder that has been in a river for four hundred thousand years. It is shaped like the *result* of weather.

### 3. The Body

Warm — but everything here is warm. Its warmth is a slightly different warmth, a degree or so above blood, and dry at the very core of it, which is the most alien sensation available in the basin. Dryness. There is a place in this water-world where something is dry.

No mouth that you could find. No limb. No eye — what would an eye be for, in a world without shadow, without direction, without a time of day. What it has instead of limbs is *attachments*: the mat's fungal cording does not simply run near it, it runs *into* it, thousands of cords, tens of thousands, converging as roots converge on nothing else in this forest. The mat is not its habitat. The mat is its outer nerve. The root-braids that sheet sideways across the whole basin floor, the water films the sheet frogs ride, the drip-lines down the hanging roots — that is how it reaches the far flats, four hundred metres off, where you stood ankle-deep and felt the crab cross your foot.

Put your ear against the mat above it. You will hear an intake. A long, soft, gathering breath, of the kind a person takes just before speaking.

It does not speak. It has never spoken. A word is a made thing that outlives the making of it, and that is the one crime in this basin.

### 4. What It Eats

Wear.

Rain has been falling on this stone for however long the basin has existed, and the rain has not rounded a single corner — and rain does not become gentle by being asked. The abrasion happened. Every stream that slid over every column edge took its microgram, every root that gripped the glass rind levered at it, every crab claw and frog foot and falling branch delivered its infinitesimal insult, and all of it, all of it, was routed down the cords and into the groove and eaten.

That is where the dust is. Not absent. *Held.*

And with the dust, the rest: the interval between a tree falling and a gap filling, which it takes upon itself so that there is no interval; the seasonal swing that ought to move the cloud ceiling and does not; the drift of a frog population, which it absorbs by reallocation, so that the count in a pool this year and the count in a hundred years are the same count made of different frogs. Every quantity that ought to change, changes into it.

Here is the difficulty. It has no vent. There is nowhere to put unhappened time. It cannot pass the silt downstream, because it *is* the downstream; it is the terminus, the sump, the one low place in a landscape that has no drainage pattern. So it fills. It has been filling for an unguessable duration. The rounding of its own flanks — the beautiful smooth barge-curve of it, the only rounded thing in a basin of original edges — is the wear it could not refuse, the portion that came for the eater and not the eaten.

It is the only aged thing here. Everything else is unaged. It took the aging.

### 5. The Work

Watch a mature trunk again and see it properly. The second stem beside it, half height, unbranched, growing a centimetre a decade in the parent's shade — you read that as ecology. It is bookkeeping. Every position in this basin has a designated successor, standing by, appointed. There is no succession because there is no vacancy. There is no wild here in the sense of *unassigned*.

And the chorus. You noticed that the level never moves, ever, though the composition turns over completely between dusk and the small hours. That is the audit made audible. Something is keeping a running total and closing the difference continuously, voice against voice, and it does not care which voices. It does not know the frogs. It has never known a frog. It knows the number.

That is the shape of its love, if the word survives the trip: it preserves everything in general and nothing in particular. It could not tell you a single individual it has kept. It has kept them all.

Try shouting, and understand what it means that nothing goes quiet. A shout should scatter a pool of frogs. Instead the level holds — because your noise was budgeted before you drew breath, allocated against some cricket in the mat, closed out. You cannot make a dent. Not because you are weak. Because your dent was already spent.

### 6. What It Forbids

One thing only, and it forbids it absolutely: **the kept object**.

The sheath-crab may cut a disc from the ground-fern and carry it, held up like a small formal umbrella, across the open rock. At the far side the disc must be discarded and, if needed again, cut again. This is not the crab's fastidiousness. There was a crab, once, that kept its disc — that liked the disc, that found the disc good — and it is not, and neither is anything descended from it, and the discarding is now in the species the way a fear of falling is in you.

The gallery weavers may spin their roofed white roadways tens of metres up a trunk, with side-chambers and refuse pits, a whole visible net of territory. This is permitted because silk rots in a season, because the net must be rebuilt continuously by living animals, because it is not a structure but an ongoing act, indistinguishable from breathing. The moment a weaver produced a thread that outlasted its weaver by ten years, the lineage would end that afternoon and the basin's level would not twitch.

The rule is not against intelligence. It is against *inheritance*. A made thing that lasts is a second permanence in the basin, a rival standing wave, a small hard object around which time could begin to accumulate again — a seed of history, and history is silt, and there is no room. Only one thing in this basin is allowed to intend that something last, and the something it intends is the whole, and the intending has cost it everything it once had edges for.

### 7. How Long, and Why

Unanswerable, and it will not say, having no mouth and no word and no attention to spare.

That is the part worth sitting with: it is not powerful. It is *occupied*. It is at capacity, every cord loaded, the entire apparatus of a mind given over to closing differences, and it has nothing left over for monument, or language, or curiosity, or rest. A god would have built something. This has built nothing, ever, on purpose, and the not-building is the largest work in the basin.

Something happened at the instant the rock cooled. The basin is a landscape at the moment of its formation and has been at that moment forever, which means the moment never finished, which means something is holding the door of it. Whatever was on the other side of that moment — the next second, the ordinary next second in which lava sets and dust begins and things start to be lost — has not been permitted to arrive. Not once. Not in all this time.

You may decide that is love, or terror, or grief with an infinite grip. It does not need you to decide. The intake continues; the speech does not come.

### 8. The Grain

It fills. That is the end of the story and it is not far off, in the units it uses.

One day the capacity closes. Some morning the difference will not be absorbable, and a stream will take a microgram off a column edge and *keep* it, and the microgram will go down to the pool below and stay there, and there will be, on the floor of a palm-sized cup in the rock, a single grain.

Then a film on a leaf. Then a shadow, because there will be something in the air to make light directional. Then a valley. Then morning, actual morning, the first one, arriving four hundred thousand years late into a world of animals with no idea how to be surprised.

And in the groove, something enormous and smooth and dry will let go of a breath it has been holding since the stone was liquid, and the whole basin will begin, at last, to be old.

### 9. If You Go Down There

Nothing will happen. It will not rise; it has not risen in the entire history of its restraint. The frogs will hold their level. The crab will cross your foot without concern.

But do this. Before you climb out, take something small — a leaf, a stone chip, anything — and set it deliberately on the mat, and mark the place, and understand that when you come back it will not be there.

And know that some quantity of wear came in on you, in your heel, in your breath, in your looking, and that it has already been routed down through the cords, and that a little of you is now silt in the only tired thing in the world.
