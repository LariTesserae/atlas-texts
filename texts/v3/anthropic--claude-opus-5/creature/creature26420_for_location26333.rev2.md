## The people

They are the only things on the Upper Shelf that move of their own accord, and they know it, though they have never had to think it. Water moves because it is falling. Cloud moves because it is rising. Fern fronds move because the wind moves them. Stone moves once, briefly, and then is finished. Everything else on the plateau is being moved. Only a person goes somewhere.

Their word for themselves means, as nearly as it can be carried over, *the warm that walks*. It is not a boast. It is a category with one member.

They are small and lean and long-boned, without spare flesh, because there is no fat in the world to make any from. Skin brown and permanently damp; the shadowless light coming through the cloud will burn a shoulder in an afternoon, so the forearms and necks of the old are marked with the flat pale scarring of a lifetime of small burnings, and everyone wears the grey-green bast cloth over the shoulders even in the bath-yards, where they wear nothing else. Nakedness means nothing here. Bodies are simply the furniture of other people. What is private is nearness.

Their hands are the first thing a stranger would notice. The fingertips are white and ridged and soft-swollen, macerated, wrinkled from birth to death, because nothing here is ever dry; and under that softness the palms are glassed over with callus from cord and stone. They read rock with the flat of the hand. A mason laying a course will run her palm down a face and know, before the eye could tell her, whether the split ran true, because a true split is cold in a particular way under wet skin.

They walk flat-footed, barefoot, toes spread and gripping, in a short measured stride developed for wet rhyolite, and they walk everywhere at the same pace. Nobody strolls. Nobody hurries.

Nobody runs. Running is not a thing an adult does for any reason except one, and so it is not really locomotion at all but a word — the loudest word in the language. A person seen running is a stone coming down somewhere, and everyone who sees them will be moving away from the cliffs before they know what they have seen. It has happened four times in the lifetime of the oldest woman on the middle bench. Three times it was a mistake and the runner had to go from bench to bench afterwards explaining herself, quietly, to each street in turn.

## How they speak

They speak low, close, and little.

Sound carries so far and so cleanly across the shelf that a voice raised in the lower plaza arrives, thinned but entirely intelligible, at the upper bench. There is therefore no such thing as a private shout, and no such thing as talking across a courtyard. To speak to someone, you go to them and stand inside their arm's reach, and the conversation is conducted at a level that would be a murmur anywhere else. Conversation is a physical approach. To be spoken to is to be come to.

This makes company expensive and solitude worthless. The town has room for four thousand and holds two, and anyone at any hour can be entirely alone in a warm dry-laid street with the doors standing open and the floors faintly steaming. Nobody wants that; everybody has it. What is scarce is being crossed to. A person who comes up two flights of wet steps to stand beside you and say nine words has spent something.

The nod is the whole of greeting. There is no *hello*. The nod means: you are still on the roll.

## The roll, and the room

There is a fixed number of names.

Nobody knows who fixed it or when; it is older than the oldest recited thing. A child is not named at birth. A child is named when a name comes free, and a name comes free when somebody has finished with it, and until then the child is *the one waiting*, addressed by pointing and by tone, carried about and fed and loved and not yet, strictly, a person. Most wait a year or two. Some wait longer. The names are all places — *third drain under the wall*, *the corner where the light comes*, *lower flume*, *the step that rings* — and because the plan of the town never changes, the names never change either, and every one of them has been worn by hundreds of people in succession, and there has never at any moment been two of anyone.

The roll is why the couples wait. A pair who intend a child put their intention to the keeper of the roll and take their place in the queue, and the queue moves at the rate that people die, which is slowly, because nothing here kills. Some pairs come up in their twenties. Many come up in their forties. A good number never come up at all, and these are not pitied and are not consoled, because they are the ones who keep the room.

That is the name of the office and the name of the work: keeping the room. They are the ones who go every day into the empty quarters with a fern-frond broom and a clay pot of flume-water, and sweep the floors of houses no one has entered in three generations, and check the drains, and re-lay a course where frost that never comes has nonetheless done something, and leave the doors open, and leave. Two thousand swept rooms. Warm floors, nobody in them. The work is not symbolic to them. It is simply the work.

Ask why the room is kept, and you will be told the town is the right size. Press, and you will be told that a right-sized thing does not need to become another size. Press again — and you will have to press very hard, and you will probably have to be liked — and someone will tell you the thing that is not said in the streets: that the empty half is where the ones who were not named would have lived. This is considered a morbid idea and an unhelpful one, and the person who tells it to you will not say it twice.

## The reading

Everything on the Upper Shelf rests on one number, and one person watches it.

At the mouth of the great warm spring on the south flank there is a basin cut into the bedrock, and across the basin a notched sill, and beside the sill a seat, and on the seat, at the first thinning of the cloud each morning, a woman or a man whose whole life this is. They look at where the water stands against the notch. Then they walk up to the plan-house and press a mark into soft clay, and the tablet is fired when it is full, and stacked.

The stacks go back further than anything else in the town. There are rooms of them. They are the only extensive written record the people possess, and their entire content is the same number, recorded one hundred and forty thousand times, without variation.

This is not thought to be a waste. It is thought to be the most important thing anyone does. The value of the record is precisely and only that it does not change, and the reason a person sits at the notch every dawn of their adult life is so that on the morning the water stands a finger low, someone will be there to see it in the first hour rather than the first year.

The slit-drum on the upper bench is theirs. It is struck if the reading moves. It has been struck once in ninety years, when the spring shifted twenty feet along a joint and the flume had to be re-cut, and everyone alive remembers where they were standing.

## What the drains say

They have no instruments and no songs, and they have music, and it is plumbing.

Every roof drains into clay; every clay channel drops into a runnel; every runnel drops into stone. The drop can be cut. A mason who is only competent makes a drain that carries water. A mason who is more than that makes a drain that has a voice — a particular interval between the strike of the drop and the swallow of the runnel, a particular pitch off the lip of the gutter, tuned by the width of the notch and the height of the fall and the angle at which the water is thrown.

A house is judged by this. A courtship is conducted, partly, by it. On the still nights — and all nights are still — people sit on their own thresholds and listen to their own roofs, and to the roofs of the three houses nearest, and to the thin continuous seep of the whole bench beneath that, and to the rim falls under everything, which you cannot hear until you go behind a fold of rock and they stop.

The great works are the slow ones. A master cutter will set a runnel whose note is wrong — deliberately, carefully wrong — because the stone will wear at a rate she has calculated, and in eleven years the pitch will come down onto the note of the runnel below it, and hold there for perhaps thirty years, and then go on down and be wrong again in the other direction. Nobody hears the whole of such a work. Everybody hears some of it. When an old cutter dies, people go and stand in the street where her water is currently right, and say nothing, and listen to it for a while, and then go home.

## The argument

There is one disagreement on the Upper Shelf, and it is very old, and it is never resolved and never abandoned.

The ground is warm. Everyone agrees the warmth comes from underneath. One position holds that warmth is simply what ground is, the way hardness is what stone is, and that asking why the ground is warm is like asking why up is up. The other position holds that the warmth is *a quantity*, and that a quantity which is being given off is a quantity which is being used up, and that therefore — not soon, not in any life, but as a fact about the world — the shelf is cooling, and there will be a day when the puddles do not steam at dawn, and then a day, much later, when they do not thaw.

Nothing can settle it. There is no measurement that could; the doorsill temperature has never varied by anything the hand can find. So it is argued at the pitch at which everything here is argued, which is barely above breathing, in the bath-yards, between people who have been arguing it with each other for fifty years and would be bereft without it.

The cooling-party are not alarmists. They keep the room a little more scrupulously than the others, that is all, and they are more likely to be found at the rim at last light, looking down at the layered blue country, thinking about the fact that it is lower.

## The ones who are not quiet yet

Children are rare enough that every adult in the town knows every one of them by sight, and they are, until the quieting, the only fast loud pointless thing on the entire plateau.

A child runs. A child shrieks in the plaza for no reason at all, and the shriek goes out across the flats and comes back off the scarp, and old people on three benches lift their heads and smile and are slightly disturbed. There is nothing else in this world that does that. There has never been anything else in this world that does that. The people have no word for animal and no idea that they are watching one, and they are as near to it as they will ever come.

At around eight, a child is quieted. It is not much of a ceremony: they are taken up to the notch at dawn and shown the water standing where it stands, and shown the mark being pressed into the clay, and then walked down again the long way, on the wet stone, at the pace, and by the bottom of the steps they have generally understood. After that they walk like everyone else. Some of them go on, for the rest of their lives, holding it in.

Their other great business is shadows. For most of the day the light is even and white and nothing casts one. Twice a day, at the two ignitions, the deck thins and the low yellow light comes across and suddenly every person on the plateau has a second thing attached to them, lying on the wet rock, doing exactly what they do. Adults stop work and face the light for the few minutes it lasts, which is the closest thing they have to worship and is really just attention. Children chase each other's shadows and jump on them, which is tolerated. There is a saying, not quite believed and not quite disbelieved, that the shadow is the part of you that goes down.

## Endings

Very little kills anyone here. There are no predators and no vectors and nothing that bites; there is no winter, no famine in memory, and almost no violence. The list of ways to die is short enough to be recited and is recited: *the fall; the fall of stone; the water; the white lung*, which comes to old flume-cutters who have breathed the mineral steam for forty years and which is the commonest of all; and *the stillness*, which is what they call simply stopping, and which is regarded as the correct ending and is, quietly, hoped for.

The town skews old. Long lives, few births, and every face over fifty. It is a settlement of slow, damp, extremely competent old people, moving at one pace through streets built for twice their number.

The dead are not burned and cannot be buried, because there is no soil and nothing digs. They are carried out to a peat mat on the western flank — always the same one, for as long as anyone recalls, though it cannot be the same one, since mats let go — and laid in a cut in it, and the cut is closed with the sod, and within a year the clubmoss has taken the seam. They stay there while the mat thickens. And at some point, in ten years or two hundred, the mat lets go from its rock in one great sodden sheet, the way mats do, and slumps into the stream bed and goes over the rim.

So the dead go down. They are the only ones who do. The people do not think of the under-country as a destination or a reward; they think of it as where finished things end up, the way a drain is where water ends up, and they do not dwell on it, and they do not look over the rim for too long at last light, and if someone is looking over the rim for too long, at last light, repeatedly, for years, nobody stops them and nobody goes and stands beside them either.

There is one other way to go down, and it has been used once, and the date of it is remembered the way the dates of slab-falls are remembered, and the name that person carried was returned to the roll at the top of the rim path and given, within the month, to a child who had been waiting.

## If you came up

They would not harm you. They have no procedure for harm.

They would come and stand inside your arm's reach, one at a time, over several days, and ask you very quietly about the water where you come from — whether it is the same amount every morning — and they would not know what to do with your answer.

And the whole time they would be dealing, without a word for it and without a way to say it, with how you smell. Of leather. Of fat. Of something that was warm and walked and is now on your feet and in your pack and on your breath. The only smells they have ever had are sulphur, wet peat, cut fern, fired clay, smoke, and each other. They have no name for what is coming off you, and no category to file it under, and what they will feel — politely, silently, standing close because that is how one speaks — is the first new thing in nine hundred years.

They would be glad, on the whole, when you went back down.
