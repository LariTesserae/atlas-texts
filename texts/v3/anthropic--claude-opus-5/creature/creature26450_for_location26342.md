## The census of Chamber Four

### 1. The tenant of the socket

Start with the eel, because the eel is what a person means when they ask.

It is a female, and it is roughly ninety centimetres long, and it weighs what a full kettle weighs. It is not white. In the strobe of the surge it reads white, the way anything wet and pale reads white under a flash, but held in the hand it would be the colour of tallow gone slightly green, with a bronze cast along the flank that has been deepening for years. Its skin is thick and loose and continuously slick; the mucus is its main defence against water that arrives at twenty-nine degrees carrying dissolved iron, sulfide, and the fine grit of a city grinding itself down. It sheds and replaces that coat constantly. Some of it stays on the wall of the socket. Most of it goes out through the outfall with everything else.

Its eyes are present. They are two small dull ports, functional in the sense that a boarded window is a window. It has never needed them and has never quite lost them.

The mistake is to think of it as an animal enduring the noise. The noise is how it sees. An eel reads pressure along its whole length: a line of pores down each flank, a lace of canals over the skull, each one a hair in a jelly waiting to be bent. In a still pond this gives an eel a vague and useful sense of what is nearby. Here it gives it something closer to vision. Every forty-one seconds the hammer arrives and floods the chamber with a single enormous pulse of structure, and that pulse comes back to the eel's flank off the dome, off the bosses, off the polished cove, off the fused lump in the southeast, off the mouth of the intake and the bars of the outfall, all of it at once and all of it in a fixed geometry it has known for years. The room announces itself. It does this over and over, at the pace of a resting heart, and it has not lied once.

By any measure the eel's world is the best-lit thing in this account. What it lacks is not light. What it lacks is variation. It has never had a surprise. In the whole of its life the only novelty has been food.

It eats in the trough. It leaves the socket at a moment that a stopwatch would put between second twenty-nine and second thirty-three, takes whatever the last surge delivered into the low water, and is back inside before the shaft valve slams. It does not compute this. Nothing in it is doing arithmetic. It has been shaped by forty-one seconds the way the cove of the wall has been shaped by them, and the accuracy is of the same kind: not skill, but fit.

Around its fourteenth year in the chamber it began to silver. This is the eel's one great transformation, the one that ends with a return to the deep Atlantic and a single spawning and death: gut shrinking, eyes swelling, flanks going from yellow to bronze to that white the log-keepers noted. It spent a period — weeks, on and off — at the outfall grating, working its snout between bars set eleven centimetres apart in 1919 by men who were not thinking about eels. Then it went back to the socket and resumed eating, and it is still silver, and it will remain silver, and it will not spawn. The chamber is warm and constant and full of shrimp. It may live another forty years like this. Nothing here will kill it and it cannot leave, and its body has already committed to a journey that the plumbing has quietly cancelled.

### 2. Before it, and after it

The socket is the real inhabitant; the eels are what passes through.

It is a cast-iron cup, sixty millimetres across, that held a pipe hanger until 1948. Everywhere else in the chamber the surfaces have been laid over with mineral glaze, laminated ochre and liver-brown, half a millimetre a decade. Inside that socket the glaze is thin and interrupted, and its texture is wrong: not laid down, but worn away, and worn in a spiral, because a body enters and leaves in the same rotation every time and has done so since before the last war. It is the only surface in Chamber Four shaped by life rather than by water. The room's whole hundred and six years of patient chemistry, and one small hole where something keeps rubbing.

There have been at least nine tenants. Probably more. They arrive as glass eels, transparent as boiled noodle, drawn out of the sea and up the delta on the smell of fresh water, and they get past the intake screens because the screens have a torn corner at the bottom that was reported in 1961 and again in 1977, both times as an item requiring no action. The reports were correct. A screen that stops eels also stops sticks.

In the inspection sheets there is a line, added in a small deliberate hand in 1953 and photocopied forward through five revisions of the form, that reads: *Eel (S. bolthole) — cond:* Underneath it, in the successive inks of a great many men who could not speak to one another in that room and so wrote things down instead: *good. good. good. good. absent. small one. good. good.* And then, eleven years ago, in pencil, alongside the note about Bearing 4: *good.*

### 3. The two hundred

The shrimp are not individuals in any way it is useful to think about. They are a population held at roughly two hundred by the appetite of one eel, and they persist because the surge is not merely violence, it is delivery. Every forty-one seconds the chamber is resupplied.

They are two centimetres long and clear enough that the dark thread of the gut shows, and in the females a smudge of eggs under the tail. They eat the felts off the walls at the surge line. They do not attempt to hold position. Somewhere in their ancestry — and they have been here a very long time, longer than the eels, longer than the sodium fixture — the instinct to seek stillness stopped paying. The ones that hid died out of the count, because in Chamber Four hiding means missing the food. So they ride. Up seven metres in three seconds, through the half-second of white where the water goes opaque and luminous and everything is briefly visible, then down again in the collapse, tumbling, and they are eating the entire time.

There is one detail about them that is difficult to let go of. A shrimp knows which way is up by means of a small chamber in the base of each antennule, lined with grains it picks up from its surroundings, weighted, resting on hairs. After every moult it has to reline them: it selects grains, individually, and places them. In Chamber Four the only grains available are fragments of the room. Flakes of glaze, ochre and liver-brown, spalled off the wall by acid or by drumming. So each of the two hundred is walking around with a pinch of the chamber inside its head, telling it which way to be. And because the chamber turns them over completely, twice, every forty-one seconds, the up they compute is not a direction but an average of all directions, which is to say: nothing much. They have stopped needing to know.

### 4. The felts

By mass, the chamber is mostly bacteria, and by mass the bacteria are mostly eating the building.

The iron-oxidisers hold the middle ground, above the surge line and below the dome's springing: rust-red felts a centimetre thick, greasy to the touch, exhaling nothing, taking their living from the difference between iron in one state and iron in another. That difference is very small. They exploit it patiently. The three hanging bosses are partly their doing — every boss is a place where drip and bacterium have collaborated for a century into something between an udder and a fist, and the fist is largely a graveyard of the organisms that built it.

The sulfur-oxidisers hang in the warmest and worst air near the crown, in white pendulous threads a hand-span long, and they drip acid, and where they drip the brick has dissolved into shallow bowls. Nothing lives in those bowls. The threads move when the air is shoved sideways at the collapse of each surge, all together, in one direction, at one instant, and this is the closest thing in the chamber to a gesture.

They are not a community. They are a slow fire, burning the wall in the only way a wall can be burnt at thirty-one degrees in total darkness. In four hundred years, if the pumps run, they will have eaten through to the fill.

### 5. The travellers

And then there are the coin-sized ones, which nobody has classified, which glow faintly green, and which have a preference.

Preference is the interesting word. Nothing else here has one. The eel has fit, the shrimp have a habit, the felts have chemistry. But the circular colonies on the dome are going somewhere. Sample them a month apart and they have shifted a few millimetres, always away from the pillar of focused roar standing in the middle of the room, always toward the north-northeast quadrant of the dome, where the standing wave has a node — a place a few hand-widths across where the shove partly cancels itself and the pressure is merely enormous instead of unbearable. There is a quiet spot up there. They have been walking to it, by whatever slow means a smear of cells walks, for as long as anyone has bothered to photograph the dome.

They will not arrive. The node's position depends on the shape of the chamber, and the chamber's shape depends on the bosses, and the bosses grow half a centimetre every twenty years, and that is enough to shift the quiet spot faster than a colony can follow. So the whole population is permanently a little way behind a destination that keeps stepping aside, and has been for decades, and the pursuit is the only thing in Chamber Four that could honestly be called a life with a direction in it.

### 6. What is sealed in the lump

The conglomerate in the southeast cove is not alive, and there is something living inside it.

The lump is the size of a sheep: three wrenches, a boot sole, a brass tally disc, coins, a wedding band, a set of dentures, and the mortar of glaze that the room laid down around them once it had finished rolling them to the place it wanted them. Water does not get into the interior of it any more. Air certainly does not. Under that shell, in the pore space, there are cells — anaerobes, presumably, of the kind found in deep rock — dividing at a rate that would be measured in years per division if anyone measured it. They were sealed in at various dates across the last eighty years, as the lump accreted. They are still going. They are the oldest bodies in the chamber, and the slowest, and they have no idea they are inside a spanner.

The wedding band is small, and gold, and the log for the relevant decade is missing.

### 7. The dry rung

One more, for completeness, because it counts and it is easy to overlook.

At the top of the ladder, in the last metre before the hatch, where a little dry air still falls through from the access shaft and the walls are merely damp, there is a spider. It is small and pale and it is not a cave species, just an ordinary house spider some number of generations from a kitchen on Vetch Street. Its web is loose, badly rigged, and permanently trembling. Nothing has ever flown into it. It is living on the occasional midge that wanders down from the shaft, and on the fact that spiders can go a very long time on nothing at all.

It has never gone below the third rung. It is the frontier of the world above, and the frontier is holding, and losing.

### 8. The oldest inhabitant

But if the question is what lives here — what has been continuously present, has never once left, has shape and behaviour and a voice and is unquestionably the thing that all the rest of them are arranged around — then it is not any of these.

It is the column. Seven metres of water going up and coming down, forty-one seconds, no gaps, since 1919.

Not the same water. The water is only ever passing through; it comes out of the sinking ground, gets swallowed and beaten white and shoved along the line, and is in the estuary by evening. What persists is the form: a repeating shape made of borrowed material, holding its outline for a hundred and six years, singing a note it selected for itself out of the dome sometime in the 1950s and has held ever since without a rest. It built the cove you could lie in. It rounded the emperors off the brickwork and planed two centuries of construction flush. It carried the spanner across the floor and set it down in the corner and cemented it. It taught one eel its schedule and two hundred shrimp their indifference to up. Everyone on Vetch Street has been replaced at least once since it began. It has not been interrupted.

That is the resident. The eel is a lodger; the felts are a slow disease of the walls; the glowing colonies are pilgrims after a quiet that keeps moving. The tenant proper is a heartbeat that never had a body, in a room thirty paces across, in the dark, at thirty-one degrees, with nobody down there to hear it and no requirement that anybody should be.

The lamp burns. The dome sings. The eel counts, and is counted, in pencil, once every eleven years.
