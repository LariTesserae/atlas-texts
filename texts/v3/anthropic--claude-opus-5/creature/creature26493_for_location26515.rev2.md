## Who is there

Thirty-one.

They know the number exactly. You will not learn it, and you will very likely not learn even that the number exists, because by the time you have walked down the pale slope and noticed the veins in the sky, you have been seen, and being seen here does not involve anyone moving toward you.

They are tall and they are thin past any measure you would call healthy. A grown one stands about a metre and a half, and weighs what a child weighs. The ribs are wide-set and visible, the chest flat, the neck long, the hands and feet broad and thin like leaves themselves — all of it surface, nothing of it stored. There is no fat anywhere on any of them. There is nothing to store fat against. In a world of unvarying warmth and unvarying supply, a reserve is just extra body to keep cool, and keeping cool is the whole problem of their lives.

Because they cannot. Forty degrees and near-saturated: sweat beads and sits and does nothing. Their skin is thin and richly vascular and slightly loose over the long bones, and it is the colour of the light, which is to say you cannot say what colour it is — brass under the leaf, and something grey and startling if you ever saw one at the rim. They shed heat the only way the basin permits: by conduction, lying flat on wet stone. Every one of them is an expert in the temperature of rock. They know which pan-margins run coolest, which slab under which column stays a degree and a half below the air because the roof is thinner there and the night reaches it better. A journey across the basin is not a walk. It is a sequence of lyings-down with walks between them, and the lyings-down are the longer part.

They move at the speed of the climbers. This is not imitation. It is the same arithmetic solved by two different bodies.

## What they eat, which is almost nothing

The plant has finished. It does not fruit, does not seed, does not bud. There is no litter, no fungus, no grub, no egg. There is nothing in the basin that was ever designed to be eaten.

What there is, is healing.

If you cut the living skin off a stem-wall — a shallow cut, a hand long, no deeper than the green — the plant closes it. That is the last growth left in the world, and they have organised their entire existence around provoking it. Each of them carries a sliver of the dark hard stone that runs in seams through the collapse blocks, and each sliver has a name and a history and is older than anyone alive, because nothing here dulls what is not used and nothing here breaks what is not dropped. There are, in total, nine knives. It is possible to say that sentence about the world.

They cut. They come back, and they do not know after how long, and they take the callus. It is the texture of soft bark and tastes of nothing whatsoever. They eat perhaps a double handful a day and drink four or five times their own volume in water over the same span, and their guts are long and slow and quiet, and they are hungry in a low flat way from birth to death and have no word for it, having nothing to contrast it with.

Every cut is a subtraction from a fixed total. They know this the way you know the ground is down. The map of where they have cut and where they have not is the largest single object any of them carries, and it is carried in the head, because there are no marks on anything, and there will be no marks on anything, because a mark here is permanent and they have decided against permanence with a firmness you will find nowhere in your own species.

## Water, and the one drop

They fetch from the pans in sealed cane-lengths. They wash constantly, which is not cleanliness but cooling.

In each shelter there is a stone with a shallow pecked basin in it, and in that basin there is always one drop of water. Not a film, not a puddle: one drop, the size of the plum-sized beads that come down out of the roof, held by tension in a hollow pecked to exactly fit it. It is not for drinking. It is not for anything. The discipline is that it must never be dry and must never be two, and a person tends that drop every day of their life, and when the drop goes — and drops do go, in a hundred years, once or twice — it is a matter serious enough to be told to others at a distance of kilometres.

You may take this for worship. It is closer to a clock. It is the only instrument they have that reads, continuously and truthfully, *now*.

## The shelters

The domes are not shelter. There is nothing to be sheltered from: no rain, no wind, no cold, no night worth the name, no animal that would touch you. A dome is three metres of swept travertine chip with a roof over it, and the roof's only function is that the person lying beneath it can see it and know whose it is.

They are graves, in the sense that a grave is a place a name is kept. Each was built for one person, from canes taken out of the lattice — and taking canes out of the lattice is the gravest subtraction there is, so a dome is built only when a new person has come into the number, and it is never unbuilt, because unbuilding would not give the canes back. There are twenty-two along the eastern pans and perhaps forty more elsewhere, and there are thirty-one people.

The empty ones are swept. All of them. This is the work: to walk the long slow circuit of the domes of the dead, sweep the chips flat with the flat of a hand, and fill the drop. A young person has three or four to tend. An old person has thirty. It is the only quantity in the basin that increases, and it increases at exactly the rate people leave, and they are aware of the joke in this and do not find it funny, but they do find it sufficient.

And all the doorways face the same way. You were right that it is not north and not sunrise and not the weather. It is the southwestern narrows, where the rolled rim of the roof-leaf lifts highest off the rock — the one place in eleven kilometres where a person carrying another person can pass out without stooping.

## Speech

The silence is absorbent and so their voices are quiet and close. Two of them talking sit with their heads a hand apart, and at ten metres you would hear nothing at all. They can go a season without speaking and this is not loneliness; the nearest other is usually a day's lyings-down away.

When they do meet, what they exchange is inventory.

One recites what is in a region — the angle of a propped block, the count of curtain-layers in a hall, which stem-walls are cut and which are closed, where a particular climber was, and before that, and before that. The other listens for drift. If the two accounts differ, they walk, together, sometimes for days, to the disputed place and look at it. This is their science, their gossip, their court and their love. Almost always the accounts agree and the world is confirmed to be exactly as it was, and this is not boring to them. It is the news, and the news is good.

They log the ticks. A fibre going in a cane under load, somewhere, once in a while, never twice in the same place — that is a *change*, an actual event in the fabric, and they can locate one by ear at two hundred metres and will go and find it and look at the cane for a long time and then carry the fact of it across the basin. A tick is a small story. There are people remembered chiefly for a tick they found.

They have no collective name for themselves, having nobody to be distinguished from. They have no word for wind. Their word for the outside is the same as their word for afterward.

You cannot tell who is old. Nothing wears here, including them — there is no dust to line the skin, no sun to ruin it, no cold, no labour, no disease, because disease requires an agent and no agent ever got in. They break, sometimes, and then they mend. They die of simply running out, at ages none of them can name, because the only way to reckon age is by the depth of a person's account: how far back the recitation goes, how many holders of a fact stand between the speaker and the fact. The oldest are those whose accounts reach furthest. The deepest thing any of them holds is not an origin or a myth. It is a description of a person nobody now has ever seen, standing at the eastern pans with both hands in the water, looking up.

## The climbers

They do not eat them and never have. A few hundred, not increasing, not decreasing — the same closed arithmetic, the same finished world, arrived at independently.

But the people know them individually. Each climber has a name and a life recorded in the accounts: where it was, which decks it works, which vein it drinks from, who first saw it and who has it now. A climber crossing a chamber over twenty minutes is, in a basin where the only other event is a noise from the roof twice a day, an unfolding. There are people who have watched the same climber for sixty years and can tell you every deck it has ever slept on, in order.

The climbers do not care. There is no fear in them and nothing has ever taught them any. They will take a hanging drop a metre from a seated person and neither will alter.

## The leaving

Nothing decays. This is the central rule, and here is where it finally bites.

A body in the basin would not go. It would dry in the heat under the constant light and stay, intact, pale, forever, the largest and most permanent thing anyone ever added to the world. So they do not permit it.

When a person is running out they are carried — or they walk, slowly, in stages, over many days, with others lying down beside them at each stage — to the southwestern narrows, and out under the rolled rim into the gap where the white light comes in and the air moves and smells of dust and distance. They are set down past the edge, on the ordinary weathering rock, in the wind, where there are bacteria and flies and frost and consequence. Then the ones who carried them come back in.

That is the whole of it. No ceremony beyond the carrying. They call it by the same word as *afterward*.

And the reason, when you finally get it out of them, is not reverence. It is the roof. Twice a day a primary vein groans under the accumulated weight of its own sweat and the sound rolls the length of the basin and takes eight or nine seconds to die, and every one of them, every time, stops whatever they are doing and listens to the end of it. They know what the sound is. They know the leaf is under load and holding and that *holding* is a verb with an end in it. Whatever is inside when it comes down will be kept, pressed, perfect and unrotting, for as long as there is anything at all.

They will not leave their people to be kept. They are a people whose single organising conviction is that they are owed an ending, and who go to considerable trouble, one at a time, to fetch it from outside.

## You

They will follow you at a distance of a hundred metres and you will not see them.

They will watch you eat — the noise of it, the *volume* of it, four or five times what a person takes in a day, taken in one sitting, and then again — and they will find it difficult to look away, the way you would find it difficult to look away from a man setting fire to money. They will note where you cut and how deep and whether you closed it. They will find your footprints in the soft margins of the travertine, which cannot be swept out and will not wear, and they will stand over them for a long while, and then carry the fact of them across the basin to the others: the shape of your foot, the length of your stride, the direction you were going when you were in a hurry.

And when you walk out under the rim into the white violent light and the first moving air, going the way their dead go, they will watch you the whole way and understand exactly what you are doing and be, very slightly and very kindly, in error about why.

You will be in the account for four hundred years.
