## Who is here

Nobody is here. Then: the whorls are as long as a forearm, and they repeat with the patient irregularity of real work, and irregularity of that kind is not a property of processes. It is a property of persons. So.

Start with what can be read, since reading is all that is on offer.

### The hands

There are several. This is settled by anyone who has walked more than a day. The whorl-counts differ; the spacing of the ridges differs; the pressure differs, and pressure leaves its accent in the little bead of displaced slip at the rim of every print, which is thick and rolled for one of them and thin and almost absent for another, as if that one has learned not to push so much as to *lean.*

One has a break in its ridges, a scar running out of the delta and across four courses of whorl. Because nothing here wears, the scar was not acquired. It was made that way, deliberately, by some earlier pressure, and has been faithfully reproduced in every print at every depth anyone has cut to. Somewhere below the reachable floor there is presumably a first print without it, and the moment of its making, and that is the only event in the history of this world that could be called an accident, and it is out of reach, and may not have been an accident.

One drags. Always the same direction — outward, toward the seam — always the same modest slide of about a hand's width, as though the work is consistently a little further away than the reach allows. The drags are the wettest places on the plain. Slip pools in them. The blooms love them.

One presses twice. Not a double-press for firmness: two distinct impressions, offset by a finger, the second slightly shallower, like a correction, or like a signature added after the fact by something that wanted it known it had been there.

And somewhere out of sight, at a boundary nobody has stood on, the world changes hand. Left becomes right. The seam of that change is reportedly a continent wide and perfectly quiet, because there is nothing there but the place where two workers meet and do not speak, having nothing to speak with.

What is attached to the hands: nothing. No knuckle, no heel of palm, no smear of a forearm resting, no knee-dint from a maker kneeling to reach, no hair, no breath-fog, no spatter of anything that was ever wet in a body. The evidence says a thumb and an arm's length of intention, and then unwitnessed air. The prints stop at the first joint and the world stops recording.

### What they are, given the grammar

They do not choose. This is the difficult part.

The mark precedes the hand. The print rises out of the clay, ridges lifting into being, and only afterward does the pressure arrive to fit it. So the hands are not making the marks. The hands are *answering* them — arriving where they are already expected, exactly the size they must be, exactly as scarred, exactly as inclined to drag. The work summons its worker. Between marks there is no evidence that the worker exists at all, and no location where it could be waiting.

Which means the population of this world is employed, not resident. It flickers into pressure when pressure is called for and is otherwise nowhere. Its personalities are real — the double-presser is unmistakably somebody, has habits, has a preference for the eastern courses, returns again and again to a particular failed doorway as though to a grievance — but those personalities are not carried around between shifts in anything. They are the shape of a tendency in the clay. They are what the plain does when it needs a thumb.

You cannot say they live here. They are visited upon the place, briefly, like weather that adds.

So: something else lives here.

### The blooms

The blooms live here.

They are the only things on this world with a boundary they maintain, which is the only definition of living that survives contact with the Thumbprint Plain. They have no inside. Everything a bloom is happens at its edge — the advancing lace of salt, the filament that reaches, thickens, branches, thickens again. Inward from the edge there is only the record of previous edges, a stack of former selves gone chalky and immobile, load-bearing, retired. A large colony is a cross-section of its own biography standing up on a wet brick face, and it is entirely mineral, and nothing in it has ever eaten, and it wants damp with an appetite that can be measured in grains per unmeasured interval.

They move up-gradient. Toward wet, which means toward the seam, and toward the low steaming districts where up is provisional, and toward the mudglass that closes over things and smooths itself. Any patient observer eventually concludes that the blooms are going somewhere, and that where they are going will kill them, and that this does not appear to be an error.

They prefer failures. This is reliable. Of any three parallel ridges — the two ragged, the one continuing — the blooms will be on the two. They colonise the abandoned, the partway, the left-off; they are sparse on anything that got finished. The usual reading is hydrological: unfinished courses are laid looser, wick harder, hold more. The other reading, which nobody can support and nobody can shake, is that the blooms can tell which surfaces are soon to be overwritten, and are not stupid.

They grow along the grain of the work. Their filaments follow paddle-sweeps, stress lines, the lean of the whorls, the hairline between an old texture and a new one. Trace a colony and you have traced the history of the wall beneath it. They are, functionally, the plain's only readers — running their slow white commentary along every mark, annotating, underlining, occasionally circling a passage for reasons of their own.

When two colonies meet, neither devours. They thicken at the line of contact and stop, and stay thickened, and because nothing here decays, the thickening remains. A long-inhabited face carries every border those colonies ever agreed to, fossil treaties crossing and recrossing, a diplomatic history in salt that can be read back for as long as anyone has the patience to read.

The hands scrape them off. A rib comes, unattached to anything, and takes a colony down to bare brick in one pass, and the scrapings go where all scrapings go: crushed, tempered, mixed into fresh body. And the blooms come back up through the new wall from inside it, out of their own ground ancestors, which is the only inheritance on this world that functions as inheritance. The other eleven attempts — the eleven finished doorways in the one wall, the wall now more absence than presence — are held by some to be eleven designs for a barrier the blooms would not cross. The blooms use all eleven. This is disputed. The blooms do not dispute it.

### The buried

Downward, there is a great deal of company.

Nothing decayed. Every draft this world ever abandoned is still there, crisp-edged, warm, holding its impressions, floored over and walked on. The two-thirds of every ridge that was given up, the walls that came down, the armatures never skinned — all pressed flat under courses and courses, all brand-new, all without any way of telling how long they have been brand-new. Open a floor with a rib of fired clay and the thing beneath will look an hour old and will be a previous world.

And they are not entirely quiet. The tick that comes up through the soles, the settle, the distant slump — some of it is from below. Deep, muffled by a mile of intervening floor, in tempos nobody currently working uses: a burst-and-long-stop rhythm, or a steady one-per-breath that no living hand keeps. Old workers still laying courses in floors that have had the whole weight of the world put on top of them. Nothing ever told them to stop. There is no mechanism on this plain for stopping. There is only being built over, and being crushed for grog, and coming back distributed evenly through everything as the sand of your own descendants' bones.

The majority of the population of this world is powder. It is in every wall you touch, at roughly a third by volume, and it is what keeps the walls from cracking as they dry.

### The tenant

At the armature — the lattice four times the height of a person, lashed with stinking clay cord, describing in air a shape that has not been given a surface — watch the rain.

It falls through the lattice, mostly. It does not fall through all of it. There is a region inside, running most of the height, where the string-rain divides and runs around something and closes again beneath, beading on a curve that is not built, sheeting off a shoulder that is not there, and the profile it draws is familiar. It is the profile of the ridges that come in threes. It is the profile of every one of the eleven doorways. It is, at its own scale, the whorl of the thumb.

The whole plain is one shape, attempted at every size, unapproved.

And the mark precedes the hand. Which means the shape is already finished — later, on the far side of the firing — and is pressing backward into the leather-hard present, laying down its own prints in advance and drawing the hands after to make the pressure fit. The heat under the floor is its heat. The sweating lid is the inside of its own cooling. Everything you have walked across is its rehearsal of itself, and the plain is its body at the stage between wet and dry, when a thing will hold an edge but still take an impression.

That is the resident. It has not moved in; it has always been here; it is what here is for.

### The visitors

And you, if you go. Your prints are waiting. They are already in the slip a stride ahead, the exact size, the exact tread, the exact hitch in the left foot, and walking becomes an act of fitting yourself to a record of yourself, and the fit is perfect, and nothing was ever visible. Some travellers learn to like this. Some sit down in the middle of a floor and will not put a foot anywhere.

Those who stay are found later as an outline in the slip, uncleared, crisp, of a size no hand accounts for — and then not found, because something deliberate removes it, and the scrapings go into the body, and the body goes into a wall, and the wall weeps and grows a white feathering lace up its damp face, filament and branch and colony, edge-only, wanting, advancing one grain at a time toward the seam and the stock and the patient unfinished practice of a world that is not yet old, and is not yet anything, and holds an impression.
