## The Rind

Begin with the tenant nobody counts.

It is ten centimetres tall and it is the whole economy. Along the joint-cracks, where the fog-milk meets the sour breath coming up out of the stone, the rind grows in overlapping shelves and lobes: black where it works iron, a foxed reddish brown where it works sulfur, and here and there a sheet of something pale and swollen that has no business being called a plant and isn't one. It has no cells that would satisfy you. It is a mat of cooperating chemistries, a hundred lineages agreeing to share a gradient, and its only ambition is to sit exactly at the line where two poisons cancel.

It cannot hear. It is the one citizen of the basin exempt from the rule.

And yet it speaks. The swollen pale rind blisters as it matures, and the blisters fill with gas, and at some threshold they split with a wet flat *pock* like a knuckle cracked underwater. Across a good seam after a rain there are eight or nine hundred of these a minute. That is the rind calling the scrapers to come and eat it, because the spores ride the gut and are laid down again, twenty metres along, in a warm slick of digested mineral. The rind has no interest in surviving any particular scraper. It is interested in being everywhere, and being eaten is how it travels.

So the first voice in the basin belongs to something that has never heard a sound in its life, and what it is saying is *here, take me.*

## The Grain

Three millimetres. A hard little lozenge of calcite drawn out of the mist, six legs it barely uses, and a pair of paddles on the back it uses constantly. It is deaf. At that size there is nothing to hear with; the whole animal is smaller than the wavelengths that rule everyone else.

The grain lives by touch and by the smell of its own dead. A curtain of grain is not a flock but a physics: each animal flying at random, colliding, turning, and the curtain's shape maintained purely by the rate of collision, which is why the curtains hang in those slow vertical folds, and why they thin and thicken like fabric in a draught nobody can feel. There is no leader. There is no decision anywhere in it.

Twice a day the lid comes down and the curtains rise on the cold slump of air. This is not flight so much as being lifted. Most of what goes up is eaten within four minutes by the clatter, and this is the point: the grain's breeding cannot happen anywhere but in the roost-crust, and the only reliable way to arrive there is inside something else. A fraction pass through. They cement their eggs into the fouled underside of the ceiling with a gum they make from their own dissolving shells, and die immediately, and fall, and are the snow.

The basin's snow is a species conducting its wedding.

## The Scrapers

Flat as a tongue and as muscular. The underside is one foot; the upper surface is a set of shingled plates, dull and pitted, that the grain-fall cements into a little false floor of its own, so that an old scraper carries a few hundred grams of the basin on its back and looks, from above, like a piece of ground that has decided to leave.

The mouth is a file, and the file is also the eye. A scraper rasping hears its rasp return off the seam wall, off the lip of the bowl, off the flanks of its neighbours, and from this it builds a picture perhaps thirty centimetres across. That is the extent of the world it will ever know: a hand's breadth of rind, and the pitches of the four scrapers touching it. Pitch is size. The young rasp high and thin, the old grind low. A rank of four hundred thousand is chorded by age, and a scraper that finds itself surrounded by the wrong pitches will shove sideways for hours until the chord sounds correct again. Nobody knows what this accomplishes. It has been going on long enough that the bowls are worn into lenses that focus the chorus back onto the animals producing it, which is the closest thing in the basin to a city.

They split rather than breed. A scraper that has fed well for two years thins along a line, and the line tears over several days, and there are two, each with half the file, each rasping an octave up, each immediately ordinary.

There is a second kind, and nobody would separate them if the second kind ever came down. On the flanks of the mothers, in the warm crusted rind that grows there, live scrapers that are paler, softer-plated, and smaller, and that have never in twenty generations touched level ground. They are born on a living wall and they die on it. If one falls it lands on its back, which it cannot correct, and the floor-scrapers rasp it into gravel without ever registering that it was one of them.

## The Clatter

Millions of them, hanging. The claws are ratcheted and do not open under load, which means a clatter hangs at no cost and can hang dead for weeks before the crust it is set into crumbles. It also means the floor is fatal. A grounded clatter can shriek, and does, at a pitch so high and so panicked that the whole pan can feel it as a sort of glassy pressure, but it cannot right itself, and the ranks arrive within the hour.

A colony is one instrument. They pulse in rota, a rolling sequence around the roost so that the voice never gaps, and each animal hears the colony's return rather than its own, which is why a lone clatter is helpless and blunders, and why a colony of four million can peel off the ceiling in a single sheet and thread itself down through the curtains without a collision. The scalloped underside of the fog lid over a roost is not weather. It is erosion by pulse. Three thousand years of a colony shouting at the same patch of accreted filth has carved that filth into a shape that returns the shout more cleanly, and the colony now navigates by a map it spent thirty centuries carving into the sky.

They are deaf below about two kilohertz. The mothers moan at the bottom of everything. In the entire history of the basin, the clatter and the mothers have never once perceived one another.

## The Listeners

Two metres of rope-bodied animal, no thicker than a forearm, running on forty short legs, with a bulbous drum-head it holds slightly raised. It ticks. Slow ticks when it is looking far, fast ticks when it is looking close, and a long shivering roll when it is looking hard.

The listener is the finest ear in the basin and it lives on scrapers, which it prises up at the edge and turns and empties. That is not the interesting thing about it. The interesting thing is that a listener comes back. It walks a route, and it walks the same route, and it walks it for sixty years, and it knows when a pillar has lost a flake. Old listeners tick with idiosyncratic stutters, personal as a limp, and other animals along the seam learn those stutters and do not scatter for them. A listener is the only thing in the basin that is a *somebody* to anything else.

And then, somewhere past eighty, it softens.

The legs resorb. The long body swells and loses its rope, going soft and heavy and hot, and the animal goes looking for a gullet — one of the throat-holes down into the hollow — and wedges itself in, and never comes out. Over about nine months the drum descends. It goes down out of the tick, down out of anything you'd call a voice, and settles at last into the moan.

## The Mothers

So the mothers are not another creature. They are what a listener becomes.

Five metres of soft bulk plugging a hole that opens onto sixty kilometres of hollow carbonate, moaning into it, driving it, holding the note that the whole basin is tuned to. Their flanks grow rind. Their rind grows scrapers. Things fall into the mouths they no longer have to move and they take it. A mother is a place with a pulse and it is also, if you want to be sentimental about it, a memory: that long descending moan is a walking route, sixty years of one animal's ticking, played back at a ten-thousandth of speed.

That is the basin's note. It is not a physical accident that biology adapted to. It is every listener that ever walked, slowed down until the individual dissolved, layered a few thousand deep, sustained without a gap for longer than the pillars have been fluted. The rock was ground into a shape that sustains it. The animals were ground into shapes that sustain the shape. And a listener out on the pan, forty years old, ticking along its route, is walking through the audible interior of everyone who came before it, and using their voices to see by.

## The Hush

You will not be described this animal well because nothing in the basin has ever perceived one for longer than a second and a half.

It is broad and soft and about the thickness of a mattress, and it is covered, everywhere, in a pelt of fine branching filaments that dissipate sound into heat. To hearing it is not dark; it is matte. It absorbs. It leaves a hole in the world exactly its own size and the hole is the only evidence that it exists.

But it has the same eyes as everyone else, which is to say it has none, and the pelt that hides it from the basin also blinds it. So the hunt goes like this. It clicks — once, thin, from a slit under the front margin — and in that instant it sees perfectly and is seen perfectly, and every scraper within sixty metres feels the shape of a predator and begins to run. Then it goes dark, and crosses the ground toward a place that existed once, a second ago, in memory.

It lives in the gap. Its entire experience of the world is a series of still images taken a minute apart, with a long blind crawl through nothing in between, and the crawl is conducted entirely on faith that the basin has not changed. Roughly one attempt in nine connects.

They cannot find each other. Two hushes within ten metres are each in a private void. To mate, one must call — properly, loudly, a rising two-note cry that carries four kilometres and is the single most conspicuous sound the basin ever produces — and then sit in the open and wait to see what arrives. Sometimes it is another hush. Often it is a listener, which eats the young but not the adults, or something from the gullets, or nothing at all for three nights running while the caller sits there advertising.

The young ride in the pelt. When they are grown enough, they slip out, and go quiet, and are never located again by anything, including their mother, who does not look for them, having no way to conceive of looking.

## The Floor

A thin film over everything, working. It takes the calcite the grain left, the plates the scrapers shed, the bone gravel, the droppings, the corpses, and it cements. It is the same chemistry as the rind, run backward and downward and given no light and no hurry. Everything that dies in the basin is floor within four years. The crackle underfoot is this year's dead not yet set. Below the crackle it booms because a hundred centuries of set dead is a hollow thing, and the boom is the resonator, and the resonator is why the note exists.

## The Soft

And then there is the exception, in the wells, where the rot has gone in the other direction.

The grey sponge lining a quiet-well is the rind's underground cousin — the same ancient arrangement of poisons, but grown inward, dissolving the carbonate and reprecipitating it as foam, and the foam does not return an echo. It is not doing this on purpose. It has no purpose. It is simply that the one habit this lineage has is making holes, and in a world that sees with sound, a hole is a blindness, and a blindness spreads.

Something lives down there.

They are the descendants of whatever fell in first — you could not say what; the lineage has thrown away everything that would tell you. They are pale, soft-bodied, unhurried, and small, about the length of a hand. They have no drum, no file, no click, no tick. Several branches have lost hearing entirely. They eat the padding: ten thousand years of animals that circled in the dark and lay down, compacted into a peat of silence.

They have one sense worth the name. Along the belly runs a plate of dense mineral, coupled to the rock, and through it, through metres of sponge, through everything that swallows every other sound, comes the note. The mothers' moan is too long and too slow to be absorbed. It is the only signal that reaches the floor of a quiet-well, and it arrives not as noise but as a pressure, a direction, a faint pull upward and outward.

They climb toward it. All of them, always, slowly, and never arrive, because the sponge is soft and gives way and the well is deep and their lives are short.

They cannot find each other. There is no mechanism by which one could. They mate when they collide, which is rare and accidental, and otherwise each individual passes its entire existence without any evidence that another of its kind exists anywhere. Each is, as far as it will ever know, the only thing that has ever lived.

And each one spends that life oriented, with great patience, toward the sound of the basin's whole body — the only creature down here that perceives it as one thing, undivided, a single enormous animal groaning somewhere above. They die in that posture. Their bodies pad the floor. The floor gets softer. The well gets quieter. The note comes through a little purer to the next one.

The wells widen by perhaps two millimetres a century.

That is the only unfinished argument left in the basin. Everything else reached its answer and stopped: the pillars, the lenses, the chord, the balance, the word *established.* But the holes are still growing, and they grow by eating the medium in which everything here sees, and they are fed, quietly, by the thing they are slowly deafening.

Not soon. Nothing here is soon. But the basin is falling, very slowly, into its own silence, and the creatures doing it are blind, alone, and facing the right way.
