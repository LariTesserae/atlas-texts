## The Omission

The account above is very nearly complete, and it has one hole in it, and the hole is shaped like a person.

Look again at the ramps. *Old landslip terraces grown over and softened into staircases for something with long legs.* Landslip does not make stairs. Landslip makes rubble, and rubble under this much rain makes a slope, and a slope grows over into a slope. What makes a stair is a hundred thousand years of the same weight coming down in the same place, at the same interval, high-stepping, and the moss healing the tread behind it.

Look at the single knock of wood, *somewhere, very occasionally.* Nothing in the described fauna knocks. Bills tap and seed capsules tock, and neither carries; a knock carries eighty meters through wet air and a hundred and twenty at dawn, which is precisely as far as it needs to.

Look at the last twenty minutes before the fog closes, when the crowns show their corona across the valley and some of them are steaming and some of them are, apparently at random, going dim.

## The Body

Standing, two and three-quarter meters. Weighing, at that height, about as much as a large dog: seventy kilos, which is the whole engineering problem of them solved. The Shelf will not carry weight. Concentrate mass and the mat parts and the peat takes it, and the wound in the mat is a wound in the ground plane, and that is the nearest thing they have to a crime. So: light bones, long bones, a chest like a lantern frame, and feet.

The feet are the best part of them. Three toes, splayed to the width of a shovel blade, webbed between with stiff bristle that mats with moss and stays matted, so that each foot is by middle age a small permanent raft of the plateau's own surface. A footfall depresses the ground a hand's breadth and the ground comes back. Step and it comes back. They walk on the chest of the sleeping thing and never wake it. A print fills with water before the foot has finished lifting and is closed by growth inside a day, and this is not stealth; it is only what happens here to anything that does not keep moving.

The legs fold high, the knee-equivalent up near the hip, so that in fog a person reads first as a pair of hanging arms and a dark face at four meters with nothing beneath it. The arms are longer than the legs. That is the kinship: they are cousin to the slender wet-eyed browser in the canopy, the insomniac that eats charge-grazers by the hundred and never hurries, and if you saw the two of them together you would call them the same animal and you would be nearly right. One went up into the crowns and stayed small. The other came down onto the peat and got tall and got the other thing.

## An Address

Nothing is bare. That rule does not stop at the skin.

A person's pelt is not fur so much as a substrate: coarse, hollow, held open by its own crimp, and laid down in it deliberately, over decades, is a garden. Mat moss first, the conductive kind, iron and manganese in laminae, so that the animal is continuous with the ground plane and feels itself to be — a walking patch of the plateau, at no potential difference from its own valley. Then liverworts along the shoulders where the run-off channels. Then the film, and in the film, animals. A grown person carries perhaps four hundred grams of other lives and a slow drip off the elbows, and smells warm and green and faintly of storm, like the inside of a stack tree, which is where they sleep.

Two areas are kept clear, and kept clear obsessively, with a wetted finger, several times a day.

The face. Dark, hairless, wet, wide-eyed in the way of things that live in even light and have never needed to read a shadow; and across the brow and both cheekbones, a fan of fine white filaments, five or six hundred of them, which lie flat in wind and stand up when a person stops to listen. That is the array.

The hands. Four fingers, no claw, palms bare and slightly salt. They lick them before touching anything that matters.

## The Sense

We say the gradient is always on and mean it as a fact about the landscape. For them it is the floor of experience, prior to sight, and about as remarkable to them as the pull of the ground is to us.

A stand of stack trees in full pump is, to the array, a lit thing — a lattice of columns each holding potential at its crown, each bridged to the moss by warm vines, the whole of it humming at the very bottom of nothing. A dead tree is a hole in the lattice. A slumped bank where the peat has cut is a cold slash that will take ten years to close and can be felt across a valley like a missing tooth. Weather arriving is a change in the field an hour before it is a change in the air. Two hundred meters away, a body, warm and wet and moving, drags a small shadow through the potential; a person feels another person coming long before either could possibly see, and knows from the shape of the shadow how tall, how heavy, how old, and often who.

They do not need to see far. Seeing far is not available. What is available is *knowing where you are*, continuously, in a hundred-kilometer wired room, with your eyes largely shut and your face turned slightly aside.
