## The People of the Ninth Sill

### Bodies

Start with the breathing, because they do.

At rest an adult of the plateau takes about twenty-two breaths a minute and does not know this is unusual. The chest is not barrelled; the adaptation here went the other way, toward flow rather than storage — wide vessels, thin blood, a resting ventilation that would look like mild panic in a lowlander, and hands and feet that stay warm and pink in air that would kill the extremities of a visitor in a night. They handle rime-stiff rope barehanded. Their fingers are thick, blunt, cracked across the knuckles, and almost never frostbitten.

The face is the recognisable thing. Long high-bridged nose with an elaborate interior, built to warm and re-wet the air on its way out; deep-set eyes under heavy lids, because the light at four kilometres is a physical assault by nine in the morning; lashes long enough to carry their own crop of ice, so that a person waking in a cold room blinks and feels the small crackle of it. Skin dark and greased. The grease is rendered ungulate fat mixed with soot, applied every morning to face and hands, and it is the reason the town smells faintly of tallow and smoke at all hours.

From the breathing comes almost everything else about their manners.

At this altitude, in this dryness, a body loses more water out of its mouth than out of its skin. So they do not open their mouths. Nose-breathing is trained into children with a strip of felt knotted under the chin at night, and an adult caught mouth-breathing on a climb is not pitied but faintly embarrassed, the way one might be embarrassed at spilling. Spitting is the great obscenity of the plateau — the deliberate expenditure of water on contempt — and there is no worse thing you can do in the Ninth Sill in front of witnesses. Their curses are all about ice, not fluids. Their greeting is to press the bridge of the nose against another's cheek and breathe out once, deliberately, a gift of warm moisture, and among close kin it is done slowly, and among strangers it is done with the speed of a handshake.

And they are quiet, which the thin air explains only partly. Loud speech is wasteful speech; a shout costs you breath and water both. Volume is understood as a kind of incontinence. A person who raises their voice in an argument has already conceded it. Anger is expressed by slowness — by a long pause, by setting an object down more carefully than necessary — and the plateau's humour is almost entirely in timing, in the half-second before an answer, which is why lowland traders come away convinced these people have no sense of humour at all and the people come away convinced the traders are children.

### Households, and what a household actually is

A household is a cistern with people attached.

Blood matters less here than volume. The legal unit — recorded on slate, stacked in the archive rooms like roof shingle, tens of thousands of tablets going back through four sills — is the catchment: so many metres of comb-frame on such a ridge, feeding such a channel, feeding a dome of such capacity, held jointly by such persons. Marriage merges catchments. Divorce partitions them, badly, and the partition disputes are the substance of most law. Children are not strictly born into their parents' catchment; a household whose cistern cannot carry another mouth fosters the child out to one that can, and this is neither shameful nor sentimental, and the child keeps both sets of obligations. Nearly everyone in the Ninth Sill has two mothers in the practical sense and can recite both cisterns.

What is shameful is to go dry, and every family does, eventually.

This is the cruelty built into the place, and they know it perfectly well. The fog line creeps down the rock at roughly a fingerwidth a year, and so the great houses of the upper blocks — the ones with the enormous old plastered domes, the ones whose ancestors' names are cut into lintels — are watching their combs catch less every decade. No fortune here outlives four generations. The water leaves it. A family peaks, dries, and descends: first by selling comb-frontage, then by taking water-work for downhill households, then by moving, one room at a time, into the newer, meaner, wetter blocks nearer the pan. The uphill quarter is called the Ossuary Side, and is inhabited by the proudest and the poorest simultaneously, often in the same building, and it is the only part of town where you will hear the old formal speech.

So the shape of an ambitious life is downward. A young couple with nothing gambles on a new catchment at the lower margin, borrows against fuel to have it plastered, and either becomes rich enough to be resented within twenty years or spends their lives carrying for someone else's dome. This is the central plot of every story they tell.

### Hair, fuel, debt

Three materials run the economy, and none of them is metal.

**Hair.** They do not cut their hair except twice: at the initiation, around fifteen, and at death. Both cuttings are braided into comb-rope. The combs on the ridge above the Ninth Sill — ten thousand frames, kilometres of black warp — are woven of the hair of animals and of everyone who has ever lived here, and this is not spoken of as sacred, exactly. It is spoken of the way one speaks of a wall being load-bearing. Rope retires after about fifteen years, goes into lashings, then into mortar as fibre, then into plaster. Nothing is thrown away, and nothing is carried uphill.

**Fuel.** Dung heaps are managed like orchards, turned on a schedule, owned by the metre, and stolen more often than water is, because water theft is easier to prove. The great fuel expenditure of a lifetime is the plasterers' kiln: burnt gypsum, the lime that makes a cistern hold, requires an amount of dung that no single household possesses. So a new cistern is financed. The neighbours contribute cakes against a slate instrument that specifies repayment in fuel over twenty or thirty years, with interest also in fuel, and the whole social fabric of the lower town is a web of these fuel debts. The plasterers' guild keeps the recipe, sets the price, and is loathed with a steadiness that has lasted centuries.

**Wings.** Twice a year, for ten days, the sky fills, and the entire city stops. Nets go up between the comb-frames, poles are raised over the standing water, and everyone from the age of six to the age of blindness works through the nights in the fog with a headstrap lamp of burning fat. The fowling gangs drown people most years. Afterward there is meat on racks for a month and the town smells like a slaughter floor, and the feathers go into the quilted coats, which are the only genuinely warm garment anyone owns, which are inherited, patched, re-patched, and valued at a small cistern each. And then there is the guano. The pan margins are owned in strips, taxed by the wing-fee, and scraped by children with bone spades, and this — bird shit from a continent away — is the entire nitrogen budget of the terraces. They know it. There is a saying that translates roughly as *everything we eat has flown over us first.*

### Sound

There is almost no ringing in the Ninth Sill: no metal, no wheels, felt underfoot. But it is not a silent place, because of the whistling.

Speech is for close range, murmured, nose to cheek. Distance is handled by whistle. The plateau has a whistled register — a full one, tonal contours mapping onto the spoken language, not a code but a transposition — and it carries across a basin in thin air where a shout dies at a hundred metres. Herders whistle. Comb crews whistle. Mothers on roof-paths whistle children home in a phrase four notes long. The whistled register is grammatically slightly reduced and considered blunt; you cannot be polite in it, and people say things whistled that they would not say aloud.

It also overlaps, awkwardly and usefully, with the rodents. The burrow-cities on the ridges have an alarm grammar of their own, and every child learns to read it: the flat single note for the soaring bird, the doubled note for the long-legged cat, the ragged chorus that means a person is walking on the colony. Herders use the marmots as a distant-early-warning system and the marmots, over a very long time, have learned to treat certain human whistles as alarms, so that a crew working a ridge at dawn can empty a hillside of rodents with three notes, and does, for the pleasure of the silence afterward.

Their music is a drone. In a rising wind the combs sound — thousands of taut hair ropes at slightly different tensions, producing a low, wide, unsteady tone that everyone in the city hears from birth. The singing imitates it: a chest-drone from the adults, a whistled melody laid over the top by one or two voices, usually children's. There are bone flutes but they are considered thin.

### Who else is on the plateau

Forty thousand people in one basin, and the plateau is two thousand kilometres across, and so the answer is: a great many, and all of them numbered.

Every settled basin counts its sills. A city of the Fourteenth is ancient and insufferable about it; a city still on its Second is a frontier place full of people with bad manners and new cisterns. The numbers function as a diplomatic language and a form of boasting, and there is a standing scholarly quarrel about whether certain very old ruin-fields on the highest slopes of the eastern rim represent sills Minus-One and Minus-Two, or merely bad building.

Between the cities are the folded people, who own no dome at all. They carry their combs rolled, pitch them on a new ridge every eleven or twelve days, live under hair felt, follow the fog line and the ungulate herds, and consider the townsfolk to be persons who have chained themselves to a hole in the ground. The townsfolk consider them filthy, unreliable, and indispensable, since they carry all the news, all the copper nodules, and a good deal of the marriage market between basins. A town daughter who leaves with the folded people is mourned with real grief and visited annually.

And from below, rarely, the caravans: salt going down over the rim passes, grain and dye and worked copper coming up. The lowlanders who make the climb arrive bleeding from the nose, sleepless, sick for a week, and talk far too loudly the whole time. The plateau word for them is *the panting*. The plateau's own caravan men are a strange caste — they descend into the thick air and come back intoxicated by it, and a certain number of them each year simply do not come back, and their households hold a bone-less funeral and carry a stone up instead.

### The accounting

They have almost no theology and an enormous amount of bookkeeping, and the bookkeeping does the work.

The dead stay at the altitude where they died. A body is carried up by the young — one long day's work, wicker frame on the back — and laid on the old dry roof-slabs of whichever abandoned sill is currently in use for the purpose, where the two-metre birds do what they do, and the bones go into that sill's ossuary. Nobody is brought down. Since the city quarries its ancestors for lintels, a stone occasionally comes down with a human bone still mortared into it, and the rule for this is immediate, unglamorous, and universally obeyed: the bone goes back up, that day, by whoever found it, at their own cost. It is the single most resented obligation in the culture and nobody has ever proposed abolishing it.

From this comes their whole sense of time, which is spatial. The past is up. The future is down and inward. Their language marks it in the verbs: an event can be conjugated uphill or downhill of the speaker, and the grandmother is *up-of-me*, and a thing that cannot happen is *uphill water*. To be nostalgic is, literally, to face the rim. When they say a family is finished they say it has *gone up*, and when they execute someone — for breaching a cistern with intent, or for cutting the cork-wood, which are the only two capital matters — they do not kill them. They carry them up and leave them at a dead sill. The verb is *sent up*, and it is used for nothing else.

### The argument

Two hundred years and running. Forty metres lower, eight hundred metres inward, cairns already set, plan already drawn on a slate the size of a door in the lower archive, and no consensus on whether the catchment down there will actually hold.

The downhill households and the comb-wrights want to go, and have wanted to go for six generations. The upper blocks — dry, proud, and in possession of most of the archive — argue that the lower basin's fog inversion is unreliable in the second cold, that the salt is closer to the surface there, and that the old ridge has never once failed. Both sides are partly right and both sides know exactly which of their arguments are actually about money. The dispute has produced a vast literature of scratched slate, at least three schisms in the plasterers' guild, one brief and embarrassing war with the basin to the east, and a permanent office — the wet-markers — whose sole function is to maintain the notched posts and record, once a year, the mean height of the rime line against the rock, so that the argument can at least be conducted with numbers.

The posts show a fingerwidth a year. Over a long life, half a metre.

That is the thing worth understanding about these people. They are one of the few populations anywhere who can see geology with the naked eye, and what they see is domestic: a stain on a wall, lower than it was. An old woman on the fourth terrace will put her hand flat against the stone at the height where the rime stood when she was a girl, and then at the height where it stands now, and the distance between her hands is the width of her own life. She will not say anything about it. She will note it, the way one notes a level in a jar, and go in to the fire.

### Four of them

**The blind rope-feeler.** Dung smoke indoors takes the eyes of a certain proportion of the old, and the best comb inspectors are drawn from them. They walk the ranks before dawn with a stick in the left hand and the right hand open, running along the warp, reading tension by touch and by the note the rope makes when struck with a flat wooden clacker. The clack is the loudest permitted sound in the city. A good feeler can find one slack line in four hundred and tell you, within a year, when it was braided and whose hair is in it.

**The child on the flagstones.** Six years old, out in the dark of the fourth hour with a hair broom, sweeping rime off the roof-paths into the gutter-troughs before the sun can take it. It is the first job anyone is given and it is done badly for about two years and then well for the rest of a life. The whole city, on any clear night, is quietly full of children sweeping ice.

**The bonded carrier.** His grandmother broke the wall of a neighbour's cistern in a dispute about comb frontage, sixty years ago. The volume was assessed, the debt was cut into slate, and it descends. He carries water for that household four days in seven and will die owing about a third of it, and his daughter will inherit the rest, and none of this is considered unjust by anybody, including him, though he has views about the interest.

**The surveyor of the Tenth.** Forty-one years old, fifth of her line in the office, spends the dry months out in the pan resetting cairns that the ungulate herds have knocked over, and is privately certain — she has the posts, she has done the arithmetic on the inversion depth — that her opponents are right and the lower catchment will not hold, and that the Tenth Sill will have to go somewhere else entirely, further in, onto ground nobody has surveyed. She has not said so. She is assembling the slates. She expects to present the case in about eleven years, to people who will hate her for it, on behalf of a city that will be built by strangers a hundred and fifty years after she is carried uphill and laid out for the birds.
