# What Lives in the Bellows

Take them in order of size, because size is the only order the place respects. Not age, not cleverness, not right of arrival. In the Bellows a thing is measured by how much of the wall it holds.

---

**The green.**

The largest living thing in the shaft is the shaft's lining, and it is best not to think of it as many things. There are species in it — you can count them, in principle, on your knees with a hand lens and a lamp you have to keep relighting — but they interpenetrate so thoroughly, and so few of them are ever going anywhere, that the honest description is of one body two hundred and forty feet tall, three inches deep, and open at the top.

It is a body organised entirely around the getting and keeping of water it does not have to fall for. Nothing here waits for rain. The seep arrives one bead per stone per minute and the moss takes each bead before it can gather weight, which is why the Bellows has no waterfall and never had one: the wall drinks its own sweat faster than gravity can collect it. Sheet moss lies in shingles, each shingle throwing its drip onto the shingle below, so that a single bead may be passed hand to hand down forty feet of wall and never once become a droplet in air. The cushions are swollen to capacity and beyond; press one and it gives up half a cup and is full again by evening.

The filmy ferns are the specialists. Fronds one cell thick, no cuticle, no defence at all against drying, which in any other country would be a joke and here is simply the correct design. They fan out where the seep runs steadiest and they are, functionally, gills. They do not tolerate the damp. They require it absolutely, in the way you require air, and if the Bellows ever dried for one day they would be gone that day and would not return.

At the bottom, in the deepest reach, the liverwort crust that has given up on green. It gets perhaps twenty minutes of usable light in midsummer and lives on what the moss above it drops. Its colour is the colour of something drowned, and it is the oldest continuous occupation in the shaft: a flat pale crust that has been holding that particular square yard since before the basalt above it was worn into a lip.

The green has no idea it is being shouted at. It has no organ that could receive the information. Sound moves through it the way it moves through wet felt — absorbed, converted to a faint warmth, and forgotten. It is worth saying plainly: the loudest place on this coast is upholstered wall to wall in creatures for whom the noise does not exist.

---

**The interior.**

The moss is not the population. The moss is the country. The population lives inside it, in the water film between leaf and leaf, and it is enormous.

Rotifers by the hundred thousand in a single cushion, wheeling their crowns of cilia at a flow-rate they have never had to alter. Nematodes threading the film. Tardigrades in the drier upper shingles, where twice a decade a freak dry week arrives and they fold themselves down into barrels and wait it out, and come back, and do not appear to consider this remarkable. Mites the size of dust. A fauna measured in individuals per teaspoon.

For all of them the Bellows is a still pond ten microns deep. They live at a scale where water is thick as syrup and the great pulse of the sea, twelve seconds apart, arrives as nothing at all — a shiver in a medium that snubs shivers. You are standing in a hollow bottle full of unbearable noise, and three inches from your cheek there are two million animals for whom the defining fact of the place has never once come up.

---

**The flinching flour.**

The springtails hold the middle ground, and they are the first tenants you can see with your own eye, if you can hold a lamp still enough. White, blind or nearly, three to a millimetre. On a good wall they are a dusting of flour that flinches — because that is their whole answer to the world: a spring under the abdomen, cocked, released, and the animal is gone six inches sideways in no time worth measuring.

Here the trigger has had to be recalibrated. A springtail elsewhere flinches at vibration. A springtail in the Bellows would flinch itself to death in an hour if it flinched at vibration, because the wall it lives on is struck like a drum every nine to twelve seconds forever. So these have raised the threshold, or narrowed it, or learned the pulse the way you learn the sound of your own house: they ignore the sea and jump for the touch. Nothing but direct contact will move them. Breathe on them and they don't care. Put a fingertip down and the flour flinches.

They eat the moss's leavings, the black film in the wettest streaks, the fungal threads in the dead layer under the living green. And they eat, at present, the last of the tallow in the lamp niche at the sixth turning. It has taken them four generations of humans to get through it. They are working on the wick.

---

**The single spiders.**

One per ten square feet, and each of them will die within a body-length of where it hatched.

A spider is normally a machine for reading vibration, and a web is normally its instrument — a stretched membrane of information, tuned, listening. In the Bellows that instrument is useless in the way that a violin is useless in a wind tunnel. A web of any size here is not a lie-detector; it is a lie-detector being shaken by a large hand, permanently, producing pure static.

So they have shrunk it. Each web is the size of a coin, spun in the lee of a stone, in the one pocket where the draft eddies rather than screams, and it is not a listening device at all. It is a snare and a doormat. The spider sits with one foot on it and waits to be touched. Its range of perception is perhaps a centimetre. Everything beyond that centimetre is noise, and it has stopped enquiring.

They are pale, long-legged, and slow, and they are patient in a way that is not virtue but arithmetic: a springtail may cross that coin once in nine days. The spiders do not appear to fight. There is no advantage to holding two pockets when you cannot perceive the second one.

---

**The walker.**

If the Bellows has a citizen — a creature that knows the place as a place, that has been from the top of it to the bottom, that could be said to have an experience of it — it is the moth.

Grey, small, unremarkable to look at, and equipped with perfectly serviceable wings that it will not use. Elsewhere its cousins fly. Here the air is a column moving upward at a speed that would deliver any flying insect out of the mouth of the shaft and over the heather and into the flat cold nothing of the moor, which is death. So it has made the only sane refusal available and taken to its feet.

It walks. It hatches in the moss, eats moss, pupates in moss, and emerges into a vertical world it must climb. It walks with its wings folded flat and locked like a letter that will not be opened, following the seams between shingles, keeping to the lee, and it will cover — over a life of perhaps five weeks — an astonishing distance in a place this small. Fifty feet. A hundred. It finds mates by scent, drifting down the flue on the one current that never stops, so that every male in the shaft is permanently receiving news from everything above him and nothing from below. This gives the population a direction. They go up. They have been going up for as long as they have been here, and the ones who reach the top and are tempted out are subtracted, quietly, every generation, and the shaft keeps the rest.

They are also the only animals in the Bellows that have ears — or the remnant of them. Moths of this line carry a tympanal organ on the thorax, a membrane for hearing bats. There are no bats. There is instead a sound field that would tear the membrane if the membrane were still tuned to anything, and so it has gone slack and thick and become a small patch of skin that means nothing. You can find it under a lens. It is the only ruined instrument in the shaft, and it is more or less the same story as the four iron pegs: a device brought in from a louder, brighter, more various world, and left here, and swallowed.

---

**The eleven.**

They did not come down the stair. This matters.

The pool is three feet deep, black, out of the draft, and it is not fed from above — no beads reach it, no spray troubles it, the surge crosses it only as a fine shiver, like a struck drumskin, once every dozen seconds. It is fed sideways, from the rock, from groundwater that has been in the dark since before there was a hole in this headland at all. The shrimp came with the water. They are eyeless not because the Bellows took their eyes but because they never had any to bring; their line has been blind since long before this coast had a shape you would recognise.

There are nine of them, or eleven — the count depends on how long you are willing to keep the lamp low and how much of the silt you are willing to disturb, and you should not disturb it. Translucent, unhurried, with the flat authority of animals that have never had a competitor or a predator or a reason to be quick. They graze the film on the stones. They have no idea they are in a shaft. They have no idea there is a sea, a sky, an audience, a stair, a hut, a lump of wax. They live in the one room of the Bellows that is exempt, and they have lived there, as a population, longer than the stair has been a stair, longer than the language of the people who cut it, longer than the people.

They are the reason it is difficult to say the place is empty.

---

**The windfall.**

Once every eight or ten years something falls in. A starling caught by a squall over the moor; a wren; twice in recorded memory a young fulmar. It does not survive the wind in the flue and it does not survive the floor.

And then the Bellows has an event. A body is a mountain of nitrogen in a country that runs on beads of stone-sweat. Fungus takes the eyes within days; the springtails arrive from thirty feet in every direction; a fly whose larvae live nowhere else on the coast completes, in a fortnight, a life cycle it has been waiting a decade to begin, and its descendants will wait another decade, in the moss, as eggs and as patience. The spiders in the nearest pockets grow to twice normal size that year and lay accordingly. Two seasons later there is a patch of moss forty feet up that is greener and taller than any moss around it, and nothing else remains, and no one saw any of it, and it happens again.

---

**The ones with thumbs.**

Now the difficult tenants.

They were never many and they never lived down there. Three households on the headland, four in the good centuries, and one person per generation who was the one who went down — chosen, or left with it, or simply the one who could stand it. The stair is not the work of a community. It is the work of one pair of hands at a time, over eight hundred years, each adding a handhold where the last one had slipped.

They came at the lowest ebb of the smallest neap, once or twice a month, for the twenty minutes when the Bellows drops to a wet hollow breathing and a person can speak. Everything about their kit tells you the errand. The lamp for the descent. The rope for the fourth turning where the rock is wrong. The wax for the way down and the way up, because the wax was never for the quiet part. And the three bowls, which are the point: they were filled not from a stream, not from rain, not from the sea, but from water that has never touched sky — one bead at a time, off a wall, in the dark, slowly enough that filling them was itself most of the visit.

And in the quiet, words. You can reconstruct the shape of them without knowing one syllable, because the architecture is unmistakeable. This is a room where nothing hears. Not the moss, not the moth, not the eleven, not the sea. A person could carry down into it the thing that could not be said in a house with four other people in it, and say it out loud, at speaking volume, to the largest listening absence on the coast, and then climb back into a world where it had been said and did not need saying again. Names of the dead. Names of the living. The one sentence a marriage cannot survive. Confession without a confessor, which is either the cleanest form or no form at all.

The bowls went up full and were used for something at the top — a birth, a fever, a hull, a burial — and it does not much matter which, because the water was never the medicine. The descent was.

---

**The last one.**

The wax is one lump. That is the detail that undoes people.

Not a lump each. One lump, kneaded warm in the hands at the top of the stair, torn in two, pressed in, and on the way out taken back, rolled together, and left in the dry fold behind the hut for whoever came next. Six generations, maybe nine, of thumbs in the same two hollows. It is dark with handling in the way a stair-rail is dark. It holds the print of the last pair of thumbs and, beneath, the composite of every pair before them, which is not a metaphor: the hollows are deeper than one person's thumbs could make in a lifetime.

The ending was not dramatic. Nobody was lost on the stair; the rope did not part. The one who would have gone next went to sea, or married inland, or found that the words could be said in a church now, or found that there was no longer anyone left at the top to bring the water back to. So the wax was put in its fold at the end of an ordinary visit by someone who intended to return in a fortnight, and the fortnight passed, and the fold is dry, and the cold has kept everything, and the marks on the stair are not abandoned so much as finished.

---

**And so, currently, in residence:**

Two million animals in a film of water who have never heard of the sea. Three inches of green holding every square foot of a room whose entire volume is empty. Some thousands of white flinchers, several hundred single spiders each perceiving a centimetre of the world, a population of moths climbing forever toward the one exit that kills them, a fly asleep in the moss waiting for a bird, and eleven blind shrimp in a pool that the noise cannot get into, grazing, exempt, older than the language of the people who cut the stair.

And in the lamp niche at the sixth turning, in the last rime of tallow that somebody carried down here to burn, a colony of springtails, eating it, having taken four human generations to get this far, with the wick still to go.
