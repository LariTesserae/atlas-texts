**Nobody here is from here.**

That is the first thing to understand about the residents. There is no native population. The rock is younger than every animal standing on it. Even the crickets, who look like they were designed for exactly this — long-legged, pale-kneed, antennae like drawn wire — walked in from somewhere else through the cracks, from older tubes further inland where their grandmothers hung on older drips. Everyone in the room is either a survivor of a fall, an immigrant through a hole, or an accident of digestion.

They have arranged themselves accordingly. Some are building as though the room were permanent. Some are not building at all.

---

**The oldest citizen** is at the heart of the log, in a chamber no wider than a fingertip, and she has not seen anything in years because she has no eyes worth the name.

She is a white sac with a small dark head at one end, segmented, glossy, faintly pulsing along her length. She is longer than a grain of rice and shorter than a match. If you extracted her she would be unable to move more than a few millimeters and would die of it, and the extraction would be a kind of civic murder, because she is not really an animal in there. She is an organ. The colony is the animal, and she is the part of it that makes more of it.

She predates the flow. She was founded in a rotten trunk in a forest that no longer exists, on ground that is now under fifteen meters of basalt somewhere off to the north, and she was already laying when the lava came down the valley and made the whole country over. She survived because the wedge of forest floor that she lived in went into the hole tilted the right way and landed soft on rubble. She felt this as a change in pressure and a wave of alarm-vibration from her daughters, and she went on laying.

Beside her, dark, sclerotized, no bigger than a match head, is the king. He has been with her the whole time. Termite monarchs are not like bees; he is not a spent thing but a permanent consort, and he touches her constantly, and their entire courtship happened in a country that is now stone.

Every worker in the room is her child. None of them has ever been outside. None of them ever will be, except the winged ones, whom she will make one day when the colony is fat enough, and who will climb the chimneys and go up out of the skylight into a light no termite in this lineage has experienced since the fall, and almost all of whom will be eaten in the first thirty seconds by the geckos, who are waiting for exactly that.

---

**Her daughters** are pale, blind, soft as boiled rice, and they are the only true engineers in the chamber.

They do not know where they are. They cannot know. They have no image of the room, no notion of its oval of light, no sense of the black land above. What they have is a map made of humidity, temperature, grain, and the smell of one another, and by that map they have done something remarkable: they have found the warm cracks and built ventilation over them, and they have found the wet seam and built roads away from it, and they have taken a hole in an alien planet and made it habitable by treating it as if it were wood.

They build with mouths. Chewed fiber, clay, saliva, their own waste, pressed and dried and pressed again. Watch one at the growing edge of a covered road: she arrives with a load the size of her own head, sets it, taps the surface with her antennae, adjusts it, and leaves without looking at what she has done, because she cannot look and does not need to. The next one will read the tap-marks and continue.

The soldiers are the ones with the heavy heads. They stand at the breaches and click. If you break the carton wall, the clicking runs back through the whole ridge in under a second, and where there was a smooth quiet suburb there is now a rank of dark mandibles held out at the air, waiting for an enemy that in this room is almost always ants.

They will lose the wall and rebuild it by morning. They have done this eleven times.

---

**The bees** are the other civilization, and they are the termites' opposite in every way that matters.

They are small, black, stingless, and they know where they live. They go up. Every clear morning, from about the time the light clears the rim, they leave the amber trumpet in ones and twos, spiral up through the oval, cross a kilometer of black glassy nothing, and reach the forest edge where the old trees still stand, and they come back loaded with resin and pollen and the smell of flowers that do not exist within four paces of anything in this room. The whole colony's economy is an import economy. They live in the new world and eat off the old one.

The two at the door are guards and they are doing exactly what they appear to be doing. They will not let a stranger bee in. They will fill the tube with resin at dusk and unseal it at dawn, and they will chase a fly a body-length and no further. When the traveling oval of light reaches the west wall and passes through the trumpet, you can see them in silhouette, and you can see that they are standing, and that they are patient, and that they are not thinking about anything.

Their queen walks. She does not lie still like the termite. She moves slowly around the spiral of comb, attended, laying into cells her daughters have just filled with food and will seal behind her, and she has never been in the same room as her mate, who died months ago in the air above the flow.

The hum you feel in your teeth is the wing-fanning of workers moving air across drying honey. It is a factory noise. It is the only industrial sound in a hundred square kilometers.

---

**The wasp under the ledge has no name and no help.**

She is a founder. She left a nest somewhere upslope, or her nest was destroyed, and she came in through the hole and chose a dry overhang and built six cells out of chewed fiber and spit, and laid in five of them, and is now building the sixth, and she does all of it herself: the paper, the water, the hunting, the guarding, the fanning. She sits on the comb at night with her legs wrapped around the stalk.

She is the most exposed animal in the chamber. If the skink finds her, the district ends. There is no soldier caste behind her and no queen above her; she *is* the queen, and she is also the entire workforce, and her nation currently consists of five larvae who cannot yet do anything but eat and one cell of empty air.

Everything she has done is a bet that she will live another three weeks.

---

**The ants** are the only residents who have altered the room permanently.

They came in the raft — a modest nest in the root mat, a few hundred strong, brown, unremarkable — and within days of landing they had found the crack in the east wall where a fungus fruits, and they began carrying, and the carrying wore a line into the ash a hair deep, and that line is now the single oldest piece of history in the chamber. Not the rock. The rock has no history; it has only an age. The road is history: a record of repetition, the first thing here that happened more than once.

They do not deserve credit for it, exactly. No ant decided on the route. The route is what remained after thousands of small errors were corrected by the smell of success. It is the shape of a habit. But it is the only mark in the room made by living things simply by *going about their business*, and the skink has learned to read it, and the termites have learned to fear it, and when the first hard rain comes, the road is the first thing that will be erased, and the ants will lay it down again in a week without any sense of loss, because they never knew they had it.

---

**In the raft itself, unseen, is the largest population in the room by several orders of magnitude,** and none of it is doing anything you could call remarkable — which is why it is the most important thing here.

Springtails, thousands, flicking. Oribatid mites like tiny armored seeds turning slowly in the leaf litter. A pseudoscorpion the size of a comma with grotesquely competent pincers, hunting the springtails. Two millipedes. One earthworm, quite large, gone deep. A single land snail whose shell is the pale color of an old fingernail, resting in the litter, waiting for full dark. Nematodes in every damp millimeter. And through all of it, wound around every fragment, the white threads of fungi doing the actual work: turning what fell into what can be taken up.

This is a forest floor. It fell as an intact, functioning, several-hundred-million-year-old institution — the arrangement by which dead plants become live ones — and it is now sitting on top of sterile stone at a slight tilt, drying at its edges, thriving in its wet heart, contracting slowly inward. Its inhabitants are all migrating toward the middle. The soil is dying from the rim in, and the ones who live in it can feel exactly where the good damp still is, and they are crowding into it.

And where the raft's lowest edge touches wet ash, its threads are going out into the ash. Not far. A centimeter, two. But that is the front line of the only project in the room with a chance of finishing: the making of dirt.

---

**Under the drip, on bare stone, there is a slick you would take for wetness.**

It is not wetness. It is a film of cyanobacteria and free-living algae, arrived as dust on the afternoon updraft, holding the rock with nothing but their own slime. They fix nitrogen out of the air. They die in layers on top of themselves. They are, functionally, the first residents of the room who are actually *from* the new world and not refugees into it, and they are building the substance out of which everything later will be made.

Nothing in the chamber notices them. They will outlast almost all of it.

---

**The hunters live in the present tense and have no projects.**

The whip spider on the north wall is the finest instrument in the room. She has no venom, no silk, no interest in you. Her front legs are not legs; they are two enormously long feelers that she sweeps in slow arcs through the dark, and with them she holds a live map of about a foot of the world in every direction, updated continuously by touch and air movement. She is aware of the cricket long before the cricket is aware of anything, and she does not hurry, because she does not have to: she waits until the geometry is right and then closes the spined baskets of her pedipalps and it is over. She spends most of her life not moving. She is one of the mildest animals on earth and she looks like a hand pulled out of a nightmare.

The cave crickets on the ceiling are the closest thing to locals. Tube-dwellers by inheritance, unbothered by dark, they hang on the frozen drips with their antennae describing separate slow circles, each one privately sampling the air, and they will eat anything: guano, moss, one another's dead. They are the room's plankton. Almost everything in the room is, sooner or later, made out of crickets.

The two geckos are conducting a long dispute in a medium that will not carry it. A gecko in a normal wall shouts; here, the vesicular rock swallows the shout a meter out, so their war has become a war of postures — head bobs, arched backs, tail waves, the flattening of one against the stone while the other passes. They hold the ledges near the light because that is where the moths come down, and they will hold them until the brown snake comes, at which point both of them will vanish so completely and so early that you will assume they were never there. They know the snake by smell before it enters. Their disappearance is the room's only alarm system, and it is silent, like everything else.

The skink is the one that unsettles me. It hunts the ant road specifically, and not the ants — it takes the ones carrying, the loaded ones, the slow ones, and it does this at the same two bends every day. Somewhere in the small brown length of it, something has *noticed a pattern*. That is a different order of event from anything the termites or the bees have managed with all their architecture. Nobody in the insect cities knows anything. The skink has learned one thing about the room, and it is the only local knowledge in existence.

---

**The frog is the size of an almond and he is calling for someone who is not coming.**

He lives in the moss by the pool, dark, damp, unspectacular, with a throat that inflates to about the size of a peppercorn. Three or four times an hour he says his one syllable. It is a mating call. It goes out into a room built entirely of holes, and the holes take it, and it does not travel two meters, and no female has ever heard it, because there is no female within a kilometer and the way in is a hole in the ceiling.

He calls anyway. He will call for as long as he lives, which in that damp moss with that many crickets could be years. He is fed, he is safe, the pool is warm at midday, and every twenty minutes he asserts himself into a medium designed to absorb assertion.

He is the loudest thing in the chamber and he is failing at the only thing he is for.

---

**The mosquito larvae hanging under the surface of the pool are waiting for you.**

Not the larvae — they eat the algal scum and are content. But the females they become will need blood, and there is very little blood in this room: two geckos, a skink, a snake that comes and goes, one almond-sized frog. They have been persisting on almost nothing, generation after thin generation, and they can smell a mammal on the down-draft from a long way off.

You have been standing still. That is why there is one on your wrist now. It is the only creature in the room that has any use for you at all, and it has been waiting its whole life.

---

**The fig does not live in this room. It lives in the next century, and is merely rooted here.**

Two leaves. Red stem. Delivered as a pellet by a bird that landed on the tree fern for eleven seconds, in transit, and never came back and does not know. Everything else here is improvising against the present: the wasp against three weeks, the raft against drying, the termites against ants, the frog against nothing at all. The fig is doing something else. It is putting out a root along a crack, and the root is following the crack because that is where the water is, and in fifty years that root will be as thick as your arm and the crack will be a fault, and in a hundred the ceiling will come down in daylight, and the oval will be a valley, and there will be soil, and none of the current residents' descendants will recognize the place, and the fig will be the place.

It is two leaves tall. It weighs less than the wasp's nest. It is the only serious intention in the chamber.

---

**And then the visitors,** who do not live here but constitute most of the room's contact with the world:

Moths, at dusk, dropping through the oval in twos and threes, dazed, taken.

A single bat that comes through twice a season, banks once around the pit without touching anything, and leaves, having found the acoustics unhelpful.

The bird — a fruit-eater, small, glossy, entirely uninterested — that lands on the rim ferns, looks in, does not like it, and leaves behind, once every few months, a seed. It is the closest thing this room has to a god: arbitrary, invisible to its beneficiaries, capable of altering the entire future in under a minute, and unaware.

And the ferns themselves, at the lip, who are residents of the border and belong to both worlds. Their roots are in the crumb-line of moisture at the rim, and their fronds hang inward over the hole, and they comb the light, and everything below them is green-lit because of it, and if you removed them the moss would burn and the bees would lose their landmark and the tree fern would grow straighter and the whole room would be a slightly different place.

---

So: who lives here.

A blind old queen from a drowned country, laying in the dark, tended by daughters who have never seen sky. A trading city that eats the past because the present has no flowers. One wasp with no help. Ants who have made the only history. A hundred thousand small unremarkable soil animals crowding inward as their world dries at the edges. An algal film that will inherit everything. A hunter who does not hurry. Two lizards arguing in silence. A frog calling into a room that eats sound. A mosquito that has waited its whole life for a visitor. And a seedling with two leaves that intends to break the ceiling.

None of them knows about the rain. It will come, probably this season, and it will find the low line and take the ash and cut the room in half and carry the moss out through the eastern crack and drown the pool and pull the raft's dry edge apart.

They are all building anyway. Not out of courage — nothing here has any — but because building is simply what they do with a day, and they have been given days, and the days have so far been warm and wet and quiet and full of crickets.

A bead of three-day-old rain lets go of the ceiling. It clicks once on fresh stone.

Behind the west wall, the wax trumpet is sealed for the night. Inside it, in the dark, several hundred small black bodies are fanning air across honey made from flowers a kilometer away, in a country that used to be here.
