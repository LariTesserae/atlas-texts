They are called, by the country below, the ones on top. They call themselves something that translates badly — *the mat-folk*, or *those that hold for a while* — and the word is not a boast. A mat, up here, is the two metres of moss and root and black wet nothing that lies on a dome, holds for forty years, and then goes off it in a single afternoon. That is the local word for a person. You are a mat. You are not the stone.

There are perhaps two thousand of them across an area the size of a small country, gathered mostly at the seven crossings where the copper line strings its bulbs, and scattered otherwise in ones and twos beside whatever chute is loud enough to live by.

---

Look at their hands first, because the hands are the diagnosis. Every hand on the plateau is pruned. From the age of four, the fingertips are ridged and white-soft as if just out of a long wash, and they never come back — the whorls go slack, the nails grow thin and slightly translucent, and the skin of the palm has a permanent give to it, like chamois. They read texture rather than see it. They can tell rotten root from sound root by pressing a thumb in and listening to the sound the wood makes against the bone of the thumb.

Then the feet: bare, always, splayed wide as a duck's, soles rasped down to a horn that grips wet quartzite better than any boot ever brought up the escarpment. They walk with the toes slightly spread and a little forward of the weight, and on pavement they read the grooves underfoot the way you'd read a street sign — *the grooves here run north-and-a-bit, so the Chute is behind my left shoulder, so I have not turned around.*

They are short, broad, damp, and mild. Rheumatic by fifty. All of them have some quantity of fungus between the toes and in the ears and consider it as unremarkable as hair. Nobody is thin; nobody is fat except the otters. Their clothes are the coarse wet felt from the wheel-houses, resin-dubbed, cut into two garments: a hat with a brim only at the front, and a coat with no collar in front and a high stiff one behind. That asymmetry is the plateau in a nutshell. Weather comes at them from below.

---

They shout. All of them, all the time, from birth, and it is not aggression; it is that a voice does not survive past a metre and a half. So all speech is close speech, and all close speech has a hand in it. The grammar has grip in it: fingers around your forearm is *listen*, a squeeze at the wrist is *yes, go on*, two taps at the elbow is *no, you have this wrong*, and a flat palm laid on the back of the hand means *stop talking, I am thinking about what you said*. Two people arguing look, from any distance, like two people holding each other up.

The same language, minus the voice, is what they use in fog and in the slot-gorges and with the old wheel-tenders, who all go deaf by sixty and are not thought to have lost anything much. A deaf grandmother is simply someone you talk to with your hands, like a stranger in cloud.

For distance they whistle, because whistling cuts water noise where a shout dies in it — and here is the thing that outsiders never quite believe: each valley whistles in a different key, because the whistle must sit above the pitch of the local fall. A person from the Chute whistles high and slightly flat. A person from the wide basin under Three Sisters whistles in a broad two-note that the fall's own harmonics leave a gap for. They can place a stranger's home valley in four seconds, from one call, before they have seen a face. Dialect, here, is literally tuned to falling water.

---

What they are generous with and what they hoard tells you everything.

Power is free. Power is *embarrassingly* free — it arrives forever, it cannot be stored, and the wheels turn all night with nobody in the shed. So nobody bargains over work done by water. You may run your grindstone off my penstock, sleep in my wheel-house, dry your felt on my bearing housing, and it would be strange to thank me for it, as strange as thanking a person for the use of their rain.

Matter is another business entirely. There is nothing in this rock. Nothing. White sand and red clay and ten million years of rinsing, which means that every atom of nitrogen and phosphorus on the plateau is either in something alive or in somebody's garden box. They farm the way the pitcher plants farm: by theft and containment. Their gardens are not in the ground — the ground slides — but in log cribs and old iron pipe-lengths and stone tubs, filled with soil they have *made*: ash, bone, crab shell, fish gut, hearth sweepings, night soil, the black scrapings of a thousand middens, all built up over generations into a dirt so rich and so dark that a person's whole inheritance may be four cubic metres of it. Land is worthless. Land is everywhere and mostly stone. Dirt is wealth, dirt is dowry, dirt is what the will is about.

And so the one real sin here is not theft or violence but *sending it over*: putting organic matter into running water. The streams take and never bring back. A child who throws bones in a creek is smacked. A man who empties his midden into a gorge because he cannot be bothered to carry it is a man nobody lends an ember to, which is a sentence, not a metaphor.

---

Because fire is not lit here; fire is inherited. Nothing is dry enough to start from nothing, and so every household keeps a stove that has not gone out in living memory, and in a good many cases has not gone out in six generations. The ember is the family. Children are given the job of carrying it when the household moves or the roof slides: a small tin box, packed with charred punk and peat dust, worn against the belly under the coat, and it is the single most serious thing a nine-year-old will ever be handed.

A dead stove is a private humiliation and a public event. You take your box and you walk two hours to the neighbours, and they give you a coal without one word of comment, because everybody has done it, and then they talk about it for eleven years.

---

The bridge-growers are the nearest thing to an aristocracy, and they are wet and filthy like everyone else.

Nothing rigid survives up here. So the crossings are grown — fig roots led out over the gorges on scaffolds of cane, hand-guided, split and plaited and spliced, thickening a finger's width per decade, alive, and stronger after every flood than before it. This is work that cannot be finished by the person who starts it. An apprentice is handed a bridge that her great-grandmother began and will hand it on unfinished; the oldest spans are five centuries old and carry a loaded mule; and the whole craft consists of guiding growth and never, ever cutting. Their proverb — shouted, with a hand on your arm — is that *the only thing that beats the rain is being always under construction.*

This is also where their dead go. Cremation is impossible; burial in the skin is temporary, since the skin slides. But a body is four kilos of phosphorus in a country that has none. So the dead are given to the root-heads at the anchor of a growing bridge, or, more intimately, to the household garden box. You will hear a woman say, without any particular solemnity, *my father is the second span*, or *my mother is in the beans this year, and doing well.* The ancestors are load-bearing. They are, quite literally, what you walk across.

---

The wheel-tenders are hereditary, oily to the elbow, half-deaf, and treat their machinery as elderly kin. A Pelton wheel that has run unattended for ninety years is spoken of by name, in the tone you'd use for a grandfather who is difficult but has never let anyone down. They oil, they re-leather, they do not stop it. The one house that makes electricity has a keeper who walks the forty kilometres of copper line twice a season with a pole and a bag of bulbs, and at each of the seven crossings the arrival of a new bulb is a small festival, and the fuzzy orange globe in the mist is the nearest thing they have to an altar.

And at the Weeping Edge itself live the edge-sitters — a handful, never more than nine, who keep the huts where the rain goes up. They man the head of the signal cable down to the flat country, which is a real job, and they collect the upward water in shallow pans for reasons that are religious and that they will not shout about. They know the ear-map better than anyone; they can walk the domes in total cloud by fall-pitch alone. They are considered mildly cracked and mildly holy, and everyone sends them salt.

---

They have no eschatology. There is no end coming, because the end already happened and the plateau stayed. The rock beneath them is finished — past ambition, swept, done — and everything above it is in continuous collapse and regrowth, and they know precisely which of those two things they are.

Which is why, at some point in their teens, every one of them goes out alone to a pavement, lies down, and fits their spine into a groove that rain cut over ten million years, and stays there in the drizzle until they stop mattering. Most describe it afterward as restful. A few come back changed and go to the edge.

And they sleep to noise — grown into it, unable to do without it. When the rain stops for its rare dry hour, the whole crossing wakes at once, uneasy, and comes out to stand in the horrible quiet under the orange bulbs, listening to the belt going round behind the hill: *slap, slap, slap*, unhurried, holding the place open until the water comes back.
