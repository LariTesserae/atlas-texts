# The Listeners

**Begin with the fact that they have already heard you.**

Not for a moment — for an hour, probably. They heard the wrongness of your step on the rimstone lips, which you put your weight on at the centre like a fool instead of the rim; they heard the pitch of your breathing and put your height at a little under two metres; they heard the water coming off you.

That last is the one that marks you. On the plateau everything cooler than the air wears a skin of condensation, and for your first season here you will be cooler than the air, and you will drip. They will hear it: a slow irregular tick off your elbows and your chin, the exact sound of a leaf under the cane. Their word for a newcomer is their word for that sound. It is not a kind word. It means *a thing that is being rained on but is not answering.*

By the time you know they are there, they have already decided what you are. If you did the natural thing and went quiet to listen better, they have decided you are a mimic, and there is no explaining your way back out of that, because explaining is exactly what a mimic would attempt.

---

**The body.**

They are not tall and not heavy, and they are warm — warm through, not warm-blooded in the sense of holding a temperature against a cold world, but *tuned*, running at almost exactly the temperature of the plateau's air, so that nothing condenses on them and they make no sound they did not intend. It takes a child three years to acquire this. It takes an adult from elsewhere a season, if ever. You will know you have become a resident on the day you stop dripping.

The face runs smooth from brow to cheek. There is a shallow warm hollow where the orbit was, and it is a socially important hollow: it is where a thumb goes. The ears are large, mobile, and set asymmetrically — the left a good three centimetres higher than the right, universally, in every lineage. That asymmetry is how they know whether a sound is above them or below them, which on a plateau of shafts and terraces and overhangs is the difference between a route and a fall.

The feet are wide, with a hard rim and a soft sole, built to read a rimstone lip the way you would read a page — where the calcite is thick and will hold, where it is a crust over nothing. The forearms and the backs of the hands carry fine hair that stands before a downdraft, which gives them about eleven seconds' warning. Eleven seconds is enough to get low and get a hand on a string.

In the mouth: a ridged palate for the near-click, fast and fine, used within arm's reach; and deeper, a throat-knock, heavy, slow, a blunt woody sound that runs four or five kilometres over still water on the terraces and comes back off the scarps as a return. They measure distance in returns. A thing is *three returns away*. A thing is *no returns*, which means it is out over the rim, which means it is not a thing.

---

**Name, and the wearing of name.**

Nobody wears cloth for warmth here; there is no cold to speak of. They wear noise.

A garment on the plateau is a harness of cord strung with cane-nodes, drilled bone, split shell, and small waterworn stones chosen for tone, and its purpose is to announce you continuously and uniquely for as long as you are alive. That announcement is your name. It cannot be written and it cannot be spoken, only worn and walked; the rhythm depends on your gait, so a person's name is the sound of that person specifically, moving.

A baby is given a provisional rattle of three elements and carried inside the herd, where four hundred drum-backed grazers tapping across a terrace field make the safest noise on the plateau. At maturity you knot your own, and the knotting of it is the only genuinely private act in the culture — you go off alone for a day, which is to say you go and sit in a counting-shed with the door open and everyone politely does not hear you. Married pairs braid their two patterns into a single one, and thereafter the district hears them as one thing arriving. Unbraiding is done publicly, each party taking back their own elements, and it is a miserable, chiming, humiliating business, and it is why most of them stay married.

A widow wears the dead one's rattle on the opposite hip for four hundred shifts, so that the dead go on arriving in rooms for a while. At the end of it she walks to a vent and drops it, and everyone within earshot stops and listens to it going down, and that is the second and final funeral.

---

**Speech, and what they will not do with it.**

Three languages, and they are not dialects of each other.

**Near-speech** is breathy, fast, enormously complex, full of clicks used as grammar rather than as words, and it does not carry past about four metres. It is the language of the whole interior life. It has a vast vocabulary for texture, for the grain of rain, for the quality of a silence. It has no word meaning *to see*. It has no word meaning *to appear*.

**Far-speech** is knocks, and it has perhaps three hundred terms, all of them concrete: water, hot ground, herd, turn, come, do not, how many. It is slow and formal and everyone in three valleys can hear it, so it is used for emergencies, insults, and weddings.

**The strings** are the third. Knot-code read with the fingertips, hip-height along the routes, coiled in the sheds, worn in bundles by anyone with responsibility. The strings are how the plateau remembers. A route-string is not a map — they have no concept that corresponds to a map, no notion of the plateau having a shape — it is a duration with landmarks knotted into it at their proper intervals, so that walking your fingers down the cord and walking the ground are the same operation performed at different speeds.

Their word for *true* is the word for *heard from two places*. One witness is not a witness; testimony requires two people who were standing at different distances, and a fact attested by a single person is filed under the same heading as a sound with nothing behind it. Which is the same heading as a lie. Which is the same heading as a mimic.

And so: they do not imitate. Ever. There is no mimetic art on the plateau, no impressions, no onomatopoeia, no children's game where you make the noise of the thing. A child who reproduces the click of a grazer for fun is struck hard enough to remember it, once, and never does it again, and grows up and strikes her own children for the same thing and feels bad about it and does it anyway. Their music is abstract to a degree that would strike you as austere and strikes them as the only decent way to make noise — pattern that refers to nothing, that stands for nothing, that is exactly and only itself.

Do not, at a gathering, do the funny voice you do. They will not find it funny. They will find it the single most frightening thing that has ever happened at that gathering.

---

**How they live.**

They live inside the herds.

A camp is not a place; it is a position relative to four hundred grazers, maintained by herders who keep the animals cropping in a slow spiral over a terrace field for a season and then move them. The herd is the wall. Nothing that hunts by listening will come into that much noise. The herd is also the larder, the alarm system, and the clock, because grazers move on the hammering and a herd that moves off-count means the count is wrong or you are.

Around and between the herds: the cane-cutters, who work in pairs and never in silence, because a cut cane screams for a few seconds and then sighs, and a mimic that has learned that sigh can draw a whole crew off a terrace edge. The curtain-gatherers, who go into the hanging root-hair at the base of the scarps — hundreds of metres of warm wet filament, full of small shrieking climbers — and come out with mineral crust and eggs and rope. The string-walkers, who maintain the knot-lines, and who are the closest thing the plateau has to a government, because they decide which routes get repaired and therefore which districts exist.

There is no fire. There has never been fire in living memory and there will not be. Food is boiled in rimstone pools at the edge of the boiler-lands, where a vent keeps a pan of water at a shuddering simmer, and those cook-pools are inherited, quarrelled over, and occasionally killed for. A family is not defined by a hearth. A family is defined by a cord.

The deaf are not pitied for long; they are employed. A deaf person is given the cords, and becomes an archivist and a cutter of tallies, and sits in the sheds with a hand on the wall reading the shift through the stone. Their word for deaf is their word for *holder*. It is the only calm profession on the plateau.

---

**The count.**

Every person over the age of nine is counting, all the time, at the bottom of the mind, the way you carry your own heartbeat without attending to it: shifts since last shift, shifts since the gathering, shifts since birth. Age is a number of shifts and they can all produce it instantly to the unit.

To lose the count is the local madness. The phrase is *adrift*, and it is understood as a condition rather than an error. The rule is absolute and everyone obeys it: if you lose the count, you sit down and you make noise and you do not move. Someone will come. There are people whose whole occupation is walking the strings calling for the adrift, and the culture treats them with the exhausted affection you would give a good midwife.

There are listener towers at intervals — stacked rimstone plates with a hollow throat that gathers the drone — and in every one of them, someone is sitting with a cord and a spike, knotting the count. Some of those cords run eleven thousand shifts. Some run four. Nobody moves the short ones. They hang where they were left.

---

**The gap.**

Forty seconds, twice a day, when the machines are silent and the only sound on the continent is rain.

They sit. Everyone, everywhere, all at once, across a province — down into a crouch with one hand flat on the stone and the other on a string or on the wrist of a child, because in the gap you must know where everything is without moving to find out. Nobody speaks. Nobody eats. Infants are held with a palm over the mouth, which is the only sanctioned silencing of a human being on the plateau and is done with the tenderness of an apology.

This is their religion, if they have one: not belief, but a twice-daily communal practice of doing nothing extremely well. Their highest praise for a person's character translates roughly as *good in the gap*.

And in the gap people hear things. Mostly the dead, which is understood and unremarkable — grief has a way of filling forty seconds of rain. But there is also the thing from below the rim. A very low note, long, at the absolute floor of hearing, felt in the sternum rather than the ear, and not every gap and not everywhere, and there are two positions on it that have been argued for as long as there have been cords. The first is that it is the plateau's own drone returning off something far down, an echo, and means nothing. The second is that it is an answer.

They will discuss this with you at great length and with great courtesy and neither side will raise its voice, because raising your voice in that argument is considered an admission that you have nothing.

---

**The mimics, who are the other people.**

You will have been told what a mimic is: an ambush predator that reproduces sound. That is accurate and it is not what they are afraid of.

What they are afraid of is that a mimic learns a *rattle*. It happens. A pattern gets taken — copied off a body it ate, or off a hundred hours of listening — and then a woman's name starts arriving in camps she is not in. The remedy is immediate and total: the family unknots and reknots every pattern in the lineage, all in one shift, and the old names are abandoned, and are never used again, and are not even knotted into the cords, and if you ask about a gap in a family's record you will be told the number of shifts and nothing else.

Against this they have the challenge, and the challenge is the foundation of their entire theory of persons. You call, and the answer must not be a repetition — it must be a *continuation*. You give the first half of something, and it must be completed in a way that has never existed before. Riddle-chains, nonsense-cascades, running jokes a family has been extending for six generations. Any adult can generate a hundred of these an hour. A mimic can produce anything it has heard and nothing it has not.

A person is a thing that can be surprised, and go on from there.

The cost of this is written into the cords in a sober, regular hand. The old fail the challenge. The concussed fail it. The grief-mute fail it — there is a wordless period after a death, recognised, exempted, and yet every generation somebody in that period is caught alone in the dark by neighbours with their blood up. Three calls are given. Three is the law. After three, the thing in front of you is handled.

They do not pretend this is fine. There is a specific ceremony afterward for a group that has handled one of its own by mistake, and it is long, and the whole district comes, and the cord it produces is knotted tight enough to read with a whole hand rather than a fingertip, so that no one can skim past it.

And there is one more thing, which they will not raise and will answer if you raise it: that in the last several hundred shifts, in three districts, a mimic has answered the challenge. Not repeated it. *Answered* it. Wrongly, clumsily, in the manner of something very good at sound that has only just understood that sound can be a question. There is no agreement about what to do. There is an argument, growing, quiet, conducted in the tone people use when a thing is too large to raise the voice about, over whether the challenge is a test of what a creature *is* or of what it has *learned to do*, and whether those were ever different, and whether the plateau is about to acquire neighbours.

---

**The ones born with eyes.**

Rarely — once in some thousands of births — a child is born with the organ.

It has no function. There is nothing here for it. But it works in the sense that it is alive and sending, and what it sends, in the absolute absence of anything to send about, is invention. These children describe. They describe continuously, from very early, with enormous specificity, things that are not there and that they have no word for and that nobody else can check; and because a fact requires two witnesses standing at different distances, and these children are always the only one standing where they stand, nothing they say can be true.

The culture has not resolved what to do about them and has instead built an institution out of not resolving it. The eyed are fed, protected, listened to, and never wholly believed. They are kept off the strings and out of the herds. Their descriptions are knotted onto their own separate cords, which are stored apart, in the oldest sheds, and which are consulted — this is the part nobody will explain to you — *before* certain decisions, and not others.

They are also, without exception, the people who ask what the machines are for.

---

**The unstrikers.**

Somewhere in every large lineage, one person carries a small hard pouch, and does not open it.

Inside is a lump of iron pyrite, a fractured flint, and a twist of cane fibre kept dry in a wrapping of gut, dry being the rarest commodity on the plateau and the maintenance of that dryness being a continuous, tedious, lifelong labour.

They do not use it. Using it is the oldest prohibition there is. The pouch is passed down, and the unstriker's single duty is to keep the fibre dry and to hold the vigil once a generation, in which they sit in a shed with the pouch open in their palms for an entire shift, alone, and then close it.

Ask why they keep the means of breaking the rule and you will get the only piece of abstract philosophy they will volunteer to a stranger: *a rule you cannot break is a rule nobody remembers.* They keep the temptation alive so that the refusal stays a live act rather than a fact about the world. It is the one thing they do that is not practical, and they know it, and they do it.

And there is a cord in one of the old sheds concerning the last time it was struck. It is not long. It has eleven knots. The first four are a place and a count, and the fifth is a number of people, and the sixth is the same number, and after that the knots are of a kind that occurs nowhere else in the record, and there is no seventh reading, because the seventh is where the cord is cut.

They will let you put your hand on it. They will not say more than that. They will wait, politely, with their heads slightly turned, listening to you drip, until you take your hand away.
