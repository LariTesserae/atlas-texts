## The Nine

The first thing to say about them is that every one of them is older than the world they live in.

This is not a figure of speech. The rock outside their walls is eleven weeks old; Adhel Vosk is sixty-one. Cor is older than that and won't say by how much. Even Sennet, who is nineteen and looks less, has more history in him than the entire mountain — more weather survived, more names learned, more dead relatives. Nothing on this peak has a past except these nine, who carry theirs up into it like contraband. They are the only old things here. It shows in them the way age shows in a tool brought into a room full of new ones.

The second thing is that they all have the same hands. Not the same shape — Bel's are broad and Pell's are small and Rhoan's are a surgeon's, narrow and unsentimental — but the same condition. The stone flour that falls from the ground ceiling takes the oil out of skin the way a solvent does, and then the dry air between exhalations finishes the job, and so every person in the room has black lines set into the creases of their knuckles, fine and permanent, like a diagram of a hand drawn on a hand. They grease them at night from a shared tin. It is the only thing in the town that nine people touch in a fixed order.

They are all, also, slightly deaf in the way that people go deaf who never hear anything loud: which is to say not deaf at all, but recalibrated, tuned so far down that ordinary speech would come at them as violence. A visitor arriving on the hoist speaks at a normal volume and nine faces flinch in unison and then, politely, unflinch. They have a register they call *room voice*, pitched somewhere under a whisper and over a breath, carried by consonants rather than air. Laughter, which cannot be permitted its usual shape, has been reduced in them to a hissing exhalation through the nose. They call it *sanding*. It is a full and genuine laugh; it simply sounds like a man cleaning a small piece of metal.

And they are all thinner than when they came, by an amount Rhoan Okolo can predict to within a hundred grams, because Rhoan weighs everyone on the fourth morning of every lift and has been drawing the line out to where it goes.

---

**Adhel Vosk** tends the frames, which means she walks four hundred paces of black floor eleven times a day with her back to the thing she is inspecting.

You cannot read a stint-frame by looking at it. Looked at, it is grey armature on a plinth, dull and dead and uninformative. It only does its work in the periphery, in that column of dark brilliance that isn't there when you turn your head. So Vosk stands beside each frame with her shoulder to it and her eyes on the empty floor and reads the after-image on the inside of her own eye: colour, steadiness, the particular sourness in the air that means a frame is working harder than its neighbours. She has done this six hundred times a week for eleven weeks and there is now a permanent grey absence in the centre of her vision, a soft coin of nothing, so that she sees people best by not looking at them.

She is the only person here who understands what is actually happening in the frames, and she has never been able to explain it to anyone in fewer than four hours. Her short answer, the one she gives at the meal, is: *the mountain is arguing with itself and we are the pause in the middle of the sentence.* She says this without any appearance of poetry. She is not a poet; she is tired, and it is the most compact true thing she has.

She is also the one who will know first. Everyone understands this. When Vosk is late back from a circuit there is a particular stillness in the kitchen that nobody acknowledges and everybody participates in.

---

**Cor** found the bubble. He was on the bore-crew, thirty-one men and women driving a pilot shaft into a plate that had started doing something the instruments called impossible, and his bit went soft and then went *through*, and he put his lamp to the hole and looked into ninety metres of empty dark that had been sealed since before there was anyone to seal anything against.

He named it in the report. *Held room* was his phrase, written before there was a single frame in it, when it was not held at all and would not be held for another nine days, and he has never been able to account for the word. The rest of the bore-crew went down. Cor stayed, and was quietly reassigned to sweeping.

He sweeps. Twice a shift he goes out with a rubber-bladed pusher and takes the flour off the streets and the plate-tops and the cistern lids, and he barrows it to the mixing tun, where it goes into the cast stone, so that the town is being built — has always been built — out of its own ground ceiling. He finds this satisfying in a way he doesn't discuss. He is the best-liked person in the room and the least known. He has not looked up in about six weeks. Not once, not by accident. If you ask him to check something overhead he will go and get Pell.

---

**Imre Tallow** keeps the water.

Two exhalations a day, four hundred litres give or take, and Imre is at the plates for both of them in a hooded coat with a stylus, watching condensate move down ribs he has memorised. He measures at the plate because the rule says so and because the rule is right: the tap will tell you what you took, and the plate tells you what there was, and only one of those numbers is the world.

He is dry-humoured, precise, socially easy, and he keeps two ledgers. The public one is cast into the day like everything else here. The other is a folded sheet in his coat, and on it he has been carrying a column of small discrepancies — nothing, really, eleven litres here, six there, the sort of drift you get from evaporation and spill and human sloppiness. He knows what nine people drink. He has known it since the second week. The private column is not accusatory; Imre does not think anyone is stealing water. He is simply unwilling to stop counting a thing that doesn't add up, and he has never mentioned it, because the only sentence he could make out of it would be an absurd one, and in a room where you cannot raise your voice you become very careful about which sentences you spend.

---

**Bel Kavarry** and **Sennet** do the lift.

Every eight days they unbolt a street, jack it a storey, and weld it down, and then seal the level beneath and leave it to go orange. Bel is forty and built like a person who has been doing physical work since childhood without ever being praised for it. She has the town's only real temper and has kept it, for eleven weeks, entirely inside her own chest, because there is nowhere in this room to put a temper down. She is the fiercest enforcer of *do not open a sealed level*, and she is fierce about it because she wants to open one so badly that it has become a private geography in her: she knows exactly which plate she would lift, and what is under it, and what she left there in week three when the sealing schedule moved up by a day.

Sennet is the one who shouted.

It was early — fifteen days in, before anyone had really learned the room — and he was twenty metres out on the floor and something moved at the edge of his eye near the cisterns, and he did the thing a nineteen-year-old does. The pressure came back off the frames and put every person in the town on their knees with their hands over their teeth, and when they looked up there was a hairline in the ceiling, a bright thread of it, fifty metres long, the only mark on an otherwise perfect surface in the entire world.

It has not grown. Vosk checks it daily and it has not grown.

Sennet has spoken, by Hazi's count, about four hundred words since. He works beautifully — he and Bel move a street together like two hands of one person — and he eats and sleeps and sands quietly at other people's jokes, and nobody has ever once brought it up, and this is both the kindest thing the room has ever done and the heaviest, because it means the thing is still there, uncorrected, fifty metres long, directly above them.

---

**Hazi Mun** keeps the log, and has become a writer against his will.

The log is a technical instrument with ruled columns: frame readings, plate yield, cistern levels, lift status, *conditions*. It was the *conditions* column that undid him. Eleven weeks ago he was writing *nominal*. Then he began writing what the daylight coin did — how it comes in hard-edged and goes out soft, how it crosses the fifth sink-plate at an angle that changes by about a finger a week as the season turns somewhere far above. Then he started noting the drop-rate at night as a rhythm. Then, the week the sinter fur bent around Pell's blocking plate, he needed a word for a mineral going after what it wanted, and he wrote *behaviours*, and knew as he wrote it that it was a word doing a lot of work, and wrote it anyway.

He is aware that the log is the only history this mountain will ever have of its first summer. He is aware that this is an absurd thing to be moved by. He is moved by it.

---

**Rhoan Okolo** is the medic, and therefore the cook, because there is not enough for nine people to do that isn't two jobs.

The kitchen is the only place in the room where an amber lamp is permitted, one, at the meal, for forty minutes, against the sink-plate budget — a cost Imre logs every single day without comment, which is his way of saying he approves. Under that lamp, once a day, nine people find out what colour they are. It is the second of the room's two lights and the only one anyone chose.

Rhoan does extraordinary things with compressed protein cake and vinegar and dried allium and the eight-day cage. He also weighs everyone on fourth mornings, and takes blood on twelfth, and has a chart he does not show anybody, and a second chart he does not show anybody that has nothing to do with weight: it tracks who has stopped going out to the coin of daylight. There have been two. Both came back. He has told no one that he is watching this, on the grounds that being watched is itself a weight.

---

**Pell** is the assayer, which in practice means Pell is the only person in the room with something to love.

The sinter fur on the eastern wall is not alive. Pell knows this, has run the tests, has written the word *mineral* on the sample cards in a steady hand. Pell also has forty-one rosettes under observation, each with a number, and each, unofficially, with a name, and the largest — a grey-green cauliflower mass the size of a folded coat, just left of the third vent-pore — is called Aunt. Pell goes out to the wall between exhalations and measures growth with callipers and photographs the reaching, and sometimes stands there for longer than measuring requires, in a warm damp corner of a room that is otherwise dry and black, beside something that is getting bigger.

The blocking-plate experiment was Pell's. Pell has not repeated it. When Hazi asked why, Pell said: *I already know the answer,* which is not a scientist's sentence, and Hazi wrote it down anyway.

---

**Ianthe Ruse** came up to assess.

She is from below — from the company, or the authority, or whatever the chain of offices is that signs off on the cost of eleven refusals — and her brief was eight days: inspect, cost, recommend. She has been here twenty-six. Her return slot on the cage has been given over to jacking stock three lifts running, each time with a perfectly good reason, and she has signed off on each reallocation herself, and she has stopped examining why.

She sees more flicks than anybody. This surprised her and surprised nobody else; the others take it as obvious that the room shows itself most to whoever is newest, in the way that a house is loudest to a guest. She has begun keeping a tally, which the others find indecent and cannot stop asking about. Her count is ninety-one. She has never seen two at once. She has never seen one in the coin of daylight. She has stopped believing the artefact theory and has not replaced it with anything, and when Imre — one night, at the plates, in room voice, not looking at her — finally told her about the private column and the extra litres, she did not say *that's absurd*. She said: *Show me the sheet.* And they stood there together in the mist with two small lamps, doing arithmetic that had no destination, while the wall breathed out around them.

---

**What they are for** is a question none of them can answer briefly.

The room produces nothing. It mines nothing, refines nothing, ships nothing down the cage. Its only export is the fact of itself: eleven frames holding open a cavity inside six thousand metres of live, rising, closing rock, day after day, in conditions that should have shut it in forty minutes. Somewhere below there are people for whom this is a proof, and a technique, and a number in a column. From a hundred kilometres away it is a smoking peak. From inside it is nine people, sweeping.

They keep an eight-day week and call it a lift. They sleep in one hall in nine cots because separate rooms cost heat, and they can each identify the other eight by breathing alone, and none of them will ever sleep well anywhere without the tick. They have a vocabulary that is eleven weeks old and already closed to outsiders. They touch each other's shoulders constantly and casually, in passing, in the dark, because in room voice you cannot call across a street and a hand is louder than a word. They are, in the fashion of people with no foundations, extremely calm.

And every one of them has done the arithmetic.

Twenty-six storeys stacked in a chamber that is ninety metres at the crown; seven levels of them lit and nineteen going orange beneath; a floor coming up at a hand's width a night and a ceiling that does not move, cannot move, is the one thing here that has been forbidden to move. Nobody has ever said the number aloud in the chamber. Bel worked it out with a tape and her thumb. Hazi worked it out in the log and then wrote *nominal* in the conditions column that day, the only lie in eleven weeks of entries. Vosk has known since before the first frame was cast, because she cast it. Ianthe was sent up to write the number in a report and has instead spent twenty-six days learning the names of Pell's rosettes.

Three more lifts. Perhaps four.

They have not decided anything. They are not, as a body, capable of deciding; that is what the cage and the offices below are for. What they do instead is this: they get up, and grease their hands from the shared tin, and sweep the ceiling into the walls, and measure the mountain's breath at the plate, and walk eleven circuits with their backs turned, and cook under one amber lamp, and at the appointed minute they go out — most of them, most days — across two hundred paces of black floor to stand in a travelling coin of savage white light and look at the actual colour of their own skin for as long as it lasts.

Then the coin thins, and goes, and they walk back into the grey, nine old things in a new world, listening with their whole bodies to a sound like a knuckle cracking in the dark, which is the mountain settling its shoulders, and which must not stop.
