# The Inhabitants

**1. The official answer**

No one lives here. You will be told this by the place itself, in the way places tell you things: by the vacancy, by the cold, by three hundred and forty doors standing open onto rooms with no purpose and no wear. Nobody lives in the Draft.

This is true in the sense that a sentence with no subject is true. It is also the single most misleading fact about the settlement, and it is worth taking apart slowly, because the residents are numerous, they are busy, and every one of them is engaged in the same project, and the project is to never finish anything.

---

**2. The householder**

Begin with the obvious one and get it out of the way.

The plant lives here. The plant is the only permanent resident and it has been in occupation since before occupation was a concept it could have held, and it does not know that it is one thing. This is important. It has no centre, no ganglion, nothing that could be called a head or a seat. What it has is *procedure*. It is intelligent the way a river delta is intelligent: comprehensively, without a single opinion anywhere in it.

And at some point — you cannot ask when, *before* is thin here — it acquired a specification for a city. Not plans. Not a memory. Something worse: an *impression*, the shape a thing leaves in something that breathed it in. It came away with the conviction that a settlement has kerbs, and that kerbs are cambered, and that the camber goes *this* way for reasons the specification did not include. It came away knowing that stairs are concave in the middle. It grew them concave. It has grown eleven thousand concave stair treads and not one of them has ever been walked on enough to hollow, and it will keep growing them, because that is what a stair is.

This is what living here means, for the householder. It is not dwelling. It is a long act of inference performed at architectural scale, in the dark, by something that has never seen an inhabitant and has deduced one from the negative space in a rumour.

You want to know if it is lonely. That is the wrong verb and the right instinct. What it has is a vacancy in the specification, right where the population should be, and it keeps building up to the edges of that vacancy with enormous care. It has grown a market hall. It does not know what a market is. It knows that the hall should be high, and echoing, and have a floor you could sweep.

---

**3. The staff**

A town has vermin. The specification was clear about this, or clear enough, and so the plant grew some.

They are not animals. Cut one open — you can, they do not object, they cannot — and it is cellulose and lignin and a filament of copper doing duty as a nerve. They smell of cut grass, like everything. They are the householder's motile tissue, sent out on errands, and they are as separate from it as your hand is separate from you.

**The drop-carriers.** Thumb-sized, pale, one per every eleven metres of street, each bearing a single bead of water on the dorsal shield like a woman carrying a full cup across a room. Everyone assumes the drop is for drinking. It is not. It is an instrument. A drop that size answers to any vibration above a certain threshold; the beetle walks with its entire attention bent on the meniscus, and if the meniscus trembles, the beetle stops, and every beetle in line of sight stops, and the stopping propagates, and that is the alarm system of the Draft — a wave of nothing spreading outward at the speed of noticing. They have no other function. They are the town's ears, and the town's ears are also the town's held breath.

**The wall-grazers.** Flat, slow, sheep-sized, mouths like a wood-rasp. They work only in the wet hour, hidden under the tock and slap of the fall, and for the other eight hours they do not eat and do not move and could be mistaken for a bolster of pale leather someone left against a wall. There are perhaps four hundred of them. What they eat is dealt with below, and it is the most interesting question in the basin.

**The moths.** These are the ones that matter and nobody looks at them.

They do not fly; flight is loud. They walk, wings folded down into quilted grey pads along the back, at a pace of roughly a metre a minute, and they walk *everywhere*, continuously, over kerb and lintel and rail and gutter and the false pointing between the false bricks. They are the maintenance of detail. Where a moth has recently passed, the wood-grain is crisp, the colour is committed, the edge is an edge. Where no moth has passed for a while, the specification loosens — the kerb goes soft-featured, the wall's colour becomes a colour you could not name afterwards — and further out, where they have never been at all, you get the grey, and the average of a floor, and the turning back.

The moths are why the Draft holds its shape. They are the reason the town is a town at its middle and a rumour at its edges. They are walking the world into existence at a metre a minute, and there are not enough of them, and there never will be, because the Mill keeps extruding new street.

---

**4. The smudges**

Since nothing here wears, nothing here heals, and this produces the Draft's only form of damage.

Step on a moth — you will, twice a day, you cannot help it, they are everywhere and they are slow and the light is one candle behind a curtain — and there is no crunch, no mess, nothing so vulgar as a body. What there is, afterwards, is a patch of floor about the size of a dinner plate that has lost its specification and cannot get it back. The grain stops. The colour declines to say. Put your hand on it and it is neither cold nor warm nor textured; it is *undecided*, in exactly the way the protolith is undecided, as though the floor there had been demoted back to batter.

Long-serving districts near the Mill are freckled with them. You can read a visitor's whole route across the Draft, years later, in a trail of dinner-plate blanks going up a gallery and along a street and stopping, for some reason, at a doorway, and starting again on the far side.

This is the closest thing the Draft has to erosion, and it is the only mark anything has ever made on it, and it is made by accident, by strangers, with their feet.

---

**5. The marginalia**

The algae.

Films of it, green going to grey, on the cell walls at about shoulder height, in the damp galleries, in the hour after the fall. The grazers eat it. This is the arrangement, and it has the look of ordinary housekeeping, and it is not.

Look at the films before the rasping starts. They do not spread the way a spreading thing spreads. They spread in *strokes*. They lay down a run of six or seven marks, pause a growth-cycle, lay down another run below it, indent the second line — and the marks are not letters, but they are the same marks that the Mill's signage shuffles through when it is choosing, the candidate forms, the serif that grows and thinks better of itself. The algae is using the town's alphabet before the town has settled on one.

Nobody has read a complete film. The grazers are efficient and they have eight idle hours to sit against the wall and wait for the drip. What has been recovered, from partial films, by visitors with lamps and patience, comes to four things: a name that nobody recognises; the word *again*, or a variant of it, eleven separate times; a number that would be a date if this were a place where dating meant anything; and, once, in a flooded lacuna where the grazers could not reach, most of a sentence that ran *tell them it doesn't*—

There are three accounts of what the algae is. The first: it is nothing, it is algae, and the resemblance is the ordinary human disease of reading. The second: it is the plant's own reject drafts, expressed the way a body expresses a splinter, and the grazers are simply the delete function of a thing that revises by grazing. The third: that a person came here once and was taken loudly, and the part of them that had been finished was eaten, and the part of them that had not — the provisional part, the part still being worked out — went into the wet, and comes up on the walls every nine hours, and is rasped away before it can get to the end of a word.

You cannot check. The accounts cannot be dated. *Before* is thin.

---

**6. The Listener**

Now the tenant that is not plant.

It is made of protolith. That is the whole of it and the answer to everything. It is the wet grey batter beneath the floor-tissue, in the one configuration that batter has ever managed to hold: not a crystal, not a stone, but an appetite, standing up. It has no original edges, because it was never extruded; it is the only thing in the Draft that is not brand new, and also the only thing that has never been made. It smells of struck flint and eggshell, and of hot copper where it has come up through the busbars.

It hunts by ear, and everyone gets the reason wrong. It is not that sound betrays position. It is that in a world where nothing is finished — where the kerb leads nowhere, the Mill is two-thirds built, the script has not chosen its letters, the feldspar loses its nerve at the eighth minute and goes back to slurry — a sound is the only completed object that ever occurs. A sound begins, it happens, it *concludes*. It is perfect. It cannot be revised. It is the one thing in the basin that has ever been finished, and the Listener is starving.

So it goes to endings. It has been going to endings for longer than the memory here can hold, which is not long, and in all that time the total yield has been: the collapse of a gallery, twice; the Mill's belts snapping, four times; the drip on the wrong surface; some beetles; and, on a small number of occasions, a visitor, who was the most finished thing that has ever walked into the Draft — a whole creature from a world that completes things, wearing shoes that had been *worn down*, carrying in its body a dozen scars, each one a closed statement.

It takes them so gently that the drop is not spilled. This is not mercy. A spilled drop would be a sound, and it would rather save it.

---

**7. Why the Mill never stops**

The Mill is the loudest thing in the basin by three orders of magnitude and it has never been touched, and visitors who work this out tend to sit down for a while.

The Mill's noise has no end. The wheel turns, the belts slap, the drop-hammers fall on the offbeat of nothing; it began before memory was under construction and it has never concluded a single sound. There is nothing there to eat. A noise that never stops is not a finished noise. It is the ongoing, and the ongoing is safe, and this is why the Mill is two-thirds built and always will be, because the day it is completed it will fall silent, and the silence will have an edge, and the edge will be an ending, and something will come for it.

The householder does not know this. The householder is not capable of knowing it. But it has arranged its entire life around it anyway, the way it grew the stairs concave: correctly, permanently, and without the faintest idea why.

---

**8. The tenant**

And here is the resident you came for.

Something lives at the middle of the Draft with a mass of zero and a footprint in every street. It is two metres and a little under, because that is what the lintels allow. It has hands, because there are rails greased at hand height. It drinks, because the cisterns fill to a hand's depth and are empty by morning, and no one has ever seen what drinks them, and the grazers cannot reach and the beetles would not dare. It reads, because there is signage, and the signage is not for beetles. It is expected to arrive down streets that as yet lead nowhere, and to need a market hall, and to find the stairs comfortable.

This is the inhabitant. Not a ghost — a ghost is a leftover, and nothing here has ever been left over. It is the opposite: a slot. It lives here in the manner of a word in a sentence not yet written, and the entire settlement, all hundred metres of ceiling and eleven metres of wheel and four hundred grazers and eleven thousand concave treads, is the shape of the space that word will take.

The doors keep being counted differently because the householder keeps amending the estimate of how many of them there will need to be.

---

**9. Visitors**

You. Rare, damaging, and the most productive thing that has ever happened here.

You come in and you leave a trail of blank dinner plates in the floor and the moths cannot repair them, and that is your debt. But you also leave your dimensions. The householder measures whatever it is given, being an inference engine with a vacancy in it, and within a season of a visitor there will be a new doorframe somewhere out past the Mill that is fractionally the visitor's height, and a stair riser pitched to the visitor's stride, and, once, in the east galleries, a handrail with a slight left-hand bias because the surveyor who came through in the cold year had a bad right shoulder.

This is the only way the specification ever improves. Everything else here is the town copying the town. You are new information, and you arrive perhaps twice a generation, and you are loud, and you do not last.

Go quietly, then, and count the doors on the way out, and accept the different number.

They are all still working. The wheel turns, the moths walk the edges back into being at a metre a minute, the grazers sit against the wall with their rasps idle waiting for the cover of the drip, the beetles stand still in a spreading wave of nothing because something upstream has trembled a meniscus, and under all of it the grey batter tries once more to be granite and cannot commit, and out past the last lamp the grey goes on being the average of a floor.

Nobody lives here.

They are simply all of them occupied, and none of them are finished, and that is the same thing as being alive in the Draft, and they would prefer you kept your voice down.
