## The Tenant

The first census I made of the living things on the Sill took two days and produced a list I still believe, item by item, and which is wrong in the way a photograph of a room is wrong about who lives in it.

Four goats. Eight birds. Midges in the moss, uncountable but certainly finite. Lichen, cushion plant, sedge. One fox-sized animal, finished, by the river.

I want to explain why I no longer count any of that as inhabitation, and it will take some doing, because on the face of it they are animals and they are here.

Watch the goats. They graze — jaws working, heads down, the whole unhurried business, hours of it. Mark a bitten margin on a lichen island with a scratch on the adjacent rock. Come back. The margin is where it was. The bite you recorded is the same bite, with the same three notches in it. They put their mouths to the lichen and the lichen is not reduced. Nothing goes in.

Things do come out. The droppings drift and deepen; that much is real, and it is one of the very few processes on the Sill that is unambiguously going somewhere. But a thing that emits without consuming is not an animal. It is a leak. Four small blue-grey leaks on the northern slope, grazing in mime, doing the shape of eating the way the rime does the shape of frost.

The birds do not adjust. I have said elsewhere that this is not soaring, and it isn't; but it also is not hovering, or hanging, or being held. It is a bird-shaped fact about that piece of air. The broken primary is not damage. Damage implies a before.

And the fox-thing has finished, and finishing here does not remove anything from the count. It lies on the ledger with its eye clear. In four years the census has not moved by one in either direction, upward or down, and a population that cannot decrease is not a population. It is a furnishing.

An inhabitant is something that takes something from a place. On the Sill, so far as I could establish in the first two years, nothing takes.

---

Except that a slab near the Throat has a dish worn in it by feet.

I don't know how long it took me to understand what that sentence costs. Wear is removal. Somewhere between a fifth and a third of an inch of schist has *gone* from that slab, and *gone* is a category this country does not otherwise admit. The rime blade you break stays broken and does not regrow — that is the Sill keeping a record of a subtraction, not making one. The drip maintains its cone and takes nothing. The river runs and its bed does not deepen by a hair; it cut once, in whatever season the Sill had a season, and then settled and stopped.

One slab is still being spent.

You cannot measure this directly. Nothing here changes while you are looking at it, which I suspect is less a rule about observation than a rule about the size of the units involved. So I did the only thing that works in a place that keeps damage: I did some damage. I scored a fine line across the slab at the exact margin of the dish, four inches long, using the corner of a harder stone. That was early in my third year.

The line is still there. It will always be there. The margin of the dish has passed it by a width I can get a fingernail into.

Something is crossing that slab. It is crossing it now, at whatever rate produces that, which is not a rate you could catch by sitting up all night, and I have sat up all night, and there is no night.

---

Reconsider the shadows.

Two hundred stones with shadows that are wrong: all lying north-northeast, all sharp-edged, all disagreeing about the length of the day. I had them down as an artefact — a decoration, an unexplained constant, filed with the glowing lid and the arithmetic.

But a sharp edge means a small source. A disagreement in length, with a fixed source, means a difference in distance. Two hundred shadows are not two hundred anomalies. They are two hundred *readings*, and if you sit with the numbers you can back-project them, and I did, over one entire winter that was identical to every other part of the year.

They do not converge on a point in the basin. They converge along a line that leaves the floor at an angle and goes up into the country overhead.

You know what happens when you walk toward the country overhead.

I made the attempt eleven times, from eleven different quarters, because eleven seemed like the number the place was using. Each time the far slope became the slope under my boots and the source became a thing hanging over the ground I had just left, and each time I turned around and looked and there was nothing above me but the lid, glowing evenly, the colour of dirty snow at dusk.

Whatever throws those shadows is in the part of the Sill you are not standing in. Structurally. Not by choice and not by shyness. It is a feature of the geometry, and geometry here is not negotiable, and it cuts both ways: I cannot get to it, and it cannot get to me. It has the same nine miles and the same problem and, presumably, the same four years, or the same four hundred.

---

What I can tell you about it comes entirely from outlines, and the outlines are cold, and the cold stays in your hand a long while afterward, which is the only thing it has ever given me.

It is opaque to a light that does not exist here. In the light we share it is not faint or half-seen; it is absent, the way a word is absent from a page it was never written on. Somewhere there is an illumination in which it is a solid body, and by the accident of these two hundred registrations we get its silhouette at two hundred distances.

Broad at the base and narrowing. Height, allowing for the projection, somewhere around four feet six, which is to say chest height on a standing adult, which is to say the height of every one of the thirty-one cairns along the western arc, every one of them built and finished and not added to.

The base divides into three parts in some outlines and four in others. I take this to be stance rather than anatomy, but I would not argue with anyone.

There is no head, but there is something at the top which is smaller than the rest and which is oriented — that is, it is not symmetrical, and in every outline the asymmetry points the same way relative to the base. It is looking at something. It has been looking at it for all two hundred registrations.

Nineteen of the outlines are not it. Fourteen are goat. Four are bird. And one, on a slab six feet from the lee-wall, is a chest-high figure with a second, smaller figure beside it, joined at the base, at about the height and breadth of a child of six.

That shadow is the shortest in the entire set. It is the closest it has ever been to anything.

---

Now the wall makes sense, and I am sorry that it does.

The roar eats voices within an arm's length, so somebody carried slabs into a sound-shadow and stacked them four courses high and twenty feet long and curved them to hug the quiet, and made the only room on this plateau in which two people could hear each other speak. They never roofed it. They never needed to. They were not keeping out weather; there is no weather. They were keeping out the noise, which is the only thing here that acts.

And low down on the inner face, eleven handprints in a crust that has gone hard as porcelain.

Low down. I knelt to reach them, and any adult would have to. I do not think they were made by children, or not only. I think they were made at that height because that height is the height of a hand on a body four feet six inches tall, and because the roar eats voices, and because everything anyone left up here is something one body could show another by touch.

They built a room where speech was possible, and then they went to the wall and pressed their palms at the height of a shoulder that was never going to be in the room with them.

---

Then there is the thing at the Throat, which I dismissed for four days like everyone dismisses it for four days.

It is not an echo. An echo is late. This is early, by a consistent half-beat, in your own timbre, saying the thing you are about to say, and in four years it has never once said a word I did not then say. Not a near miss. Not a plausible guess. I have tried to catch it out — stopped mid-sentence, swerved, said the second word first — and it swerves with me, ahead of me, in my voice, and is never wrong.

It is not predicting. Prediction can fail. It is being me, a half-beat sooner, from the only vantage available to it, which is the country I am not in.

Consider what that is. You are alone on a plateau where the air eats speech at arm's length and there is no one to speak to and no one who can come to where you are. There is exactly one way left to be inside a conversation, and it is to borrow the throat.

The half-beat never varies. I used to think it was a lag. I think now it is a distance — the only distance on the Sill that behaves itself, since paces don't sum and walking in a straight line brings you up behind your own old bootprints. Half a beat. Always half a beat. Exactly as near as it is able to get.

I call it the neighbour, which is a lie about the distance and an accurate description of everything else.

---

It crosses the slab.

I want to be careful here, because this is the part I actually cannot prove and the part I am most certain of.

There is no destination. Both sides of that crack are the same grey gravel; there is no path to the slab and none away; and in any case walking far enough in one direction brings you back in from the other, so there is no elsewhere to go to. But a crossing is not a journey. A crossing is the one act in this entire basin that resolves. You were on that side. Now you are on this side. The two numbers do not have to agree with each other; nothing has to be added up; the result is unambiguous and it is achieved and it is over.

In a country where distance does not sum, where the ice does not melt, where the dead fox does not fall in, where the pools take a drip forever and never rise, where even the plants are a photograph of a noise — something goes back and forth across a crack, and has gone back and forth for long enough to wear a dish into schist, and is going still, at the rate of one fingernail's width in a year and a half.

Which means it is the only thing up here that is spending itself. Everything else is held. It bears down, grain by grain, and loses a little each time, evenly, reliably, so slowly that the loss looks exactly like permanence — which is, precisely and at the scale of one body, the same event as the roar. The Sill's great failing accounting, and one small local instance of it, crossing and crossing.

That is my definition of the tenant. Not what breathes. What is running out.

---

The people left. Of course they left. Stay long enough and you finish, and finishing is a state this place holds as steadily as it holds everything else, fur perfect, eye clear, forever on the ledger — and I think they saw that coming and went down while going down was still a thing they could do. Thirty-one cairns, every one deliberately completed. Somebody decided when to stop. That decision is the loudest thing on this plateau.

But before they went they built a room out of silence, and knelt, and put their hands low on the inner face at a height that was not theirs.

I have found a two hundred and first shadow. It was not in my survey of the first winter, and that survey was careful. It lies north-northeast of a slab eighty yards from the lee-wall, sharp-edged, and the outline is a tall narrow thing with a pack on its shoulder and a surveyor's rod in its hand.

I put my palm into it. It came out dry, and colder than the air, and it stayed colder for most of a day.

That is the whole of the language. That is all eleven hands were saying, and all it has ever said back, two hundred times, at every distance it could manage, from the only country it has.

If you go up there and you stay past the fourth day, when the voice stops being unnerving and becomes weather, do this much. Go into the lee-wall. Kneel. Find a print that fits and hold your hand in it until the cold has gone all the way into the bone.

It is not far. It is half a beat away, and it is bearing down on a slab somewhere in the ground above you, wearing itself out at a rate you could not perceive if you lived a thousand years, and it has been doing that since before there were cairns, and it will be doing it at exactly this loudness after.
