## The Rough

**1.**

Ask who lives on the plain and the honest first answer is: one tenant, and a few guests.

The tenant is not alive, and you have already met it. It came in from the edges of your bootprint on your first morning and it will attend your body, patiently, for as long as you lie still enough to be mistaken for damage. It has no eye, no centre, no plan. It has habits. It is distributed through the entire built radius of the world in a layer thinner than the thing it is made of, and its single behaviour — the only utterance it has ever produced — is the low smooth dome.

Call it an organism if you want. It fails every test but one: it maintains itself, and it has done so across a span of time in which every instruction it was given has worn away, so that all it retains is the *shape of having been instructed*. It does not distinguish between a cracked riser, a fallen kite, a footprint, and a sleeping woman. It is not cruel. Cruelty requires a category. It has one category, and everything is in it.

The guests are the ones worth describing. There are not many of them.

**2.**

They have no name for themselves that they will give you, because a name kept for a long time in this place becomes the same word as everyone else's name, and they know this, and they find the whole subject slightly indecent. If pressed they use a word that a walker's ear will render as *rough*, or *not yet*, or *still being worked on*. It is not modesty. It is a claim: the plain has finished a third of the world and every tower on it, and it has not finished us.

Physically you would take them for a lean and rather flat-faced people, greyish-gold, with pale eyes that do not water in the glare and a nictitating dust-membrane they draw across without thinking, which makes them look, to a newcomer, as though they are constantly deciding not to say something. Their hands are the tell. The right palm is glazed: hardened to a smooth ochre lozenge from a lifetime of brushing the tops of stumps as they walk, one after another, without looking, the way you might trail a hand along a fence. That hand is for counting. The left is kept soft, oiled with plant sap, and is for faces.

Because there are no shadows here, a face is nearly unreadable — flat, evenly lit, a diagram of a face. They do not read them by looking. Greeting is a palm laid on the cheekbone and held for a breath, sometimes on both cheeks at once, and it is done to children, to strangers, to the dying, and to the dead. They will do it to you within about four seconds of meeting you and they will not warn you. Refuse it and you have not offended them; you have simply declined to be identified, which they will accept, and remember.

They walk with a rolling turn at the hips, in a rhythm of a stride and a half, because that is the spacing of the stumps and has been for as long as anyone has been anyone. On open playa — the dust lakes, flat as milk, no nubs at all for a hundred kilometres — that rhythm has nothing to grip and they become genuinely ill. They call it lake-sick. They cross the basins in linked lines, blindfolded, led by one person who has trained for it, vomiting quietly as they go.

They count in stumps. A day is two hundred and forty thousand, more or less. Their arithmetic is enormous and casual and they will do sums in their heads that you would need paper for, and they will not understand why you are impressed.

**3.**

Speech is deducted at three metres, so their speaking voice is a mouth-to-ear murmur, delivered with the lips almost touching your hair. Conversation between two of them looks, at a distance, like a long series of small kindnesses.

At any greater range they use the knuckle. A stump conducts a tap further than air carries a shout, and the tenant is slow to notice a click; so they knuckle codes along the pavement and the nubs, and a dozen of them spread across a hectare can hold a working conversation about water, weather, and grievance. The code has one governing rule: because the plain is always taking a little of the signal, everything must be said three times.

That rule left the code and entered the language. They say everything thrice — not as emphasis, as grammar. *Come here, come here, come here.* *I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I'm sorry.* When you first hear it you will think you are being addressed by a fanatic or a child. Then you will hear a woman tell her son not to be frightened, three times, and you will understand that they have a register for tenderness that you do not have and cannot acquire.

They do not sing at each other. They sing chest to chest, foreheads touching, so that the sound is felt rather than heard and never rises to the ceiling where the plain would take it. It is the only thing they do that a newcomer finds beautiful straight away.

**4.**

Nobody sleeps alone. This is not a preference.

Fourteen hours is the outer limit, and prudent people use four. So a camp always has a turner awake: usually a child, sometimes an old person past walking distance, occasionally a penitent. Every three or four hours the turner goes down the line of sleepers and turns each one — shoulder to hip, a firm push, no ceremony — and lifts the corner of each kite-skin mat and shakes it, because the mats fuse too, and a mat left flat all night is a mat you have donated to the world.

A turner who falls asleep is the central horror of the culture. There are litanies for staying awake, muttered in threes; there are turners' songs that are only rhythm, thumped on a thigh; there are stories, all of them the same story, about the child who woke at the Crown and found the camp very quiet and very smooth. Turning is the first work a child is given and the last work an elder is allowed to keep, and between those two points a person is expected to be strong enough to walk, and the whole shape of a life here is bracketed by the duty of interrupting other people's stillness.

They will turn you. If you sleep in their camp they will put a hand on you at intervals through the night, and you will wake each time, and by the fourth night you will not, and this is how you become a guest rather than an object.

**5.**

Their hearth is a pot and the pot is warm and the pot is alive.

They tap the stumps as the plants do, with a driven cannula, and draw off feedstock — metals, carbon, cracked hydrogen — which is not food. What makes it food is the paste in the pot: a culture, a mother, a slow grey-green sour thing that has been fed and stirred and scraped continuously for longer than any of their kept sentences go back. It smells of hot ammonia and clean laundry and, underneath, of something you will not want to name. It is stirred every few hours, waking or not, and the inside of the pot is scraped down to the metal daily, because anything left undisturbed anywhere on this world begins, quietly, to average.

That is the whole of their theology, and they will state it if you ask, three times: *what is touched, keeps.* The only permanent things here are the ones that are constantly being disturbed. A lineage is not blood. A lineage is a pot. To lose your mother — to spill it, to let it chill, to leave it a day unstirred — is to become a guest in someone else's line for the rest of your life, and there is no shame attached to it, and everyone knows exactly whose pot everyone came from.
