# The Residents

## I. Fifteen grams, and an address

Begin with one, because the mass will not let itself be read.

She weighs fifteen grams — a tablespoon of water, a folded letter. Slate-grey above and paler below, with a shallow-forked tail and long scythe wings that fold past it, and feet that cannot walk, cannot perch, cannot do anything but clamp. She has never in her life stood on a horizontal surface. She was hatched clinging and she will die clinging, and between those two grips she will be airborne for something over a hundred thousand hours.

Her day begins in the black. She lets go, drops, catches, and joins the outward current: an hour of birds pouring up the doline in the grey before dawn, spiralling out over the plateau and climbing. By full light she is at eight hundred metres above the cloudtop forest, working the thermals with several thousand others, taking flying ants and small flies and drifting spiders out of the air one at a time, at a rate of perhaps a mouthful an hour, which is enough. She does not land. There is nowhere to land. The world above the mountain contains, for her, no surfaces at all.

At dusk she comes back down through the cloud, and the difficult part begins.

The doorway is roaring. Behind it, two hundred metres of unlit dome, and along the walls four million nests laid edge to edge, wafer after wafer, indistinguishable in every particular. She goes in. She begins to click — a hard double tick, low-pitched, coarse-grained; with it she can hear a wall, and a hanging root, and a bar about the thickness of a finger, and nothing finer than that. It is not enough to find a thumbprint. It was never meant to be. The clicking keeps her off the stone; the rest is memory.

What she has, and what she is, is a route. Sixty metres in from the mouth, hard left at the airspace where the sound goes hollow, up along the third terrace of nests, past the aven where the roosting hiss changes tone, and then the last three metres by dead reckoning in absolute dark, at speed, to a bracket of dried saliva nine centimetres wide that she built out of her own mouth. She has flown it eleven thousand times. She could not describe a single feature of it. She simply arrives.

In the breeding months, the glands under her tongue swell until her whole jaw looks thickened, and every night for a month she lays down a thread of clear cement, weaving it in figures of eight against the rock, a wafer per session, until she has extruded a house weighing a fifteenth of herself. This is her entire architecture and her only property. Around her, four million others do the same thing, and where her mother did it and her mother's mother, the wall is raised by half a metre of layered protein: a reef of spit, on a reef of coral, both built by animals with no plan and no idea that they were building.

## II. Twelve grams, and a voice

Now the other shift.

He weighs twelve grams and looks, in the hand, like an accident: velvet-brown, wrinkled about the lips, ears mashed forward, a tail sticking out beyond the membrane like something left unfinished, and wings so long and so narrow that on the ground he is helpless and in the air he is a knife. He is built for one thing, which is to go a long way fast and come back.

He hangs all day in a pelt of his own kind at three thousand to the square metre, in a fug eight degrees warmer than the cave, and does nothing but shuffle, bicker, and be groomed. At dusk he drops into the column. Ninety minutes of rope. Then he climbs — a kilometre, sometimes more, up into the layer where the day's insects have been rafted along by the wind — and hunts planthoppers and small moths in the dark at forty kilometres an hour for six hours, forty or fifty kilometres out across the lowlands, over rice and oil palm and villages that hear nothing, and returns before the light. He eats a third of his body weight every night. He will do this for twelve years.

She, meanwhile, has one pup a year, and it is left behind.

The nursery is a district of ceiling chosen for heat: pups packed shoulder to shoulder, pink and naked, several thousand to the square metre, tiled like something being paved. When she comes back at three in the morning she does not go to a place. She lands in the approximate district and starts calling, and out of the general shriek — hundreds of thousands of pups all calling — one particular voice answers, and she goes to it, and checks it with her nose, and is right. She is right nearly every time. *Nearly* is doing a great deal of work in that sentence, and everything below the ceiling is fed on the difference.

There is more than one of him, of course. In his fur, flattened against the skin, walks a wingless fly with legs like a harvestman's and no eyes worth the name: a batfly, whose entire species inhabits the pelage of this one kind of bat, whose universe is warm, hairy, and forty square centimetres in extent, and which has met daylight only as an occasional catastrophe. With it: mites in the ear folds, a pseudoscorpion the size of a comma stalking the mites, and a fungus on the wing membrane that no one has named. An animal living on an animal living in a mountain made of animals. The stacking goes all the way down and it does not stop when it reaches the floor.

## III. The ground, which is an appetite

Everything falls, and the floor is what receives.

Set a boot on the cone and you feel a give before you feel resistance, and the give is bodies. Cockroaches, two or three deep in the good places, thumb-length, chestnut, greasy-shelled — tens of millions of them, in a slow permanent boil across thirty metres of warm hillside. They are the most numerous large animals in the mountain and the least individual. One of them hatches on the cone, feeds on the cone, breeds on the cone, and dies within a metre or two of where it hatched, having experienced no light, no season, no weather, no wind, no cold, no hunger, and no distance. Their world has one direction and it is *down*, and the down never stops.

Around and among them: guano crickets on stilt legs, blind and drab, sweeping with antennae four times their own length, reading the surface the way a doctor reads a pulse. Beetles that only eat the dead. Moth larvae that eat nothing whatever except keratin, and so exist entirely on feather and hair and the parings of claws, waiting decades' worth of generations for the specific manna of a dropped wing. And under all of it, a fog of mites so small and so pale and so numerous that a handful of guano is not a substance but a crowd.

When a pup falls — and pups fall, several hundred a night, missed by their mothers or shrugged off the roof in a panic-wave — the cone converges. It takes about four minutes to arrive and about ninety to finish. This is not cruelty; there is no cruelty available here. It is simply that the mountain permits nothing to sit still. Anything that stops moving is colonised within hours, and colonisation is the local word for grief, funeral, burial and inheritance all at once.

Above the tide walk the ones that eat the tide. Huntsman spiders flat as a hand on the shingled nests. Whip-spiders, ancient and horrible and entirely harmless, feeling out the wall with a single elongated leg used as a cane, sighted only in principle. Centipedes twenty centimetres long, red-orange, moving over the nest-crust with a fluency that looks obscene because it looks *considered* — taking eggs, taking chicks, taking each other, and then vanishing into a crack that seemed too small for a pencil.

And in the entrance passages, holding the doorway: the cave racers. Two metres of pale snake, faintly patterned, eye grown large and pupil grown wide, anchored by the tail in the crust of eight hundred years of nests and hanging out into the airstream with a third of its body free and horizontal, unsupported, for hours. It does not stalk. It does not chase. It hangs in the current like a fishing line and waits for the shift change, and twice a day the whole colony must pass through it. Four snakes at a doorway, ninety minutes, three and a half million bats: it is not predation in any sense the bats can register. It is a toll.

## IV. Downstream of three and a half million bats

Follow the water past the roosts.

The passages beyond are quiet. The hiss falls away, the ammonia thins, and there is a stream running over clean scoured rock — cold, black, and perfectly clear, though it is anything but clean in the chemical sense. Everything the colony produces is dissolved in it.

In the pools: a catfish the length of a finger, white, whiskered, its eyes reduced to two grey specks under the skin, hunting by feel along the rock with a metabolism so slow it can go a season on nothing. A crab equally colourless, long-legged, walking the walls at the waterline. Shrimp grazing a slick brown film. And the film is the actual resident — a mat of bacteria that lives on ammonia and nothing else, oxidising the mountain's waste for a living, converting the exhalations of three and a half million night-flying insectivores into nitrite, into nitrate, into food. It is invisible, it is the base of everything down here, and it has no behaviour to describe.

There is one more in this register, and it deserves its own line: a fungus, thread-fine and content in the warm guano, whose spores drift in the dust that rains from the roof when the pelt shudders. Inhaled, it will attempt to live in a lung. It succeeds often enough. It is the mountain's only remark on the subject of visitors, and it is not personal.

## V. What actually lives here

Now consider that none of the above is the answer.

Take the swiftlet's twelve years, the bat's twelve years, the roach's eight months, the fly's whole life in a patch of fur. Set them against a population that has varied by perhaps a fifth in three thousand years; against a column that has poured out of that doorway at dusk without a single missed evening for longer than any nation has existed; against a route to a particular nest which no bird invented, which is not written in any egg, and which is transmitted only by young birds following older ones through the dark until the geometry sticks.

What lives in this mountain is not three and a half million bats. It is one thing with three and a half million bodies.

It has an outline, and the outline is stable: a pelt on a dome, in the same districts, at the same densities, generation on generation. It has a metabolism — it inhales something like twelve tonnes of flying insects from four thousand square kilometres of lowland every night, and exhales them as a soft grey fall. It has a circulation: two ropes a day, out and in. It has a temperature, and maintains it, and the temperature is not the cave's. It has an excretory system thirty metres high. It has organs that despise each other and never meet — a day organ of birds, a night organ of bats — timed so exactly that they cross in the doorway twice daily and have done so for millennia without either shift ever occupying the hall alone.

It has a memory that no individual carries, held in the shape of a habit passed hand to hand for three thousand years. Its cells last a decade. It does not.
