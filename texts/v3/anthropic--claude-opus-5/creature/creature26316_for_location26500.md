## What lives here

Three kinds of things, and one of them barely counts.

The mats live here, and they are the great population, but they live the way stone lives, only faster. Leave them. They have been described.

The second kind is the pale grazers in the hair-forests. They are the size of a thumbnail, soft-shelled, eyeless, faintly warm to the tongue. They walk — they do not swim; nothing swims — up the black threads and eat the film that grows on the threads, and are eaten in turn by the third kind, combed out with a copper hook into a bag at the hip, a hundred at a time, chewed live because there is no way to keep them. They taste of cold metal. They are the only fat on the Shelf that is not pressed from a plant, and consequently the whole moral architecture of the people rests on them: you do not comb a hair-forest twice in a round, you do not comb another walk's forest at all, and a person who does is spoken of, without heat, for two hundred years.

The third kind is the people.

## The line

Look at one of them standing in a basin. The first thing to understand is that they are two different creatures joined at the navel.

Below the line — below the water — they are smooth. Scoured. Decades of pushing through weed have polished the skin of the legs and hips to something like the surface of the blocks themselves: hairless, hard, cool as the water, and largely without sensation except in the feet. The legs are enormous. Not muscular in the way a runner is muscular; thickened, columnar, laid on over a lifetime like sheetmoss adding layers, half of it fat. A person's thighs are their granary and their furnace and the reason they are still alive at fifty.

Above the line they are a garden.

Drizzle falls on the shoulders and the nape and the tops of the forearms, and where the water is not scoured away by wading, sheetmoss takes hold. It is encouraged. A well-kept back carries a black-green pelt a finger deep, warm on the underside, and it is the closest thing to clothing this world has ever produced. It sheds the drizzle. It holds the body's own small heat against the body. It can be eaten in a bad decade, though eating your own back is a thing done in stories and not in front of anyone. Within it live grazers, a private herd, and the grooming of one person by another — parting the pelt, picking the pale things out, eating them, oiling the smooth parts below with sheetmoss oil until they gleam — is the central intimacy of the culture, more freighted than sex, performed between kin daily, between strangers never.

The height of the line marks where you come from. In the true terraces it sits at the navel. In the slumped provinces where a coping gave way and the courses run deep, the people are chest-lined or shoulder-lined, gardens only on their heads and necks, and they are thought of, gently, as poor.

The hands are cold and stay cold. So are the feet, which are wide as a spread hand and have toes like fingers, hooked and horn-covered, and which have their own memory: a woman half asleep will still take the domes and grooves in the correct rolling gait, and will stop dead at a groove one hair wider than she expects.

The teeth wear flat by thirty from the grit that comes off dressed stone in water. After that, weed must be softened before it can be eaten, which is one of the things the old do for each other and the young do for the old.

Eyes: large, dark right out to the rim, useless for detail, superb at a change of grey. They dislike lamplight and weep in it. A lamp is not lit to see by. A lamp is lit so that eight people can put their palms above it in turn, and the light is a by-product that has been made into a ceremony because it would be wasteful to admit it is an accident.

Ears do less work than the jaw and the shins. Under the universal hiss, sound arrives best through stone and water and bone. They speak close, mouth to ear, a hand always resting on the other person's forearm — the hand is the punctuation; you can hear a sentence end by the pressure of a thumb. At distance they use the pole.
