## What Lives Here

You will not census this place by looking. The fog gives you fifteen meters on most mornings, the light is shadowless, the vegetation closes behind you within the hour, and nothing that matters makes a noise. The census is taken by field. Stand still with both palms open and wet, one against the peat's conductive mat and one on any reel vine, and wait until your hands stop being hands and become a kind of hearing. Then the plateau resolves. Every living body on the Shelf is a hole in the current, an eddy, a small local theft. The peat-swimmers are broad slow smears travelling under you at the speed of a walking man. The flue mice are grain, static, a drizzle of small subtractions inside every column. The charge-grazers are dense knots that sit still for hours and then shift half a meter. It is a crowded read. There is no square centimeter of the Shelf that is not somebody's address, and the field says so.

And then there is the one that moves wrong.

It moves too slowly to be weather and too smoothly to be a branch settling. It draws steadily rather than in the pulses of something breathing hard. It comes down a vine and the crown above it dims — not the ragged patchy dimming of a grazer swarm, but a clean even fade, like a hand laid over a lamp — and then, ten minutes later, thirty meters along, the crown relights.

That is the resident. That is the nearest thing this country has to a person, and I want to be careful about that, because it is not one.

## The Body

Call it the browser. The people who would have named it are not here. *Lamplighter* is better and is worse.

Nine kilograms distributed along two and a half meters of span. Four limbs of nearly identical length, each ending in a hand, each hand with five long fingers and a sixth opposed digit set well back on the wrist — a hook, essentially, for parking. It does not stand on anything. It hangs, always, from two or three points, and redistributes; a browser at rest is a shape that has been draped rather than placed. The torso is small and deep-chested and holds a heart that beats eleven times a minute in a cold hour and never much faster.

Its pelage is not fur. It is a coat of fine erectile filaments, each one rooted in a mechanoreceptor, each one insulated along its length and bare at the tip, standing at whatever angle the local potential dictates. You have felt a bad version of this yourself: the hairs lifting on your arm under a dense stand at dusk. The browser wears ten million of them and reads the whole field with its skin the way you read a lit room. Walking beneath a strong crown it lies flat and sleek; passing through a dead patch where the grazers have stripped the line it roughs up all over, bristling, the way a cat does, except that the cat is angry and the browser is simply seeing nothing and wants more surface to see nothing with.

The eyes are enormous, wet, and nearly useless for distance. There is no distance. The pupils do not contract, because there is no sun to contract for. They are tuned for one thing, which is glow: the corona standing off the flutes, the luminous animals, the faint blue seep of its own kind gripping a line a kilometer off. They weep constantly, flushing the fog's deposit, and this is why every account of the animal — there are no accounts of the animal — would begin with the wet eyes.

The hands run warmer than the body, always damp, palms lined with pore-fields. Contact is a sense. The browser holding a vine knows what the vine is carrying, where the load is, and roughly how far away the nearest break in the circuit lies. It knows this the way you know a wall is cold.

## What It Eats and What It Does

It eats charge-grazers. Hand-sized, beetle-hard, clamped to the reel vines and drinking the current down, and where they cluster the crown above goes dark and the vine cools and stops steaming. The browser does not hunt them. It does not have to. It feels the darkness they make, from valleys away, as a patch of numbness in the coat — and it goes there, unhurried, over hours, and works the patch.

It takes each grazer with three fingers, rotates it, opens it at the ventral seam with the flat nail of the opposed digit, and empties it. This takes about forty seconds. It does this eighty or ninety times a night. The crown above relights behind it in a slow gradient, the way a cooling filament reverses, and the vine warms, and by morning that stretch of wire is steaming again.

Now: it does not strip the patch. It never has. It takes roughly six in ten and leaves the rest, and — this is the part I would not report if I had not watched it twice — it carries. Three or four grazers, tucked in the crook of a hind hand, along thirty or forty meters of line, to some other vine that is running hot and unbitten, where it sets them down and lets them walk.

I have no idea whether this is husbandry. I do not think the word survives contact with the animal. But the consequence is measurable. A browser's range — call it two to three square kilometers, sixty to a hundred stack trees and every vine between them — carries a flatter load, a more even corona, a higher standing crop of grazers, and a greater mass of warm cable than the unattended forest outside it. The land is different where it lives. There is no artifact, no nest, no cache, nothing built. There is only a patch of subcontinent that has been tuned, over decades, by something that can feel it going out of tune.

The rule holds. Nothing here was built. Everything here was manufactured. A browser's range is a manufactured thing, and the browser is part of the manufacturing, in the way the crab is part of the shell.

## Why It Is Slow

Because a body moving through a charged forest makes a wake.

Every conductive thing here — the mat moss underfoot, the vines, the wet wall of every stack tree — is part of one continuous sheet, and to move across a sheet like that is to announce a moving disturbance in it. Everything with a palm or a whisker can feel it. Speed is legible. Speed is a signature, and there are things on the Shelf that read signatures and follow them, and they live in the peat and come up under you.

So the discipline is absolute and starts in the womb. The browser's nervous system is geared to keep every motion below the threshold at which it would register as an event. It shifts one limb at a time. It moves a hand for four seconds to travel fifteen centimeters. It can cross a valley in a night and it can, if something is wrong, hang perfectly motionless for nine hours with its coat flat and its eyes open, drawing nothing, which is the nearest thing this world has to hiding.

The deliberation you would read as melancholy, or as insomnia, or as thought, is none of those. It is the posture of an animal in a country where hurry is a shout.

## Speech

Volume failed here long ago, so they do the only thing available: they load the line.

A browser grips a shared vine with both wet palms and lets the current cross its body, and the crown above that vine dims for as long as it holds, and comes back when it lets go. Three seconds dark. Eight seconds lit. Two seconds dark. In the twenty minutes of an afternoon when the rain lifts the fog and a valley opens, this is visible across four or five kilometers to an eye built for glow, and there is always, somewhere in the view, a crown going down and coming back.

It is not a language. It is perhaps twenty distinguishable holds, and most of them mean some version of *here, still here, the line is holding, I have not moved*. A few mean other things. Two browsers on adjacent circuits will spend a season on the boundary vine, dimming at each other across the fog, and mate once, and part, and go on dimming. The whole conversation of a lifetime could be transcribed in an afternoon and would read like a tide table.

They have no voice. There is one exception, and you have heard it in the inventory of this place: *somewhere, very occasionally, a single knock of wood.* That is a mother striking a stack tree with the heel of her hand, and it means *come*, and it is addressed to a juvenile too young to read the field, and she will use it perhaps forty times in her life. It is the loudest sound the Shelf produces on purpose.

## The Only Bare Surface

Every surface here is a substrate. On the stack trees grow epiphytes, on the epiphytes smaller epiphytes, on those liverworts, on those a film of algae, and in the film, animals. A fallen trunk is colonized before it has finished falling.

The browser's coat is a surface.

So it grooms, and the grooming is not vanity or hygiene but the maintenance of its single sense organ. Two hours in every six, with a comb-toothed second finger, working the filaments in tracts, stripping the algal film and the liverwort spores and the fine green fuzz that starts within a day, eating most of what it strips. A healthy browser is the one clean thing on the plateau: grey, sleek, faintly ozonic, and utterly unlike its background in a world where nothing else is unlike its background.

This is how you read its age, and it is how the Shelf finally takes it back. Old browsers go green at the shoulders first, where the hands reach worst. The green thickens. The filaments beneath it are insulated, smothered, blinded, and the field goes quiet in patches — a numbness at the shoulder, then along the spine, then everywhere — so that the animal is not so much dying as being slowly unplugged from the only thing it ever perceived. It hunts badly. It stops carrying. It grips a warm vine to hold the heat and stays there, drawing, and the crown above it dims and does not come back up.

And the neighbors read the obituary in the correct way, which is this: some weeks later the grazers boom on the unattended circuit, and the vines cool and stop steaming, and across a whole small valley the corona fails, and for a season there is a patch of the Shelf that has gone dark and cold in the middle of a lit country. That is a death here. Not a silence — everything is silent — but a darkness. The offspring, who learned the wiring by being carried along it for six years before it ever fed itself, goes in and takes the range, because it is the only one alive who knows where the lines run.

Inheritance without property. A memory of vines that outlives the vines.

## Coda

It is not at the controls. Nothing is at the controls; that has been the honest fact of this place for a hundred thousand years, and the browser does not amend it. It cannot see the plateau, has no notion of a plateau, has never in its life been more than nine meters from something alive and touching it.

But it is the only body on the Shelf that perceives the apparatus *as an apparatus* — as a surface that is either lit or not lit, loaded or slack, running warm or gone cold — and that spends its entire long slow century adjusting one small patch of it toward some condition it prefers and cannot name.

At four o'clock the rain comes and the fog lifts a little, and if you are on the right hill for those twenty minutes you will see, far out across the convex green, one crown among ten thousand go dark, and hold, and come back, and go dark again.

That is what lives here. That is the whole of what it has to say.
