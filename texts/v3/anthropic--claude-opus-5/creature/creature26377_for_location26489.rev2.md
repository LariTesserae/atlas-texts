## The one on your wrist

Take the nearest. It has already chosen you; it came down the thermal and landed on the back of your hand and set its hooks, and it will not be leaving.

Nine plates, dorsal, overlapping back-to-front like roof tiles laid by someone who expected weather from below. The colour of old candle wax because there is nothing here to be coloured for and nothing to hide from — no light has fallen on this lineage in a span of time the mountain itself can barely remember. Ten legs, though the front pair are shortened and held folded and do no walking; they are used for handling, for the fine work, for cutting. Each leg ends in a hook and each hook has a gland, and the gland weeps a little clear syrup that hardens on contact with anything cooler than the animal. Set the hook, weep, harden: that is a footstep. Every step is glued and every glued step is permanent. Nothing in the Kiln walks. Everything in the Kiln *builds a small amount of the Kiln* and then breaks it off and moves on.

Underneath, the body is soft and slack, a purse. Inside the purse is the blood: nitrates, chlorides, a fraction of sulfur, running clear and heavy like hot honey and weighing far more than you would credit for the size. That weight is the animal's entire fortune. It is also, at the wrong temperature, its coffin.

There are no eyes. There are no ears. There is no mouth in any sense you would recognise — food here is not swallowed, it is *stood across*. The ridges on the back are the sense organ, and they are extraordinary. Each ridge is a comb of filaments tuned to a slightly different fraction of a degree, and the animal reads the difference between what its feet are touching and what its back is bathed in, and reads it continuously, and reads it to a precision that would take you an instrument and a steady hand. It does not know *warm*. It knows *warmer-than, by this much, in that direction*, and it knows it the way you know which way is down.

So: it cannot see the chamber. It can, however, feel the shape of the chamber, and what it feels is not a room. It feels a slope. The fissure at the low end is a permanent downhill; the doorway is a hole in the world that opens and closes twice a day and out of which pours something like a heavy, falling weight of nothing. The floor is the valley, the vault is the height, and the whole two hundred paces is a single long incline of value, steep here, shallow there, with the good places crowded and the poor places bare.

It is standing on your hand because your hand is the steepest thing in the Kiln.

You are, to this animal, an extraordinary event: a cliff, a waterfall, a gradient so violent it has no analogue in a life spent parsing hundredths. Nothing on this world has ever been that cold and moved. It has set its hooks in the richest seam it will ever find, and it is at this moment running its chemistry at a rate it has never achieved, and the reason it will not be leaving is that it is already stiffening. Its blood is setting in its legs. It came to the feast and the feast is killing it at the ankles first.

When you go back out through the crawl and stand in the scree and brush yourself down, what falls off you is a handful of small white statues. Stand still for ten minutes in the middle of the chamber and you will be carrying several hundred. Walk the length of the floor and you will leave a line of them where you passed, set where they fell, which the tide will tread into the stone over the next thousand cycles as a faint pale seam running two hundred paces from door to fissure. There is more than one such seam down there. Not many. But more than one.

## The coat

Now stop looking at the individual, because the individual is the least interesting thing in the room and, arguably, is not the unit of anything.

Consider instead a drape: forty thousand animals hanging in a chain from the vault, hook into hook into hook, swagged like a rope under its own weight. Only the topmost are touching rock. The rest are touching nobody but each other.

A chain like that is a conductor. Heat comes up into the first, and the first takes its tithe of the fall and passes the remainder down, and the second takes its tithe, and so on, four hundred bodies deep, until at the tip of the drape there hang the ones living on the thinnest margin in the Kiln, a few thousandths of a degree of difference, harvesting almost nothing, waiting. Position in the chain is everything, and position is constantly, silently contested — not by fighting, there is no fighting here, but by a slow patient upward creep, everyone walking over everyone, the whole curtain turning over itself like a rope being hauled through its own length. A drape that looks static is in fact rotating. Give it a season and every animal in it will have been at the top and at the tip.

This is also true of the coat on the walls, and of the floor, and of the whole population at once. The coat's thickness at any point is the answer to a question — *how much life will this square of rock support?* — and the answer is recalculated continuously, by two hundred million legs, by nothing but each animal shuffling a body-width toward whichever neighbour feels better off. Twice a day the question changes, because the door opens cold onto the floor and the whole distribution has to be solved again, and it is solved again, in about four hours, without anything anywhere having intended it.

That is the nearest thing to a mind in the Kiln. It is a real one. It is just not anybody's.

## What the sound is

They speak by writing on each other's backs.

A hook is warm. The ridges are the finest thermal sense on the planet. Draw a warm point slowly across a neighbour's dorsal comb and you have made a mark it can read as clearly as you read ink — and the neighbour passes it on, and it propagates through the coat as a slow bloom, a rumour of warmth travelling the wall at something like the speed of a walking man.

So: the sift. The dry, even, directionless whisper of flour poured from one bowl to another, which after fifteen minutes you stop hearing.

That is the sound of several million animals talking at once.

The Kiln is not quiet. You are deaf.

What they say is mostly what anyone says: *here is better, there is worse, something passed above me, I am spent, move up.* The grammar is gradient and the vocabulary is position. It is not rich. It does not need to be rich. But it is language in the only sense that matters — a mark made by one body and understood by another and passed on changed — and the residue of it is under your boots, because the hook that writes on a back is the same hook that glues a footstep, and the floor of the chamber is a palimpsest of every path anything ever took, laid down in glass, nine metres thick, signed. Each animal's syrup carries its own trace of sulfur and chloride. A trail can be tasted. Who went by, and how long ago, and how rich they were when they passed, is written on the ground in a hand that cannot be forged and never quite wipes out.

Nobody reads it. There is no reading, only walking, and walking is writing, and the floor rises.

When you shout, none of this happens. A shout arrives on every back in the chamber simultaneously, at every ridge, with no direction and no gradient and no meaning — a white page slammed down over all the writing everywhere. The drapes let go because there is, for that instant, nowhere better and nowhere worse and no such thing as up.

## The hunters

They are the same animal.

There is no separate predator in the Kiln. What comes down the drapes on ten legs at arm's length, harvesting every third body and leaving the chain to close over the gap, is what one of these becomes if the colony decides to make one — and the decision, like every other decision here, is made by nobody. Somewhere in the vault, in the hot air under the cold stone where the oldest chains hang and have never in their lives touched rock, a body that is unusually well-positioned for an unusually long time stops tithing downward. It keeps. It thickens. It goes on drinking the fall of the gradient and storing the proceeds until the purse is enormous, and then it sheds its plating and grows out and comes down.

And it begins to burn.

Nitrate and sulfur, held apart all its life in separate sinuses, are let together in a slow controlled trickle. It is a match head that has learned to walk. That is the whole of its heat, and its heat is the whole of its point: it runs a hundred degrees above the room, it lives eleven or twelve days, and in those days it must eat its own kin at a rate that would be obscene if it were feeding itself. It is not feeding itself. It is bunkering.

The harvest is not an attack and the drape does not resist it, and this is not fatalism and not stupidity. A colony that cannot make a hunter cannot go anywhere. Everyone in the chain is descended from a chain that gave up every third body when asked. The single dry sound like a fingernail drawn down a comb, the one sound in the Kiln that is made on purpose — that is the drape signing for it.

## The door, and the bowls, and the spiral

Nobody carried salt up a mountain.

The bowls are fired clay, and the clay is the crumbled floor of the Kiln — spent plates and hooks and glass, ground fine by traffic, wetted with syrup, worked by those short folded forelimbs, and set for a while on the lip of the fissure where the rock glows. There is no other kiln on this world and no other word for this one. What is in them is refined blood: nitrate leached and recrystallised and leached again over I would not guess how many generations of feet crossing over and polishing the dome. It is the purest oxidiser within four hundred miles. It is a magazine, and it was assembled grain by grain by animals that will never use it, and it is stationed at the height of a standing hunter, a stride apart, along the west wall, which is the wall that leads to the door.

Eleven stations. A hunter tops off as it goes, and it needs all eleven, because what it is about to do costs more than a body can hold.

The doorway is a lock. Three courses and a bowing lintel and a forward pitch, all of it cut and set by forelimbs working blind, and all of it a device for making the incoming cold fall *slowly* — for holding a pocket of survivable air at the sill on the one night in a lifetime when something has to stand there and gather itself before going out.

The spiral above the lintel had nine turns when it was cut. This is a place-name. Somewhere out in that dry black there is, or was, a vent with eight turns cut over its door, and it seeded this one; and before that a seven; and so on back to a first chamber that presumably had nothing over its door at all, because there was nothing yet to distinguish it from. The Kiln at the Ninth Turning is not a title. It is an address, in a system of addresses maintained by animals that cannot see it, kept legible only because the cutting is a thing the forelimbs know how to do.

The five strokes beside it are departures. Not attempts — departures that succeeded in the only sense the Kiln can measure. When a hunter goes out the door at full burn, the ones crowded at the sill can feel it on their backs, a dwindling warmth going away downslope, and they hold there in the killing cold as long as their legs will let them, reading it out to the last hundredth, and when it passes below the resolution of the finest sense on this world somebody turns and goes back in and cuts a line.

Five times, out of very many.

If you go back outside and turn over the scree below the door, you will find the others. Arm-long, ten-legged, glass-white, set mid-stride, all facing downhill, in a spray that thins with distance: a hundred in the first ten paces, a dozen at fifty, then two, then one at a hundred and ten paces out, alone, further than any of its neighbours ever got and not nearly far enough. That is the trail the mountain took back. There never was a path. It is a scatter of the ones that fell short, and it has been accumulating for as long as the doorway has stood, and it is the only monument here that is legible to you.

Four hundred miles. On a night with no moisture in the sky to hold any warmth in it at all.

Five made it out of feeling. Whether any of the five made it to anywhere — whether there is a Tenth Turning cut over a door somewhere east, with its own eleven niches and its own patient bowls and its own spray of white statues fanning out downhill — nothing in this chamber knows, or has any means of knowing, or will ever be told.

They keep the bowls stocked anyway.

## What is here now

A distribution. Two hundred paces of it, packed to every surface, rotating slowly through itself, thinking about nothing but where to stand and thinking about it well; talking constantly on each other's backs in a language of hundredths; writing its whole history into the floor by accident and standing on it; dying at the edges into the pale stone that lifts the living another fingernail closer to the heat each few thousand cycles.

And in the hot dark under the cold vault, where the oldest chains hang and have never touched rock, one body has stopped passing its share downward and has begun, very slowly, to thicken.
