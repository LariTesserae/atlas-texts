## Notes on the Residents

**On the word.** Nothing in the room is alive in the way the word usually means, and everything in it is a resident. The two facts have to be held apart or the census makes no sense. Life, as it is generally practised, is a debt taken out against the future: you eat now in order to move later, you breathe in order to keep breathing, you build one course of a wall so that the next has somewhere to sit. The room has no later to lend against. What it has instead is occupancy — a set of things holding position, each one complete, each one at no point in its own story. They are not living there. They are *there*, thoroughly, in the way a chord is there.

So: not an ecology. A tenancy.

---

**The wall population.** Take the crickets first, because they are the loudest and the most misunderstood. Visitors assume many crickets making a noise together. It is one noise, wearing crickets. The hiss is a single waveform and every fissure in the room is a place where that waveform has a body. Look closely at a stridulating leg — you can, they permit approach, they have no startle because startling is a change — and the leg is not moving through its stroke so much as *occupying* it, the way a spoke occupies a wheel that is turning too fast to have a position. Nothing about them tires. Nothing about them warms up. They are the room's throat lining.

The chalkmoths walk a circuit that has no first step. This has been checked. Follow one — mark it, if you like; the mark will not survive, but it survives long enough — and you will find it comes back to where it began, having passed through no beginning. There is no moment at which a chalkmoth sets out. They are all always partway. Their wings, crusted in the same mineral as the wall, are not camouflage and not disguise; the wall and the wings are the same event, and the moths are the part of the wall that has been given permission to circulate.

The snails are the room's arithmetic. Eleven hundred and four is a reliable count and a meaningless one, because in four centuries no visitor has ever managed to count the same snail twice — not because they lost track, but because the snails do not support the operation. What is reliably eleven hundred and four is the number of *positions* at which the wall is currently being a snail. The shells are grown out of the rock without having grown. If you pry one — please don't; the edges of this room are struck-flint sharp and your hand will open and stay open — you find no attachment scar, because attachment is a history.

The mites are texture. They are how the room manages resolution. In the film of water on the crystal faces they simmer at a rate that never varies, and their function, insofar as anything here has one, appears to be to keep the water from being smooth, since a smooth water surface would hold a reflection, and a reflection is an image of a moment, and moments are exactly what this room is trying not to have.

**The nine and the twenty.** The rosettes and the moss cushions are the room's allowance of green, issued whole. All the rosettes are the same size because size here is not an outcome. They are not young and not old; they have no seedling and no death; they are the shape a plant makes when you subtract growing from it, which turns out to be a shape that looks slightly obscene to us, the way a face without expression does. They are rooted in hairline cracks that do not widen. They are, in the strict sense, the only decoration.

---

**The mason.** Somebody built the nine courses against the south wall and it was not a visitor. Visitors cannot leave anything; the wall is left. Whatever built it is roughly the height of your palm, has hands disproportionate to that height, and comes and goes through the door carved beside its work — a door correctly jambed and lintelled, with a threshold worn concave, opening onto unfractured stone. The door is not decorative. It is the appropriate design for a thing that does not travel by distance. In a room with no route in, a doorway into solid rock is not a joke; it is the only honest kind of doorway available.

You will never meet it. This follows from the arithmetic of the place. The room runs at a fixed total and defends the total, and a visitor is a substantial addition — warmth, moisture, fog, noise, an open cut. Something has to be deducted to make room, and what gets deducted is the mason. It goes out through its door the instant you arrive inside your own interrupted sentence. This is not eviction. From the mason's side it is presumably a very long series of brief absences, or one absence with visitors sprinkled through it. There is a single account, from a woman who came out of the room mid-breath on the slope above Kilnhow in 1911, of something at the sill at the exact moment of leaving — grey, hand-high, upright, in the act of stepping back in, not hurrying. Leaving is a change of state, and during a change of state the total slips.

Everything about the mason is worn and nothing about it wears. The flake in the pocket above the sleeper is blunted along its working edge and has a groove cut for a haft that never existed. That is not a broken tool; that is what a tool looks like in a room where use is a property rather than an event. The mason's calluses, if it has them, were not earned here. It arrives already worn, the way you arrive already mid-sentence. Its whole biography is somewhere else, in a place where things wear, and it brings the results in with it like weather in a coat.

What it is building is a dam. Two hands high, nine courses, mortarless, exquisite — and hopelessly small, and pointed the right way, which is to say it faces the west end, under the brightness, where the seepage will one day begin. The tenth stone is shaped for its place and lies beside the place. Set it yourself and it returns, same angle, same attitude of readiness. The room will not permit the tenth course, not because the room fears a dam, but because ten courses would be a wall *finished*, and finishing is the same forbidden category as growing, rotting, healing, and sleeping. So the mason's work is held in the identical condition as everything else it lives among: excellent, complete in every part, and unstarted.

The four tally marks by the pool are its marks, cut with something harder than the wall, and they are not days. They are near-wakings. There have been four. They were not made in sequence — the room cannot hold a sequence — and so they arrived as a group, the shape of a count rather than a count, which is why nothing about them suggests either interruption or completion. There is going to be a fifth.

---

**The sleeper.** Cat-sized, six-limbed, furred in a grey that returns the ambient light at precisely the intensity it receives, which means it has no shading, no modelling, and no edge you can trust; several visitors have described it not as an animal but as an animal-shaped hole in the room, filled in with the wall's own value. Twelve breaths a minute. It has been asleep for the entire duration of the room. It is not thin.

It is not thin because it is eating, and what it eats is the subtraction.

Every deduction the room performs is a swallow. Your body heat, taken; your breath-fog, ceasing rather than dispersing; the name you scratched into the wall, gone before you can look up; your blood, which does not stay; the eleven seconds of sound you are permitted to carry out but not one second more. The room does not defend its fixed total out of some abstract fastidiousness. It defends it the way a diaphragm defends a rhythm. Nothing decays here because decay is another mouth, and this room has one mouth and it is asleep and it is provided for.

The pool is its water. Always exactly full to the line, and the line is not a stain but a change in the mineral present from the beginning, which is the room's way of saying: this is a measured ration and the measure preceded the drinking.

And the dream is the room. That is the least defensible of these notes and the hardest one to shake once you have been inside. Consider the evidence as a dreamer's economy: no shadows, because shadows require a lamp and a dream has no lamp; no dust, because dust is the residue of having-been and a dream has no yesterday; a soundtrack that loops without a seam, because ambience is expensive and a sleeper reuses it; plants at one size, because a dream renders the plant, not the plant's childhood; a ceiling seam that rises three metres and simply ends, because that is precisely as far as the detail was needed.

The sustained tone in the seam is the exception, and it is the most important thing in the room. Nothing moves in the seam and yet the resonator is excited. What excites it is the water — not water that is there, water that is coming. Forty metres of limestone above, a slope grazed by sheep, rain that has not yet made its way down: the room is already sounding the note of its own future, arriving early because in a place with no process there is nothing to make the future wait its turn. That note is what you take out. It does not fade with the decades because it is not a memory. Memories are made of decay. That is a fragment of an event, and it is still ahead of you.

When the water comes, the cave begins: the ceiling droops, the shoulders round, flowstone lays down, silt gathers in the corners, the edges lose their flint. It is going to be a very good cave. On that day the crickets will have to start and stop, the moths will take a first step, the snails will become countable and then fewer, the rosettes will have seedlings and the mosses will have a bad year, and the mason will place the tenth stone at last, uselessly, and go out through its door, and cut a fifth mark on the way.

And the sleeper will wake, thin at last, and it will get up in a room full of shadows, and it will need to be fed by something other than subtraction.
