## The Census

Take it properly and it runs to five entries, and the whole of it — every cell, every cushion, every animal in the basin — weighs less than a roe deer. Thirty-odd kilograms of living matter, wet, distributed through a bowl four hundred metres across and sixty deep. You could carry the biota of this lake up the haul road in two trips.

But it is not nothing, and three of the five entries are doing work.

---

### The mats

Four of them, on the first bench, at nine metres, where the light is still frankly daylight.

Look closely at the quilting and it resolves into cells. Sixty microns long, torpedo-shaped, six or eight bright chloroplasts each, a red eyespot at the front end like a pinhead of rust. *Euglena mutabilis.* It is not a plant. It is the descendant of something that swallowed a plant and did not digest it, and it still carries the mouth it no longer uses. It eats light. When light fails it eats whatever is dissolved around it, without preference, without urgency.

It swims. This is the thing to know about the mats: they move.

At first light the cells climb. They work up through their own mucilage into the top fifth of a millimetre of the film, packing there shoulder to shoulder until the mat visibly darkens, olive by mid-morning. Through the afternoon they hold. As the light goes off the west wall they descend, and by full dark they are a millimetre down and the mat has gone pale again. Two millimetres a day, up and down, performed simultaneously by every cell in the colony.

Over thirty-one years that is twenty-two and a half metres of travel, all of it vertical, all of it cancelling out. It is the only migration in the basin. Nothing else here has anywhere to be.

The mats are also, strictly, the only architecture. The cells exhale oxygen; the oxygen meets dissolved iron; the iron falls out as ochre directly onto the film that made it, and would bury it in a season. So the mat climbs through its own precipitate — grows a fraction upward, sheds a fraction below, and over three decades has laid down forty millimetres of layered orange stone beneath four millimetres of living slime. They are stromatolites. The oldest building method on the planet, at the scale of a card table, in a hole dug by draglines.

The basin was made entirely by subtraction: a hundred million tonnes carried away. These four tabletops are the only things in it that were made by adding.

The filaments that all lean the same way record a current that stopped existing in the third year of flooding.

And the colony is almost certainly one individual. Euglena divides and does not mate; four mats, some trillions of cells, one genome, one founder. That founder did not arrive after the lake. It was here first — in the sump puddles on the pit floor, in the orange ditchwater beside the conveyor, living off the highwall drainage while the machines still ran. It watched the water come up in the sense that it was already in the water that came up.

It divides constantly and the mats have not changed size in twenty years. Every birth is matched. What you are looking at is a shape maintained by turnover, like a flame, like a standing wave — the same object made of continuously different material, holding its outline against a place that has no further intentions.

### The ones that made the water

These are the population. Everything else is a rounding error.

A million cells to the millilitre in the upper layer, a thousand times that in the ochre drapes. *Acidithiobacillus. Leptospirillum. Ferrovum.* Total mass across the whole lake: perhaps three kilograms.

They do not eat. There is nothing to eat. They take electrons off ferrous iron — a transaction so poor in energy that a cell must process hundreds of times its own weight in dissolved metal to assemble one new cell. They are the most patient chemistry on the site. They work at the rock face the way the machines did, on a margin, in volume.

The drapes are them. That floury ochre that smokes off the stone at a touch and takes twenty minutes to settle is not a mineral deposit in any simple sense; it is excreted sheath and spent bodies and the iron they dropped, thirty-one years of a species' entire industrial output hanging in the water for a third of an hour and then lying back down exactly as it was.

And they made the conditions. The pyrite in that banded siltstone would oxidise on its own, given a few million years. With them it goes a million times faster: sulphuric acid, dissolved iron, pH 3.1, and the flat prohibition on shells and soft tissue that keeps the fish and the worms and the water mites out of this basin forever.

The lake is uninhabitable because it is inhabited. The emptiness is a waste product.

They are also the only process here still genuinely running. Slowly, seam by seam, they are eating the walls. In two or three centuries the reachable pyrite will be gone and the acid will begin, very gradually, to fail, and the place will start being somewhere else. Nothing that lives here now will be present to see it.

### The gas

Below the chemocline, in the dark, under the ochre in the fine-graded toe of the talus, there is a second congregation running the first one backwards.

Sulphate reducers. Iron reducers. Cells in micro-pockets where the acid is locally buffered by a grain of carbonate, living off whatever organic carbon rains down from the mats forty metres above — a few grams per square metre per year, the thinnest ration of any life in the basin. Their generation time is not measured in hours. A cell down there may divide four times in a decade.

They make the belch. A pocket assembles over months out of nothing, works free, and goes up in a string of pearls that sounds like a finger drawn round a wet glass rim.

That is the only noise in this lake produced by something alive. Once or twice a day. There is no one to excuse itself to.

They are also, at a rate indistinguishable from zero, consuming the acid that the upper bacteria manufacture. Given a proper supply of carbon they would sweeten the whole basin inside a century. They will never get it. They hold their line by not quite losing.

### The sixteen

The moss cushions on the north wall, in the splash zone, fists of *Pohlia*, metres apart, sixteen of them, each about the size of a closed hand and about as old as the waterline.

They do not drink the lake. They drink the sky — rain, and the capillary film that creeps a few centimetres up the rock, and the condensation that forms on cool stone in that windless bowl. A centimetre lower and the chemistry would kill them. They live in the one band where the lake's authority runs out.

Inside each cushion, down among the rhizoids in the wet crumb at the base, are the animals.

Bdelloid rotifers, mostly. Some tardigrades. In two of the sixteen, a nematode. A few thousand per cushion; forty thousand or so in total; collectively about the mass of a grape. This is the entire fauna of the pit lake, and it is not in the pit lake, it is in sixteen damp sponges glued to the wall above it.

They came as dust. Nothing walked here, nothing swam, nothing was carried in on a heron's foot — the water forbids all three. They blew over the rim as desiccated tuns and eggs, from the birch scrub, from the spoil heaps, from somewhere else entirely, and landed in the only sixteen places in six hundred thousand square metres of rock where a landing works.

And their time is not continuous. The cushions dry out; the animals dry with them, shut down completely, and resume when it rains. A rotifer in cushion nine may be twelve years old and have been alive for eight months of that. The bdelloids are all female and have been for forty million years.

In a basin where everything that could happen has already happened, the only creatures whose lives contain events are the smallest ones present, and every one of their events is weather.

---

### What falls in

Winged aphids in July. A moth. Birch seed. Once a jackdaw's primary, once a pipistrelle.

They land on that unmoving surface and the tension holds them for three or four days. Then they sink — a minute and a half to the first bench, longer to the floor — and they arrive intact, because nothing meets them on the way down and nothing is waiting at the bottom. They are not consumed. They tan, chemically, and go under the year's ochre, and they are filed.

Which means the thirty-one couplets of that legible sediment are not merely a climate record. They are a complete inventory of everything that has ever entered this basin from the living world, in order, by year, undisturbed.

In an ordinary lake these are food. Here they are documents. The lake keeps an exhaustive archive of a world it has no contact with, and there is nobody in it who can read.

The same fact keeps the idler rollers turning. Bearing grease is a hydrocarbon; in any normal water a consortium would have found it within a season and the rollers would have seized by the tenth year. Here the grease has no predators. The machines survive on their indigestibility. Push one and it goes round with a grinding reluctance and stops when you stop it, because nothing in this lake is hungry enough to want it.

---

### And the thing that holds the level

Forty kilometres away there are pumps, and behind the pumps a shift, and behind the shift a quota written into a licence by people who have not thought about this basin in years. Nobody on that shift knows the hairline exists. It is not on their instruments. It is not anyone's responsibility. The hum in your sternum is the breathing of an animal in another room that has never heard of you.

One day the pumps will stop. The water will begin to rise — half a metre a year, then more — over the splash zone, over the sixteen cushions, which will not survive it and will not know.

The mats will go up with it.

They are the only inhabitants here capable of responding to the only change that is ever coming: a colony that can swim, climbing a wall that has stood dry for thirty years, two millimetres a day and then, at last, in some direction that isn't cancelled.
