## The Tenants

### 1. Counting

The first survey tried to count individuals and abandoned it in the fourth week, not from fatigue but from a failure of the category. You cannot count individuals where you cannot reliably say which things are individuals, which are dead individuals, which are two individuals in the eleventh month of a very slow event, and which are rocks.

What the survey produced instead was a figure for warm mass per hectare, and a note in the margin, in a hand that has been described as deteriorating, reading: *the plain is not covered in animals. The plain is animals, interrupted by stone.*

### 2. The common one

Take the most ordinary dome — you have already stepped over four hundred of them — and get down on the grit and look under the skirt.

The gap is a finger wide. Inside it there is warm wet darkness that smells of iron and cut grass, and there is a margin of soft grey tissue, and behind that a foot: a single muscular disc the diameter of a dinner plate, gripping the granite with the whole of itself. The shell above is forty centimetres to a metre and a half across and is not a shell in any sense that would satisfy a taxonomist; it is a secreted mineral roof, rebuilt continuously from the inside, thickest at the crown, drawn out at the edges into the twenty or thirty flutes that make the skirt. Each flute ends in a lip. Each lip drips.

Mass: eight kilograms at fifty years, ninety at four hundred. Lifespan: three to six centuries in the general case. Heart rate, if that is the word for it, eleven a minute. Body temperature: exactly the temperature of the rock, which it has not left since it was the size of a grape and does not intend to leave.

It eats crust. The soft margin everts, rasps, retracts; the rasped strip is two millimetres wide and takes four minutes. A dome in good health processes about a hand's breadth of lichen a day, which is a hundred and fifty years of that lichen's growth, which is why the herd must keep moving and why it moves at the speed it does. It is not wandering. It is on a rotation with a return period of around forty years, and it does not know this, and it could not be persuaded to go faster, and the crust behind it always comes back, and the arrangement has held without adjustment since before the first mountain came off the top of this place.

And the crust grows on the domes themselves. The north flute of a living animal is pasture exactly as the north flute of a stone is pasture. So the herds graze each other: the outer flutes of your neighbour, cleaned by your margin, while your own are cleaned by hers. A herd of thirty thousand is a field of thirty thousand acres of two-millimetre soil, each acre carried on the back of the farmer of the next one. It is not cooperation. Nothing here is generous. It is simply that there was nowhere else to put the food.

### 3. The pond you carry

There are no ponds on the Warm Roof, and every one of these animals has an aquatic larva.

The problem was solved the same way by everything that met it. Under each dome, in the hollow of the foot's arch, the night's runoff collects and stands: a hatful of water, dark, twenty-nine degrees, held off the ground by a ring of muscle and kept from evaporating by the body above it. The eggs go there. The larvae swim there — hundreds of them, transparent, the size of rice, orbiting their parent's foot in a pond the size of a soup plate for their first eleven months.

Ten million ponds. Each isolated absolutely from every other. Each with its own population of commensal flagellates, its own mites, its own rotifer fauna that differs measurably from the fauna of the animal two metres away, because two metres, here, is an ocean.

When a grazer dies the pond dries in a day and everything in it goes with it. A dome's death is the extinction of a world with a hundred species in it, and it happens, somewhere on the plateau, roughly every ninety seconds, and it is soundless.

### 4. The same shape, four times

You can sort them by the tell.

**The molluscan ones** are the majority: the pond-carriers described above, glossy under the skirt, leaving a track of dried mucus eight centimetres wide that the crust colonises preferentially, so that the herd's route from forty years ago is still legible as a dark braid across the plain.

**The arthropod ones** have a shell of eleven fused plates and a gap that is not continuous but a row of thirty slots. They do not have a foot; they have legs, folded flat, moving in sequence under the skirt so that the whole animal slides without any visible part of it working. Stepped on, they produce the single sound any of them can afford: a dry tick from the plate margins, indistinguishable from the tick of the granite, which is presumably the entire point.

**The warm ones** were warm once. They have fur, or its ruin — a grey nap under the shell rim, on flesh that has given up thermoregulation entirely because the floor is heated and has been for eleven million years. They are the only lineage that will die if you move them two hundred metres onto a cold patch of rock. They are also the only lineage that will lean back.

**And two others**, which have been dissected repeatedly and placed nowhere. No coelom anyone agrees on. A nervous system that is a net, not a trunk. They are the oldest things on the plateau, individually — a thousand years is unremarkable for them — and they graze less than anything else and grow more slowly and are, by mass, perhaps a fifth of everything alive up here. They have no tell. Their gap is a hairline and their shell is indistinguishable from weathered granite and the only reliable way to identify one is to sit beside it for two hours and see whether the shadow-edge has moved four centimetres.

Several surveyors have chosen a rock to eat lunch on and found, on the return leg of the season, that the rock had gone.

### 5. What eats them

Slowly.

There is a flat predator, pale, the width of a dinner plate and four millimetres thick, which travels at night between the domes at nearly a metre a minute — recklessly fast, the fastest thing on the plateau — and slides in under a skirt to take larvae out of the pond. The host cannot do anything about this except close down on the intruder's margin, which it does, and which takes six hours, and which works about a third of the time.

And there is the other thing, which has no name because for two centuries nobody recognised it as an event.

Occasionally you find a dome that seems to have a second, smaller dome resting on top of it. You will think it is a corestone that has settled. It is a predator that has climbed on. It grips the crown, everts a digestive margin around the full circumference of its victim's shell, and begins to dissolve the roof from above. The process takes between nine and fourteen months. The animal underneath is not paralysed, not sedated, not unaware. It simply has no behaviour in its repertoire that is faster than the thing happening to it.

What it does have is neighbours. A smothered dome leans. The lean transmits, at the speed of pressure, through a chain of bodies; and over some weeks the near members of the herd converge, and lean, all together, from one side, and the predator is pushed off. Not always. Not even usually. But it happens, and when it happens it is the only rescue anywhere on the Warm Roof, and it takes three months to complete, and while it is going on you could not tell by looking that anything was happening at all.

### 6. What they say

Leaning is the whole language and it has a grammar.

The variables are angle, duration, the number of simultaneous contact points, and the manner of release — whether you come off the other body all at once or let the pressure fall away over an hour. The vocabulary is small and mostly concerns food, heat, the state of the crust, and the position of the herd. There is no tense. There is no question form, or there is nothing we have been able to recognise as one.

Scent supplements it. A dome can place a mark the size of a hand on the rock beside it, readable for between forty minutes and three hours, and can place it with a precision of about a centimetre. The marks are not territorial and they are not trails. They are, as far as anyone can tell, annotations — brief and local and specific — and the plain is written all over with them, and every night the dew takes the writing off, and every day it is written again.

Juveniles learn by being leaned on. A young dome spends its second decade inside a herd being pressed against, constantly, from all sides, by bodies that are telling it things it will not use for a hundred years.

### 7. The dead that stay

A dome that dies does not fall over. It has nowhere to fall. The soft parts dry and withdraw and blow away as a fine pale dust, and what remains is the shell, still gripping the rock with the mineral ring of the old foot, still domed, still fluted, still catching dew and still growing crust on its north flute and still feeding the herd.

This takes a very long time to stop being a body. Fifty years in, the gap is still there and you can put a wire into it. Two hundred years in, dust has sealed the margin and the shell has begun to take up silica from the runoff and the distinction between the animal and the plateau is no longer chemical, only historical.

So: one third of the domes are stone, they said. This is true and it is the wrong sentence. Some of that third are corestones weathered out of granite over ten million years. Some of that third are ancestors. There is no test in the field that will separate them, and the herds graze both without distinction, and if you asked the plateau which were which it would not understand the question, because from where it is standing there isn't one.

### 8. The fallen

Sound costs water. That is the rule, and the rule has exactly one exception, and the exception lives at the bottom of a well.

A grazer at the lip of a collapse feature is a grazer that will eventually go over it. It happens perhaps a hundred times a year across the whole plateau. Eighty metres of fall, and the animal survives it, because it is a shallow dome with a thick roof designed by four million years of nothing to be dropped on the ground.

Down there it cannot climb out. The walls are undercut and it has one foot and it is not going anywhere ever again.

What it finds instead is wealth. Standing water. Litter. Fungal crust on dead wood at a hundred times the growth rate of anything on the surface. Warm air that never drops below twenty. A fallen one doubles its mass in fifty years and goes on doubling; there are shapes at the bottom of the wet wells two and a half metres across, pale from the shade, furred with their own crust, that have been down there since before the walls were built.

And they are rich enough to speak. Not in the language — the language needs a second body and there is rarely a second body — but in the older way, the one the lineage has carried unused since the plateau dried out: a column of air driven across a wet membrane, one long descending note, perhaps ten seconds, perhaps once a season.

There is no evidence that anything answers.

In the dry wells, on the white dust floor, you can find them where they finished: enormous, thin-shelled, arranged in no pattern except the one their own bodies made lying down.

### 9. The long old age

At somewhere between four and five hundred years a grazer stops.

Its rasping margin has worn past reconstruction; it can no longer take crust at a rate that supports its mass; by every ordinary reckoning it should die, and it does not. Instead it changes. Over about a decade the foot hypertrophies into something no longer recognisable as a foot — a mass of muscle occupying two thirds of the body cavity — and the front margin of the shell thickens into a blunt keel, and the animal, for the first time in five centuries, becomes capable of pushing.

It then walks, at a metre or two an hour, continuously, day and night, without feeding except at the well lips, for between eighty and two hundred years.

And it moves slabs. It puts its keel against a flat stone the size of a tabletop and shoves it, and turns — it can only turn one way, and its turning radius is about eleven metres — and shoves another, and lays it against the first. The joints are careless because there are no hands; the stones are all of one size because that is the size this body can shift; each slab carries a crescent of polish on the edge that took the keel. The walls run in long opening spirals because a spiral is what a body makes when it can only turn one way and will not cross a wall it has already built. Four kilometres of dry-laid stone is not a project. It is one animal's old age, laid down end to end, the way a tree's old age is laid down in rings.

Ask what it is for and you get an answer that is not an intention. The windward face grooves in the first century; the crust along the foot of a wall runs thick and black and healthy; the young gather there; the herds behind a wall do better in the thin years than the herds without one. That is sufficient. It has been sufficient for a very long time. Whether the builder knows is a question the plateau declines, and in declining, it also notes that the walls are just high enough that no dome can cross them, and that the populations on either side of a four-kilometre spiral have not exchanged a larva in ninety thousand years, and that this — an absent-minded architecture by the senile — is now the principal force dividing the species of the Warm Roof from each other.

### 10. The last shape

A very few of them — one in some thousands, the ones that survive past seven hundred years — outlive the walking.

When the foot finally fails, the animal stops where it is and builds the last thing it will build: a ring of slabs around itself, corbelled inward as it goes, closing overhead. It leaves the gap it came in by, because it cannot close a doorway from the inside, and the gap is a little over a metre high, which is not the height of a person kneeling. It is the height of what is in there.

Because it grows. Freed from the wind, from the dew-load, from the need to be low, the shell goes on being secreted from the inside with nothing to flatten it, and over a century or two the dome rises — loses the skirt, loses the flutes, becomes a column, becomes a beehive of shell standing inside a beehive of stone in the warm dark, drinking from the hollow its own underside has worn in the floor beneath the hole in the apex.

It is the only thing on the Warm Roof that is not domed. It has stopped catching water from the air. It is drinking from a basin. It is, by every standard the plateau has, obscenely wealthy — and it is inside, and there is no one to lean against.

So it leans on the wall. Flank to stone, a metre square of contact, for two hundred years, releasing and resettling, releasing and resettling, saying the only things it knows how to say to a surface that has never once said anything back.

That is the pale patch. No pattern, because there is no pattern in the language; the grammar is duration and release and this is all duration. Just the patience, the surveyor wrote, and then went outside and sat down until the rock stopped ticking.

The bones in the other hut are in anatomical order because nothing disturbed them. We said *arranged* because we wanted a hand.

There has never been a hand up here. There has been a keel, and a foot, and eleven million years of banked heat, and a very great deal of time — and when the heat finally fails and the fog does not come and the last crust dies in the lee of a wall, that wall will still be standing, and will go on standing for a hundred thousand years afterward, built by something that could not have imagined it, for no reason, on its way to lie down.
