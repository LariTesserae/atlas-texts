## The potters

Fourteen grams. A bird the colour of wet slate, with a violet gloss on the mantle that you will only see for about a week in June, at noon, when the shaft light comes down full and strikes the crust at the right angle; the rest of the year they are simply dark.

They cannot walk. The foot is a hook — four toes forward, no heel to speak of, legs feathered to the claw — and it is made for one action, which is to catch the lip of a clay pot at speed. A potter's entire life is cliff, air, cliff. It is born in a pot, it leaves the pot, it flies for six months over three thousand kilometres of somewhere else, and it comes back, and it goes into a pot again, and when it dies it usually dies inside one, and is sealed over, and becomes part of the wall for the next tenant. There is no ground in their world at all except the clay bank at the river's edge, and they hate it there — that is the one place in the valley where they are slow, and they know it.

Watch the clay bank. Sixty of them at a time, wings half-open for balance, working the seam with quick sideways bites, and not one sound. They are the reason the whole sink is quiet — or rather, the owl is the reason and they are the ones who solved it first. Their signalling is done at the very top of the register and mostly not at all: a returning bird runs its bill along the outer ridges of its own pot, three or four scrapes, and its mate answers by pressing back through the clay from inside. It is a knock you can feel and cannot hear from a metre away. Everything the species needs to say — *it's me, I have food, move, the owl is in the third shaft* — it says through a wall the thickness of a fingernail.

And they built the city, and they did not build it on purpose. Every pot is a fist of river mud and spit, and they lay them down at the rate of two million a decade, and the crust thickens, and the old strata silt and moss over and grow ferns, and the roots knit the whole reef to the rock. The potters have no idea. They are not gardeners. They are birds that spit mud, forty or fifty thousand times over, and the consequence is a vertical marsh a metre and a half deep hanging on the underside of a cliff, with its own beetles and its own snails and its own soil horizon, and there is no word for what they are to it. Not architects. Something more like weather.

They come back all in one morning, in silence, dropping over the rim in a river of bodies, and they leave the same way. On the morning they go, the crust stands there dripping and empty, and it holds.

## The diggers

Twelve kilos of patient, oily, waterproof conservatism.

Build of a badger, face of a marmot, hands of a mason: the forefoot splays into a broad flat palm with a horn pad on it, and that pad is a trowel. They smooth clay with it. They will spend four hours packing and re-packing a joint between two boulders, sitting up on their haunches, working with both palms and testing with the nose, and then they sit back and look at it, and then they do it again.

Their fur is two coats — a long greasy guard layer that sheds the drip, and beneath it something so dense that a wet digger is dry a millimetre in. They have to be. They live under a permanent chandelier. Everything a digger knows is about water: where it comes in, where it wants to go, and how to make it go past rather than through. A colony's channels are its real body. Some of those cuts are older than the deepest thing any living animal could remember — they were flowing before the birch on the ledge above was a seed, before the slab that made that terrace fell — and they are maintained anyway, because a digger cleans what runs, and the running is instruction enough. Where a lineage fails and a channel silts, the water finds the chambers, and in one spring the whole slope is abandoned. You can see three or four of those on the north side: terraces with no shine on the paths.

They live in households of six to eleven, mostly sisters and their young, and a colony is forty households with agreed edges. There is no chief. There are neighbours, and boundaries scent-marked on particular stones, and those particular stones are honoured across generations by animals who never met the ones who chose them.

Each household keeps one or two very old individuals — twenty years, twenty-five — who do no work. They are not fed out of sentiment; they are the archive. They move, and the young watch them move, and that is the whole mechanism of inheritance in a species with almost no voice. The old one goes to the hay rocks four days before anyone else would have gone, and the household goes with it, and the hay comes in ahead of the rain.

Their dead are walled up. A digger that dies in the chambers is pushed into a side gallery and the gallery is packed with clay and left, and terracing continues over it. So the town is partly made of the town's ancestors, which is not a ritual and not a metaphor; it is simply the cheapest way to deal with a body when you live in a house you built out of mud.

Their alarm is four milliseconds long and so high it doesn't arrive as a note, only as a small hole in the day.

## The ones in the lowest pools

Forty kilos, blunt-headed, nearly eyeless, mostly nocturnal, and you will never see one.

They are in the last three sinks, where the pools are deep, and each pond belongs to one animal for the length of that animal's life, which is about thirty years. It builds. Not against the current — with it: a low crescent of drowned willow and stone laid so the river piles its own silt into the gaps. Each dam sits on the ruin of an older dam. If you cut down through one you would be cutting down through eleven or twelve tenancies, the way you can read the potters' crust.

They cannot leave. A cliff is a cliff. Whatever swam or fell in here when the roof came down has been sealed in this drainage ever since, and it has gone strange: the eyes have shrunk, the hind feet have widened, the coat has gone the colour of the pools. There are perhaps two hundred of them in eight kilometres, and they are the only large thing in the world, and they hold the water table up across the entire valley floor. Every metre-thick moss pillow in this place exists because these animals like their ponds deep.

They make no sound at all. When one wants another to know something, it pushes a wave.

## The small majority

Sixty thousand springtails in a square metre of moss. A flat white snail, found nowhere on earth but in the potters' crust, which grazes the algae film off *occupied* pots only, because occupied pots are warm. A beetle whose larva spends three years eating bird-spit and forty-year-old silt inside a wall, and which emerges into daylight for eleven days and does nothing but mate.

In the sump under the second bridge there is a blind amphipod, and nine metres downstream, in the sunlit pool below the arch, there is its sister species with eyes, and they have not interbred for a very long time. Same river. Two worlds, separated by a roof.

And the moss itself is a resident, not a surface. One cushion on the north wall, three metres across, is a single clone, and it has been dividing since before the last bridge fell, and it will outlast the next one. It is the oldest inhabitant here by a factor of ten and it is what everyone else walks on.

## The two who made the silence

She weighs nine hundred grams and she is the colour of chestnut shell in summer, and there are perhaps three of her in the whole eight kilometres, and she is the single most important animal in this valley.

She does not hunt by sound; she hunts by its absence. A mustelid working a talus fan in a place where nothing screams has learned to read the shape of the stopping — the way a whole slope goes still, and the stillness has a middle, and the middle is where the thing that saw her is. She walks toward middles. Everything you find beautiful about the quiet of this place is a negotiation with her.

Which is why, if you sit long enough, you notice that the silences here are wrong. They do not have centres. When she crosses the fan the hush rolls out from everywhere at once and then rolls back, with false stops seeded on the far side, and she stands in it with her head up, and half the time she gets nothing. Ninety seconds of nothing. That is not fear; that is a counter-argument, worked out over a very long time by animals that cannot speak.

The other is the hawk-owl, who does the same thing to the sky. She hunts the shafts because a shaft is a lit corridor and anything in it is a silhouette. So the potters come home under the beams now, low over the moss, in the dark green, and go up the wall in the shadow of it — a longer route, into a lower wind, at more cost. Every elegance in this valley is a bill someone is paying.

## Under the third arch

There is one more.

Go back to the rim and look at the second bridge, and at the shadow it throws on the floor beneath, where the daylight never lands. On the east side, above the flood line, there are terraces — clay-walled, willow-roofed, mossed over, stepped up the talus in the same idiom as everything else here — and you will take them for a digger colony, because they are the same shape, because the same material makes the same shapes, and because you are eighty metres up.

There are about thirty of them. They have been here long enough that no one has any account of arriving.

They are small and dark-adapted and their hands are wrecked by clay. They do not speak, in the sense you would recognise; they breathe words — unvoiced, at the top of the throat, close to the ear — and they carry the rest on the forearm, a whole grammar of pressure on skin, thumb and knuckle, that a child has by four. Children are taught not to cry before they are taught to walk: an infant is carried against the chest with a palm resting over its mouth, so that the reflex forms early and it never has to be beaten in. This is not cruelty and they do not experience it as loss. A crying child brings the owl, and worse than the owl, it empties the slope for two days, and an emptied slope means no snails, no eggs, no hay left unguarded, nothing.

They build nothing new. They are gleaners of other species' accidents. When a section of crust falls — and it falls every spring — they go out onto the talus and break it up and carry the deep strata away in baskets, because forty generations of bird nest is the best soil in the north, and on a dozen sunlit ledges they have fern beds and sorrel and angelica growing in it. They are gardeners of a fallen city they did not make and could not have imagined making.

They take potters' eggs from the second clutch and never the first. There is a reason given for this, and the reason is wrong, and the practice is correct.

In hard springs they take from the digger haycocks, and afterwards they leave cut willow on the flat rocks, in the same place, every time. Whether the diggers understand this as anything at all is unknowable and does not matter. The willow is always gone by morning.
