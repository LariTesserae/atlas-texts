## Who lives here

Start with the wrong answer, because it is the one the eye gives you. The Floor looks empty. Thinly populated at best: a grey drift of grazers a kilometre off, a glider hanging on the rib-wind, a dozen people every half-day's walk. You could stand here and conclude that almost nothing lives on the Floor.

The correct answer is that you are standing inside the animal.

## The clapper

Hand-length, armoured in overlapping plate the colour of a wet nut, ten legs of which six are for walking and four for housekeeping, and one claw grown out of all proportion — cocked, held, fired. It is nearly blind. It has no hearing worth the name. It knows the world as pressure on the fine hairs along its flanks, and it knows the world very well.

A clapper does not live alone. The unit is the pair, and the pair is the burrow: a tube driven down through mat and grit to the wet gneiss, flared into a chamber, walls sealed with a secreted plaster that sets underwater. One partner tends the chamber, cropping the mat that grows on the walls, weeding out what it does not want, thickening the useful crusts. The other holds the door. Holding the door means firing the hammer at intervals into the water outside — not aimed, mostly, not defence in any particular direction, but the continuous assertion of a boundary made of shockwave. Two burrows too close together burn each other out; the pressure kills eggs, ruptures the fine plaster, and one pair moves or dies. So the beds pack themselves to the exact density at which everyone can just tolerate the volume of everyone else, and that density is four hundred million to the square kilometre, and the roar you are standing in is not communication and not display. It is a fence. It is several billion fences, all of them shouting the position of their corners.

The consequences run through everything. The burrows turn the plain over, tube by tube, and pump oxygen and iron-rich water into the anoxic layer beneath, and so the mats grow richest exactly where the noise is worst. The beds are the ploughing. The beds are also the funeral service, the reef, the nursery, and the alarm system for the whole world. The clapper is not a feature of the Floor's ecology. The clapper is the Floor's ecology, and everything else, including the people, is a tenant living in the walls of its house.

## The abundant, considered properly

The **grazers** come in two builds. The boot-sized ones move in shoals of thousands, plated dorsally, soft underneath, hauling themselves across the mat-fields on a muscular sole and rasping with a tongue faced in magnetite. The shield-sized ones are solitary or in threes, thirty years old, plates the diameter of a serving platter, and they are the timber and stonework of this world: every dome on every rib is built of their backs. Neither can hear. Both feel: a grazer shoal turns as one body when a pressure front crosses it, and the turn propagates outward faster than any animal in it can move, because it is not a decision, it is a wave passing through a sheet of skin that happens to be divided into individuals.

The **sheet-swimmers** are four metres of segmented predator that hunt the flood by lying still and letting the shoals come over them. They are patient in a way that has no analogue in a world with weather. They also cannot hear, and they do not need to; a grazer's sole makes a signature in the water at ten metres and the sheet-swimmer's whole flank is tuned to it.

The **filter-swimmers** arrive on the biggest tides, broad and pale and edge-on, sculling in over the drowned flat at thigh depth like doors being walked through a house. They are the fat of this world. Their throat tissue renders into a heavy white grease that will not go rancid and will not take up water, and everything the people cook, burn, waterproof, or seal begins in a filter-swimmer's throat — including, and this matters more than the rest of it together, the wax.

The **gliders** are the only inhabitants with distance senses worth having. Their heads are two-thirds eye and their ear-region is a blank plate; they hunt pools by sight from the standing air off a rib and drop feet-first, and they are silent because sound here is a medium, not a message. The people watch gliders constantly. A glider circling low is a report about water you cannot see.

And there is the thing that makes silence. It travels beneath the black leathery pavements, lifting them as a hand lifts a blanket, and the burrows above it seal and shut up as it comes. You never see it. You see a hole in the roar, three hundred metres wide, moving at a fast walk. There is a tap-phrase for it whose literal sense is *the wrong quiet,* and children learn it before they learn their own names.

## The oldest resident

The mats are also alive, and they are the reason any of this is possible, and they have been here longer than the mountains were. They eat the rock's leaking, and they are eaten, and they compose and decompose on a schedule of decades: a district that is hip-deep felt now will be grazed to bare gneiss in twenty years and sour and black in forty and back to felt in eighty. Nobody manages this. The people do not think of the mats as an animal or a plant or a crop. They think of the mats as the place itself, doing what a place does, which is receive things and give them back changed. The dead go into the mat. So does everything else.

## The people

There are perhaps three thousand of them on the whole Floor, if the tap-net can be trusted, and it broadly can.

**Bodies.** Short, wide, heavy through the chest, ribcage thickened — you can feel the roar on your sternum, and they have grown sternum. The feet are the most worked part of them: broad, splayed, toes long enough to grip a rib's rounded shoulder, soles like the sole of a grazer, and cared for with a diligence that will strike you as religious until you understand that skin macerating in permanently wet conditions is the leading cause of death here, well ahead of drowning, well ahead of the sheet-swimmers. They oil. They sleep with their feet up in slings. They have eleven words for stages of foot rot and no word for dust.

No hair anywhere; unpigmented under the grease, so that the visible colour of a person is whatever the mats have put on them — rust across the shoulders, oil-white on the forearms, the black of the pavement felts ground into old scars deliberately, because a mark on the skin is legible by touch and a person's history can be read in the dark by a hand moving down the arm.

The hands are enormous. The fingertips are wrapped for work and unwrapped for speech, and to injure someone's fingertips is a graver crime here than to break their leg.

**Hearing as an age.** Nobody here is deaf as a misfortune. Hearing is a stage of life, like milk teeth. The young hear, and are therefore useless: too battered to concentrate, too frightened, and always startling. By twelve most have gone quiet inside, and this is when a person becomes competent. The tap-codes were not designed to be heard; they were designed to be felt, through jawbone and palm and the flat of a bare foot on quartz, and the deaf receive them better than the hearing do, because there is nothing else on the channel. Skill rises as hearing falls. The best receivers on the Floor are the oldest people on it, sitting with their jaws to a polished dish of stone, reading a rib for two hours at a stretch and then signing out three sentences of news from a band forty kilometres away.

**Three languages, no speech.** The near language is hands and face, fast, spatial, capable of everything. The far language is knuckle on quartz: terse, formulaic, heavily punctuated with courtesy, because a rib is a shared throat and to tap across another's transmission is the local equivalent of shouting a man down in his own doorway. And the third language is the one nobody warned you about. In the dark of a dome, chest against chest, they hum. Not audibly — there is no audibly — but a low buzz conducted bone to bone through contact, felt in the other body's ribs. Mothers use it on infants. Lovers use it. The dying use it, and it is the last thing that goes. It has no grammar. It is the only sound any of them ever makes on purpose, and it can only be received by someone who is touching them.

A person's name is a rhythm. Not a word: a rhythm, four to nine beats, and it belongs to a lineage, and it is re-used. When you take up a name you take up its whole history on the ribs, and to hear it come down the quartz is to hear something older than yourself asking whether you are still there. The word for stranger translates as *unheard.*
