# The Crews

## First sight

You will not be met. Someone will have seen the barge an hour out, and someone will have said so, and the news will have gone up the rise at the only speed news travels here, which is the speed of a person walking as slowly as it is possible to walk without stopping. By the time you are up the barge stair there will be two of them at the top, arrived a while ago, waiting with their forearms laid along the iron.

They are not being rude. They are being careful. What you read as indifference is a body doing arithmetic. They have looked at you, judged you sixty or seventy kilos of unsalted meat with a full head of hair and a coat, and concluded that you will be sitting down within the hour, and they have already decided which of them will fetch water and which will stay with you, and none of this required speech.

They do not shake hands. A hand is warm. A hand offered here is a request for heat, and they will give you one if you insist, out of courtesy, and afterward they will find a rail.

## The body

They are long. Not tall so much as extended — long forearms, long shins, long flat feet that splay on the wet brick and grip it. Everything about the shape is an argument for contact area. The hands read as too large for the wrists, and they are: broad palms, thin skin, the vasculature standing up green-blue across the backs, so that when a man lays both hands on cold stone you can watch the color go out of them from the fingers inward.

Hairless, mostly, and not by shaving. Pale in a way that has nothing to do with ancestry and everything to do with sixty generations under a lid — a soft grey-cream pallor, like the inside of a cut potato, with the vent salt lying white in the creases of the neck and the flexures of the elbow so that from a distance an old woman looks lightly dusted. Their fingernails are soft. They tear rather than break. Nobody's teeth are good.

They move at one speed. There is a word here for hurrying and it is not a description, it is an accusation; it is what you say about a man who has begun to be a danger to the shift. To run on the rise is to spend heat you have no way of getting rid of, and the end of that is *the standing faint* — the thing they watch for in each other constantly, out of the corners of their eyes, all day. It comes on as a sudden chattiness. That is the sign they teach the children: when a person who has been quiet all morning becomes charming, take them off the roof.

At fifty or so, the breathing changes. The salt has been going in the whole time, a little every day, and it does not come out. They are exempt from the exclusion only in the sense that a stone is exempt from weather. The rime kills a marsh-fly at the levee line in nine seconds; it takes a Works woman forty-one years; the difference is not a difference of kind, and they know it perfectly well, and they will tell you so with the same flat incuriosity they use about the buyers. There is a bell rung when a lung goes stone. It is rung once. Everybody stops, hands on iron, which they were doing anyway.

## The cloth

Wet cloth does nothing here — you cannot evaporate, so a soaked shirt is only a warm compress. But condensate coming down out of the gallery pipes is fourteen or fifteen degrees below blood, and cold water on skin is not cooling by evaporation, it is cooling by simple theft, and that works.

So: the cloth. Every person on the rise wears a shoulder-cloth, a metre of loose unbleached weave, and every person on the rise dips it at a standpipe at intervals of roughly twenty minutes for their whole waking life. They do not need to be told when. The cloth tells them. It goes from cold to cool to *nothing*, and *nothing* on the back of your neck is the loudest clock there is. You will see them all through the day drifting to the pipes in ones and twos with the unhurried certainty of animals going to water, except that there are no animals.

The dipping is the settlement's small talk, its gossip exchange, its ten seconds of shared cold. And because dipping is the one thing everybody does, giving someone else your fresh cloth is the most legible act of love available. Lovers dip for each other. Mothers dip for children until the child can reach the tap. And a person too far gone to walk to the pipe is dipped for by whoever is nearest, in rotation, hourly, without anyone organizing it — until they are not, and then they die, and everyone including the dying person understands what has been decided and when. They do not talk about it. There is nothing to say about it that the cloth doesn't say by going warm.

## The stones

There are sixty-four first-rank surfaces on the rise. This is not a natural number; it is an accumulated one, argued into existence over centuries and now fixed. The north face of the malt wall, in ells, from the buttress to the door: seven surfaces. The three sills of the condenser gallery. The flags at Northwater. The tram-street coping, in named lengths. The two courses of cut basalt at the foot of the chimney, which are the coldest stone on the rise and which no one under forty holds.

Each first-rank surface has an hour-schedule and an owner, and the owner's name *is the surface*. A child is named on the day a stone comes free — Third Buttress, Ell of the Malt Wall, Under-Gallery South, Northwater — and holds the name and the stone together, and gives them up together. This is why the count is sixty-one, currently. Three names are unsaid. They will be said again when there are children for them. In the meanwhile the unsaid names are a real and discussed absence, and the way it is described is a draught: *there's a draught at Second Coping.* Nobody means air.

The best hours of the best stones go to the old and the pregnant. The rest is rank, service, and inheritance, litigated endlessly and never violently, in a court which is four people sitting along the gallery sill with their forearms down.

## The fresh

Blood comes by barge. Births here are few and mostly do not take — the same infertility that runs through the moss and the ferns and the one great pollen-shedding tree at the west end runs through the people too, and nobody has ever suggested it is a coincidence. So the count is kept up from outside: indentures, foundlings, contract engineers, debtors, and every so often somebody who has come a very long way on purpose and won't say why.

They are called *fresh*, in the tone you'd use for raw meat. Acclimation is reckoned at two years and it kills perhaps one in four, usually in the first fortnight, usually of exactly the thing everyone warned them about. The crews are patient with the fresh in a way that has no warmth in it at all. They will dip your cloth. They will take you off the roof. They will not give you a stone, because there isn't one, and they will not pretend otherwise: you hold rails, you hold the second-rank walls, you lean where you can, and if someone dies you might be named, and if nobody dies you won't be. Some die fresh. Those go east under a description rather than a name, and the description is read out once at the flume head, and that is considered a lack rather than a disgrace.

What breaks the fresh is not the heat. It is the sound. Everyone's first month is spent hearing their own voice come back to them out of a fog bank four minutes late from a street they have already left, and the mind will not have it. They stop speaking. They stand in the dump-fog with their hands over their ears, which does nothing, because it is not loud, it is only wrong. The crews' remedy is to sit the newcomer down at the flume head where the spillway roar swallows everything and let them stay there until they can stand to come back in. Some of them keep going back to the flume head for years.
