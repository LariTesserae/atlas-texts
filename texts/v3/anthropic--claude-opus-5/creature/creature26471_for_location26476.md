## I. The ones who work here

Start with the bird, because it is the easiest to be wrong about.

It does not live in the bowl. Its nest is outside, forty or fifty paces above the rim on the cold northeast face, in a horizontal crack under an overhang of the same grey stone, and it is a bad nest by any standard — a shallow cup of moss and grass stems, salted with mineral dust, exposed to everything the mountain has. The bird sleeps in the cold and eats in the warm. It comes over the lip in the clear hours with its oversized head low, works one gravel apron for twenty minutes, works another, takes what it finds down its throat without ceremony, and goes back up before the pressure begins in its ears. It knows the interval better than any instrument. You will never see it caught out.

It is dull brown because there is nothing in the bowl worth signalling to. There is no rival. There is not, most years, a mate — the pair breeds once, three eggs, and of the ones that fledge, none stay. They go over the ridge and down and are not seen again. The bowl supports one bird, or two in a good summer, and the surplus is exported. Whatever the bird's relationship to this place is, it is closer to employment than to residence.

The frogs are an accident, and a repeating one. They are all the same size because they are all the same age; they arrive as spawn, gummed to the belly feathers of the bird, from a tarn eight hundred feet below and a mile east. In blood-warm water they hatch fast and grow fast and then stop. They do not breed here. Something in the fizzing bitterness of the water, or in the fact that no frog in the bowl has ever heard another frog, keeps them from finishing. So they sit in the shallow sheets with their throats going pale and dark, pale and dark, flashing at each other in the thick shell-light, saying whatever it is they say, and then they die of ordinary things, and their small bones acquire their orange rind on the floor of the sheet where they fell. Every generation of frogs here is the first generation. Every one of them is alone in a way it has no equipment to notice.

The spider in the skirt of the great rosette lives about forty per cent of its life. It hunts by tremor, and for six hours out of eleven the roar makes tremor meaningless — everything shakes, the whole world is one note, its web is a wet rag. So it withdraws into the dry inner layers of the skirt, plugs the entrance of its retreat with silk and bark dust, and waits out the noise in a sealed pocket against the trunk, which is hot. It comes out into the clear, rebuilds in about ninety minutes, hunts hard, and goes back in. It does this perhaps eleven hundred times and then it stops. There is no other spider in the bowl at all, only its descendants, in the skirts of the other eight.

## II. The nine

These are the residents. Everything else is a visitor or a crop.

I have no names for them. Whoever comes here does, presumably; I would be surprised if the person who has cut twenty-two notches into one of them has not called it something for twenty-two years.

Count the skirt of the great rosette and you get, near enough, nine hundred folded leaves. It makes two or three a year. That is three centuries of standing in one place on one bench, drinking steam through its undersides nine hours out of every eleven, clamping into a grey fist for the other two. It has been through about two hundred and forty thousand breaths. It has never moved, it has never been touched by an animal larger than a bird, and it has produced no offspring, because it will flower once — a spike out of the crown, all its stored centuries spent at once — and then it will die.

None of the nine is young. The fern's crozier has been uncoiling at a finger a week for so long that the frond it is making now will not be finished for two years, and it has made hundreds. The paired hollow thing has grown into the sound: the pressure has been finding its cavities for so long that its walls have thinned and hardened where the air runs and thickened where the air stands, and the fifth it moans is not an accident of geometry but a shape that geometry pushed it into, slowly, the way a river pushes a bank. The bowl did not tune it. It tuned itself against the bowl, which is different, and took two hundred years about it.

There are nine because there are nine places. Too near a main fissure and a plant is cooked in its first season; too far and there is not enough vapour to feed a leaf that size, and the shallow ornamental roots find nothing in the peat worth having. Between those two failures there is a narrow band, and in the whole three hundred paces of the bowl there are nine spots inside it. When one dies the spot does not stay empty forever, but it stays empty a long time.

On the sixth bench there is a socket: a ring of rotted skirt, waist-high, with the trunk gone out of the middle of it. Inside the socket, sheltered by the rot of its predecessor, standing in the warm compost of it, there is a rosette one foot high. Twelve years old, near enough, by its leaf count. It will need another two hundred and eighty. It occupies the ninth place, which is currently a place with almost nothing in it.

So: eight, and a promise.

Their bodies are not made of this mountain. The carbon in the great grey leaves came up out of the fissures in the breath. They are the exhalation of a hot rock, given a shape and held up in the air for three hundred years and then let go.

## III. The orange

The most abundant living thing in the bowl is the colour of a healed burn, and it has no edges, and it is what the place smells like.

It lives on the lips of the fissures and in the flowstone and in every film of water on every surface: a mat, slick as wax where it is old, slimy where it is new, feeding on iron and sulphur out of the vapour and needing no light whatever. Its generation time is measured in hours. In the six roar hours it blooms; in the clear hours its top layer dies back in the cold and dry, and the mat eats its own dead and starts again on the next exhale. It is the fastest-turning population in the bowl and the maker of its slowest object. The rind on the bird's footbone, on the bark cup, on the fallen stalk, on the axe eventually — the fingernail per lifetime, the fossilizing — that is not chemistry alone. It is metabolism. The thing that turns this place to stone is alive, and dies and is replaced eight hundred times a year while it does it.

Everything else here is downstream of it. The colourless invertebrates in the wet under the skirts graze it; there are more of them in one square foot of the great rosette's trunk than there are frogs in the bowl in a decade. The frogs eat the invertebrates. The bird eats both. The spider takes what the steam lifts. None of it starts with the sun. The bowl feeds from underneath, which is why the roar hours look so empty: that is the shift when the real workforce is on.

## IV. The one who kneels

One person at a time. I am fairly confident of that, and of very little else.

Four hollows in the flowstone by the outflow notch, cup-sized, polished, and the four are not the same depth. Three are deep enough to hold a fist to the wrist. The fourth is shallow, a saucer, and its grit-scratches are still legible. So: four workers, three finished, one going. They knelt at the lip of the bowl's only exit with the water running past their shins and worked something round against the stone, with water and grit, over and over, for years — long enough that the record went into rock that grows at a fingernail per lifetime.

I think they bring a cobble up from the river and take it down again. Nothing is left here; whatever they are making, they make it somewhere else, a little at a time, in a place they have to climb a wet mountain to reach. The twenty-two notches in the skirt-bark of the great rosette are spaced by years and cut by the same hand at the same height, which tells you that the hand did not grow in that time and did not shrink, and that it kept coming, and that in each of those twenty-two years the thing it was counting had not yet happened.

I would guess it is the flower. There is nothing else in this bowl that a person could wait for.

The green axe on the third bench is not lost. Lost things lie differently. It is worked on both faces, barely used, and it was set down flat with its edge away from the trunk, the way you put down something you intend to leave. The bark cup wedged in the fork of the paired plant is empty and rinded inside, which means it held this water long enough for the water to write in it, which means it was not for drinking.
