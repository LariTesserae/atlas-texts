## What you take them for

From a mile off, a person here is a fan palm.

This is the first thing to say, because it is the first mistake everyone makes, and because the mistake is not really a mistake. Both stand alone. Both mark where the water table comes nearest the light. Both are the tallest thing for as far as you can turn. In the white hour, when the fog lies waist-high and takes away everybody's legs, a person and a palm are the same object: a dark mass floating on milk, unmoving, patient, with the sun coming up behind it.

The difference is that at some point in the next hour the palm will not step.

The step, when it comes, is slow and enormous — a leg lifted clear of the sheet, water sheeting off the fringed foot in a curtain, held, and set down again a full stride on, without splash. They have had a very long time to learn how to walk in an inch of water without making a sound. Splashing is not merely clumsy here. Splashing is talking over somebody.

## The body

Two legs, long as a tall man is tall, grey-pink, exactly the colour of the floor beneath the water — so that in the low slant of afternoon a person standing in their own reflection looks like one creature balanced on the belly of another, which is how the grazers look too, and how everything here looks, and which nobody who lives here finds strange.

The feet are wide and split into four, with a stiff pale fringe of keratin along each toe like the teeth of a comb. This is a snowshoe for iron crust. In the wet weeks, when the top of the pavement goes greasy and holds a print for a season, the fringe spreads the load so that a person of eighty kilos leaves a mark no deeper than a thumbnail. They can make it deeper on purpose. That turns out to matter enormously, and I will come back to it.

The trunk is short, upright, and disappointingly plain: a barrel of muscle with a great deal of throat above it. Skin dark, damp, permeable, kept wet all day by the same air that keeps everything else wet; at the end of the driest weeks it takes on a fine white bloom at the shoulders and along the spine, salt sweated out and left behind, so that a person in the ninth dry week wears the plain's chemistry as a dusting, like sugar on fruit.

The forelimbs are almost nothing. Two thin stabilizers, elbow-jointed, ending in a flattened blade of horn — enough to fan heat off the throat, enough to steady a body against the shove of a rain wall, not enough to grip, to strike, to carry, to shape. There were never hands. There is no lost golden age of making, no fall from craft. Nothing on this plain has ever been built by anything with a spine, and the people are not an exception waiting to happen.

They still gesture with them.

That is worth sitting with. In a species whose members spend their entire adult lives beyond the distance at which a body can be read, the small blades still turn and lift and settle while their owner is speaking to somebody a mile and a half away who cannot possibly see it. The gestures are real, and grammatical, and consistent across the plain, and utterly private. They are used properly only twice in a life: in the years when a parent is walking a child, and in the rare, formal, terrible occasions of approach.

The eyes are set high and forward and are poor by our standards, ruined by glare, fitted mostly for reading ripple. The nostrils are high and small. The tongue is long, dark, and grooved along its edges with a fine comb of plates.

Everything else is ear and throat.

## The instrument

The throat is the point of the whole animal. Beneath the jaw, a paired sac of skin, slack and wrinkled as an empty bag, hangs down over the chest and can be inflated in stages until it is the size and colour of a bruised melon. It is not a shout-maker. A shout dies here; the heat eats the high frequencies within a few hundred metres. What the sac produces is low — lower than the sedge-shrill, higher than the tremor of a distant storm, a narrow band of frequency in which, on this plain, almost nothing else lives.

That gap is the room they speak in. The insects hold the ceiling. The frogs, when they start, drop the floor out. Between the two there is a clear channel perhaps two octaves wide, and every word these people have ever said has been said inside it, and their entire idea of the world is shaped by the acoustics of a hall built by animals that do not know they built it.

Because the sound is made in the sac and not the mouth, the mouth is free. This is the second great fact about them. A person eats all day — the mats, mostly, grazed with that combed tongue, plus the crabs cracked out of their plugged chimneys, plus the invertebrate mud that moves under its own density — because mat is poor food and there is not much of it per acre, and a body this size needs a square mile of shining floor to keep going.

So: they eat all day, and they talk all day, and they do both at once, and they do it to people they will never see.

A square mile per person. Everything about them follows from that number.

## How far a word goes

The day has three social registers, and they are set not by custom but by physics.

Before dawn, in the fog, with the air lying cold-on-warm in a flat inversion, sound is ducted along the sheet and carries absurdly. A voice reaches four miles, five, and on the stillest mornings a relay of tired throats will pass a phrase across twenty. This is the great hour, and it is the only hour in which anything like a whole society exists. Everyone wakes into it. It is a parliament held in a white room by participants standing on nothing, each of whom is the only person present.

By mid-morning the fog goes up, the air turns turbulent, and the range collapses to a few hundred metres. Afternoons are therefore local and low-voiced and intimate: neighbours only, the two or three whose ground touches yours, the conversation of people who have been talking to each other every afternoon for sixty years and have long since stopped needing to finish sentences.

Then evening, and the frogs, and enforced silence. Ten thousand thumbnail-sized voices holding one pure tone until it becomes a pressure behind the eyes — you cannot speak through that and nobody tries. Night is for walking. Night is for the other kind of speech.

And within all of this, the rain wall: a visible edge crossing the plain at the speed of a stroll, blotting out everything acoustically for the quarter hour it takes to pass. There is a set phrase for what you say when it clears, and it is the first phrase children learn, and its literal sense is *still here, still here, the rain went over.*

## The mark and the taste

They cannot write, but the crust can be pressed.

In the wet weeks, when the iron pavement is soft on top, a person walking with weight on the heel leaves prints that dry into the surface and stay for a season, and — this is the thing — a hardened print rings differently underfoot than unpressed crust. So a line of purposeful prints is not only a visible trail but an audible one, a sentence you can read in the dark with your feet, faintly, a change of tone every stride. They lay these lines at night, in the frog hours, when there is nothing else to do with a body.

The marks say small things. *This seep has gone sour.* *A person is missing from the east.* *I walked here in the last wet and I was well.* They are made by walking. No hand, no tool, no object; just the pressure of a body that had to be somewhere anyway. A message that takes six hours to lay down and lasts four months and can be read by anyone who happens to cross it, which may be nobody.

And beneath even that, the slowest channel of all. Water goes into the rock at one place and comes up a mile away years later, and it comes up carrying the taste of where it went in — the sourness of a particular soda ring, the iron of a particular joint, the sweetness of a mat that grew and died before the drinker was born. They read this with their legs. Standing in an upwelling, feeling the cool column against the shin, a person is taking delivery of a letter from a mile away, posted by nobody, franked with a date they can estimate within a few years. Whole reputations rest on the reading of columns. An old person who has stood in the same three upwellings for a century knows what those columns tasted like in every phase of their life, and can tell you that the plain to the west is drying, slightly, and has been for forty years, and is right.

Three speeds of speech, then. The voice, which is now. The print, which is this season. The water, which is a decade ago and cannot be answered.

## Distance

The one crime is approach.

There is no word for war and no plausible occasion for one; there is nothing to take. But there is a very elaborate vocabulary for the offence of getting closer than you were, and it is the vocabulary a decent person spends their life avoiding having to use. Coming within half a mile of another adult without four days' notice is the act of a lunatic. Notice is given in a formal register, at dawn, in the great hour, so that not only the person approached but everybody within five miles has heard it and can say afterward that it was properly done.

Actual meetings happen. Fewer than a dozen in a life. They occur at the whalebacks, on the black-and-green seep lines where the water is cool all day and everything comes anyway; the ground there is neutral because it belongs to too many people to belong to one. Two adults at a seep stand about eight metres apart, which is the closest most of them will ever be to another of their kind after childhood, and they use the small blade-tipped forelimbs, and they speak so quietly it is almost breath, and afterward, walking away, both of them are exhausted in a way that lasts for weeks.

They do not consider this sad. When it was explained to one of them that elsewhere there are creatures who live pressed against each other in the thousands, she was quiet for a while and then said the equivalent of: *then none of you can ever hear anything.*

## The map that exists nowhere

Everything has a name. Every whaleback, every fan palm, every trio of palms, every upwelling, every soda ring, every dead termite mound half-melted into its candle shape. The name-list of a person's own square mile and the three miles around it is the substance of their education, given in fragments across the first twenty years, recited back at dawn, corrected by neighbours who are also reciting.
