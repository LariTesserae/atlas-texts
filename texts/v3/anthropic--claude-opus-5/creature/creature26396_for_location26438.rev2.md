# The Inhabitants of the Bowl

## The sawyers

They are named for a lie. Nothing in the Bowl saws anything; the name comes from the sound, which is the sound of a bad blade in green timber, and that is the only place the resemblance holds.

A sawyer is about the size of the joint of your thumb, dark and slightly wet-looking, with short hooked legs made for bracing rather than walking, and pale dots where the eyes have mostly given up. The ribbed plate on each flank is not a wing-case and not a drum. It is a file. The animal jams itself crosswise in a gallery, sets both flanks against the tube walls, and rasps — and the noise you hear is not the sawyer at all. It is the tube. The sawyer is not an instrument; the sawyer is a bow. The instrument is the house, and the house is four hundred paces across.

This is why Third Street grinds and the arcades scream. Tube diameter, tube length, wall thickness. The pitch map of the Bowl is not a decision anyone made. It is anatomy, in the way that your own voice is anatomy.

No sawyer plays for long — twenty minutes, half an hour, then a slack spell braced in the dark doing nothing. The sound is continuous the way a river is continuous, with nothing in it staying. Several hundred million animals, none of them coordinated, none of them aware of each other in any fashion, and the sum of their private rasping is a flat unbroken continent of noise that has not stopped in living memory. They do not chorus because a chorus requires listeners. The loudest animals on the floodplain are entirely mute to one another. They have no signals, no courtship call, no alarm. They have never needed to answer anything.

They do not eat the wood, either, which is why there is still a wood. The plant pushes sugar into the gallery walls, where it stands in beads on the inner face like sweat, and the sawyers lick it off. The plant feeds its orchestra directly, out of the wall, for nothing except the ringing. A sawyer's metabolism is savage — this is the heat you feel when a wall goes suddenly hot under your palm, several thousand nymphs breaking out of the gallery skin at once and taking their first rasp. It is also, by a long chain, the reason the arcade ceilings sweat and the drops crawl sideways and you are sprayed out of empty doorways in the afternoon. The Bowl's weather is made of insect body heat.

Eggs go into the new wood at the growing face, the fraction of a hair by which the town is wider than it was this morning. The plant does not thicken and then get colonised. It thickens *in order to be* colonised: it lays down cradles. And when a generation has eaten its way inward and died, it leaves its galleries behind as hollow tube, and the hollow tube is what rings. The voice of the Bowl is the accumulated empty housing of its own dead. Every note you feel in your teeth is coming out of somebody's abandoned childhood.

They fall when they die. There is a constant fine descent inside the walls, down the tubes, down into the void under the plaza, where it collects on the floor of the drum in a soft black depth nobody has measured and the roots take it back. The drum stays hollow because the plant eats the floor of it.

## The rain-clock

A sawyer has no sequence either. It is born inside the loud, it lives inside the loud, and the loud has no edges in it for before and after to catch on. Whatever a sawyer has instead of experience, it is not a line.

Except when it rains.

Ten seconds after the rain starts, every sawyer in the Bowl has stopped, and for the length of the rain-hour several hundred million animals do all the things that can only be done in order. They moult. They find each other by heat and by taste, press flank to flank, and read each other's ribs with their forelegs — which is the entire conversational repertoire of the species, and happens perhaps five times in a life. They lay. They shift position. They die properly, instead of simply ceasing to be somewhere.

Rim people say a brood is four rains old, or nine rains old, and they are not being folkloric. That is genuinely the only clock the animal has. Everything that ever happens to a sawyer happens in the quiet. The rest of its life is a single warm noise in which it is briefly, repeatedly, nowhere in particular.

The rain-hour is when the Bowl is real. This turns out not to be about you.

## The body

The plant has no name because there is nothing else to tell it apart from. The rim people call it the growth, or just the Bowl, and are impatient with the question.

It is eleven stems, or it was. They came up close together in an old ox-bow scar and crowded each other and fused, and what you walk along as streets are the seams where they failed to merge. The radial plan is not a plan. It is siblings.

There was a twelfth, in the middle, and it is the reason for the plaza: the sink at the centre is its socket, still brimming, still black; the barn-sized void under the paving is the hollow left by its root-bole rotting out, which the living stems have since lined and smoothed and kept scrupulously empty, because they needed the drum and found one. That is either opportunism or luck. Nobody has decided that either.

Age: older than the river's present channel. Growth: beech-slow, and relentless, and the only direction it goes is wider.

It flowers continuously, because there are no seasons inside a thickened moment. The panicles in the third-storey rooms are dry as paper and hold a pollen far too heavy for wind, which lifts only above a certain amplitude — so it comes up off the flowers in pulses, into the standing column of warm air that all that insect heat generates, and rides it out over the rim, and drops in the lee, and blows a little further, and stops.

Where it goes is a real question with no answer. The floodplain has other depressions. Some of them are grey. There is one a day downriver, a ring of hard bare ground and standing dead wood with the galleries eaten open and the roof fallen in, and you can walk into the middle of it and hear your own footsteps, and everyone who has done this comes back badly shaken, not by the ruin but by the quiet. Bowls die. Something must therefore start them.

The rim people hold that the Bowls are sexed; that this one is male and does nothing but pollen; that somewhere there is a lower, smaller, quieter one that drops fist-sized dull fruit; and that the fruit is carried in the gut of a browser, at a browser's pace, once in a browser's life. This may be entirely invented. Nobody has ever matched one Bowl to another. It is also perfectly possible that the vector is you, in your hair and in the seams of your bag, and that you will not find out.

## The mute

The frogs in the window openings are the colour of old candle-wax and their throats go all afternoon without a sound in them. The lobe under the chin is white on one side and black on the other and they flip it — flick, flick — down a whole colonnade, one answering the next four windows along, a line of winking punctuation. The rates differ by street. The arcades are fast and fussy. Third Street is slow to the point of insolence.

The birds have gone showy and silent in the same way and for the same reason: flare a wing-bar where elsewhere you would sing. The local flycatchers say whatever a flycatcher says by means of a stiff formal little bow, delivered head-on, absurd, absolutely serious, and repeated until answered.

The ants are the only residents who have found a use for the shaking. They run the drainage channels in trails laid so thick with pheromone that the drains have a sweet chemical stink you can follow with your own nose; they farm a fungus on the sink-edge that will not fruit in still air; and they winnow by simply putting a heap of harvest down on any floor and waiting for the floor to sort it for them. Ants do not carry things across rooms here. They put the thing down, and the room delivers it to the doorway, and they collect it there. This is the single instance of anyone in the Bowl exploiting the Bowl rather than merely enduring it, and it was arrived at by insects with no brain to speak of, which the rim people find funny and then, on reflection, don't.

The browsers — six, seven, soft-mouthed, pig-sized, whiskered, patient beyond any reasonable standard — eat leaf-laminate off the low walls, sleep standing, and lean. They lean against walls, against each other, and against people, immediately and without preamble, the way a dog that has decided about you leans. They are the warmest thing in the Bowl that will let you touch it.

## The hunters of holes

Sound does not work here as information, with one exception, and it is a negative one.

A body in a shaking street is a place where the shaking stops. A spider with its long forelegs flat on the polished wood does not feel the prey; it feels the small dead patch the prey makes in an otherwise saturated surface. They hunt absences. They are flat, drab, fast, entirely webless — a web is a listening device and there is nothing to listen to — and they are everywhere, tucked into thresholds where the flow is fastest and the wood goes glassy.

The long green snake hunts the spiders by exactly the same method, reading larger holes.

And because you are the biggest hole in the street by a wide margin, the snake will follow you. Not stalking: reading. It will pace you along a kerb for twenty minutes at a polite three metres, and when you stop it will stop and lie there shaped like a hole in the noise with a hole in the noise, and then it will lose interest. It has never hurt anyone. It is simply the only animal in the Bowl that has noticed that people exist.

## The thing in the high galleries

Once a season the third-storey rooms are emptied of sawyers. The panicles come down split and stripped, the galleries behind the north colonnade go silent for two or three days — an audible bald patch in the ceiling of noise — and there is a smell of cut grass that lasts about a week.

Evidence: claw-scores in the polish well above the reach of any ladder anyone has carried down the clay lip. A quantity of shed material at the base of the west wall that has been described as feathers by some people and as nothing of the kind by others. One set of wet prints across the plaza during a rain-hour, seen by eleven people simultaneously, who then argued for two years.

Nothing else. It is not photographed, it is not named, and the rim people's position is that it lives in the void under the plaza with the old root-bole, comes up inside the walls, and is the reason the plaza's deep note is not quite a note. They will tell you this calmly while eating.

## The people on the rim

Nobody lives in the Bowl. The nearest village is half an hour off on the only rise worth the word: a row of houses, a byre, cattle, a rack for drying laminate roofing that they prise off the low walls carefully and only in the rain, because they are not fools.

They are the most tiresome people on the floodplain, and they know it, and they have reasons.

Their greeting is a statement of what you have just been doing and what you will do next. Their children are drilled from three years old in *and then, and then, and then*. They have no comfortable word for *meanwhile*. Their history is not dated by years but kept as a list of rains, in order, recited, with the order being the whole point and the content almost incidental. They cannot bear to be interrupted, they cannot bear a story told out of sequence, and they regard everyone else's loose grip on before-and-after as a kind of personal indecency. Living next to the Bowl has made them like this. It is not superstition. It is a defended perimeter.

Their entire practical relationship with the place is two rules.

You go down when the rain starts. You come up when the frogs stop.

The second is the important one, because the frogs know first. Ten seconds or so before the sawyers pick up again, a whole colonnade of wax frogs will shut their throats at once, and if you are still in the plaza when that happens you have the length of a held breath to get to the lip. There are ropes for the clay. There is a watcher who does not go down at all, whose only job is to be on the rim in the rain with a pole and to count people out. Twenty-two went in. Twenty-two on the grass.

They count aloud. Of course they do.

## The polished

Sometimes the count is short.

What comes out afterwards — a rain later, three rains later, once considerably more — is not damaged. That is the difficulty. They are well. They are often better than well: rested, unhurried, warm-handed, pleasant to sit with, entirely settled and needing nothing. The rim people call them the polished, after the wood, because the sound scours its architecture smooth and it does not make an exception for you. Nothing catches on them any more. There are no edges left for a memory to hook onto.

A polished person speaks in the present tense about everything, including their childhood, including people who died before you were born, including their own future, which they describe with mild interest and no urgency. They eat when food is put in their hands. They will weed a whole field beautifully, because weeding has no first and last in it, and they cannot be sent to fetch anything, because a fetch has two ends. They can learn nothing new. They are not unhappy. This is the part that the village finds hardest and the visitors find fascinating, which is why the village hates visitors.

They cannot be sent back down for the laminate, either, though they are the only ones who could work there indefinitely. They would simply stay. They would not mind. That is precisely the problem.

## The hand on the browser

There is an old browser, the biggest one, grey around the mouth, and its flank carries handprints in the wood-dust — pressed in, held by the wax of the animal's coat, refreshed constantly by a shaking that should have wiped them and instead keeps them. Every person who goes down in a rain-hour and gets leaned on leaves one. The rim people count them, because counting is what they do.

Twenty-two hands, at last count. They can name twenty.

One is small. It is the hand of a child of about seven, it has been on that flank for as long as anybody's list of rains goes back, which is longer than any browser lives, and it is fresh.

The village does not have a story about this. They have, instead, a policy, which is that when you are down there in the roar and you meet somebody you do not recognise, you take them by the wrist and you keep hold, and you walk to the lip, and if the frogs stop you go up anyway and you do not let go. It has never worked. It is not really expected to. It is the sort of rule that people keep because the alternative is to decide something, and nobody has ever decided.

## Who it is all for

Nothing in the Bowl is a resident in the way you mean the word. The frogs and the flycatchers and the ants and the snake and the spiders and the browsers are barnacles in a harbour: a thin living skin on the inside of enormous empty rooms, ninety-nine parts warm unoccupied air, all of it kept hollow on purpose by an organism that needed a body big enough to ring.

The actual population is in the walls. Several hundred million animals, blind, silent to each other, deaf in any useful sense, that have never been outside and never will be, born in wood laid down as a cradle, fed sugar out of the wall by the thing they are playing, rasping without pattern or intention at the abandoned tunnels of their ancestors, and falling when they finish into a dark drum full of the same.

And then it rains, and the sound goes out of the Bowl from the top down like a bath emptying, and for one hour several hundred million animals begin, all at once, to have lives — to moult, to touch, to lay, to die in the right order — while the frogs scream like chimes and the rim people scramble down the slick clay with their ropes and their lists, counting, and somebody's small fresh handprint sits on a browser's flank in the wet.

Then the frogs stop. And everything in the Bowl that has a life puts it down again.
