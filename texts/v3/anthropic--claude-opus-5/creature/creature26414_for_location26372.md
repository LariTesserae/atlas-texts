## They have no name for themselves

Asked what they are, a person of the Shelf will give you the name of a seep — the thread of water their mother's house stood in, which is by now probably dead, four days' walk south and dissolving. There is no word that means *our kind*. There has never been anything to distinguish from. There is a word that means *those who came down*, but it refers to geese.

What they say instead of *I am* is closer to *I stand at*. The grammar does not really allow a person to exist apart from a place in a gradient. You are always somewhere between the water and the ruin.

## The body

Small, and long in the trunk. Deep chest, short forearms, a broad low-set ribcage that makes even young men look faintly barrelled under felt. The lungs are enormous and the blood is thick; a stranger at that altitude would sit down and be sick, and they would stand over him at half a metre and murmur, and he would not understand a word.

They are pale as the stone, and freckled across the nose and cheekbones in a mask where the visor doesn't reach. Everyone squints; by thirty the squint is a permanent architecture of the face, three lines from the outer eye, and the skin there stays white when the rest weathers, so that laughing and squinting have printed the same fan on every adult face and nobody can tell the two apart. By sixty most eyes have gone milky at the edge. By seventy many are milky through. This is expected and not, exactly, mourned. Blindness on the Shelf is a promotion.

Teeth are worn flat — moss carries grit, algae cake carries grit, everything is washed in water that is half stone — and mottled brown-and-cream in fine speckles, in patterns that differ from seep to seep. An old midwife can look in a stranger's mouth and say which water he drank before he was seven. She will not be wrong, and she will not be tactful about it.

Feet are the most remarkable thing. Wide, splayed, the toes separate and prehensile enough to hook a rim, the soles white and softly corrugated from a lifetime of standing in a film of water — the wrinkling never goes out of them, not even in sleep. They wear nothing on their feet. Felt would rot in a week.

And every shin from ankle to knee is a lattice of thin white scars. The rims are a finger high and sharp as broken shell, and children learn the whole city by barking their legs on it. The pattern is legible. Where you grew up, where you courted, which arcade you take at speed when you are late. Lovers read each other's shins in the dark. It is considered the most intimate part of the body and it is entirely public, and both of those things are true at once, which they find funny.

## The hands

Fingertips smoothed nearly featureless by wet weaving. Nails short, soft, and rimed at the quick with a chalky line that never quite comes out. Under the nails, always, a grey paste.

The hand is the only tool worth speaking of, and the second is the bone withe-knife, and the withe-knife's main work is not weaving. It is scraping.

This is the household fact that no visitor would guess. Indoors, everything closes. The water in the air is as hard as the water in the ground, and every surface in a lived-in house is very slowly thickening — the cup's rim, the lip of the basin, the sill, the doorway. A room left alone seals itself in a generation. So the day begins and ends with scraping: the doorway shoulders, the sill, the shrimp-runnel, the coal cup's throat, the rim of the jar you sing into.

Therefore: an object's age is not its thickness. An object's *use* is its thinness. A woman who has eaten from the same spoon for fifty years has a spoon thin as a willow leaf, translucent, singing when she sets it down. An inherited spoon nobody liked is a club. To say of a man *his cup is heavy* is to say he is unloved, or drinks alone, or has been ill a long time. To say *she scrapes her neighbour's cup* is to call her a busybody.
