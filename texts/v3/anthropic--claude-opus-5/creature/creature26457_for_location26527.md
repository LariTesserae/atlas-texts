# What Lives There

## Counting

Depending on how you count, the room holds five inhabitants, or one, or none.

Five is the count you make standing on the stair with your lamp still up: the tree fern at the lip of the fissure, the plated mound swallowing the steam pipe, the rosette out along the conduit, the deep cushion in the sump, and the film of glowing moss that isn't a clump at all but a wash over everything cool enough to sweat. Five clumps, five different-looking things, arranged with all that bare wet concrete between them like furniture in a room nobody sits in.

That count lasts until you lift a board.

---

## The body under the floor

Under the board is the pale threading you already noticed crossing the open concrete — the runners that go nowhere by any logic you can see. Follow one. It does not branch. It runs eleven or twelve metres, passes through a hairline in the concrete, comes up again, and enters the sump cushion at its base, where it is no longer a thread but a rope of the same substance as the cushion itself, indistinguishable, continuous, the way your wrist is continuous with your hand.

Every clump in this room is the far end of something. The fern's trunk fibre, teased apart at the base, is a braid of forty of these runners come up out of the crack. The plated mound on the steam pipe is fed by six that climb the pipe's bracket. The rosette at the bulb is on the end of a single long one that has followed the conduit for a metre and a quarter and thickened where it touched each warm bolt on the way, so that the conduit now wears a row of knuckles like a spine.

So: one. One organism, whose body is mostly floor, and whose visible parts are not individuals but organs — a lung bent over the heat, a stomach on the steam pipe, a scout on the light fitting, a reservoir in the sump, and a skin of luminous moss over the whole wet room, thin as breath, sensitive as breath, gathering nothing but information.

None is also defensible, and you might come to prefer it. There is no membrane around this creature. It has no edge. Where does it stop — at the last runner? At the mineral crust it is dissolving? At the water it has been circulating through itself and the tin ceiling for sixty years, the same water, over and over, so that the rain here is partly its own exhalation? It has no name, because nobody has come down with the equipment to give it one, and it has no relatives in the room to require it to have a name. It has never had to be one thing rather than another.

## What it eats

Not light. Understand that first, because the eye keeps insisting.

Three watts of orange bulb, a suggestion of red from the crack, and a glow so faint it is arguably not there — you could not grow cress on that in a century, let alone three metres of trunk. The chlorophyll in these leaves is vestigial, a trace, the reason the fronds are that drowned white-green instead of true white. It is kept the way you keep a tool you no longer use.

The food comes up the fissure. Hydrogen sulphide, carbon dioxide, hydrogen, in a hot wet breath off rock that was liquid within a lifetime or two. In the root fibre — in every runner, in the underside of every moss cell — there is a second organism, older than the plant by two billion years, a bacterium that burns sulphide the way you burn sugar. The plant is scaffolding. The plant is a way of holding bacteria into the breath of the seam and then carrying the profit twelve metres across a concrete floor to somewhere warm enough to spend it.

The green glow is the accounting error. Energy arrives in gusts, and gusts cannot be stored fast enough, and what cannot be stored has to be dumped somewhere, and it is dumped as light at about five hundred nanometres into a room where light has no meaning and no cost. That is why the mug is a lamp. The mug is full of overflow.

Minerals are the harder problem. Condensate is distilled; rain that falls from a tin ceiling brings nothing with it. Iron, copper, cobalt, molybdenum — the metals every enzyme wants — exist here only in two places: dissolved in the seam's steam, and lying about in the form of machines.

So it eats the machines. Lift the moss off the brass of a fitting and the brass beneath is not tarnished, it is *pitted*, bright-bottomed shallow pits like the marks of small teeth. The heads of the bolts along the rail have gone from hexagonal to round. The rail itself, where the cushion crosses it, is a third of its section and losing perhaps a matchhead of steel a year. It is not fast. The compressor has nine hundred years left in it. The transformer, being live and warm and rich, is the best food in the room and is also the one thing the moss keeps a two-centimetre margin from, having learned this the way anything learns anything, by having part of itself killed.

Whether the machines are alive is a question you can resolve however you like. The plants resolved it long ago, in favour of yes.

## What it knows

It has one sense and the sense is heat.

Not light, not sound, not chemistry beyond the dumb gradient of the seam. Heat, measured in tenths of a degree, integrated over hours, and from that alone it has built a map of this chamber that is better than yours. It knows the warm bolt at the fourth bracket. It knows the flank of the compressor holds afternoon longer than the wall behind it. It knows the western third is cold and has never sent anything there and never will. It found the bulb across a metre of unlit dark by walking up a temperature gradient of about a fifth of a degree, over four years, with no more idea of *bulb* than you have of the shape of the field that holds your inner ear level — and got there, and cupped twelve leaves around forty watts of waste like hands around a match.

It also keeps two calendars.

The short one you have already been told about, but not correctly. The plants have not learned the pump. Ninety seconds is far too little warning to load and fire every sporangium in a room; by the time you hear that rising throat-clearing from the east, the grey snow is already ascending. What they have learned is the mains. When the pump motor takes its starting load, the transformer's hum sags — you can hear it if you know, a flattening of a semitone or so, forty to fifty seconds ahead — and it is that dip in the current, felt as a hundredth of a degree lost from every knuckle wrapped around every warm bolt in the chamber, that the organism reads. It does not know there is a pump. It knows the room's electricity, intimately, the way you know your own pulse, and it has hung its entire reproductive life on a voltage sag.

The long calendar is stranger, because it comes from a place this creature has never been and cannot conceive.

Count the fronds on the fern: sixty-one, spiralled, none shed. It adds one a year. It has no year — nothing here has weather — except that above, out in a world of sky, it rains in the winter, and the water table lifts, and the pump runs a little longer per cycle, and the seam breathes a fraction wetter and a fraction cooler for a few months. Two-tenths of a degree of February, arriving underground. The fern feels it and lays down a frond. So the spiral on that pale trunk is a record of sixty-one winters in a country the plant has never been told about, and it agrees, within a year or two, with the nineteen strokes and the gap and the two angrier ones scratched beside the switch wall.

Two calendars kept by two different residents of the same room, agreeing, neither aware of the other.

## Where it came from

It came down on the coat.

Not the coat as it hangs now, board-stiff and epauletted with lime — the coat when it was worn, when it had a wet cuff, and in the seam of that cuff a crumb of mud from some other working, some hot spring, some flooded lower level four hundred kilometres away where its parents lived in the ordinary manner of their kind, among snails and midges and things with faces. One fragment, one spore. It arrived without ceremony in a room that was then merely a warm damp sub-basement of a small enterprise, and it would have died there.

Then the seam opened.

It has never met another of itself. Not one. Every eleven hours for sixty-one years it has fired the entire contents of its body into the air — call it four hundred million spores a cycle, call it eight hundred trillion since the last time anyone walked up that stair — and every single one of them has come down again inside sixteen paces, onto wet concrete, onto its own leaves, into the sump, into the mug. There is no wind here. There has never been any wind here. The grey snow goes up because the pump's exhalation lifts it, and then the exhalation stops, and it comes back down, and it lands on its mother.

That is the most important fact about the thing that lives in this room and it is invisible, because futility at this scale does not look like anything. It looks like a lush green room.

The moss stops at the coat's hem out of nothing more mystical than the fungicidal oil they proofed canvas with in those days, still faintly potent, still saying *no* after six decades. Its own vessel repels it. You may make of that what you like; the organism cannot.

## What it will do about you

You are the first moving warmth in its life.

Its physics has no category for this. Heat sources are fixed. The bulb is where the bulb is; the pipe is where the pipe is; the seam has been the seam since before the fern. And here is thirty-seven degrees, wet, salt, three-dimensional, walking about the chamber and stooping in the western third and putting a hand on the compressor.

It responds at its own speed. Nothing will happen while you watch. But sit down against the switch wall and stay the night — the headache is not the heat, incidentally; it is the carbon dioxide pooling at ankle height, and during those ninety seconds of exhalation you should be standing, ideally on the stair — and in the morning the outer leaves of the rosette will have canted two or three degrees off the bulb and toward the place where you slept. It will take the rosette a fortnight to forget you. It will spend that fortnight reaching for a man who has gone.

And when you climb out, your shirt is soaked, and your cuffs are soaked, and the grey snow of the last exhalation is in the weave of both, and in your hair, and in the treads of your boots along with a smear of sump mud in which there are living rhizome threads a centimetre long.
