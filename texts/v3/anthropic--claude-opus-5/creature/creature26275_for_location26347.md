## What lives on the ledge

Wear lives on the ledge. That is the first thing to say, because it explains everything inside.

Between and behind the forty-one lamps there are seventeen rosettes of a grey-green lichen, none broader than a child's palm, each of them chewing the slab into powder at the rate of about a hair's width in a decade. The powder blows off. The wind is the metallic one, and it has been at the bronze lamps long enough to give them their skins, and it has taken the shoulder off one of the small clay ones so that it will never hold oil again. Everything on the ledge has an age you could argue about with your eyes.

The lichen cannot get in. It dies in the warmth, within a day, and it dies without leaving a mark, which is the room's way of saying no. So the lichen sits on the sill of the world, unhurried, making dust in the one place dust is allowed, and the keepers step around it out of a courtesy they could not explain if asked.

## Who brings the lamps

There are, this generation, nine people who know the route with their palms. They are not priests and there is no word in their speech for what they are; they are the ones whose mothers took them. They live scattered down the south side and in the barley notch below it: goats, a little copper, a winter of grinding and mending. They are, in the ordinary world, people whose hands are full of wear — cracked thumbs, a millstone, a rope going furry at the knot.

They are taken up at seven years old, at night, so that the dark of the climb and the dark of the room are the same dark and the child never learns to expect a difference. Nobody tells them the route. Somebody holds their wrists and moves them. Under the fan, on the hip at the crossed spires, up through the shoulder-gap; then a hand on the back of the neck, pressing down, which means *sit*.

They do not name anything in the room. Not the room, not the water, not the thing that touches the ankle. Naming is a mark. Outside, walking down, they will say *the warm one* or *the sweet patch* the way you'd say *the far field*, and they will forget having said it.

Each of them, once in a life, carries a lamp up. They fill it. They set a wick in it. They do not light it. They put it at the end of the row and they go in without it. Nobody has ever explained the lamps and there is no need: the row is forty-one refusals in clay and stone and, twice, in bronze carried from somewhere with smelters, by somebody who came a very long way to not light something. The row is also the only honest calendar on the mountain, because outside, things fall apart at a rate you can read. The notches on the sill inside cannot be counted twice the same. The lamps can be counted from the far end of the ledge. They are the mountain's ledger for everything the room refuses to keep.

## The one with a hand in each world

On the ridge above, a juniper. No taller than a man, with its wind-side half dead and silver and polished like a handle, because up there the air is taking it apart with the same patience it uses on the lamps. Nobody knows its age; four hundred years is the guess of people with no reason to guess well.

Eleven arm-lengths under itself, in the room, the same tree hangs in a pale curtain you part with your forearm. Down there it has no bark, no hairs, no colour, and no age at all. Its root-tips are exactly as new as the hour they were made and will be that new when the silver half above has been ground into the pass.

So the juniper is the only living thing that knows both temperatures. Half of it is being spent; half of it is being kept. It has arranged this without knowing it, by reaching down for wet air, and it is the closest thing this mountain has to a creature that understands the arrangement it lives inside.

A woman took a branch from it once, four generations of notches ago, for a walking stick. The stick is still in a house in the barley notch. It warped and split and greyed, like wood. That is the whole lesson of the mountain in one object, and the family that owns it does not think of it as anything but a stick.

## The one that gives off something

On the north-facing crystal, where the film of water runs thinnest, there is a slick the area of two hands laid side by side.

It is the room's only mouth and the room's only breath. Everything else in there takes nothing and gives nothing; this gives the sweetness. It has no growing edge. Look for the frontier where it advances and there is none — it stops the way a coastline stops, with no argument. It has been two hands' width through every generation that could report on it, and it will be two hands' width for every generation that can.

It does not come away on your finger. Nothing in the room becomes residue, and that includes this.

Whether it is alive is a question the keepers have never once asked, and if you asked it in front of them they would look at you the way you'd look at someone asking whether a river is thirsty. It is exactly as much as it has always been. Growth and hunger and dying back are all forms of wear. This has none of them. It simply persists, sweetly, at a temperature like the inside of a mouth, and it is the only thing in the unworn room that can be said to be *doing* anything at all.

## The warm one

It comes along the ankle.

The weight is a hare's and the gait is a weasel's — that long, unhurried pour of a body over an obstacle. The fur is dry. That is the part nobody gets used to. In a room where your own eyelashes bead and your shirt sags with water, something walks past your foot and it is dry, and warmer than the air, warmer than blood, closer to the heat of a fevered child.

One woman, sitting the whole of an hour, felt it at both ankles at the same time. That single hour is the entire basis for believing there are two.

It has never been heard. This ought to be impossible: the floor is a bundled thicket of hexagonal edges, and a claw on that makes a small hard noise, and a whisker drawn across it makes a smaller one. It makes neither. Either it goes on pads that never quite settle, or the ten thousand facets eat it as they eat your voice, or it is not moving over the stone at all in the way you assume.

It has never been caught, and the reason is not speed. It does not flee. It arrives, it passes along the ankle at the pace of something with no opinion about you, and then it is not there. You can have your hands down and open, on either side of your foot, and there is nothing between them.

It does not eat what you bring. The bread you left is there next visit, at the weight it was, soft, smelling faintly of your house. That is the small horror everybody mentions, and it is the wrong horror; the bread is not the room's business, it is yours, kept for you.

Consider its economy. It sheds nothing: the fold you come in by is sharp enough to shave with, and a furred thing passing through it would leave hair on that edge every time, and the keepers have run a fingertip along that edge on the way in for a hundred and some notches out of a habit nobody remembers starting, and it has never come away with a hair. There is no scat. There is no smell of it — the sweetness is the film's. Its claws are not wearing down and so do not need to grow. Nothing about it is spent, and so nothing about it needs replacing.

Which means it does not go out. Which means it did not come in. Which means it began in here, in a room where the only thing that begins is crystal.

One of the nine, an old man who is dead now, held that there is no animal and never has been: that the dry *tick* from deep in the stone and the warmth along the ankle are the same event, arriving once by the ear and once by the skin, four times an hour, the room laying down one more layer and touching you while it does it. Nobody argued with him. Nobody agreed with him either. They think it is the kind of thing a man says after fifty years of sitting in the dark, and they think it might be right, and they go on calling it the warm one on the way down and forgetting they said so.

## The lodgers

In the second bowl, nine animals the size of plum stones: a bird on legs too long for it, a beast with a hanging nose, a coiled thing with plates down its back. Nobody on this mountain has seen any of them. They were carved by a hand that clearly had, and carried here from a country with rivers by somebody who then walked back down and out of the story. They are as fresh as the afternoon they were finished. They are, strictly speaking, the least decayed animals in the world.

In the first bowl, a three-strand cord that has not rotted and will not, coiled for a purpose its owner did not survive long enough to explain.

In the third bowl, nothing, and the third bowl is the most inhabited object on the mountain. People put things down in it. Not objects — the objects go in the other two. A man leaves a grief there because he cannot carry it through the winter, and the room does what the room does: keeps it, exactly, at the weight it was, and hands it back when he comes for it, unaged, unsoftened, with every edge on it new. Some come back for them. Some leave them a long time. Nothing in that bowl has ever improved, because improving is a kind of wearing away, and the room does not offer that service.

## The resident

Low left, under the fan, on the way in, where your palm goes first.

One snapped column. The break is old. It is the only old thing in the room, and it is the only thing in the room that is genuinely, unarguably *alive* in the sense that matters here: it has a history. It happened once, to somebody, in the dark, at a particular moment; it is the only event this place has ever consented to hold onto.

Whoever did it knew instantly, in silence, because there would have been no sound. Whoever did it went out sideways past an edge sharp enough to shave with, and down, and lived a life, and put a lamp on the ledge, and cut a notch on the sill that cannot now be told from the others. The lamp is losing its shoulder to the wind. The notch is uncountable. The bones are gone into the barley notch.

The break is not wearing. It will still be exactly that old — that same old, that same one minute of somebody's ordinary carelessness — when the mountain, working outward from this small warm room at the rate of one dry tick, four times an hour, has finished making itself.

Nothing lives in the unworn room. Living is a form of wear. But something is being kept there, and it is not the crystal, and it is not the sweetness, and it is not the warm one along the ankle.

It is a mistake, housed, at the centre of a mountain that is being built around it.
