# What Lives in the Standing Pan

## The First Fact

Nothing lives in the room.

Everything else about the inhabitants of this place follows from that, the way everything about the place follows from the fog. The room is thirty thousand cubic metres of clean moving air and it is not habitat, it is plumbing; no animal has ever made a living in it and none ever will, and the creatures that live here are all, without exception, creatures of the wall, the gutter, or the ground beneath.

So: look at the wall.

The laminate is not a surface. It is a slow outward current. New leaf-bases are pressed on at the inner face; the outermost curl away as scrolls and go. The thickness stays what it has always been, which means the material of the wall is moving — from core to air, from birth to shedding — at something like a hand's width in a human lifetime.

That is the law under which everything in this place lives. The house is walking out from under its tenants at three millimetres a year. Every animal in the wall must travel inward at exactly that rate, forever, in order to remain precisely where it is. None of them outrun anything. They only decline to stop.

## The Singers

Fifty thousand is a count of songs, not of animals. It omits the females, the nymphs in their first two years, and the males who have gone quiet, and the true population of the wall is probably three times the number you can hear.

They are sap-tappers. Each one, as a nymph, works its way down a seam until its stylet finds one of the running channels beneath the laminate, and there it drinks — dilute, endless, free — and it does not move again for a long time. The slot it occupies is perhaps the length of your forearm and the width of a coin. That is its country. It will die in it, ten or twelve millimetres further out than where it was born.

Now the thing about the song.

They cannot hear it. Not one of them can hear anything in air; the tympanal organs are gone, reduced to two dry scars on the abdomen, because an animal with ears in this room would be an animal in continuous agony. What they hear is the wall. The lamina under their feet carries bending waves beautifully — it is a horn-hard plate a millimetre thick, an almost perfect membrane — and every tymbal-click goes into it and travels, and dies out at about forty centimetres.

So the sound that stops your throat, the sheet of sandpaper across the top of hearing, the thing you can feel in your fillings: that is leakage. It is what escapes into the air from fifty thousand private conversations, none of which is longer than your arm, none of which is addressed to anyone you could see. The roar is the waste heat of a great deal of small talk.

A female, finding the vibration acceptable, answers by interrupting it. Two taps. That is the entire courtship of the species.

## The Beetles, and What Rides Them

Pale, flattened to the thickness of a playing card, eyeless, long-legged, and — this is the important constraint — incapable of turning around. A slot will not permit it. Once a beetle has entered, it goes forward until it reaches a junction or it backs out the way it came, and there is no third option.

It hunts by gradient. Antennae laid flat along the lamina, it reads the local amplitude, takes a few steps, reads again. The signal strengthens. It follows the strengthening. At the end of the strengthening there is a singer, and the singer, drinking, does not perceive it, because what it perceives is the song, and the song is its own.

Which produces the standing tragedy of the wall: the silent males are safe. A male who stops singing is unfindable, and lives a long time, and leaves nothing behind. Every one of the fifty thousand voices you are hearing has made this trade.

On the beetles' backs, in the gap between the wing-cases they no longer open, ride the mites. They are parasites of the singers and they have no other way to travel; the wall is too dry and too vast for a thing that size. So the mite waits on a dying host for the arrival of the thing that is killing it, steps across, and is carried — in the dark, at walking pace, eyelessly — to the next meal. The predator is also the ferry. Neither party appears to have noticed.

## The Wet Band

Follow the inner face down to where it goes dark and slick, and you come into the only part of this place with a visible border.

There is a line, about a metre and a half above the gutter, below which the algal skin is cropped clean and above which it is not. That line is the reach of a rail's neck. And in the band above it — a ribbon forty metres long and a metre and a half high, sixty square metres of wet wall, the whole country — live the frogs.

Thumbnail-sized, flat, and so translucent that a gravid female is a visible clutch of eggs walking around inside a rumour of a frog. No lungs; the skin does all of it, and the skin only works because the water never stops arriving. No eardrum, no middle ear, no vocal sac, no larynx worth the name. Somewhere far back in their ancestry these were frogs that called, and in this place that was so obviously futile that the whole apparatus was surrendered, bone by bone, to be put to better use.

What they have instead is the water. The condensate comes down the wall not as a sheet but as a braided system of threads and beads, and a frog that plants itself in the right posture at the right point splits a thread and sends the split downward. Everything below it feels the change — a bead arriving where no bead arrived before, a cool interruption on the back at an interval that means something. This is their entire signalling repertoire. They write on the water by standing in it, and they read it with their spines, and the message takes about a minute to send.

They go round. A frog hatched on the north arc may die on the north arc, having circled the room three or four times in its life, never once having been dry, never once having been closer than a metre and a half to the floor.

## The Wedged

The bats do not look inward, and this is easy to mistake for indifference. It is nothing of the kind.

They pack the dry vertical gaps in their thousands, seam after seam of them, and they leave through the outer face at dusk. Most scatter over the pan. But for the first hour, a ring of perhaps two hundred works the oculus from above, circling the rim of the mouth in the dark, and what they are doing is collecting.

Every night, insects come to the Standing Pan. They come from the whole flat country, because the plant exhales the wettest air in three days' travel — on cold mornings you can see the plume standing forty metres over the dome before it shears — and anything that navigates by humidity is walking a road that ends here. They funnel in at the perimeter gaps, into the cool breath around your ankles. And then the room has them. The draught takes everything off the floor and up the column and out through the four-metre mouth, at speed, in the dark, into a ring of waiting animals.

This is worth being clear about. The great vacant volume is not empty because nothing wants it. It is empty because it is swept — from below by the chimney, from above by teeth. The original observation stands corrected only in emphasis: it is not like an operating theatre. It is like a throat.

The colonies drift too, of course; their gaps are being carried outward at three millimetres a year, so a seam of bats must re-wedge inward across a century, which no individual bat experiences and every colony performs.

## The Ring

The gutter is the only one-dimensional country on the continent, and it has three kinds of citizen.

The snails are all approximately the same size, because the lip they graze is one width and the algal skin is one thickness, and a snail too large for the film starves. They travel against the current, always — a snail that goes with the flow is grazing where it has already grazed — and a long-lived one completes perhaps eleven circuits. You could measure a life in laps. When it dies the shell goes into the water and goes round and goes down the crack, so somewhere under the pan there is a bone-house nobody has seen.

Then the two rails. One is dark and slightly larger and walks with the current; one is grey with a white chin and walks against it. They take different food — the dark one works the water and the drowned, the grey one works the lip and the lower wall — and so they can share a corridor a pace wide without argument.

They meet twice per lap, at two points on opposite arcs. Because their speeds are not identical, the meeting points move: a few metres a day, all the way round, completing their own slow circuit in about eleven days.

That is the only clock in the Standing Pan. There is no season, no hour, no rising or setting that penetrates the dome; there is nothing here that is early or late. But there is this: a place on the ring, moving, where two birds will step over each other. They lift the wings to do it — a flutter of half a second, the last surviving function of flight in either lineage, which has otherwise been given up entirely as a bad idea in a country you can walk across in forty steps.

The chicks are walked. A first circuit takes a chick eleven days, and it is not an accident that the number is the same.

## The Crack

Northeast arc, a hand's width, angled down into the pan.

Below it the water spreads out and slows and goes sheet-thin between the silt laminations, and in the dark there are blind pale swimming things a few millimetres long, in numbers, doing the only job available: taking apart everything the room sends down. Snail shells, drowned moths, the frogs that lost their grip, the rails at the end of their laps, the beetles that reached the outer face and were shed with the scroll and were washed in by one of the dozen rains.

And waiting in that same wet dark, threading the whole underside of the gutter, are roots.

So the circuit closes. Fog to crown, crown to wall, wall to gutter, gutter to crack, crack to root, root to crown. The animals are a tax on the loop and not a large one, and even the tax is eventually repaid. This is why there is no litter on the floor and no smell anywhere in the building. Nothing here is disposed of. Things are only in transit.

## The Ones Who Come

No one lives in the room, and no one has ever managed to sleep in it.

But there are people who live by it. Not many — a few families on a long annual circuit of the flat country, who arrive perhaps twice a year, from two directions, and stay three or four days. They sleep outside, against the base of the columns, where the roar has softened into weather and the thrum of the water comes up through the shoulder blades, and they report that it is the best sleep available anywhere in their territory.

They take water from the northeast arc, a pace upstream of the crack, so as not to take anything the ground has not already been given. They take bats. They take the outer scrolls for fuel, which burn slowly and badly and smell of nothing.

Inside, they use the hands. Since no voice survives the threshold, everything transacted in the room is transacted in a hand-language of about fourteen signs, of which eleven concern water — its depth, its taste, its rate, whether it has been touched. The spoken language of these people has no name for this place. They point, and say the word for *loud*.

And they time their business by the rails. Ask them how long they will stay and they will tell you: until the birds meet at the crack.

They do not look for the rivets. They are the only visitors who don't, and it is not because they have a story explaining the oculus. They have no such story. It is because they were brought here as infants and have never in their lives encountered a version of the world that did not contain a forty-metre room, breathing, with a round hole in the top of it, and one is not startled by the ordinary.

## The Ground

Which leaves the tenant.

Two hundred columns, one plant, no growth, only replacement — this much is visible. What is not visible is that the dome is not the organism. The dome is the vent.

Under the pan, for a distance nobody has properly measured but which is certainly more than a day's walk, lies a plate of roots: flat, braided, laid down in laminations of its own, tying the silt together and holding it at a depth where the water sits. It is the reason the ground rings underfoot. It is the reason the pan is a pan and not a field of dunes. And it is the reason the hairline cracks come back in the same places every year, year after year, inherited from cracks that are themselves long gone — they are not cracks in a floor. They are the floor drying over a map, and the map has not moved in an age.

So the honest answer to the question is smaller than expected in one direction and much larger in another.

Forty thousand singers who cannot hear each other. Their parasites, riding their predators. A ribbon of frogs signalling in beads of water. A colony of bats standing at the top of a chimney, waiting to be fed by the weather. Snails counting out their lives in laps, and two flightless birds keeping the only calendar, and a crack full of blind things taking everything apart.

And under all of it, and under you, and under the two days of flat country you crossed to get here — one individual, lying down with its mouth open, breathing out.

You were standing on it before you saw it. You will be standing on it tomorrow.
