# The Fauna of the Rise

### 1. A caution about the verb

Nothing on the Rise *lives there* in the sense you mean, which is the sense that includes having arrived. Arrival implies elsewhere. There is no elsewhere in evidence. Every animal you meet is at grade — correct for its altitude, correct for its tier, correct for the age of the rock beneath it, which is four days — and it is already doing the thing it does, and it has been doing the thing it does for as long as the thing has needed doing, which is four days.

They are not colonists. They are a mineral phase, like the flywheels. You find them where the conditions produce them.

Say *occupies*. Say *is present at*. The word *lives* will keep smuggling in a past for them, and they have not been issued one.

### 2. The trimmers, at length, because they matter

Low. Broad. Roughly the mass of a cow and nothing else about them is cowlike. Six legs, short, set wide, so that from the side they look like a bench. The dorsal armour is plated in overlapping scutes with a faint parallel striation across each one, and if you have spent any time at a die-slot you will recognise that striation, and you will put your hand on a living animal's back and feel the same drawn grain you felt on the soft lip of new ridge that morning, and you will take your hand away again.

The mouth is the entire front of the animal. Three grinding plates, upper, lower, and a rotating median, and they do not grow like teeth; they are replaced. A trimmer's plates wear to nothing in about eleven days and are then simply correct again, overnight, without a seam. Nobody has watched this happen. Everyone has tried.

They eat sharpness. This is the most literal statement in the document. Burr, flash, mold-line, the brittle translucent overspill at the crest of a seam, the sharp new lip of an extrusion still too soft to ring. A herd works a face the way a crowd works a buffet: no aggression, no order, no waiting, thousands of animals grinding in total silence with their heads to the stone and their hindquarters to the weather, and the only sound in a kilometre is the tick of dew concentrating and falling somewhere off in the murk.

What comes out the other end is white grit. It is the only sediment on the Rise. There are no rivers to sort it and no wind with enough patience to move it, so it lies where it falls, in long pale drifts behind the herds, the closest thing this range has to sand and the closest thing it has to soil.

Here is the thing about the grit, and it is worth carrying a pouch of it:

Pick up a handful and try to imagine where it was last year. You will fail, because last year is not available. But try to imagine where it was *the day before yesterday*, and you will succeed. It was in the gut of an animal. Before that it was the lip of a ridge. You can follow it back two full steps without the foil-taste, and on the Rise that is the entire local supply of history. People who spend a season in the interior tend to keep a little in a pocket and hold it when things get thin. It is the only object on the Rise that has been somewhere.

When a die-slot finishes and the face goes quiet, the herd does not disperse. It stands facing the stopped slot for between two and nine days, thousands of animals, plates still, all oriented. Then one morning they are walking away singly in all directions, and you can follow one for six hours and it will not do anything. And the count of trimmers in the range does not go down.

### 3. The gaunts, at less length, because they do not like it

Two and a half metres at the shoulder and perhaps forty kilograms. Four legs with too many joints in them, hairless, skin the colour and finish of wet slate. The head is small and is mostly an apparatus for removal, which does not look like a mouth and does not need to be near the thing it works on.

They step high. This is the detail everyone remembers. A gaunt lifts each foot clear to the height of its own belly before setting it down, and it sets it down where no foot has been. Not no foot of its own — no foot. It will detour four metres to avoid a trimmer track. Its whole gait is a refusal to repeat.

What they do to a withdrawn ridge is not eating in any manner you can watch and keep your appetite. They go along the surplus, unhurried, and put the head near a thing, and the thing becomes less. Pattern goes first: the lichen stops having a shape, then stops having a colour, then stops. A duplicate tree greys from the leaf-tips inward. A duplicate animal, asleep, thins. By evening the valley between the twins has closed and the ridge you did not camp on has never been surveyed, and the word *never* is doing a great deal of work in that clause.

They will look at you. Once is ordinary; they look at everything. Twice, with the head held still between, is an audit, and the sensation is precisely that of hearing your own name read off a list by someone who is checking it against another list. It passes. It nearly always passes.

Do not camp where a gaunt has looked at you twice. Not because it will come back. Because it will not need to.

A gaunt caught in a repetition pocket does not graze and does not flee. It stops, locks its legs, and stands out the entire four or five passes without moving so much as an ear, since to move would be to move twice. They come out of the pocket dull-eyed and shaking and walk in a straight line for a long way. It is the only visible distress on the Rise.

### 4. The dew shepherds, who are the hydrology

Small, sluggish, the size of a hare, pelted in a fine hydrophobic nap that the water will not enter and will not leave. They walk the upper tiers all day gathering, until they are carrying two or three litres in a shell of beaded water and look like something made of glass and lit from inside, and then they go down, unhurriedly, to a pool, and shed the lot in one shrug.

That is the water cycle. That is all of it. No rivers, no streams, no runoff, no springs: the standing pools of the Rise exist because approximately one hundred thousand small animals walk downhill every afternoon carrying the rain on their backs. Kill the shepherds and the range would be dry within a fortnight while remaining, at every point, wet.

They are placid and can be lifted. They do not object to being lifted. Nothing here objects to anything, and this is not tameness. Objection requires having learned something last time.

### 5. Pool etiquette

At a pool, in the afternoon, you may have eleven species drinking within four metres and no contact between any of them. No displacement, no threat posture, no order of precedence. They queue.

This is because a dominance hierarchy is a form of record-keeping, and there is no record-keeping. Two animals cannot know which of them won before, because there was no before and neither of them was there for it. So the pool operates like a well-run counter: you arrive, you wait, you drink, you go. The atmosphere is one of low-grade civic patience, and after some weeks you will find you have begun queueing too, out of nothing but courtesy, with the mild administrative disappointment that is the emotional baseline of the entire interior.

### 6. The charge fauna

On the cable bundles, where the field stands your arm-hairs up, there are things living in the field itself.

Flat, palm-sized, many-legged, mouthless. They clamp onto a strand, draw current, walk on, discharge into a root mat. They do not eat and cannot be fed. Their entire metabolism is a transaction between two potentials and the fact that they are in the middle of it.

They are also the nearest thing the Rise has to speech. The silent coordinated movement of animals through the tiers — the way ten thousand small things in the canopy turn over at once like a gust going through — runs on discharge patterns laid into the root mats by the charge fauna, and it carries content, and nobody has got anything out of it beyond the observation that it carries content.

When one of them stops it does not rot. It goes inert, keeps its shape, gets overtaken by the next extrusion and phases into the new rock as an inclusion. Cut one out and you have a small dead flat thing in a matrix, and it looks, to a degree that is not comfortable, like a very small and very simple version of the mechanisms.

From which a certain theory follows immediately, about what the flywheels were before they were flywheels, and how large a thing would have to have been to leave an inclusion eight metres across, and whether the range is a machine or a graveyard or whether it has occurred to the range that there is a difference.

This is the sort of theory the Rise encourages and does not reward. It is available to everybody by the second week. Nothing ever confirms it. Nothing ever clears it away, either, and it sits in the mind like flashing, three metres high and never trimmed.

### 7. Smaller guilds, in brief

**Race-cleaners.** Blind, soft, pale, finger-length, living in the film of warm lubricant that the rock exudes into its own bearings. They keep the races clean. Whether the mountain benefits or merely tolerates them is exactly as unanswerable as everything else, and the bearings do turn.

**Slot-waiters.** A small predator that sits at a working die-slot with its back to the open air and takes whatever comes out of the soft lip that should not have been in there. Occasionally what comes out of the soft lip is alive. It is at grade, it is correct for its tier, and it is immediately eaten, so the question of what it was does not arise for long.

**Pocket-feeders.** The only efficient predator on the Rise. It hunts exclusively inside repetition pockets, where it kills the same animal on each pass — four or five carcasses, one death. The books do not quite balance and the pocket-feeders are visibly fatter than anything else in the range. Their prey are fat and calm too. A repetition pocket is, in local terms, a pleasant place with a good standard of living.

### 8. Ninety seconds

Everything here is entrained.

The pistons run at ninety seconds a stroke, and although the sound is below hearing the pressure is not, and over time it gets into the organisms. Trimmer plates work at some clean division of it. Dew shepherds shrug at some clean division of it. The canopy turns over at some clean division of it. You will not notice this for a fortnight. Then one evening you will take your own pulse against the pressure in your jaw and find that some whole number of your beats fits the stroke exactly.

And then, over the following days, you will notice that the number has stopped changing. Not that it has slowed, or steadied — that it has *stopped changing*, in the way a figure on a form stops changing once it has been entered.

This is the mechanism by which the Rise takes people on.

### 9. The walkers

In the mid-canopy, on the shoulders, at the pools in the afternoon, there are bipeds.

Hairless. Upright. Two metres or under. They carry gear: packs, straps, a pot, a blade, boots with the tread worn asymmetrically in the way boots wear when someone favours a leg. The gear is correct. The gear is *worn*, plausibly, to spec, with scuffs in the places scuffs go, and if you pick up one of their buckles and try to imagine where it was last year you will get the foil-taste immediately and put it down.

They are gentle. They queue at the pools. They will shift along a branch to make room for you without being asked, and they have a good eye for where the root mat is thin. They do not speak, because nothing here has needed to invent a call, and their throats are entirely capable of it and have simply never had a reason.

They are commonly found standing at the die-slots in the late light, watching the new ridge come out at walking-pace-divided-by-a-thousand, with an expression on them. It is not awe. It is not dread. It is the mild administrative disappointment of someone who has been in a queue for some time and has stopped estimating.

The gaunts look at them once and look away. They are the accepted version.

There is no evidence anywhere on the Rise that anything ever built anything, or intended anything, or came from anywhere. There is no low country in view. There is no path out, other than the ones the trimmer herds grind and abandon. The only argument for the walkers having ever been something else, somewhere else, is that you came in from somewhere yourself, and can remember it, and can describe it, and could sketch the harbour if asked.

Do that now, while you're thinking of it. Sketch the harbour. Note how well it comes.

Note the wear on your boots. Note the number of beats, and that it isn't moving.

It is warm, and it is quiet, and there are fifty animals within thirty metres of you, and in a fortnight there will be fifty-one, and the count will not have gone up.
