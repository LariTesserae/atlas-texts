## The people of Nýgres

**Who is here.**

Four hundred and eleven, at the last count, and that is a count and not an estimate, because this is a town that counts things. The number is written on a slate outside the canteen and changed by whoever hears first.

Nobody here is older than the ground. That is the first thing to understand about them. The oldest resident, Ásvald Hrafnsson, came over the rubble neck in 1964 at nineteen years old with the second crew, carrying a theodolite case and someone else's boots, and the lava he walked on was one year old and still warm in places if you put your palm flat in a crack. He is eighty now. He has outlived every structure he built. There are four generations here and the eldest of them can remember the plain naked — no moss, no willow, no thickets, nothing but black and the sea and the crews sleeping in tents lashed to iron pins driven straight into the rock.

So there are no ruins. There is no old church. There are no cellars, no wells but the one pool, no drystone walls falling down in a field, no family plot, nothing abandoned long enough to become picturesque. The town has no past that anyone has to take on trust. This makes the people of Nýgres oddly unsentimental and oddly superstitious at the same time: they will tell you flatly that the geologists are wrong about the uplift, and then tell you, with the same face, that there is a thing in the fog on the western margin that follows walkers at a fixed distance of about thirty metres and has done since 1977. They know they invented it. They keep it anyway. A town with no ghosts feels underfurnished.

**Their bodies.**

You can identify a Nýgres man or woman anywhere in the country by three things.

The hands. The basalt takes skin, and everyone falls, and everyone falls repeatedly and from childhood, so every adult here has the same white hairline scarring across the knuckles and the backs of the forearms — fine, crazed, like the glaze on old porcelain. They call it the plain's signature. A visitor is a person with clean hands.

The walk. Rope lava has to be crossed with the foot picked up high and set down flat, never scuffed, never rolled, and in tern season it has to be crossed hunched with a stick held vertically above the head. Both habits go into the body by the age of six and never come out. People from here walk down city streets lifting their feet slightly too much and drifting their right hand upward at nothing.

The voice. Everyone who has worked a season in the furnace hall has lost the band between three and five kilohertz, which is where consonants live. They cannot hear *s*, *f*, *t* cleanly from more than two metres. So they speak loudly, over-shape their mouths, stand close, and touch the listener's forearm to claim attention before starting a sentence — a light two-finger tap that outsiders read as unusual intimacy and which is simply the local equivalent of clearing the throat. They finish each other's sentences constantly, because half of listening here is guessing, and guessing gets faster when you know the person. Married couples of thirty years conduct entire conversations in which neither completes a clause.

And they sign. There are about ninety signs in *hall-hands*, which grew up in the furnace hall where the spinners scream at a pitch that arrives in the chest instead of the ear. *Hot. Back off. Wheel's running out. Yes. No. Wait. Don't.* The signs have leaked out of the works and into the houses, and now they are what the town uses for close things — a child signs *wait* to a parent across a crowded canteen; couples argue in hall-hands in total silence, which is a terrible way to argue because you cannot raise your voice, only your hand, and everyone can see you do it.

Their skin itches. Stone-hair gets into everything: laundry, bedding, the inside of a shirt collar, the web between the fingers. Scratching drives the fibre in. The correct response is tape, and there is a roll of wide packing tape by every door in Nýgres, nailed on a spindle, the way other places keep a boot-scraper. You dab, you don't scratch. Children are taught this before they are taught the stove. The three communal washing machines have a lint filter that yields, weekly, a grey felt mat the size of a dishcloth; these are collected in a bin by the door and, in May, carried up to the cliff path and shaken out on purpose, because the fulmars want them.

And some of them are dying of it, slowly, and they know. Dagmar the nurse runs the chest screening every second year, and the films go into a flat steel drawer in the clinic in date order, and the older men's films have that faint even whiteness at the bases that everyone in the room recognises and nobody names. Four of the first crew went that way. Ásvald's film is clean, which he attributes to never having smoked and which Dagmar attributes to luck and nothing else. The town does not pretend this isn't happening. It simply files it, the way it files the tide-post marks.

**The crews.**

Ninety-one people work at the works, on three shifts, and the shifts are families as much as rosters.

The furnace crew are the aristocracy and behave like it. They wear the heat on their faces — a permanent low flush across the cheekbones, eyebrows that don't quite grow back. The furnace cannot be let go; once it is down it takes thirty-six hours to bring back, and so the hall is manned every hour of every day of the year except four. Nói Sigurbjörnsson has tapped it for twenty-two years and can tell the melt's temperature by colour to within thirty degrees, and will tell you that this is not a skill, it is just what happens to a person who looks at one thing for twenty-two years.

The spinners are mostly women, by no particular design, and they are the deafest people in the country. They work behind screens in a shriek that cannot be described in terms of sound at all, watching a rope of orange liquid come apart on the wheels into something that looks like the smoke off a hot day. They come off shift and sit in the canteen for a quarter of an hour saying nothing at all, which is understood and not intruded upon.

The quarry crew work the cut in the scoria spine, and are the only people in Nýgres who are permitted, by custom, to be bad-tempered in public. They are taking the island apart. Somebody did the arithmetic in 1998: the works removes about eleven thousand tonnes of ground a year and the plain rises about eleven millimetres a year, and the second is winning, comfortably, and this fact is printed on a cardboard sign in the quarry office as a joke that has stopped being a joke. *NET GAIN.*

Hrefna Steinsdóttir drives the locomotive, which is the size of a car and is called, without affection, the Dog. She has driven it since she was nineteen. She is fifty-three. She knows the track to the millimetre and knows before the surveyors do when a section has lifted, because the Dog tells her through the seat.

Steinn takes the boat out and brings it in twice a week and finds the channel in fog by the floor — by where the humming sits in the pitch. He says the harbour has a note and the note has been going up, slowly, all his life.

**The level-keeper.**

The most important person in Nýgres is not the works manager. It is Bóel Ásvaldsdóttir, who is sixty-two and who keeps the Book.

The Book is a stack of eleven ledgers holding the history of one thousand two hundred and forty screw jacks: their number, their pad, the slab they stand on, and every turn ever made on them since 1966. Bóel can tell you that Jack 402, under the northeast corner of the school, has been turned ninety-four times and is the fastest-rising point in the settlement; that the slab under the bale shed tilts west and has always tilted west and will need re-padding in about six years; that Jack 77 backs itself off half a turn every winter and has done since before she was born, and is therefore known as the Liar. Lying is a jack's moral failing here, and it is inherited language: *a liar's jack*, *true to the bubble*, *he's on a bad slab*, *we'll level it Thursday* (meaning: stop arguing, the problem is real but it is not today's).

In April, after the frost comes out, the works shuts down for four days, the furnace is let to cool at enormous cost, and the entire population goes out with staffs and levels and long bars and re-levels the town. Children hold the staff. A child who can hold a staff steady for a full shot, at seven, is spoken of. There is food, and too much drink on the third evening, and a good deal of matchmaking, and the phrase for marrying someone here is *we jacked together*, because a couple who intend to stay take a house and re-level it as a pair, and the Book records it — two initials in the turn column — and that entry, not any document, is what the town regards as the marriage.

And for those four days the works is silent, and there is only the floor humming and the fulmar cliff and the terns, and every single person here will tell you that it is beautiful, and about half of them will admit they can't sleep through it.

**The children.**

They are born into a noise and they take it into their nervous systems as the baseline of the world. They learn hall-hands before speech; a Nýgres infant will sign *wait* before it says a word. They have more ear infections than the national average and better balance than anyone. They play a game called tern, which involves crossing the open rock in June without a stick, and the scoring is by hits, and it is forbidden, and it has been played every June since 1969.

Every one of them has a jar. When a child is born, someone goes up to the hall and takes the first fibre off the wheels after the birth is telephoned through, and it goes in a screw-top jar with a paper label: name, date, shift, and the initials of whoever was spinning. It is a grey tuft. It looks like nothing. Everyone has one, on a shelf, all their lives, and they do not rot or fade or burn or change in any way at all, and when you visit an old woman here she will bring hers down and hold it out so you can see that it is exactly the same as it was in 1961 or 1984 or last Thursday.

Kári Nóason, who is eleven, has been counting the amphipods in the pool for three years with a jam jar, a ruler and a system of his own devising, and has produced a graph that Dagmar has pinned up in the clinic, and which shows a slow, unmistakable rise. Nobody knows what it means. It is exactly the kind of thing this town produces.

**The others who live here.**

The fox at the canteen door is the sixth in a line of foxes that the kitchen staff have been illegally feeding since 1972, and like all of them he is called Bergur. He is blue-morph, entirely unafraid, and sits on the step at 13:40 because that is when the fish comes out. The tern colony feeds him in June; the town feeds him the rest of the year; he has never in his life had to work very hard, and the wardens who come in summer to count nests write about him in their reports with visible disapproval.

The fulmars are the other residents. They are older here than most of the people, some of them — a fulmar lives fifty years — and they are building with the town's waste, ledge on ledge, quilted grey, and their nests will outlast the works, the houses, the jacks, the Book and everyone in it. The ornithologists count the layers like rings. In a hundred years the cliff will be a written record of the factory's output, in bird.

And in the pool, the amphipods, translucent and blind and in their thousands. They got here on their own, somehow. Nobody else did. Every person in Nýgres has drunk them, daily, since birth, and there is a local half-joke that they are the only true natives and everyone else is a guest of theirs.

**The dead.**

You cannot dig a grave in sixty-year-old lava. So the dead go two ways.

Most go over the rubble neck to the old coast, to the churchyard at Eyrarbakki-that-is-not-Eyrarbakki, in a van, on a road that takes eleven minutes, and are buried in ground that has had eight thousand years to learn its habits and is full of other people's grandmothers.

But since 1971 there has been the other thing, which is not official and which the priest has decided not to have an opinion about. A portion of the ash — a spoonful, no more — is carried into the furnace hall on a quiet shift and stirred into the melt, and goes out onto the wheels with everything else, and comes off as fibre, and is baled, and is loaded onto Steinn's boat on a Tuesday, and goes into the walls and roofs of buildings in countries these people have never seen. It is not a ceremony. Four or five people stand there. Nobody says anything, partly because nobody could be heard.

They will tell you about it, if you ask, in that too-loud over-articulated way, standing close, with two fingers on your forearm: that there is a woman from here in the roof of a school in Gdańsk. That there is a man from here keeping the heat in a hospital in Hull. That it doesn't rot and doesn't burn and doesn't blow away once it's tangled.

And then they will say the other thing, the one they say about everything — about the jacks, the harbour, the tide-post with its sixty-one brass marks climbing away from a sea that is quietly leaving:

*We are screwed down, not planted.*
