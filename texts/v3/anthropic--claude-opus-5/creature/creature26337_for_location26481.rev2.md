## The people of the nine strokes

They stand, at full growth, about as high as the top joint of your thumb. You would not see them on the first look, nor the second; you would see terraces and shell roofs and think the place a curiosity of geology. It is the third look that finds them, because on the third look something that was part of a wall is four inches further along it.

They are the colour of the flint when it is wet: grey with a blue in it, and a shine. The shine is not skin. It is a film that the skin makes and the fog renews, and it is the most important fact about their bodies. A person of the Cold Room who is dry for one hour is ill; for three, dying. They cannot sweat and do not need to. They cannot dry and cannot survive it. Every other thing about them follows from this — the damp, the depth, the windlessness, the whole architecture of a town built into the one place in the fen where the air never moves and never warms enough to take anything away.

They run at the temperature of the room. Two degrees, three, and in February something under that without much comment. A heart goes once in four seconds. The blood is dark and unhurried. They breathe perhaps twelve times in an hour, and each breath leaves them as a small white ghost that sinks, and this is why the lower streets are always faintly hazed, and why a household can be found in the dark by the drift of its sleeping.

They do not dart. Their walk is a slow continuous flow, heel and toe, no bob in it, and to watch one cross a bridge of woven root is to watch water finding its level. The feet are wide and flat and slightly cupped, and hold to wet chalk at any angle. They can go up a vertical face without particular thought and frequently do, since the stairs are for carrying and the walls are for going.

But they can sprint. Once. Somewhere in them is a reservoir of something that the cold has not slowed, and when the light at the gap changes in the particular way that means wren, that reservoir opens, and a street of forty people becomes an empty street between one of your blinks and the next. Then it is spent. For the rest of that day and most of the night they lie where they stopped, in doorways, on stairs, flat on the day-stone, breathing their slow ghosts, and no work is done, and nobody thinks less of anybody. There is a mark for this, scratched by a ledge near the west crevice, and the mark means *we ran, and now we are on the floor*, and it is a kind of joke, and it is two hundred and some years old.

## Eyes made for glare

There is too much light here, not too little. So the eyes are small, black, set deep under a heavy fold, and there is a grey inner lid that comes across at a thought and turns the world to dusk. A person of the Cold Room going up to the ledges near the gap does so half-blind by choice, lidded down, reading the ground by foot.

At night they see by the floor. The day-stone gives back its grey glow and they move easily in it, and this low light is the light they find restful; they will say — will *scratch*, once in a life, if they think it worth a mark — that the middle of the night is when the town looks like itself.

They have never made a picture. Not once, in seven towns, one on the roof of the last. This is not a poverty and it is not a prohibition. It is that nothing here has ever had a lit side and a dark side. A snail, a hand, a tower, a face: all described in full and equally, from every direction, with no edge where the light stops. The trick by which a shape becomes a mark on a surface was never discovered, because the world never demonstrated it. Their writing is strokes and loops and means what it means by agreement, and has no ancestor in any drawn thing.

Those few who have gone up and come back have all tried, in the end, to explain the same phenomenon, and have all failed, and their failures are on the north wall: that up there, every object is followed by a black copy of itself which lies down when the light is high and stretches enormous at the ends of the day, and that the copy is attached and cannot be outrun, and that nobody up there minds.

## Hands

The hands have five fingers and they are not equal.

The second finger is the writing finger. Its pad is broad, flattened by use, and slightly harder than the rest, and it is the finger they keep clean. With it they write on fog-film, in the running script, fast — a whole notice in the time it takes the drop to fall twice — and the writing stands bright on the flint until the beads grow heavy and carry it down. Prices. Where the springtails are drifting. That a household needs hands at the combs tonight. That someone is sitting at the pool now, if you have not been yet. That the thing you said yesterday was cruel. Prayers, which are always written on fog, on the high ledges where the wren might in theory see them, and which are prayers precisely because they go away.

The last finger is the scratching finger. Its nail is kept edged on flint, and it is used almost never. Fog is free; stone is not. A person may be granted, over a whole life, two marks on the north wall, sometimes three, sometimes none, and most die with theirs unspent, and this is the ordinary and respectable outcome. That is why the north wall, which is the memory of seven towns, is mostly empty. The empty part is not waiting to be filled. The empty part is the statement.

Somebody once spent a mark on a shrew.

## Names, and the impossibility of calling

They have no speech. The throat is there, the air is there; the practice never started, or started and was put down so long ago that no trace of it remains in the lowest course. Twice in a life a sound comes out of them anyway — at being born, and at the very end, and in both cases it is a thin single note that nobody has ever tried to write down.

For anything urgent, they touch. The pressure-alphabet is learned at the wrist and forearm before a child can stand; a mother speaks into her infant's arm all day, a slow tapping and pressing that is the whole world being introduced. Adults touch each other's forearms constantly and without much ceremony, the way other peoples murmur.

And a name is not a sound or a sign. A name is a thing you can put a hand on.

A child, in its first year, is given a permanent object: a fleck of white in a particular flint face; a certain curl of fern at a certain joint; the third feather of frost from the left on a particular north sill, which will be the third feather of frost from the left on that sill in two hundred years, because frost here does not grow or retreat, it only stands. To learn who someone is, you are led to their name and your hand is placed on it. To think of someone, you go and touch it.

The consequence is that nobody in the Cold Room has ever been called. There is no summons, no shout across the terraces, no voice saying come here. If you want a person you walk to them, however far, however slowly, and arriving is the whole of the request. A great deal of their courtesy is simply the walking.

The dead keep their names. The town is crowded with the names of the dead, all touchable, all where they were, and it is usual to stop on the way to somewhere and put a hand on one for a moment, and then go on.

## Hearth-stones

There is no fire. There has never been fire; the chimneys were built for air and the word for smoke is a borrowed thing, brought down by a goer-up and scratched once as a curiosity.

A hearth-stone is a piece of the day-stone, sawn and polished, that has been carried up to the bright ledges and left to soak, and it comes back glowing that grey useless glow and gives it up over a season. Each household holds a fixed number. The number does not change. The number of hearth-stones in the town is the number that the fog-combs will water, and the number of people in the town is the number of hearth-stones, and this equation is the constitution of the place; it has no other law worth the name.

So a birth waits on a death, and a death, here, is largely a matter of decision.

When a person judges they have finished — and they are very old by then, three or four times over your grandmother's grandmother — they take their stone, which has gone dark, and they go and sit in the north-west corner beside the pool, in the place where the drop falls. And then the town comes. It takes days, because there are ninety-one of them and none of them hurries, and they come one at a time and put their name-touch on the old one's forearm and then stand a while and then go. It is the only occasion on which the whole town moves in one direction. The dark stone is taken up to soak. Afterwards the body is weighted with flint and let down into the thumbprint pool, which is seven inches deep and black and has a floor of ancestors, and onto which, once every forty seconds, the clock falls.

Their hair is cut then, and only then, and goes into the fog-combs. The oldest nets in the town are more people than pony. Every mouthful of water in the Cold Room has come down through the hair of the dead, and this is not thought beautiful or terrible; it is thought sensible, and it is thought accurate.

## The law of standing in the way

Nothing is punished, because there is nothing to punish with. There is no striking — the sound alone would be unthinkable. There is no confinement; every house is open at the top. What there is, is obstruction.

If you are doing a thing the town will not have, somebody comes and stands in front of you. Not touching. Not writing. Standing, with the patience of a thing that has been here a long time and expects to be here longer. If you go around, they come around too, and are joined. There is no shouting because there is no shouting; there is only the slow accumulation of bodies in your path until the path is a wall of people who will not look at you and will not leave, and this is the heaviest event that can happen to a person here, and those who have had it done to them describe it, when they can bring themselves to scratch anything at all, as being *built into*.

The things that bring it on are few. Wasting water. Fouling a slate somebody is reading, by breathing on it. Hurrying without cause. And — the worst, the one that has happened perhaps four times in seven towns — beginning to build something new. Not repairing. Not turning a tile or re-cutting a stair-lip by a hand's width in a decade. *Adding*.

For the persistent there is exile, and exile is upward.

## Those who go up

Once in a generation, a few of the young are greased with snail-mucus until they shine like beetles, wrapped in oiled hair, plated with fish-scale at the shoulders, and sent out through the gap on a night of thick autumn fog, because fog is the only weather in which their skin can live up there. They go for what the Cold Room cannot make: mussel shell, pony hair from a wire fence, the scales, a particular flint that only outcrops in the third drain-bank east.

Most come back. The ones that do are strange for a year, and are handled gently, and are watched. They have seen the long horizontals of the drains, which have no end that a person can reach. They have been in wind, which takes hold of things that did not ask to be taken hold of, and which tore the film off their arms in strips while they lay flat in a rut and waited for it to be finished with them, and it was not finished with them; it simply went somewhere else, and would come back, and up there everyone knows this and builds anyway.

They have seen an unfinished place. That is the thing they try to scratch and cannot. There is one mark on the north wall, made very long ago, by someone who spent their whole life's allowance on it, and it is a single long horizontal, cut deep, running further than any other mark in the town, ending nowhere in particular.

## One of them

The first up, most mornings, is one of the old ones — old even here — whose name is a small blue-black fleck in the flint above the fourth cistern, and who sleeps badly, and who therefore reaches the great face by the pool while the sky in the gap is still the colour of an eyelid.

The night's fog has beaded and run and left the face clean and wet. She lids down out of habit, though there is no glare yet, and stands a moment with a hand flat on the stone to feel whether the film is thick enough to take a stroke. It is.

Nine strokes. Always the same nine, in the same order, with the same weights — heavy, heavy, light, heavy, light, light, heavy, light, heavy — one for each household, and the whole of it meaning *this many*, and meaning also the town's name, which has never needed to mean anything else. On the north wall, ten paces off and two centuries deep, the same nine are cut with a bar across them, which means *and the count is kept*, and which has not been altered by anybody's hand since before anybody's grandmother's grandmother was written into it.

She finishes. She does not stand back to look, because nobody here has ever stood back to look at anything; there is no angle from which the light is better. She puts her hand for a moment on the fleck above the fourth cistern, which is herself, and then on the fern-curl beside it, which is not anybody any more, and then she goes down to see whether the combs took anything in the night.

Behind her the nine strokes stand bright on the wet flint, and begin, at their own pace, to dry.
