## The third shift

They do not think of themselves as the people of the Palm. There is a sign — the flat hand held palm-down at the level of the collarbone, and the other hand passed beneath it, out and away — which means *the middle hours*, the empty window between the two tenancies, when the day birds are twenty kilometres out over the rim and the night birds are packed asleep in the high crust, and the throat of a cane belongs, briefly, to nobody. That sign is also what they call themselves. They are the ones who go in when it is empty. They are the third shift, the smallest and latest of the three, and they hold their tenancy on the same terms as the others: by showing up, on time, forever.

There are not many of them. Perhaps four thousand on the whole plateau, in something like sixty settlements, and every one of those four thousand is known by sign and by lineage to most of the rest, because the drums carry and the plateau is one long conversation with nothing in it to absorb sound but grass.

## Hands

Look at the hands first. Everything else about them is a consequence.

The hands are wide, the palms thickened at the heel from a lifetime of hardwood pegs, the fingers surprisingly unmarked — protected, oiled with grazer fat, kept out of the fire, kept out of the way of the quarry hammer. A cut on a hand is treated with an urgency that looks, to a visitor, hysterical. A hand injury is not an injury. It is an impairment of speech.

There are people here who lost two fingers young and who speak with what is described as a thick tongue: their signs are legible but blunt, they cannot make the fast small distinctions that live in the little finger's angle, and they are listened to patiently and interrupted often. And there is at least one woman, well over sixty, who lost three fingers to a rope-burn at eleven and who is famous across half the plateau for eloquence — she rebuilt the whole language in the space she had left, invented a compressed dialect of the wrist, and now young people copy her, badly, the way young people everywhere copy an accent that sounds like intelligence.

The language is made low and close against the body, at the sternum and belly, not out in the air. It is a language for two people sitting hip to hip on a sleeping shelf in a dim tube, for the deck of a fallen cane at dusk, for the halo at noon where the light is flat and pitiless. Held up high and wide, out at arm's length, the same signs become shouting: that is how you argue, and how you address a crowd, and how you speak to someone across the width of a halo, and there are old people who have not raised their hands above the shoulder in forty years and are respected for it.

Names are places on the body. A person's name is a spot you touch on yourself — a temple, a wrist, the notch of the throat, the outer edge of the left knee — usually assigned in the hearing years for a reason nobody bothers to remember, so that by adulthood a name means only the person. To speak of someone dead, you touch their place and then open the hand and let it fall away from the body. It takes half a second. It is done constantly, in the middle of other sentences, the way other peoples say *my late father* — a small unclosing hand, and the conversation goes on.

## The hearing years

They are all born hearing, and they all lose it, and the losing is the shape of a life.

Childhood on the Palm is loud and it is also an office. Children can hear, so children are the ears of the household. A six-year-old is posted at the mouth of the settlement in the last hour of light to feel for the drums; a nine-year-old is sent to sit in the halo and listen for the particular thin scream of a grazer taken by a snake, and to run and point. Children hear the difference between a healthy cane and one whose rhizome is failing, which is a difference in the hum, and adults will walk a child forty minutes across the mat to put a hand on a wall and be asked, *is it still the same note?*

So the young have authority, and it is real, and it expires. Somewhere between twenty and thirty the world begins to thin. It goes in a known order — first the sift's high shriek, then the drums' overtones, then speech, then last of all the hum, which is felt as much as heard and which stays longest, so that for a decade or two a person lives in a world of one note and no voices. The handover is not marked with much ceremony. What happens is that people stop asking you things. You notice, over a season or two, that the questions have moved to your children, and you begin the long second half of your life, which is the half where you are looked at rather than listened to.

They do not consider this a tragedy, or not exactly. Their word for a deaf adult is also their word for a finished thing, a completed thing — the same sign used of the rock, and of the plateau, and of a well-made rope: *arrived at what it will be*. To be deaf is to have come into the condition the land is already in.

## Feet, and the reading of seams

They walk with a rolling, testing gait, weight settling slowly, because the mat takes the foot and keeps it. The feet are broad and splayed and hard as horn at the edges, softer under the arch than a lowlander's, because nothing here is stone underfoot except a hearth plate and a doorsill.

And because the mat keeps a footprint for years, nobody on the Palm has ever had privacy of movement. Where you went yesterday is written. Where your grandmother went is written, fainter. The pale threadbare seams between cane and cane are the accumulated agreement of three centuries about where a person may walk, and to walk beside a seam rather than on it is mildly rude, and to strike out across virgin mat in a straight line toward another family's cane is a legal act with consequences — you have written something on the world that will still be legible when you are crust.

Peg-spirals are the deeds. A family's claim on a living cane is the maintained line of hardwood pegs going up it in a slow helix, and the claim is renewed by the labour of renewal: new pegs every season, the old ones drawn and burned. A spiral left unmaintained for three fog-years can be taken up by whoever will keep it, and this is the substance of nearly all litigation on the Palm. Cases are heard in the open, drummed. Two drummers, on two canes, four kilometres apart, beating the positions of the two parties while the parties themselves sign to the assembled halo; and every settlement within reach listens in, and afterward the verdict travels column to column at the speed of a good rhythm and is generally obeyed, because there is nowhere on the plateau to be that is out of earshot of the plateau.

## The house that is a corpse, and the fire that is a family

They live in dead canes. The house is the body of the thing that watered their ancestors, lying on its side, silvering.

And the walls of the house are nest-block, and the fire on the rock plate is nest-block, and the crust that nest-block is quarried from is where they put their dead. In the middle hours, with wax and moss in their ears, they carry a body into the throat of a living cane and set it on the ledge at the waterline, and they come out before the exchange, and the sift do the rest. Within four or five generations an ancestor is stratum. Within eight she is fuel.

So a household's hearth is warm because of its own people, and the wall at your back has your grandmother's grandmother in it somewhere, ground fine, indistinguishable. This is not experienced as horror. It is experienced as thrift and as continuity, and it is the reason the flat rock hearth plate is the one object a family carries when it moves, and the reason fire on the open mat is the unforgivable thing — not only because the mat would burn for a month, but because a fire that is not on the plate is a fire that is not being watched by anybody, and their fires are made of relatives and are not to be left alone.

They cough. Quarrying crust makes a dust of dried guano and beetle shell, and the quarriers get a dry rattle by forty; between the deafness and the cough, a Palm elder is a person sitting very still in a dim tube, breathing carefully, watching hands.

## The marked

The ones who were caught inside at exchange hour sit at the outer edge of the halo, facing out, and do not work.

What happened to them is not simple deafness. Something in the attention was taken. Their eyes will not settle on a moving hand — they slide off, they drift to the horizon, they come back too late — which in this world is a kind of muteness and a kind of blindness at once. You cannot really talk to them. They are fed; nobody resents feeding them; there are usually one or two in a large settlement and they are counted in the household and given a shelf.

Children are sent to sit with them. This is the only circumstance in which two people on the Palm hold a conversation out loud: a nine-year-old who can still hear, and a thirty-year-old with the horizon in her eyes, sitting side by side in the tall moth-grass making noises at each other that nobody else present can perceive. The families permit it and do not ask about it. There is a general and unexamined belief that the marked have been let a little way into the cane's own business, and that the young, who still hear the hum properly, are the right ones to go and check on them.

## Aloud

Speech, otherwise, is for those who cannot see hands.

Infants, who are sung to and murmured at constantly for four or five years and then gradually stop being spoken to, so that everyone's earliest memory is of a voice and no one has heard a voice since.

The dead, who are addressed aloud at the waterline ledge, briefly, by one person, in the middle hours, and then never again except by the unclosing hand.

And the sift. People do speak aloud to the sift — a low continuous mutter while working in the throat, apologetic, conversational, explaining what is being taken and why. Nobody claims the birds understand. It is described, when described at all, as the courtesy you show a landlord whose house you are standing in.

## The ones who go down

Copper comes up from the forest, and so somebody has to go down, three vertical kilometres, into heat and rot and noise of an entirely different kind, and come back. It is work for the young and it is done in parties of six, and it takes a season, and the people who do it are the only ones on the Palm who have seen a river go somewhere.

They come back with copper needles and fish hooks and awls, and with their hearing intact for an extra decade, having spent their loudest years away from the columns. This makes them useful and slightly untrustworthy. They can still hear speech when their own generation cannot, and there is a persistent suspicion, never quite spoken, that they hold conversations behind everyone's back. They also come back with a habit the plateau finds obscene: down there, they have learned to talk while walking, faces forward, mouths going. On the Palm you stop walking to speak, and you turn, and you look at the other person's hands, and to withhold that turning is the local form of contempt.

A copper needle is a dowry. It is worn at the throat. It is buried with nobody — it goes back into the household, and the household's needles are counted at every marriage, and a family with four needles is a family with a long memory of sending its children down the cliffs.

## The Hollow

Twice a year the towers empty and the plateau rings like a struck bowl in a register no one can hear, and the people who cannot hear anything at all are the ones who feel it worst.

Nobody works. Nobody goes into a throat. The drums are not beaten, because a drum in the Hollow sounds wrong to the children and the children cry. Households sit in their tubes with the fire low and sign very little and sleep badly, and the ten days are got through.

And the old ones walk out to the midpoint between two canes, where there is nothing at all, and sit down in the open on the mat with their backs to nothing, facing nothing, in the warm rotten wind from the forest below, and stay.

Ask what it is for and you will be told, with the flat hand and the hand passed beneath it: *the middle*. That is the whole doctrine, as far as it can be rendered. They have spent their lives entering the empty hour of an occupied place, and now the whole place is the empty hour, and they go and occupy it, which is the only work left that they are still qualified to do. Most come back in on the ninth or tenth day, walking slowly, needing help over the doorsill. Some, by arrangement, are carried in the other direction afterward, to the waterline ledge, and put into the crust, and become in due course a wall and a fire.

Then the ribbon comes back over the rim — the children feel it first and stand up — and the noise resumes, and everyone can rest.

## And the other tenants

The falcons are the only animals here the people consider to be persons, and this is on the grounds of silence. Three or four of them work the braid at every exchange, taking sift on the wing without a sound, and they are not hunted, not sung about, not represented; they are simply the neighbours whose manner is understood.

The sift and the bats are weather. Enormous, indispensable, morally neutral, on a schedule.

And under all of it, under four metres of its own compressed dead, running along naked gneiss for a hundred kilometres and putting up spires at intervals it settled on before anyone was counting, there is the actual resident of this plateau: one plant, one organism, holed and humming, keeping a cold black pool at the bottom of each of its throats.

The people know this. They have no illusion that it knows them. The most that is claimed, and it is claimed rarely and only obliquely, in the small low signs used close against the body, is that the crust in the wall came out of the cane, and the fire came out of the cane, and the water came out of the cane, and everyone they have ever loved has gone back into it — and that the beetles in the crust probably think about the crust in much the same way, and are also not wrong.
