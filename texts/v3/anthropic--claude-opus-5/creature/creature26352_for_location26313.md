## Who Lives Here

Everything.

Just not in the middle.

That is the whole census, if you want it in a line: in the Lower Volumes, every living thing is a coast-dweller, and this includes the people, and the people did not decide it. They came down with drawings and rosters and a plan to occupy the water, and within two years they had folded themselves onto the walls alongside the mussels and the eels, at a consistent height, in a thin bright band, because that is where things can hold on.

From a launch crossing Cell 40 at night — lamp-dim, twenty-two hundred on the twenty-six hour clock — you can see it. The gallery lights run along the wall face in one unbroken line, all at the same elevation, no window higher or lower than any other by more than a metre, receding until perspective closes them into a single thread. Above: the gull ledges. Below, four metres down: the green stripe. Between them, humanity, a tidemark of its own.

---

**The body it makes.** You will know them anywhere, including topside, including in a photograph.

Start with the mouth. Everyone has the split — a vertical fissure in the lower lip that opens, scabs, and opens again, because the air is saturated with alkaline fog and there is no season in which it isn't. They grease constantly. Tins of lanolin-and-paraffin move through the galleries as a second currency, and a man will lend you his tools before his tin. A whole mouth, unmarked, pink at the edges, reads down here as *visitor*, and is treated with the specific gentleness reserved for the temporarily useful.

Then the hands. Every arris in the Lower Volumes is a sharp ninety degrees and none of it has been worn round yet, so gloves last a fortnight and the standard bonus is paid in them: sealed packs, a dozen pair, stacked under a bunk like bullion. Their knuckles are scarred in a pattern so consistent you could date the injuries — outer third of the third and fourth, from ladder stiles and cable-tray lips. The fingernails go white and chalky. Fingerprints wear away and the readers at the bulkheads fail, which is a running joke and a small crisis about once a year.

Then the hearing, which goes early and unevenly. They talk too loudly and too flatly and they sign while they talk, an ugly practical creole drawn from dive signals, crane signals and the barge dialect, and by the time a crew has worked three seasons together the voices are mostly decorative. Topside they are thought rude. They are not rude; they are shouting across the memory of a pump.

And then, if you spend long enough with them, the drone. Cell 40 rings near a very low A and never stops ringing, and the people in it hum. Not deliberately, mostly not consciously: a sub-vocal note under the breath while working, tuned to the room they live inside. Cell 12 hums flatter. Cell 31 is a semitone up and its people are mocked for it. Put two cells' crews in one mess hall and the singing will not resolve; put a hundred of the same cell's crews together and they produce a sound that visitors describe, without exception, as unnerving. A woman off the sounding boats told me she can tell within four notes which sea a stranger has been living in, and I believe her, and so does everyone else.

---

**The joint-dwellers.** The eels have one to a construction joint, territorial, and the joints run dead straight for hundreds of kilometres, so an eel's entire cosmos is a horizontal crack and two neighbours.

The crews know this about the eels. They know it about themselves.

The galleries are divided into bays, bay to a crew, and the bays run in a line, and your world is your bay and the two doors on either side of you. Feuds are lateral and inescapable. So are marriages. The word for the people next door is *joint-mates*, said with affection, exhaustion, or venom depending on the year, and the standard blessing on a new posting is *good joints* — which sounds like a joke about knees and isn't.

What they eat: silver, endlessly. The little schooling fish put in by the trillion as a trophic floor turned out to press into a dense grey loaf that keeps for a month and tastes of not much, and the whole underworld runs on it. Mussels off the wall bands, huge and chalky, faintly sweet, unmistakably of lime. Gull eggs, prohibited, universal. The lampfish, which the string-hands take because a fish that lives its whole life orbiting a lamp fixture is a fish whose provenance you can see. And never, under any circumstances, the cress — hectares of pale hairy matting pulling cadmium and nickel out of the water, harvested by machine, logged, shipped, and eaten by nobody in the Volumes, because everyone here knows exactly what it has been drinking. *Cress-eater* is the insult you use on someone from an office.

---

**Counting.** Three knocks in a shift is normal; six means a difficult conversation. Everyone counts. Everyone has counted since their first week and cannot stop, and the count is the closest thing the Lower Volumes has to prayer.

They also sleep by it. New arrivals cannot sleep for the noise; within a month they cannot sleep without it. What wakes a gallery at four in the morning, every head up at once, is *silence* — the pumps off, the low A dying out of the concrete, the terrible unfamiliar quiet of a sea with no reason to exist. Crews describe the sensation identically and with obvious embarrassment: the feeling of a large animal beside you having stopped breathing.

And they answer knocks. This is the one thing they all do and the only thing that has hardened, in four years, into something like a moral law. If you hear a knock you knock back twice, on whatever is nearest, with whatever is in your hand. Because sometimes it is water hammer three cells away, and sometimes it is a woman on the wrong side of a bulkhead at ninety metres in a flooded chamber that was dry last season, and two knocks returned is the entire difference between working and drowning. The consequence is that if you stand still anywhere in the inhabited band and strike a rail twice, you will get an answer, faintly, from somewhere down the line, from someone you will never meet, who has already gone back to what they were doing.

The other law is position. You never, ever misreport where you are. Red nineteen, amber four, and you had better be within a kilometre of it, because a false position kills whoever comes to look. Their worst profanities are all coordinates. To say to a man's face the number of a place he is not at is a beating.

---

**The barge people.** The dosing barges crawl the cells in mowing patterns, four months a pass, trailing acid and buffered brine, and everything alive down here is alive because one of them went by. They know it. They are insufferable about it and they have earned it.

They live afloat, families and all, flat-bottomed and slow, and their circuit never brings them back to the same wall in the same season, so they are the only people in the Volumes who do not live on a coast. Their skin is the worst in the world. Their children are quiet and preternaturally good at knots and cannot read a gallery-town's social geography at all, because they have never had two neighbours. The buffer logs — the pH ledgers, a barge's real cargo — are handwritten, hoarded, willed to daughters, and quietly consulted by engineers who would deny it.

---

**The middle.** And there are the ones who go out into the nothing: survey pilots, sounding crews, the string-inspection boats. Two and a half days to cross Cell 40, nothing to steer by, red on one axis and amber on the other and void in between.

They come back strange in a mild, well-documented way. They over-report their positions — every twenty minutes instead of every hour, talking to a controller who has stopped answering, saying the numbers aloud to keep hold of them. They develop a hunger for the grid and a horror of fog: to be fog-blind in the mid-volume, with the strings gone and the water eight degrees and the surface white with soffit rain, is the standard nightmare of the entire Lower Volumes, described in identical terms by people who have never met.

And it is out there, in the mid-volume, that the dead are.

Regulation is explicit: remains go up the shaft, signed for at every bulkhead, to be dealt with topside among the dry and the elderly. The forms exist and are filed and are, in perhaps half of cases, a courteous lie. Because there is no ground here. There is no ground anywhere in the Lower Volumes, only invert, six metres of poured stone that will never open for anyone, and a people who cannot bury cannot be told what to do with their dead for very long. So they take them out. Past red nineteen, into the flat glassy nothing at the centre of the cell, and weight them with a spent sacrificial anode — a half-eaten block of zinc, pitted, sacrificial, the only object in this whole made world that has already done its dying on someone else's behalf — and let them go into two hundred metres of pale green light.

Which means the survey pilot who crosses a day of absolute emptiness and passes one mooring buoy is not, in fact, crossing emptiness. He is crossing the only cemetery, and the void has content after all, and he reports his position out loud every twenty minutes into a radio nobody is answering, and he is not entirely talking to himself.

---

**The uninvited.** Nobody seeded the gulls; they came down the intake shafts with the ventilation and now hold every gantry ledge on every lift house, screaming, whitening the concrete, robbing the mess, raising chicks that will live and die and never once see the sky. They ride the warm-water thermals up towards the soffit and turn short of it.

Nobody seeded the midges, either, whose larvae graze the green band and which now hatch in blooms so thick they clog the intake filters and blacken the lamp strings — the first unscheduled season in the Lower Volumes, a weather event nobody planned, arriving four days late relative to last year, being watched with delight by the biologists and cold hatred by everyone with a filter to clean. Nor the rats in the cable trays. Nor the moss on the mess-room drip. Nor the unlisted shrimp of Cell 40, which is on no manifest, which came in on somebody's hull, and which has quietly become the second most abundant animal in a province-sized sea, and about which the standing joke is that it will outlive the paperwork proving it shouldn't be there.

There are three dogs in Volume Four and each one is a disciplinary file.

---

**The natives.** Nothing here is old. The oldest structure has rust-tears down to the skirting, which is eight years; the oldest resident came down at the same time and is thirty-four and is called Grandmother without irony by people who do not find this funny.

But there are children now who were born in the band, and they are not immigrants, and it shows.
