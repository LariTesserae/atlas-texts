## The Ring

Start with the ring, because everything alive here is standing in one.

A hand's width of soaked earth around the base of a pedestal, renewed every morning in eleven minutes, drying from the outside in by about noon. That is the whole habitat. Everything else on the plateau — the benches, the domes, the glare, the enormous composure of it — is the space between rings, and is crossed rather than lived in.

Inside the ring, first, is the grass. A collar of blue-green bunchgrass, stiff as wire, its roots making a dense felted plug in the pedestal that is very probably the reason the pedestal survives at all. Each collar is a single plant. It cannot spread; there is nowhere to spread to. Eleven paces of dry conglomerate is an ocean to a grass. So each pin has one grass, clonal, dividing and re-rooting into itself, and some of those individuals were alive before the stock wall was stacked and will be alive after it has sunk to a line of scattered cobbles. The oldest living thing on the Standing Field is not the fig in the trench. It is a grass you could cover with a hat.

Beneath the grass, in the wet hour, is the *ommo*.

It is the length of a finger-joint, pale, soft, segmented, blind, and it is above ground for eleven minutes a day. It comes up through the crumb of the pedestal as the droplets start to rise, grazes the film of green slime that the constant wetting maintains on the north face of every pin, mates if it is going to, lays if it is going to, and goes back down before the light goes vertical. Everything about the animal is folded into that window. Its courtship is four seconds long. It has no dormant season and no season at all; it simply has a day that is eleven minutes long, repeated, and by that arithmetic a long-lived *ommo* has been awake for perhaps nine days in its life.

The beetles know. They are up before it, waiting in the grass with their elytra beaded, black and unhurried; they are the only creatures on the plateau that are ever in a hurry, and they are in a hurry for eleven minutes. When the field goes to sparks from horizon to horizon and the visitors are standing with their mouths open, the ground at their feet is having its entire year.

Also in the ring: the rosette plant, fleshy, flat, blue-rimmed, with a bulb at its base holding about a cup. It takes the drink and keeps it. It is the only reservoir on the plateau that is not the sky or a pin, and this makes it food, canteen, poultice, and property. The herders will not take one from a ring whose grass is thin. Nobody enforces that. It is not the sort of place where you would need to.

## The Walkers

The grazers are called nothing, because there is nothing else they could be confused with.

Stone-colored, knee-high, padded-footed, with ears that do all the work of a language. Their teeth are ruined by the age of eight or nine — everything they eat has caprock grit in it — and when the molars go the animal simply falls out of the rotation and is eaten by something. They do not seem to regard this as a tragedy and neither does anyone else.

The rotation is the remarkable thing. A collar grazed hard recovers in eleven days. The herds know this. They work a row, move, and come back on the eleventh morning, and they have been doing it long enough that the grass and the herd have arrived at a treaty neither party can now break without dying. Their trails are perfectly straight — a grazer walks the row, not the country — and from a dome the plateau shows as a ruled net of pale lines, thousands of them, meeting at right angles under the pins, wearing the one pattern the erosion cannot sort out.

Domestic herds, where there are any, keep the same rotation. They have to. The herders did not impose it and could not; they inherited it from the animals, along with the silence, along with the ears.

## The Flickering

The birds gave up their voices a long time ago and got the sky in exchange.

At this altitude, in this air, a bird two kilometers off is not a speck, it is a *bird* — you can see the moult gap in its primaries. So the whole upper register of bird life, all the shrieking and piping and territorial nonsense that a wood does, has been moved into light. Wing-patches flare white and vanish. Tail-fans open a quarter and shut. A pair of coursers arguing over a bench conduct the entire matter from three hundred meters apart, in perhaps forty flashes, and an experienced herder can follow it the way you would follow raised voices in the next street.

Their courtship is at the drink, and it is the only excessive thing on the plateau. The male climbs until he is a mote, hangs, and then falls in a shuttered stoop through the rising sparks, on-off-on, strobing, and the light is horizontal so that each flash throws a two-second-late shadow across the bench below him, and what the female is actually watching, if she is watching anything, is a stutter of darkness arriving on the ground after the bird that made it has already pulled out.

Chicks cheep. It does not last. It is the loudest sound the field ever makes and it is always brief, one way or the other.

## The One Who Uses the Lag

There is a cat on the western rim who has been sleeping on the roof of the white structure, on and off, for eleven or twelve years. She is the size of a large dog with half again the leg, the exact color of unweathered caprock, and she is not the only one, but she is the one everybody means.

Everything here has learned to disbelieve shadows. A shadow on this plateau is a statement about two seconds ago, and two seconds is the distance between a grazer and its liver, so the herds have been selected, hard and for a long time, to ignore the ground and watch the thing itself. This is a correct adaptation and she lives inside it.

She works the gap. She stalks so that her shadow does what shadows do — arrives, meaning nothing, dismissed — and then she is not where the shadow said and not where she was, and the ears come up, and by the time the white rump-flash goes around the herd she has already made her choice and closed. She has no interest in being quiet. She has never needed to be quiet in her life. She hunts with her body in one place and her evidence in another, and the herders, who have watched her do it since she was a kitten on that roof, do not think of this as cleverness. They think of it as the local form of being a cat.

## The Small Governors

The bee nests in the hollow of old pedestals, one colony to a pin, and navigates by the grid.

It does not steer by the sun, which is unreliable in the wet season, or by polarized light, which the lens does something unhelpful to. It counts. The dance in the dark of the pedestal is not an angle and a distance; it is two numbers — so many pins along, so many across — and any bee in the colony can resolve that to a collar of flowering rosettes without ever having been there. They are the only creatures on the plateau that speak in absolute coordinates, and the herders, who also do, follow them when they want to know where the flowering is.

At dusk there are moths in a density that would be a roar anywhere else and here is a soft continuous pressure on the face and hands, like being leaned on by weather. There are no bats. There cannot be; the suspension in the air eats exactly the frequencies a bat would need, and any animal that tried to find its dinner by shouting at it would starve in a week. Instead there is a wide-mouthed, long-winged, entirely silent nightjar sort of bird that does not aim at anything. It opens and flies. In that soup, aiming would be a waste of effort.

And down in the trench-river, in the string of green pools, there is a fig growing out of the same crack it has always grown out of, and inside its fruit there is a wasp that has never existed anywhere but in that tree. Every wasp of that kind that has ever lived was born in it. Their entire species is one organism's tenant, and has been since before the doorway was carried up here, and neither of them knows.

## The Herders

There are perhaps four hundred people on the whole plateau, and on most days each of them can see none of the others.

They are big in the chest and small everywhere else, dark-skinned, and they go covered — not against the sun overhead, which a hat handles, but against the glare that comes back up off the white caprock, which does not care about hats. The characteristic injury of this country is a burn under the chin and along the underside of the nose. There is a word for it. There is a salve for it, out of the rosette bulb, and the older herders wear soot in a band across the cheekbones and a slotted visor of plaited grass, and most of them are half blind by sixty and all of them are entirely blind to the far end of the blue.

They speak low. The air strips the top off everything, so the language has gone down and round over the generations — long vowels, m and n and ng and a swallowed l, almost nothing that hisses. Two people conversing stand shoulder to shoulder, facing the same way, which is why the herders of the Standing Field look, to a stranger, like people who never look at one another. At distance they use the face: the mouth shapes are exaggerated to the point of grimace, framed by a raised hand, and readable at four hundred meters in this air with practice. For anything urgent there is the patch — a hand-sized square of bleached hide sewn to the back of the coat and flipped up on a thong, flash and gone, flash and gone. They took it off the grazers. They know they took it off the grazers. The gesture for *I am here and unhurt* is the same one a herd uses for *nothing is wrong*, and no one on this plateau finds that odd.

And they sing, which is the only wholly unreasonable thing they do. The ground-voice: very low, very slow, pitched to survive the crossing, so slow that a single verse can take the length of a bench, so low that a listener a hundred paces off feels it in the sternum before recognizing it as a person. Their word for it is a word that also means *waste*. It is a thing you spend. You sing to hold a herd together in the milk-light, you sing at a birth, and you sing the lists — because the lists have to live somewhere.

Here is the shape of a life, in the only coordinates available. A child is born under a pin, and the pin is counted, and the count is her name for the rest of time: eleven-and-four east, nine north, of the stone door. Strangers meeting on a bench do not exchange names, they exchange pins, and from that each knows the other's family, rotation, water, grandmother, and probable grudges. Nothing here can be surveyed — the ground goes down, the cairns settle, the wall sinks a millimeter a lifetime — but the grid has never once been found to misalign, and so this is the only country in the world where property is exact and unarguable and there is no law about it whatsoever. All the law is about timing. Whose herd enters which rows on which morning; who owes water; who moved onto the eleventh day early. That is what they fight about, and they fight about it in a murmur, standing side by side, looking at the ground.

They will not pull a pin. There is a square in the east — thirty rows by thirty — where somebody once did, a long time ago, for some purpose nobody has preserved, and the pedestals there collapsed and the collars died and nothing has come back, because grass here travels only in the gut of a grazer and a grazer will not walk a row with no water in it. It is a flat, clean, pale patch, perfectly rectangular, visible from two domes away. They call it by a name that is not a swearword but is used as one. A herder who finds a dead collar anywhere on the plateau carries dung to it. Nobody teaches this. Everybody does it.

The roofed hut is always occupied and never for long. Under a lattice of thousands of pins the air stays four degrees under the air, condensation runs day and night, and the cut stone basin never empties: it is the only water on the Standing Field that does not keep a schedule. By custom the keeper is somebody who has lost their herd. It is at once the plateau's charity and its exile, and it is also its archive, because the keeper is the one person here with an address — everyone else being a slowly moving pair of numbers — and so the keeper holds the lists. Which child under which pin. Which year the trench ran. Which families are the same family. The whole memory of the place is sung, very low, in a cold shed, by a bankrupt.

Weddings happen at the doorway on the dome, because it is the only place on the plateau where *inside* exists without a roof, and you have to go somewhere.

## The Dead

You cannot dig the caprock and there is nothing to burn.

So they carry them west, out past the last bench, through the place where the air thins and turns suddenly cold and the seam is directly overhead and then behind you, and they sit them up facing back in under the lens, and leave.

Out there nothing rots. The cold and the dryness take the water out of a body in a season and leave it, and the ground goes down around it the way the ground goes down around a pin, and after long enough a dead herder is standing on a pedestal of his own making, a column of protected soil, with the wind having had its way with the rest. The oldest are far out on the flats where nobody goes, hollow, leaning, still facing in. They are the only other things on the plateau that do not erode, and they make, out beyond the rim, a second and much sparser grid, whose spacing is not eleven paces but one generation.

Passing them, you greet them. It is the ordinary greeting, mouthed, with the hand beside the face, and it is done because they can see you perfectly well — in this air, at that distance, anything with eyes could.

## The Tenants Before

Somebody cut that doorway out of a stone that does not occur within three days' walk and stood it on a dome to frame nothing, and the ground inside it has been greener by a small consistent amount ever since, and the herders have no theory about this and want none.

Somebody stacked the wall and paid for it in calendar: inside the enclosure the pedestals are trodden flat and the grid is a ghost, which makes it the easiest place on the plateau to water a herd in a storm and the only place where fifty thousand years have been rubbed out by hooves. Every family that shelters there knows exactly what it is spending. They do it anyway, and they do not add stones to the wall, because the wall is itself a clock — caprock cobbles sinking at the caprock's rate while the pins inside stand still — and adding to it would spoil the reading.

And somebody, long before any of that, laid the pins in a grid eleven paces on a side across three days of country and hung a lens over the whole of it and went away.

## Not Alive, Probably

The faint milk in the air before dawn. The suspension that eats the high frequencies and has no taste and cannot be filtered out of a jar of water. The seam at the zenith, the shade of green on one side.

Nothing lives in the white structure on the western rim. It is the only place on the Standing Field of which that can be said — the collars have their grass, the pedestals have their bees, the trench has its fig, the flats have their standing dead — and the herders notice this, and mention it, in the way you would mention an empty house in a full street.

Sand has banked against its north side. Grass has taken the bank. And on the roof there is a patch polished smooth by twelve years of a large cat lying down, which is the only maintenance the thing has ever received, and is probably the only maintenance it will ever get.

## Why They Don't Look Up

Because everything a person needs is on the ground.

The damp is on the ground, and the damp is the day. The shadows are on the ground, and the shadows are two seconds old, which is the only past this country keeps. The pedestals are on the ground, rising around the ankle as you walk east, and they are the calendar, the deed, the census, and the grave.

The sky has the sparks and the seam and the enormous unusable blue, and the sky is on a schedule that was set before anybody's grandmother, and will not be affected by attention.

So they walk the benches in the horizontal light with the whole field going upward in silence around them, singing very low, watching their feet — and they are not indifferent to it. They are simply people who live in a machine that finished running long ago, and have come to terms with it the same way the erosion has: by treating it as weather, and getting on with the animals.
