## Count

There are people here. A hundred and six at the last census anyone got far enough through to be worth writing down, which was eleven years ago, and which was not finished either — the western terraces were left out because the man doing the counting reached them in the wrong season and turned back.

The city was built for something in the range of six thousand. You can tell from the gutter capacity.

So the density works out to roughly one person per six hundred thousand square metres, and the practical meaning of that number is this: you can walk nine hours through an organised, lamplit, functioning settlement and meet nobody, and nothing is wrong. Nothing has happened. That is simply the population.

## Bodies

Nobody lives on the floor. Forty-two degrees and saturated means sweat does no work; you are a sealed vessel, and the only thing you can do about it is leave. Two hours at rest, less if you carry anything. The floor is a place of business.

They go down in cold jackets: a bladder of Cold Face water worn against the spine, tubes stitched through a quilted vest, a hand pump at the hip. A jacket is forty minutes. Distances are given in jackets, not metres. *Two jackets down and one back* describes a job, a risk, and a morning. If your jacket empties on the floor you have perhaps six minutes of usefulness left and after that you are cargo, and everybody on the terrace above knows what it costs to carry cargo up a catwalk in that heat, and everybody knows the number of people it takes, and everybody keeps count of their own water.

They are therefore not a heat-adapted people. They are an *equipment*-adapted people, which is different and less flattering.

What the basin does to them over time is consistent. They are tall and they are thin, not from hunger but from the total absence of any reason to be otherwise. They cross thirty degrees of temperature twice a day and their circulation has the whipsawed look of divers': they flush in blotches, they blanch at the fingers, they go grey around the mouth when they climb. Below the second terrace nobody is ever dry, so the skin of the trunk and thighs is permanently softened, white at the creases, and tears like wet paper — every adult has the same tissue-thin scarring in the same places. Their hands go hard at the knuckles by thirty-five, genuinely hard, a fine deposit in the joint capsule, so that the last ten degrees of grip are simply gone. They have almost no teeth by forty; the condensate is sour and they drink fourteen litres of it a day. And most of them get the white cough somewhere in the fifth decade, which is silica in the lung, and which nobody treats, and which takes about four years.

They know all of this in advance. It is not a tragedy here. It is closer to a timetable.

## Salt

The water is distilled. It carries nothing, which is the whole point of it, and it takes everything out of you at fourteen litres a day.

So salt is the currency, and the greeting, and the only thing anyone actually wants. A hundred kilos of sawn pipe-rind goes over the rim twice a year and assays at gold, silver, a little antimony — and what comes back up the track is salt, grain, oil, glass, and wire, and of those the salt is what people count.

When you come to a stranger on a catwalk you hold out a pinch of it in the palm, and he licks it off your hand, and then you can talk. This is not ceremonial. Both of you need it.

## Lengths

Nobody owns a house. There are no houses, only hung screens and a plate floor and somewhere to sleep out of the drip. What people own is a *length*: a stretch of pipe, header, gutter, and screen for which they are responsible, and which they walk daily, and which is their address, their work, and their name.

She holds eleven hundred metres of the east header. He has the fourth terrace outfall and everything that feeds it. A length is inherited, or granted, or broken up among neighbours when its holder dies and there is nobody to take it whole, which is what usually happens.

Names come from the pumps. Every knocker has a voice and every person is known by the one nearest them, so names in the basin are sounds rather than words — a two-beat, a slow single, the one on the third terrace that thumps and then shudders. Two people on the same pump are Upper and Lower. When a knocker silts and stops, the person named for it is temporarily nameless, and there is a joke about this that is nine hundred years old and still gets used.

## Speech

They talk by knocking on the pipes. A message from the Cold Face to the south terraces takes four to six hours, depending on who is awake to pass it, and gets there intact because of how they build sentences: every message carries its own context, states its own origin, answers the question it anticipates, and ends. There are no questions in the knock-code. A question would require someone to be listening twice.

This has gone into the mouth as well. Face to face they speak in whole blocks, sixty or ninety seconds at a time, complete and self-sufficient, and then they stop, and there is a long gap, and then the other one starts. They do not interrupt. They do not nod along. To an outsider it reads as either enormous courtesy or total indifference and it is genuinely difficult to tell which, and the honest answer is that it is neither: it is a speech habit belonging to a place where the reply arrives at dusk.

## Visiting

A visit takes a day. Your nearest neighbour is three kilometres off and four hundred metres up or down, and the walk has to be done at the walk — a slow, loose-armed, closed-mouth three kilometres an hour that everyone here learns before they learn to read. Hurrying is the local obscenity. If you see a person running in this basin, something has happened that you will be dealing with for the rest of the year.

You arrive. You are handed water immediately, before anything, and you give up the salt. Then the two of you sit on opposite sides of the gutter and say nothing for ten or fifteen minutes, because you are both streaming and out of breath and there is no possible conversation until that settles. This silence is not awkward and is not meaningful. It is the same as taking off your boots.

Then you talk, in your blocks, with the gaps. You talk about the length: what the rind is doing, where the weeps are, which vent looked slow. You talk about who has died, which is news that travels by lamp and by knock and is always already known, and is discussed anyway, at length, with great precision about dates.

When you go, the host walks the first kilometre with you and then turns back without a goodbye. Everybody does this. Nobody has ever explained it and it would not occur to them that it needed explaining.

## The lamps

The living lamps are the census.

You light your lamp when you take a length and you keep it lit as long as you hold it. When you die, whoever finds you lets it go out, and it stays out for a year, and then it is either relit by your successor or it is not.

So the scatter of orange across eight kilometres of lattice — dim, irregular, most of it dark — is not decay and is not disorder. It is the map of everyone currently alive in the basin, updated in real time, readable from the rim by anyone who knows it, and superimposed on the unlit sockets of every length that has been let go in the last six centuries. The pattern that looks like nothing at all is a hundred and six people, and behind them, in the dark fittings, everybody else.

## The unsintered

There are perhaps fifteen children. They are raised at the rim in the frost, in the only enclosed buildings in the basin, and they do not descend to the floor until fourteen. First descent is the only ceremony these people have, and it consists of being fitted for a jacket.

More often the intake comes over the rim from outside — people who arrive with the salt caravan and stay behind when it goes. A newcomer is *unsintered*, and the word is not an insult, just a status, the way *wet paint* is a status. You are unsintered until the water starts to leave something on you: until your hands harden and your teeth loosen and you have a length of your own and a pump you answer to. Fifteen years, roughly. After that you are rinded, and after that you will not leave, and the reason given is never health.

The reason is noise. A basin person taken down to a market town stands in the street with their hands over their ears, unable to do anything at all. There is no cure for this and they all know about it before they stop being able to leave, and they stay anyway.

## Falls

Nine seconds of warning. The light changes, the knocking stops, and there is that long exhaled sound.

The word is *sit*. You sit down at once with your back against the nearest upright and both hands through the grating, because half a metre of forty-degree water moving down a terrace will take you over the lip and there is nothing at the bottom but rimstone. Children are taught to sit before they are taught the walk. Adults do it mid-sentence, without looking up, and finish the sentence sitting.

Afterwards the fall is walked back to its origin, which takes a few days, and it always has an origin, and the origin is always a vent nobody cleared. The person whose length it was is told what was found. Nothing else happens. Nobody says anything to them about it, ever again, and that is understood by all parties to be the punishment, and it works.

## The dead

Nothing rots here. There are no flies in this basin — that is worth sitting with for a moment — and there is no burial and no burning, because there is nothing to burn.

The dead are laid in a running pool on the sixth terrace with the water crossing them, and in a year they are a shape, and in ten they are white stone, and in forty they are a swelling in the rimstone with a suggestion of geometry in it. The terrace is not visited. It is not a place of mourning and there is no path to it. The mourning, such as it is, happens on the day the lamp goes out, three kilometres away, seen from a catwalk.

The chair on the fourth terrace, the coil of rope, the glove on the railing with nothing inside it: those are not dead people. They are just things somebody put down. The glove is a glove. It is only that you cannot look at it for long without doing the arithmetic, and everyone who lives here did that arithmetic in their first month and has not thought about it since.

## What they will not say

Three things function as law, and only one of them is ever stated.

The stated one is the ashwings. Nobody touches them, nobody catches them, nobody scrapes a panel they are working on. They eat the mineral film off the wire; they are the only maintenance in the basin that costs nothing and asks nothing; and they are treated with a formality that the people do not extend to each other.

The second is the walk, which is not a law, only something no one has ever broken twice.

The third is the film in the shallows. Nobody steps on it. Nobody diverts a channel across it. If you ask why, you are told it is slippery, and that is true, and that is not the reason, and the person telling you knows that you know.

They pull the *hair* out of the hot pipes in handfuls and boil it, and they eat that, along with the pale thumbnail crustaceans — which are dense in a few pools and absent from all the others, and the reason for that is simply that those pools are tended, seeded, cleaned, and watched over on a rota, and have been for longer than the rota has had a written form. The mystery of the crustaceans is that someone is farming them. That is the whole mystery.

The film they leave alone.

## The swifts

Twice a day something comes in over the rim and drinks from the underside of the lake at speed and leaves.

People stop for it. Whatever they are doing, wherever they are, they stop, and they watch the circuit, and then they go back to work. It is not worship and there is no name for it and nobody discusses it afterwards, not even in the long blocks with the gaps in. It is just the one event in the basin that is fast.

## What they call themselves

They have a word for themselves and it is the same word they use for the film on the gutters and the rind in the pipes and the white layer that comes off the Cold Face at four in the afternoon.

It means *what forms on the cold parts*.

They are not the operators of this place; there are no operators, and they know there are no operators, and when told that the whole circuit turns over for nobody they will agree with the sentence and see nothing bleak in it. The basin is doing what it does. It has been doing it since before anyone arrived and will do it, worse and worse and still working, long after the last lamp goes out and stays out. But a system that runs a hundred and eleven degrees of difference between its floor and its rim, hour after hour, century after century, will eventually deposit something at the interface. Something thin and specific, and slow, and adapted to exactly one narrow band of the gradient, and utterly dependent on the thing it grows on.

That is what lives here. They would tell you so themselves, in a single ninety-second block, and then wait a long time in case you had anything to add.
