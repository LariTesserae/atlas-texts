## A Census of the Sunk Crown

The honest first answer is that the mat lives here, and everything else is a way the mat has found of moving around.

It is not one organism. It is a consortium, a treaty of perhaps two hundred kinds that have been pressed together so long they have divided up the chemistry between them the way a village divides a water right: this one takes the sulfur, this one takes what the first one leaves, this one takes iron only in the warm months, if there were months. The film is six or seven layers deep in the galleries and thicker near the shaft, and thickness here is time — a hand-thick mat in a hot cell is a structure of a few centuries, laid down like the dome itself, course by course, by something that has no hands.

It grows toward nothing. There is nothing to grow toward. It grows where the water delivers, which means it maps the plumbing of the building exactly: a bloom at every inlet, a shadow of thin poor grey felt in the dead cells where the convection has not turned properly since some joint sagged in the fourteenth century. If you could read the mat you would have the hydrology, and the hydrology is the architecture, and the architecture was the intention. But the mat is not legible. It only is.

Its light is an accident. The faint bluish fog is a by-product of a reaction the organisms would run whether or not it glowed, and it glows into a place where nothing has ever had an eye. Ten thousand years of illumination, uninterrupted, addressed to no one, spent.

---

Then the shrimp, who are less a species than a weather.

They are so numerous that they should be described in the same terms as the water: at any moment the galleries hold a suspension of them, a graininess, and when you push through a doorway you are pushing through a body of them and they go around you like smoke going around a hand. Individually they are two centimetres of glass. They have no eyes and no pigment and their antennae are longer than they are.

What they have instead is pressure. A shrimp in an open chamber and a shrimp in a corbelled vault are living in different worlds, and they know it instantly, because the water bounces differently off a curving wall than off a flat one, and the bounce arrives in the antennae as a shape. They read the building by the way the building throws their own motion back at them. They are the only inhabitants who have any sense of the whole room they are in — not the whole dome, no one has that but the eels, but the *room*, its volume, its keystone, the step of the corbelling. A shrimp perceives a vault. It perceives it in a way I cannot make you feel, as a pattern of returns, as an inside-out object. Every ornamental decision the builders made about the shape of a ceiling is still being received, several thousand times a second, by something with no brain to speak of.

---

Then the snails, who are the important ones, and who are eating the building's only remaining copy of itself, and who are — this is the part that takes some sitting with — also *becoming* that copy.

A snail here travels perhaps four metres in its life. Four metres, in this honeycomb, is one chamber and part of a gallery. It is born on a wall, it grazes that wall, it lays on that wall, it dies on that wall, and the animals on the next wall but two are its relatives the way a valley is related to the valley over the pass. There are something like four hundred chambers. There are, by any reasonable definition, something like four hundred kinds of snail: separated not by mountain ranges but by twelve feet of doorway that nobody has the ambition to cross, and separated for long enough that the shells have drifted apart from one another in small, consistent, meaningless ways. Whorl count. The pitch of the spire. A ripple on the lip, present in the third gallery, absent everywhere else.

And here is the thing. Where the carving was deep, the mat was thick, and where the mat was thick the snails grew heavy shells, and where the frieze ran shallow or plain they grew light ones. A chamber that once held a dense field of spirals produced, over an interval no one should try to imagine, a stout short-spired animal. A chamber whose walls said less produced a thin one. The pattern of the differences between the populations is the pattern of the frieze. Not an image of it — no picture survives — but its distribution, its rhythm, where it was emphatic and where it fell quiet, all of it transposed out of stone and into calcium carbonate that swims.

They are still doing this. The transcription is ongoing and lossy and drifts a little every generation, and the original is being consumed in the act of copying, and the copy is being eaten by eels.

Their shells fall. Every floor in the Sunk Crown is ankle-deep in them, graded fine at the bottom and coarse at the top, a shell midden that is also a stratigraphy that is also, if anybody wanted it, a frieze in powdered form. The deepest wells are half filled with the ground-up remains of what somebody once wanted to say.

---

The eels are the travellers and the only citizens who know the building.

There are not many — perhaps six hundred in the whole dome — and a given eel spends most of a given week lodged in a corbelled joint with its head out, doing arithmetic on the water. When the convection turns, it goes. It rides the rise up the shaft, drifts out along a gallery, drops down a flanking well, and lodges again somewhere it has never been, and over a life of forty or fifty years it goes everywhere. It is the only thing in here with a map. The map is not visual and not remembered; it is a set of expectations about where warmth is, which is the same thing.

They feed by inhalation, mostly shrimp, and they do it without appearing to have moved.

Four or five times a year a mortar joint two thousand years past its patience lets go and the hill knocks once, and the eels attend. It is the nearest thing to an event, and they come for it — from two and three galleries away, arriving over the following hours, because a collapse opens a face of fresh stone and fresh stone means a flush of warm mineral water and a bloom that will feed everything for a decade. They gather at the new wound and hang in it, and that is the only occasion on which more than two of them are ever in the same place. It is not a congregation. But if you were down there in the dark with a hand on the wall, feeling them come in, you would be forgiven.

---

The ceiling slug is the only violence and the only speed.

It is palm-sized, soft, flat as a dropped cloth, and it lives on the ceilings because the ceilings are the one surface the snails colonise thinly. It moves at about a hand's breadth an hour, which is the tempo of everything, and then once or twice a month it does the fastest thing that happens in the Sunk Crown: it lets go. It sinks. It arrives on a snail nine or ten seconds later and folds around it and stays there for a day and a half. Nine seconds. Nothing else in this building has ever managed anything in under a minute, and the slug does it in the dark, having judged the drop by nothing I can name.

---

In the deepest, hottest, heaviest brine, in the wells where the water arrives least tired, the tube-dwellers sit shoulder to shoulder over whole walls and have sat there without moving house for as long as there have been walls to sit on. A colony is a clone. A clone has no particular death. Somewhere down there is an individual — in the only sense that word survives at this depth — that was already old when the last hand came off the last block, and that has done nothing since but open and close its fan in a warm current, several hundred million times, and it is not waiting.

Their combined fanning makes a current of its own. A chamber that feels furred is also a chamber that is breathing very slightly on you.

---

Then the ones who have never been in the water.

Inside the stone, in the pore space of the tufa and in the packed clay of the joints, there is a whole slow population that exists on the seep and the chemistry of the mortar itself. They are the demolition. When the hill knocks, it is because they have spent four hundred years converting the inside of a joint into something that is no longer load-bearing. They do not know there is a building. They do not know there is water. They are the most consequential inhabitants of the Sunk Crown and they have never met any of the others.

And inside the eels there are worms, and inside the snails there are things with three life stages, and for these the eel is not an animal but a warm country forty centimetres long that moves, occasionally, at the turn of the convection, for reasons its inhabitants will never have cause to consider. Nesting all the way down. A building inside a building inside a building.

---

Last, up under the crown, in the hand's span of cool fresh shimmer that the heavier water holds up like a shelf: a thin, poor, entirely separate community of very small things that experience the forty minutes of grey. One of them carries a patch of pigment on its back that reacts to the difference between light and no light. It is not an eye. It cannot form an image and there is nothing to form an image of. It is a clock. It is the only clock in the Sunk Crown, it keeps a day that matters to nothing else in the dome, and its owners do not descend.

---

So: who lives here.

Nobody who wants anything. Nobody who is going anywhere. A landlord with no body, a weather made of glass, four hundred nations of snail carrying an inscription they are simultaneously destroying and becoming, six hundred surveyors, a handful of assassins with a nine-second dive, an immortal furred lining in the hot cellars, an unseen crew slowly unbuilding the walls from inside, and, in the attic, a few hundred creatures keeping time for no one.

Count them all and you would have the floor plan, because their densities are the plumbing and the plumbing is the architecture. Weigh the shells, chamber by chamber, and you would have the frieze, in the only edition that still exists.

Nobody will. They are quiet, they are pale, they are doing what they do at a few microns a day, and in the meantime the hill holds the shape a great many hands gave it, and loses it, and the shrimp go around your wrist like smoke, and the mat glows into the dark for nobody, and has done, and will.
