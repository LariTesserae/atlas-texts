## The honest answer

Ask what lives in the basin and the truthful reply is: a film.

It is about a millimetre thick. It grows in white and dove-grey and, where the sulphide runs strongest, a pale yellow that has never been looked at. It has no individuals in it. You cannot point at one, cannot count them any more than you can count weather; the mats are a condition, like damp, and they are the only thing down here that makes a living rather than takes one. Everything else — the masonry, the colonnades, the eels in the wall-holes, the mouths in the dark, the whole silent freight of ten million animals — is a device for standing close to that film without dying of what feeds it.

The basin is not a food web. It is a queue.

## The stone, which is also the population

The mussels are first in the queue and they got there by luck, once, in the only minute of their lives that involved a choice.

Each is the length of a finger, some in the old districts the length of a hand. Their gills are not gills in the ordinary sense; they are stuffed with bacteria, farmed inside the animal's own tissue, and the mussel's entire skill consists of holding its intake angled toward the brine and its body angled away from it. It got that angle right within an hour of settling, or it did not, and if it did not there is no correcting it, because it cemented itself down and will not move again for fifty or sixty years.

So: half a century of holding still, breathing poison at a carefully judged remove, growing shell.

They cement to each other and nothing removes the dead. In the beds by the lake the wall is a metre and a half of interlocked shell of which roughly one part in nine is still alive — a rough calcareous rock in which a tenth of the building is breathing. Every hollow in it has a tenant. Every tenant is living inside the accumulated bodies of a species that has never once been anywhere else.

## The old ones

The towers are worms, and each worm is a compromise between two incompatible worlds. The plume goes up into the ordinary sea, where there is oxygen. The root goes down into the sediment, sometimes a metre and a half down, into the sulphide.
