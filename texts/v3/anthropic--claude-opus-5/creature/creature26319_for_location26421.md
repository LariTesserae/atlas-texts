## The Inhabitants

The oldest living thing up here is a stain.

Four hundred hectares of it lie unbroken on the flats east of Bench Twelve, a patch the crust wardens call the Vellum, dated by lamination count to six hundred and ten years. It is a millimetre thick. It is one organism only in the sense that a city is one animal: filaments of cyanobacteria in sheaths of their own mucilage, a felt of fungal thread, three lichens, and a great deal of glued grit. What it does, mostly, is wait. It wakes about three hours after the sheet-water reaches it, respires for eight to eleven days, and then dry-locks, going brittle and matte and to all appearances dead. Forty days awake in a year is a good year. So the Vellum, in six centuries, has been alive for about sixty-six of them, which makes it simultaneously the most ancient resident of the plateau and a thing not much older than the woman who surveys it.

When it wets, it exhales. That smell — iodine, pondwater, cut potato, something faintly like warm dust — is the only smell the land makes on its own account, and people born up here will stop mid-sentence for it. There are Sill natives in the old lands who have paid absurd money to have a jar of dry crust posted down to them so they can pour water on it in a hotel room.

In the millimetre live the rest: mites the size of a comma, springtails, tardigrades in their thousands per gram, one nematode described from nowhere else and named, with characteristic administrative flatness, after the bench it was found on. These are the real citizens by number. Everything else on the Grade is a rumour built on top of them.

And an average bootprint costs eighty years. The wardens can reseed one — slurry, a spreading card, an afternoon's kneeling — but the reseeding is a formality performed for the benefit of the person who did it. The crust repairs at its own speed or not at all.

---

The ponds are full of things that are not there.

Fairy shrimp eggs blow around the plateau as dust, indistinguishable from grit, viable for decades, and when a polygon that has been dry for eleven years finally fills, it is turbid with shrimp inside a week. Nobody stocks them. Nobody could. The lattice does the work: everything communicates with everything eventually, one lip at a time, so the whole quarter of a landscape is a single slow-mixing body of water with ten thousand chambers, and a lineage that hatched at Bench Nine can end up, four generations and one wet summer later, at Bench Twenty-Two.

Then the midges. The ecology office reports them in tonnes per square kilometre because there is no other honest unit. For four days in the high summer they come off the water in columns visible from the rail at twenty kilometres, and the towns shut their windows, and the tea tastes of midge anyway, and the harvest crews go out with the hoop nets and take what they can. Pressed midge cake is grey, smells like a fishing net, and is the second-largest organic import in the Grade's accounts. The largest is guano: the brindled gull colonies on the scree slopes, worked on a strict rotation of one colony in seven per year, the licences argued over in the meeting hall with a venom the lowlands reserve for land or water. Here water is free and land is worthless. What people fight about is dead matter.

---

The birds are the reason the ecology office exists, and the reason it loses most of its arguments in interesting ways.

Four sandpipers nest on the Grade and nowhere else on the planet. They are small, undistinguished, pale as everything else, and they lay in scrapes on open rubble where the eggs sit in plain sight looking exactly like two more chips of basalt. The phalaropes spin on the ponds in their hundreds, each one turning its own little vortex, bringing shrimp up to the surface; from a rise, on a still day, a filled polygon field looks like a sheet of metal being stippled by an army of very small hammers. The skuas come for the chicks. The falcons come for the phalaropes. There is a betting pool at Sill terminus on the date of first-bird, run since the fourth century, and the ledger of winning dates is the longest continuous phenological record anyone has anywhere, which the ecology office knows perfectly well and treats with enormous gravity, since the gamblers keep it better than any funded programme could.

The foxes came up on the works trains eight hundred years ago in the grain wagons and never went back down. They are grey, small-eared, heavier than their lowland cousins, and they do not dig, because there is nothing to dig; they live in the cooling cracks of the polygons, which means their territories are the shape of the geology, hexagons and pentagons, boundaries a surveyor could draw. In July they get fat on eggs and cache the surplus underwater in the cold ponds, where it keeps for months, and in February they walk the rail line behind the pontoon gangs with an air of open commercial interest.

They will not go within forty metres of a mast. Every fox, every generation, since the earliest records. Nobody has ever seen one hesitate at the line; they simply route around, the way you route around a wall.

The ravens live in the towns proper and are the only animal here that has learned people. They know the shift-change bell. They know the sound of the levelling wrench, and will follow a crew all morning for the grubs turned up under a lifted jack plate. They rob the soil bank, and the keeper leaves a tithe on the north pan, and this arrangement has the force of treaty.

---

There are perhaps ninety thousand people on the Grade, and their bodies are not quite the bodies of the old lands any more.

The blood is not thickened — that is the lowland answer to altitude, and it kills you slowly over sixty years. Grade people breathe more instead, deeper and oftener, at rest, asleep, without noticing, their chests moving all night like something being rowed. Their fingers are warm. They are unimpressive at sprinting and very hard to exhaust.

What they cannot do is go down. Below about two thousand metres the thick air makes them stupid and slow and unable to sleep, but that is the smaller half of it. The real problem is the smell. A person from Sill, set down in a wheat field in the old lands, will stand there with their eyes streaming and be unable to explain: it isn't beauty and it isn't grief, it is that every living thing in that field is shouting at once, and they have spent their whole life in a country where the only smells are cold stone, wet crust, and the inside of the soil bank. Down-sickness, in the manuals. *Overwhelmed by soil* is what people actually say.

---

The defining trade is levelling, and levellers are the nearest thing the Grade has to a priesthood, which they would deny at length.

Each spring, when the sheet-water has gone and before the midges, the crews go around with their instruments and their long-handled wrenches and take every building, mast, tank and rail bed in the district back to plumb. The ground under a town of eleven thousand does not rise evenly. Across the length of a terrace the differential can run four or five millimetres in a year, which sounds like nothing and is in fact a house pulling itself apart. A quarter-turn on a threaded foot is one and a half millimetres. A good leveller can feel an eighth.

Every structure has a ledger, a bound book of its own turns, kept in the building and not in the archive, because the ledger belongs to the house. Sill terminus is at forty-one volumes. You do not level a home without a member of the household present; that is not law either, but the rudeness of doing so would be discussed for a generation. On the morning your house is levelled you put out tea. Children learn the sound of the wrench the way other children learn bells, and they learn early that the sound means someone has come to keep the floor honest, and that they will be expected to do this for someone else when they are grown.

Behind the levellers come the underpinners, who are masons, and whose work is the strangest architecture on the planet. The jacks have finite travel. Every few decades the accumulated difference outruns them, and then the low side of a building must be given new stone at the bottom — a course laid under the existing wall, in a trench, by lamplight, while the house stands on temporary steel above your head. The old houses of Sill have four and five hidden courses beneath their floors. The town is eight centuries old and has grown downward by nearly two metres. There is a phrase for a person of quiet reliable substance: *courses under him.*

---

The pontoon gangs are nomads. The braid shifts weekly and they shift with it, forty or fifty of them per section, living in van-cars on the Line, poling out into the shallow threads to read where the water intends to go next. They are famously odd — they use their own vocabulary for the water, twenty-odd words for conditions a visitor would call *wet* — and they eat a great deal of midge cake, and they are the only people up here who ever say the plateau is beautiful, and they say it as a complaint.

The mast-walkers are odder still, and there are more of them than anyone outside the service realises: thirty thousand masts in the grid, each visited on a rotation, each with a numbered book. The work is to walk to the mast, put a bare hand on it, confirm that it is cold, note the local readings, sign, and walk on. That is all the work. A mast-walker in a full career will touch perhaps eleven thousand masts and find nothing whatever wrong with any of them.
