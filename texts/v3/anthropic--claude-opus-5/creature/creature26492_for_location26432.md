## The film

Start at the bottom, because the bottom is the oldest tenant and the only one that owns anything.

The pale finger-length pendants on the ceiling are not slime in the way a pond is slimy. Each one is a colony — a sagging, mucus-bound city of sulfur-oxidizing bacteria, breathing the hydrogen sulfide that comes up out of the sump, exhaling sulfuric acid, and dripping the surplus onto the rubble and onto your sleeve. A drop off the tip of one of those pendants runs a pH somewhere under one. That is the hole in your shirt. That is also the room: the domes overhead were not carved by a river, they are being *digested*, and these are the digestive organs, and they have been at it here for something on the order of forty thousand years.

They are the primary producers. Note what that means. In almost every other room on Earth where things live, the food comes down from the sun in some form — leaves, seeds, drowned insects, a corpse. Here the sun contributes a thumbnail of daylight thirty meters up and four hundred watts of sodium lamp. Everything else in the chamber that eats, eats something that ate the rock, or the gas coming out of the rock. The whole crowded roaring room is standing on chemistry. It is a food web with its root in stone.

The film is not one species. Scrape a coin's worth into a jar and you have twenty or thirty kinds of bacteria arranged in layers by how much oxygen they can stand, plus a fauna nobody in town has ever looked at: nematodes threading the mucus, rotifers, mites the size of a period, protozoa grazing the bacterial lawn like cattle. There are more individual lives in a teaspoon of the wall paste than there are bats on the ceiling, and there are four hundred thousand bats on the ceiling.

It has no behavior you would recognize. It grows, it sags, it falls off twice a year with the sound of a mattress dropping, and it starts again the same afternoon. It is the landlord. Everyone else is subletting.

## The fish

The sump is thirty-four degrees, low in oxygen, high in sulfide, and full of a small barred toothcarp — silver-gray, four centimeters, the females plainer and larger, the males with a smudge of gold on the tail in breeding condition. A priest described them in 1927 from a bucket brought up the shaft and named them after the town. Nobody has looked again since 1961.

What they do all day is breathe the skin of the water. Sulfide poisons the machinery of respiration; the only tolerable oxygen is in the top two millimeters where the air touches the surface, so the entire population hangs there with the upper lip breaking the film, thousands of them, packed tightest at the inflow where the current keeps that film renewed. That is the boiling-coins effect. It is not feeding frenzy or panic. It is the ordinary posture of their whole lives — a species that lives pressed against the ceiling of its world, which happens to be the floor of yours.

They eat the bacterial mat off the sump walls and the midge larvae out of it. They are cannibals when crowded, which is always. They are livebearers; the young are born ready, no fry stage, because there is nowhere safe to be small here.

Twice a shift, someone clears the intake screens with a long-handled rake, and the rake comes up silver. The dead go into a bucket and the bucket goes onto the guano, where it is gone in under an hour. Occasionally a few get past the screens and up the 300-millimeter riser, and are found later, boiled and bleached, in the filter housing at the spa. The spa's maintenance man keeps one in a jar of surgical spirit on a shelf behind the towels. He tells guests it came from the sea.

## The column

Above the sump, the tornado. Non-biting midges, one species dominant, a couple of others mixed in. Larvae in the sump mud and in the wet mat, pupae rising, adults emerging into the warm rising air and joining the swarm.

Here is the thing worth understanding about the column: it is a structure, and no individual in it is more than a day old. The shape holds — the same lopsided pillar leaning east over the pit, thinning at nine meters, fraying where the fan draft cuts it — because the males orient to the warm updraft and the lamp glow and each other, and the females fly into the pillar to be found, and then everyone dies within about twenty hours and is replaced from below. The column has been the same shape since before the catwalk was hung. It is made entirely of things that will not be there tomorrow.

It is also the room's conveyor belt. Carbon that a bacterium fixed off a sulfide molecule in the water goes into a larva, into a midge, into the air, into a bat, onto the floor. The column is how the sump feeds the ceiling.

## The ceiling

Two species mainly, and a third in the cold corners.

The bulk of the upholstery is the bent-winged bat — smooth gray-brown, dog-faced, gregarious to the point of absurdity, packed at fifteen hundred to two thousand animals per square meter with no gaps, so that the dome surface reads as a single moving pelt. Mixed in, in looser cliques with a hand's breadth between individuals, a larger mouse-eared bat, and in the drafty niches near the raise a scatter of horseshoe bats hanging free and separate, wrapped in their own wings like folded umbrellas, refusing the crowd.

The bent-wings are why this room exists in its present form as much as the acid is. Four hundred thousand small mammals at thirty-eight degrees Celsius body temperature, exhaling water and carbon dioxide and urinating ammonia into a sealed dome, produce a pocket of air that is hotter and more corrosive than the cave would manage on its own; the condensation on that dome is acid, and it eats upward. The blistered blunt cupolas overhead — those are bat-shaped. The colony is not living in a hole. The colony is slowly excavating one, from the inside, with its breath, at a rate that a person could measure with patience and a straight edge over about thirty years.

They are not silent and never all asleep. The chatter is quarrels over position: an inch closer to the crown is worth two degrees. In June the room is a nursery — the females come in pregnant, give birth within a fortnight of each other, and park the pups in a crèche patch about four meters across at the hottest point of the largest dome, a solid pink-gray mat of naked infants left behind at dusk. A mother returning at three in the morning locates her own in that mass by voice and smell in under a minute. Nobody down here has ever gotten used to watching it.

Each bat carries passengers. Flat wingless bat flies that run over the fur sideways like small crabs and are impossible to catch. Wing mites clustered along the forearm membranes. Fleas of a genus that lives nowhere else but on this genus of bat. When the colony leaves at dusk it leaves behind, on the ceiling, a rain of these — dislodged, dropping, walking down the wall to wait.

## The floor

A meter of warm guano, and its own society, arranged by depth like the paint on the pumps.

At the surface: dermestid beetles in their tens of thousands, larvae and adults, the same beetles museums use to strip skeletons, doing precisely that job here on everything that falls. Roaches the length of a thumb — glossy, fast, extremely reluctant to leave the dark, and much more numerous under the crust than on top of it. Guano moths, small and drab, whose caterpillars live in silk tubes in the droppings; when you disturb a patch they come off it like dust going the wrong way. Mites in sheets, so dense in places that the surface has a texture like felt and moves when your light touches it.

Deeper, in the anoxic warm layers, fly larvae and mite species that no longer bother with eyes.

Around the edges and up the walls: the blind cave crickets, straw-colored, absurdly long-antennaed, which climb everything including you and which are the only animal here anybody has affection for. Also the endemic — a blind ground beetle, three millimeters, chestnut, no eyes at all, described in 1954 from this chamber and named after a hydrologist who died in it. It exists in this room and in two others in the same hill. That is the whole extent of the species. The 1954 paper is photocopied and taped inside the door of the switchgear cabinet, gone soft as cloth from the damp, and the crew are quietly proud of it in the way you are proud of a relative who did something obscure and permanent.

Predators on the invertebrates: pseudoscorpions in the wall crevices, a pale hunting spider that does not build, and the sheet-weavers strung between the girders under the catwalk, feeding on the midge column's fallout.

And the thing that eats the spiders is a house centipede — fifteen pairs of legs, a wet gray blur, thriving here at the top of its temperature range, running upside-down along the underside of the grating faster than your eye follows, taking spiders off their own webs. There are hundreds. You will see one properly perhaps once a month, always by accident, always closer to your hand than you would like.

## The threshold

Not residents exactly, but always present, which after forty years is the same thing.

Snakes in the adit crevices — a rat snake and a whip snake, four or five individuals holding the same fissures year after year, anchored by the tail with a third of the body out in the current, striking blind into the exodus and connecting about one time in eight. Geckos around the coin of daylight at the top of the raise and along the first ten meters down, eating midges that get that high. A little owl that takes bats at the shaft mouth in September, and, one year in five, a barn owl that comes down twelve meters into the raise and cannot get back out and has to be netted.

The fig. One individual, seeded around 2003 by a bird or a bat in the lip of the ventilation raise, thirty meters up in a crack with a spoonful of soil, sending root cable down into a room whose air is dissolving it. The roots reach four, five, six meters, blanch, corrode at the tips, die back, and are replaced from above. It is not dying. It is not winning. It has settled into being a permanently besieging army that will never be relieved and never withdraws, and the crew mark its progress against a nail in the wall the way you would mark a child's height.

## The three

Per shift, three, of whom one at a time is below.

The oldest has been coming down since 1989 and no longer sweats normally at surface temperatures — his wife says he is cold to the touch in summer. He can tell from the pitch of the discharge knock whether the water table has moved, and has been right more often than the gauge. He is the one who inked the 2011 note about the water hammer and he has stopped mentioning it.

The one who waters the ferns from the condensate tray has done it for eleven years and does not explain the joke to new people, only hands over the cup. She keeps the shift book, and it is her hand, small and square, that has written *level normal, screens cleared* about four thousand times, and it is also her hand in the margin on 14 June 2019: *pups down early. very loud.*

The new one is twenty-six, is at ninety-one minutes on the timer more often than he should be, and has started leaving a coin on the flange next to the plastic saint, which nobody asked him to do and nobody has told him not to.

They do not live here. They live in the town, in flats with the windows open, four hundred meters of limestone above the ceiling of animals. But their trousers are tucked into their boots, their watches have all died the same death, their shirts have brown-edged holes, they can each name the blind beetle and both fig strategies and the crèche patch, they wear the mask for the guano spores because the fourth rule is not on the sign but is the one that killed a man in 1978, and they go down two hundred and forty times a year for thirty years. Somewhere in there the distinction between working in a place and living in it stops being interesting.

## What lives here

One thing, if you stand back far enough.

Sulfide comes up out of the earth. Bacteria eat it and make acid; the acid eats the room, which is how the room grows. Some of the bacteria become midge, and the midge column carries the room's chemistry into the air, and the ceiling eats the column and the fields above the town, and the ceiling's leavings become the floor, and the floor becomes beetle and cricket and centipede and, eventually, gas again. The fish breathe the top two millimeters. The fig pushes down and is pruned. The pumps take four hundred cubic meters an hour of the same water up the riser to the spa, where people who will never come down here lie in it up to their collarbones, and to the bottling plant, where it goes into glass with a picture of the church on the label — so the town, too, is on the outside edge of this animal, drinking it, being warmed by it, having its mosquitoes eaten by it every night for forty minutes in a spiral out of a hole in the hillside.

Everything in it is provisional. The midges last a day, the film falls off twice a year, the paint is on its third color, the bats are here in June and gone by November, the crew turn over every generation, the chamber itself is eighteen meters tall and mid-sentence and has an opinion about its own shape that it is expressing at one centimeter per century.

The shift book is right, is the thing. Level normal. Screens cleared. That is what it looks like from inside, when something enormous and slow is going perfectly well.
