## The Keeper

Begin by getting it wrong, the way everyone does.

You will look for them in the rooms. The rooms are the obvious place — the dressed lintel, the cleared floor, the laid-out blades. But a workshop is empty most of the time, and you can sit in one of those rooms for eleven days with your hand on the grinding hollow and meet nothing at all. They are not scarce. They are simply not *there*, and they are not anywhere in particular, because their work is not done in places. It is done along.

You have already touched one. Crawling the hundred meters, among your two dozen living things: the fifth or sixth thing you took for the sitting kind, the one that was slightly cooler on its upper surface than the floor beside it, that had a rim of dust banked against one side the way dust banks against anything that has been still for a day and a half. You put your palm on its back and felt nothing move and moved on. It was attending to something four hundred meters away and did not consider you worth the interruption.

---

Take the measure of the body while it is still.

Something under two meters from mouth-plates to tail-root, and low — the trunk carried horizontal, never more than knee-height off the floor unless it needs the ceiling. It can rear. It rears to press its face into an overhead seam, and in that posture it is nearly upright and its back is a hand's width from the rock, and it does not like the posture and does not hold it long.

Six limbs. This is the thing to understand about them, and everything else follows from it. The front pair and the rear pair are feet: broad, splayed, four-lobed, soled in a horn so thick that glass teeth score it without reaching anything that can feel. They are resurfaced constantly by walking. They are the reason the trackways are polished and the reason the polish stopped deepening.

The middle pair are not feet. The middle pair are carried folded against the chest, and they have never touched the floor, and they will not. They end in a spread of nine filaments each, pale, unscarred, dry as paper, and they are the most sensitive tissue in the Blister Country. A keeper can find the knot in a line by the disturbance in the air above it. It can feel the grain-direction in obsidian before it strikes. When a keeper is frightened — and they are, sometimes, in a slow way — it closes the middle hands into the chest hollow and covers them with a forefoot, and this is the entire visible vocabulary of fear here.
