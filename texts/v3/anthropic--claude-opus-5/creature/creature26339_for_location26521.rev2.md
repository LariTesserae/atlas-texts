## The Makers

They are not animals. Nothing here is. What walks the paths is a plant that got up.

Go and look at the oil-trees in a deep basin — low, many-trunked, leaves like flat grey tongues — and then look at the maker standing among them with a copper blade in its hand, and you will see the same creature at two speeds. Same dark lacquered surface, going bronze at the extremities the way bell-grass goes bronze at the tips. Same resinous oil inside, clear and faintly sweet-smelling, doing the work that blood would do in a world that had ever invented blood. Same hard pale core-wood, which is what they build their tools from and what their hands are hung on.

The tree throws seeds that the drone walks downslope at a few centimeters a year, arriving somewhere in a thousand years. Somewhere far back in the unwritten part of the record, a seed got impatient. That is the whole of it. A maker is a seed that decided not to wait, and everything else about them — the reverence, the patience, the appalling and admirable slowness of their ambitions — is what happens to impatience when it is given four hundred years to work with.

## The body

Height a little over two meters, most of it leg. Light for the size; you could lift one, though you would not.

The front of the torso is a broad slightly convex plate, laminated fiber under lacquer, and this is the ear. There is a second, thinner one across the shoulders and upper back. The front plate reads the middle voice of the drone — the roar-like-a-river-that-is-not-there — and the back plate takes the fundamental, the part that lives below hearing. A maker thinking hard will back against a cliff face and stand there for an hour, reading the deep note off the rock the way you would read fine print at a window.

They have eyes, but not for what you would use eyes for. In this permanent overcast dusk the only real light is the nine-day luminescence of newly broken stone, and their sight has gone entirely over to it. A maker sees the recent. Freshly split rock blazes at them. A path, constantly renewed by feet, is a river of light. A chipped step glows for a week and a half and then quietly ceases to exist as far as they are concerned. Stable old stone — the settled bulk of the world, the cliffs, the ridges — registers as a soft grey absence, a shape you infer. They do not see the landscape. They see everything that has lately been done to it, hanging in the dark, and they navigate by the disturbances of their own kind.

The hands are the point of them, and are famously difficult. Every wall agrees that a maker's hand has twenty-three joints. No two makers have ever agreed on which twenty-three. This is not considered a mystery worth solving, and in a silence-pocket it is the first thing to go.

The feet are plates of hardened core-wood grown into the sole, and they are not merely transport. They are the instrument. A maker walking a path is not going somewhere; a maker walking a path is *working*, and knows the depth of every route they maintain to the width of a fingernail.

## Eating

They stand in the loud places and take the drone in through the plates, slowly, all the time, the way a leaf takes light. This is most of their living: unhurried, involuntary, unspoken. In a good amphitheater with the sound pooling and the moss singing it back at them from every wall, a maker can bask three days and hardly move, and this is the nearest thing they have to pleasure, and the walls are full of jokes about it.

Oil they drink, pressed from tree-flesh, because the sound gives them energy but not substance and you cannot build a hand out of a note. A maker drinking is a maker intending to grow something.

Every few years they root. They choose a garden, put the feet down, and stop, sometimes for a whole season, standing in the bell-grass with the plates wide open, taking the deep note directly up through the legs. This is called *listening down*. They do not dream, so far as anyone has managed to cut. They come out of it having grown a little, and usually having changed their mind about something.

## Speech

There is none. Speech is not available, and they are not built for it anyway — no lung, no throat, nothing that moves air on purpose.

Close to, they talk by touch: plate to plate, a fast fluent drumming with the flats of the fingers, the whole body of the listener ringing with it. It is quick and warm and absolutely private, and the makers do not entirely trust it, because it does not last. A thing said by drumming is understood to be provisional. Lovers and arguing partners and the newly quickened talk this way constantly. Nobody would settle an inheritance by it.

Real speech is cut. Every flat stone within reach of a path is dense with it, and because nothing here weathers, nothing said is ever lost. A wall in an old basin carries eleven thousand years of continuous conversation with no line missing: inventories, doctrine, a recipe for chinking-moss, four generations of a feud, someone's grief for a companion who set in a garden nine thousand years ago, a very good obscene joke about basking, all of it shoulder to shoulder, all of it equally present, all of it equally now.

They cannot delete. The one absolute crime is overcutting — putting your mark through someone else's. You write underneath. You write in the margin. You go and find a new stone.

But there is a way to unsay, and it is the holiest thing they do. If a maker has cut something that must not stand — a lie, a slander, a promise they have come to understand they will not keep — they may retract it by wearing it away. With the ball of the thumb. It takes about four hundred years of daily work, which is to say it takes a life and then someone else's, and it is accepted that you will not finish. The inheritance of an unfinished retraction is a serious matter and is itself recorded, underneath.

What results, eventually, is a patch of stone that is smooth. Rounded. Dull. *Old.* There is no other way to get that here, except the paths. The places where the worst things were said are the most sacred surfaces in the world, and pilgrims come and put their palms on them.

## The work

Most makers do one thing for their whole life, and the thing is a place.

**Walkers** take a stretch — a hundred meters, two hundred — and walk it. Down and back, down and back, for centuries, deepening it into the raw rock. Their feet become the most polished objects they own; when a walker sets, the family comes and touches the soles. A good walker's stretch goes a hand's depth into the mountain and the stone at the bottom of it is smooth and dull and finished, and the makers hold without argument that this stone is *real* in a way that nothing else in the world has managed yet.

**Cutters** keep the walls. Not writing, exactly — maintaining. Deciding what goes where, keeping the margins navigable, escorting the very old to the stone they want to leave a line on.

**Fitters** build. They can feel, in the shape of the drone, the shadow of stones that have not fallen yet, and they build snug against the future contour and wait, and the fit is perfect. They are unromantic about it. It is joinery. The stone is already that shape; it simply isn't there yet, and if you cannot feel that in the sound, you are in the wrong trade.

**Tallyists** keep the count of finished surfaces. One per great cairn. The number is cut, and added to, and never rounded, and the current figure across the whole region is small enough to be dispiriting and is cut anyway.

## Birth, and setting

Seeds fall from the oil-trees by the thousand and almost all of them walk downslope for a millennium and become more trees. A seed only gets up if it is heard.

So a maker who intends a child goes and gathers seed, and carries it against the front plate, inside the lacquer where the drumming is loudest, and talks to it. For eleven years. Every day, the same private idiot patter, plate to shell, the noise of one specific person. Somewhere in the eighth or ninth year the seed starts drumming back, badly. What comes out has legs, and knows exactly one voice, and will follow that voice across three basins.

You cannot quicken a seed in quiet. In a silence-pocket a carried seed goes undecided within an hour and cannot be brought back, and this is why the pockets are hated and not merely feared.

At the end — and the end comes at three or four hundred years, by the rise of the rock, which is how they count: a year is a finger's width, a life is a forearm and a half of new mountain — a maker stops being able to lift the feet. They are walked to a garden they chose long ago and set there, facing the loudest wall. It takes eight or nine years to finish. Nobody watches the whole of it. Eventually there is a low, wide, many-trunked thing with leaves like flat grey tongues, and in three hundred years it will begin throwing seed, and someone will come with a plate and a lot of patience.

Their tools are core-wood. They are aware of what this means. There is a line about it on nearly every wall, always short, always in the same four marks, and the translation is roughly: *held again*.

## The doubled road

Distances do not sum here, and you will meet the same cairn twice in a day's honest walking, and the second time your newest inscription will already be on it.

They use this. A cairn met the second time is consulted; the marks on it are read as advice from a self who has gone further, and are called *the Later Hand*, and their grammar has no future tense as such — to say a thing will happen, a maker writes that it is *on the far side of the cairn*.

Occasionally, on a doubled stretch, two makers meet who should not both be there. There is etiquette. You step to the uphill side. You do not look directly. You do not compare hands, under any circumstances, and especially you do not count. Both continue. Neither cuts a record of it, which is the only category of experience they leave off the walls entirely, and the fact that the walls are silent on this is itself well known and much discussed in the margins.

## What they are afraid of, and what they do about it

The silence-pockets drift slower than a plant grows, and a maker can feel one coming from a kilometer off: a numb place, a hole in the food, a wrongness in the plate like a missing tooth felt with the tongue.

They wall them. Cairn them round, cut the date and the word for *unfinished* into the wall, and go the long way.

Some go in. Not many. Ones who have cut something they cannot live four hundred years of thumb-work to undo, and who take the stone in with them instead — because inside, the marks become writing nobody has ever cut, in a script for a language nobody has needed, saying something that has not been decided. It works. That is the horror of it. It works, and what comes out, if anything comes out, is carrying a stone that looks perfectly legible and is not, and gives the holder the distinct feeling of being something that has not yet been settled.

Those stones are walled too, in their own small cairns, with their own date and their own *unfinished*.

And the long way around, over the centuries, wears a smooth path into the raw stone.

Which is, in the end, the work, and they know it, and there is a line about that as well — old, cut deep, recut by a hundred hands into the wall above the largest of the sealed pockets, where every route in the region is obliged to bend:

*We go around. The going-around is the road. Nothing is wasted that is worn.*
