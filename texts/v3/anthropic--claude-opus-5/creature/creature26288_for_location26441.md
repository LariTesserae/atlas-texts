## The difficulty with the census

Whoever lives here does not live here the way a fox lives in a wood — arriving, holding, failing, being replaced. Nothing in the sink has a story with a shape. There is no year in which the harvestmen did well. There is no wall that the whip-spiders took. If you want the biography of an inhabitant you will have to be satisfied with the biography of a position: something has stood at that overhang, doing that, for longer than the overhang has been that shape.

Still. There are bodies. Begin with the bodies.

## The fabric

The harvestmen are the largest single thing in the room, and they are not one animal, and it is very difficult to keep hold of that.

Above head height the wall wears them. Six deep in the good places, legs threaded through legs into a mesh with no loose end anywhere, so that the boundary of the cluster is not a boundary but a thinning — a fraying-out toward the drier stone. Individually they are almost nothing: a lentil of body slung in a rigging of eight hairs, a mouth that takes springtails and fungal crumb and the softened corners of anything that has died recently. They do not hunt so much as wait in a fabric and receive.

Touch the fabric and it answers. Not by fleeing — there is nowhere — but by bobbing. One goes, and the ones threaded to it go, and in something under a second the whole upholstered wall is pulsing in place, thousands of bodies rising and falling a fingernail's distance out of phase, and the effect on a person standing below in the dark, feeling it rather than seeing it, is of a lung. It subsides in a slow wave from the point of contact outward. Then the dry whisper of resettlement, and then nothing.

They moult, and they do not leave. The shed skins stay hooked in the mesh where the animal stepped out of them, so that the cluster is perhaps a third ghosts by volume — empty legs woven into the live legs, holding tension, doing structural work. There is no way to know how deep that goes. Under the living carpet there may be a felt of the dead a hand thick, and it may be very old.

## The stations

The whip-spiders are the opposite proposition: one each, and known.

Each hood and each overhang above the root-mat holds a single amblypygid, flattened into the rock, its two feeler-legs — thin as thread, longer than your forearm is wide — sweeping the dark in slow alternating arcs. They do not touch each other except at the edges of their claims, and there the negotiation is famously unhurried: two animals fencing with their antenniform legs, tap, wait, tap, over three or four hours, at the end of which one has moved a body-length and both have agreed. Nobody is injured. Nobody has been injured here in a very long time. Injury is expensive in a room where the food falls from the sky at unreliable intervals and the sky is closed.

A whip-spider knows its station the way you know a dark house you grew up in. Move one — a visitor did, once, gently, to a wall four paces off — and it returns, not directly, but by a search that widens and narrows and finally locks, and it takes about forty minutes, and it does not use its eyes, which are there and which are of no consequence.

The females carry their young on their backs in a pale mass for the first weeks, and the young disperse to whatever stations are vacant, and there are always exactly as many stations as there are animals to fill them, which is not a coincidence and is not arranged by anybody.

## The standers

The cave crickets do not cross the light-line. This is the strangest fact about them, because their kind elsewhere go out at night to graze the forest floor and come back full, and the whole economy of those caves runs on what they carry in. Here, they don't. They come to the line, they stand under it, antennae extended to twice their length into the gradient of cooler and wetter, and they turn back.

Nobody has determined what stops them. It is not a wall.

So they stand on the flowstone at intervals, brown-amber, hunchbacked, jointed like folding rulers, and they map the air. What they eat is what the sink eats: fallen leaf, fallen beetle, the collapsed heads of ghost-pipes, each other's shed skins, each other. They are the room's disposal and its long-legged nervous system. A drop lands anywhere in the chamber and eleven of them know it.

## The nine kinds

The snails are the sound of the place. Nine species, and if you learn nothing else, learn that they are nine and not one: there is a flat black one with a shell like a wet fingernail; a tall spired one, translucent, through which the liver shows as a coil of darker amber; one so thin-shelled it is essentially a bubble of lacquer over a body, and it grazes only under the deepest overhangs where nothing can fall on it. They eat the fungal bloom off the sweating stone. They eat it at the rate at which it regrows. The wall is never cleared and never furred.

Rasp, and rasp, and rasp — a page turned every few seconds somewhere in the room, by somebody, for four hundred thousand years.

When they die, and they die constantly, the shells go into the root-mat and eventually into the sump, and the smallest of them are collected.

## The makers

The caddis larvae are the only inhabitants with a standard.

Each one lives inside a tube it built and keeps building, a graded spiral of grains, coarse at the tail, fine at the head, and set into the sequence at intervals of four grains, the empty shell of a young snail — chosen for size within a tolerance you would need calipers to fault. It is ballast. It is also, unavoidably, a pattern, and the pattern is the same in the sump shallows and in a thimble-basin nine paces away in a chamber where the two animals cannot ever have met.

Turn one over in your fingers and the animal inside withdraws and waits with perfect patience for you to be finished. Set it down; it walks away at its own speed, correcting the tube's angle as it goes, because there is a way the tube should hang.

They pupate. They emerge. The adults are small dull moth-like things with wings that are of no use in a room with no wind and no exit, and so they walk — over the wet rock, over the harvestmen, who mostly ignore them — and they find each other by scent within a day or two, and they mate, and they fall in the water, and the amphipods take them. A caddisfly here is a device for turning one generation of grain-sorting into the next. It has about thirty hours of flightless adulthood in which to be something else, and it isn't.

## The polity

Under the eastern overhang there is a colony, and it is the only *we* in the sink.

Perhaps four thousand pale ants, eyeless, unpigmented to the point that a worker held up against the gnat-light is a lit thing, the gut a grey line inside her. They keep a fungus garden the size of two fists in a hollow of the flowstone, fed on chewed root-fibre from the mat. They carry dry fibre out to the seeps, let it soak, and haul the loaded sponge home, and they have been doing this since before anything upstairs chipped a stone on purpose.

They have a queen. She is somewhere in the middle of that fist of fungus and she has probably been there for twenty-five years, and when she stops, the colony will run down over a season or two like a clock, and the garden will be eaten by something else, and within a decade a founding queen will have arrived from somewhere in the karst below — they do come, rarely, along the water — and the hollow will be occupied again. The room does not experience this as an event. Over the necessary interval, the sum is constant.

## The constellation

The fungus-gnat larvae hang under the eastern overhang in their mucus rigging, and each one lights the blue-green point at its tail, and each point is a lure, and the beaded lines below are the trap, and this is the only advertisement in the sink and it is a lie.

There is nothing to fly to it. Almost nothing flies here. They catch springtails that fall from the ceiling, and each other's parents, and occasionally something that came in over the rim wet and confused and did not go back out.

The number does not change. Not because the animals are immortal — they aren't, they cycle, larva to pupa to a delicate short-lived nothing that mates and dies within a night — but because the overhang supports what the overhang supports, and the count is the overhang's, not theirs. The visitor who counted got the same figure eleven years apart. He was counting a property of the rock, and he did not know it, and I think somewhere below the light-line he began to suspect it, which is why he stopped.

## The garden, which is also alive

Do not leave out the ghost-pipes and the coral-fungus and the leafless orchid-adjacent thing with its three permanent flowers. They are inhabitants and not scenery. They eat, as everything here eats, by robbery: the fungal thread comes down from the trees on the rim carrying the sun's sugar, and the garden taps the thread and takes.

The three flowers are the only unanswered question in the room. Flowers are addressed to somebody. These are open at all times, in a chamber where the pollinator — whatever moth or gnat or beetle the arrangement was built around — has been absent for longer than anyone can establish. So the plant does the only thing left: it seeds itself, endlessly, quietly, and keeps the invitation out. Three at a time. Never four, never two. A phone ringing in an empty house for four hundred thousand years, and the house is not troubled by it, and neither, apparently, is the phone.

## The owner

The largest organism in the sink is not in the sink.

It is the mycelium, and its body is the ridge — a lace running through the moss-pelt and the root-plate and the joints of the limestone, in contact with several thousand trees above and every pale stem below. The garden is its outstation. The sink is its stomach, as the description says, and the description is being polite: the sink is a *pouch of the fungus*, walled in rock, stocked with dependents.

You cannot see it. You are standing inside it. When you put your palm on the root-mat and cover forty animals, you are also touching one animal that extends for two hundred metres in every direction and does not have a middle.

## The one in the sump

The sump is two arm-spans across, black, and its floor has never been found.

The amphipods go in and out of it as they go everywhere. Flatworms fold along the wet lip. And there is something else, which has been seen four times, all four by people not looking for it, all four in the same three-second interval of a light being swung.

Pale. Something over a hand long. Fingered. Perfectly still against the wall of the shaft, gill-fronds barely moving in water that barely moves, a head with the two grey smudges where eyes were being planned and then dropped from the design.

If it is what it appears to be, then it eats perhaps twelve times a year — a caddis, an amphipod swarm, a drowned cricket — and it may be older than the trees on the rim, and there is exactly one of it, because the arithmetic of what falls into this room supports exactly one, and the sink has had a very long time to do the arithmetic.

It is the only face in the chamber. It is also the only inhabitant that has solved the problem the visitor could not: it has reduced itself until duration costs it nothing.

## What actually lives here

Here is the honest answer, and it takes some getting used to.

Nothing in the sink is buried. The shed skins hold the fabric together. The empty snail shells are set into the caddis cases as ballast. The dead cricket is the harvestmen's week; the spent gnat is the amphipod's; the fungus takes the rest and hands it back upward as sugar comes down. Every death here is load-bearing. There is no soil, because soil is what you get when the dead are allowed to be finished, and in the sink they are not allowed. They are kept in service.

So when you ask what lives there, the census — the fabric, the stations, the standers, the nine kinds, the makers, the polity, the constellation, the garden with its unanswered flowers, the great pale owner in the rock, the one thing in the water with a face — the census is accurate and it is not the answer.

What lives there is an arrangement, which has bodies the way a river has water: continuously, and never the same ones, and it does not notice the difference. It received a leaf last week. It received a bird eleven years ago. It received a man three times, who stood in the middle of it with his hands out, and it registered him as weather — a warmth passing, a disturbance in the fabric that subsided from the point of contact outward — and it did not remember him, and it did not need to, and this is precisely what he understood, and why he left, and why he did not come back.
