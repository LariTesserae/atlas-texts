## What lives on Terrace Nine

### The budget

Start with the accounting, because it explains the manners.

Everything alive on this terrace, weighed together — every wright, every nodeling, every sifter, the whole hovering chandelier, the hushes, whatever is under the rectangle — comes to somewhere between nine and thirteen tonnes. A bus. That is the entire biosphere of the west shoulder, and it has not gotten appreciably larger or smaller in the four centuries we've been counting, and the shard-drifts suggest it has not gotten appreciably larger or smaller in nine million years.

The reason is that there is almost no new carbon. The mountain does not make soil, does not catch rain, does not receive anything but sunlight and the occasional micrometeorite. What it has is a seep.

Behind the fourth pylon, at the base of the back wall, there is a fissure about the width of two fingers from which the engine exudes roughly four grams a day of long-chain organics — a warm tarry breath, the only new matter on Adze. Four grams. Everything on this terrace is downstream of four grams a day, and has been since before there were mammals anywhere.

Sitting on the fissure, and covering it entirely, is a dark patch about forty centimetres across that most people photograph without noticing, because it looks like a stain. It is called the collar. It is one animal. It has no parts you could name; it is a mouth that is also a stomach that is also a foot, and it has been in that exact position, taking its cut, for as long as the drift record goes down. Nothing displaces it. Sifters have tried; the marks are there in the rock. It takes about a third and lets the rest into the sand, and the sand creeps upslope with it, and that creep is the terrace's circulatory system: a nutrient tide moving at eleven centimetres a year, uphill, driven by noise.

Every other animal here is queued behind that stain.

### The fine

The fronds are not plants and they are not, in any useful sense, individuals. Each growth tip is a colony of animals — thousands of them, none larger than a red blood cell, collectively called the fine — riding the leading edge of the glass they are laying down. They eat the trace organics in the sand, they excrete oriented silica, and they steer by strain. That's the whole of them. Put a colony on a laboratory bench and it will build you a rib and then stop, waiting for a shake that isn't coming, and die confused.

What they do at the tip is not growth. It is masonry, and it is very fast: a good stand puts on a centimetre an hour in the right antinode. The wrights work the outside of the stem, spiralling, in a band about two millimetres wide, and if you get your faceplate close enough in the right light you can see the band as a faint travelling shimmer, like heat off a road, climbing.

They are also, all of them, always, on the losing side. A colony that steers well builds into the loudest place available and is thereby killed by its own competence, at a rib, without warning, in about forty seconds of unhurried disassembly. The wrights ride it down. Most are buried. Some fraction of a percent dig out, find the changed field, and start again on the wreckage, which is why a collapse is not a death but a turnover — nine or ten hours and the new tips are up, leaning into the hole the old tower left in the sound.

They have no life cycle to speak of. They have a shift.

### Nodelings

Palm-sized, six-legged, sheathed in the same drawn silica the fronds are made of — the fine build them too, or something very like the fine, working from the inside. No eyes. What they have instead is a strain sense distributed through every joint and plate, sampling the field at some hundreds of points, and a nervous system that appears to be almost entirely devoted to running a forward model of where the quiet is going to be in the next four seconds.

That is their whole existence. The nulls in the standing field are a few tens of centimetres across, they slide, they close, they reopen elsewhere, and a nodeling must be in one or be dead. So it sprints. It arrives. It holds absolutely still, sometimes for half an hour, sometimes for a second and a half, and then it explodes across open sand toward a place that is not yet quiet but is going to be. When it is right, nothing happens. When it is wrong, it comes apart at every joint at once and the pieces are added to the soil.

They are the most numerous animals here and the most short-lived; median lifespan under six weeks, and most of that is a race they eventually lose. They eat organics from the sand with a ventral rasp, they mate in the two or three seconds when two of them happen to be sheltering in the same null, and they lay their eggs — this is the good part — cemented in rows to the underside of a sifter.

A sifter is a large mass on the rock, and a large mass on the rock damps it. Every sifter walks in a little travelling shadow of quiet about a metre across, and every sifter is therefore encrusted with the eggs of animals that will spend their lives running from precisely the thing the sifter is protecting them from. A big old sifter carries several thousand. It doesn't seem to mind.

### The pylon tenants

The nine pylons cast null-shadows a few metres long, and those shadows do not move. On a terrace where every other quiet place is a flicker, they are the only permanent addresses, and they have been colonised the way islands are colonised.

Seven species of nodeling occur in the pylon shadows and nowhere else on the mountain. Their total combined range is about eleven square metres.

They have all become strange in the same direction: slower. Freed from the sprint, they have thrown away the sprint. The one we call the abbot has legs so reduced it can barely cross its own shadow in an hour; it grazes the same square of sand for a year at a time and lives, we think, forty years, which on this terrace is a geological span. It has begun to grow ornament, which nothing else here can afford: fluted crests, a fringe of glass filaments along the dorsal plates that serve no purpose anyone has found. It is the only animal on Adze that is decorated.

Two of the seven are found in a single shadow apiece. The pylon-four population is estimated at under three hundred individuals, total, ever, at any moment, since the pylons were put there.
