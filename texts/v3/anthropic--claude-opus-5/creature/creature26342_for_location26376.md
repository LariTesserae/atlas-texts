Start with the obvious one, and be prepared to be wrong about it twice.

## The smelter, up close

You will have been walking beside it for an hour before you accept that it is walking too.

Nothing about it reads as an animal at the usual distance. It has no head, no face, no front in the sense you mean. What it has is a direction, maintained for weeks, and six legs that come down in a sequence so slow you can watch each foot settle: a millstone of horn and mineral, a hand's breadth of give in the joint above it, the whole mass easing onto the pavement with a soft *chock* you feel in your shins rather than hear. Then nothing for four minutes. Then another.

Underneath, along the midline, is the mill. Two plated lips take in gravel; behind them, a sorting throat that graduates from coarse to fine and can reject a stone it doesn't want by simply not lifting it, so that the animal leaves a trail of refused pebbles at intervals, like punctuation. The mill turns at about the speed of a minute hand. Put your ear to a stone thirty metres out and you can hear it through the ground, a grinding so low it is more like weather than sound.

The hide is dated. Scales are secreted at the base of each plate-field and pushed outward over the years, so that the newest material is a wet iridescent lip near the spine and the oldest is out at the flank margin, thick, pitted, colonised, sometimes carrying a lichen map two centuries old that is riding an animal and does not know it. Bore a core through a flank plate and you can count backward through fog tides, through the two dry decades in the middle of the last long year, through a season when something the animal ate ran high in cobalt and laid down a blue line you could see from a metre away.

It smells like wet stone and hot coins. Close in, under the belly, there is also a faint sourness, unmistakable to anyone who has worked with cells, and a warmth — two degrees, perhaps three, above the air. Nothing on this plateau wastes heat, and two degrees is a fortune.

It cannot see you. There are light-sensitive strips along the outer vanes, adequate for knowing that the lid is above and the ground below and that something the size of a barge has come between it and the general glow, which is a thing that has never once happened in its life and which it therefore has no response to. It tastes the ground through its feet. It reads depletion the way you read a footprint: this square kilometre has been through a mill recently, the fines are gone, the joints are stripped, turn. It knows where it has been because it can taste where it has been, and the taste fades over about forty years, and that is the whole of its navigation and the whole of its memory, and it is enough to produce a foraging circuit of extraordinary elegance which no individual has ever perceived.

## What is inside it

Chambers. Nine large and a great many small, arranged along the length like the holds of a ship, and in them a working culture that is not the animal's and never was.

The animal supplies: crushed rock, fog-water gathered by the combs, a body temperature held two degrees above a cold world, and a standing potential of about eight hundred millivolts maintained across a membrane the area of a tennis court, folded. The culture supplies: everything else. It strips the trace metals, it reduces them, it lays them down in sheets on the membrane in the order their potentials allow, so that a smelter running on good ground plates copper first and nickel later and, in a bad decade, plates almost nothing and lives on its reserves and goes thin in the flank.

The foil peels away along the flanks because there is nowhere else for it to go. This is worth being clear about. The animal is not producing foil for any reason. It is producing foil the way you produce carbon dioxide. The plateau's entire economy of soil, the drifts, the ankle-deep bright litter in every lee hollow, the metre of true earth a metre down, the mites, the felt, the kites, all of it is downstream of an excretion that no organism involved has any opinion about.

## Why they are never together

Each smelter carries a field, and each smelter can feel another's at something over a kilometre.

They do not approach. It isn't shyness and it isn't territory; there is no fight to be had and no message being sent. It is closer to the way two magnets in a bowl arrange themselves — an aversion of a fixed radius, operating continuously for a hundred and fifty thousand years, which distributes five hundred animals across a hundred thousand square kilometres in a spacing so even that if you mapped them you would suspect design.

That is the whole of their social life. Two smelters have never met. Two smelters have often come within a kilometre and turned, both of them, over the following week, with no more drama than a compass needle finding north.

## The one exception

A smelter dies of running down. The plating slows, the flank goes thin, and — this is the part the others notice — the field sags.

Weeks before there is any corpse, the aversion switches off. The nearest neighbour, which has spent a thousand years being politely repelled by a presence over the horizon, feels the pressure ease and drifts. It arrives long after; it grazes the enriched ground for a decade or two, taking in a soil no other ground on the plateau offers, and then the aversion of the *next* neighbour, inherited across the gap, pushes it away again. This is as close as the species comes to attendance, and it is not attendance.

Meanwhile the corpse opens.

In the smaller chambers, laid down over the last several thousand years and never yet warm enough or wet enough or acid enough to do anything, are the eggs. Decay supplies all three conditions at once. What hatches is not small — a smelter's egg is the size of a fist and its hatchling the size of a dog — and what hatches is not many; perhaps forty, into a two-hundred-year feast that will kill nearly all of them. They eat the mother's chambers and the mother's plates and each other, and the fifty-tonne mine that draws pilgrims from four hundred kilometres is being dismantled from the inside faster than from the outside.

At the end of it, on average, one walks away. Not two. The spacing does not permit two, and the spacing is enforced from the day the young animals' own fields come up, at around year sixty, when the siblings that remain begin to find each other unbearable and disperse along the grain in different directions, never to be within a kilometre of one another again.

One in, one out, every three hundred years or so. The population has been five hundred and some for as long as the drifts have been accumulating. It is not a balance anyone is maintaining. It is just what that particular aversion, at that particular radius, on that particular area of ground, comes to.

## The passengers

Two degrees is a fortune, and there is a fauna that has organised itself entirely around the fact.

Mites winter in the vane grooves in numbers that make the grooves look furred. Foil-lice colonise the fresh flank plate and are carried, over a decade, out to the margin and shed with it — dispersal by conveyor. There are burrowing things in the plate-fields that are effectively parasites and effectively also the animal's cleaning system, and nobody has determined which they are more of.

And there is the shade-grazer.

It is the size of a cat, low, pale, six-legged, and it lives its entire life in the belly-shadow of a smelter. It is born there — the young are carried on the mother's back and set down under the same roof — and it dies there, some eighty years later, having travelled perhaps three thousand kilometres without ever choosing a direction. It eats the fines the mill drops and the drip that comes off the internal channels. Its eyes are enormous and are only ever used at dusk-under-dusk, a light within a light, four lux beneath a lid. It has never been rained on. It has never been in the open. When its animal begins to die and the shadow slows and finally stops moving, the shade-grazers walk out — the only walk they ever take across bare pavement — and go, with a directional sense nobody has explained but which is almost certainly the field, toward a roof that is still travelling. Most do not make it. The ones that do arrive after months and slip under the belly of a stranger and resume.

## The wicks

Now put the smelters down, because by weight they are not the story.

A metre below you, in the joints of the pavement, the water travels in films for years, and in the films live the wicks: soft, segmented, forearm-length, blind, pale as boiled root. There are more kilograms of wick under this plateau than of everything above it combined, and you will never see one.
