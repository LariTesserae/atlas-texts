## Who is on the wall

You will meet them by hand before you meet them by eye.

Put your palm flat on the inner rind anywhere in an inhabited stalk and wait. Under the hum — which you feel in the bones of the face, and which you will stop noticing in about nine days, and which will have taken the top third of your hearing by the end of a year — under that, there are taps. Not many. Four or five in a minute, from three or four directions, at different distances, arriving slightly separated so that you can feel which are near and which have come a mile around the curve of the wall. Most of it is nothing: *here*, *still here*, *ice*, *going down*, *the mats are wet*. The equivalent of a cough in another room. It is the sound of a household the size of a mountain keeping loose track of itself.

Then something answers you, because you have been leaning on the wall like an idiot and your pulse has been broadcasting, and a hundred yards above you a light-tissue patch is briefly interrupted by a shape, and the shape comes down.

They come down very fast. That is the first thing. Descent is the one direction this world gives away free, and they take it — a controlled fall on a gum-set fibre line, braked with a wrap around the thigh and a plate of shed rib held in the off hand as a friction shoe, the wall going past at a speed that would kill them if the line parted, which it does, sometimes, and which is the second most common way to die here.

## Bodies made in thirty years

Do not look for a people shaped by this place. There has not been time. The range is younger than most of the adults in it. Everything you see on their bodies is wear, not inheritance.

So: forearms out of proportion to everything else, and shoulders that have thickened forward, and a slight permanent flexion in the fingers so that an open hand is never quite open. The palms are not callused the way a rope-worker's are; they are callused in bands, at the finger roots and the heel, with soft skin between, because the grip here is always a hook and never a squeeze. Nails grow thick and get filed back to the quick weekly; a long nail catches on fibre and peels off backward, and you will meet people who have lost all ten and do not much mind.

They are, most of them, shorter than you and nothing like as heavy, and they have a gait on flat ground like a man walking on the deck of a ship he does not trust. The younger ones — the ones born in, who have never in their lives stood on anything level for longer than it takes to eat — are worse. Put them on the plain and within an hour they are nauseated, sweating, looking for something to put their back against. They call it the flat-sickness and they are not embarrassed by it. Their parents, who grew up on nothing but flat, find it very funny and also unbearable.

Ears: ruined. Uniformly, progressively, by age. The old ones are stone deaf to air and exquisite to tremor; you can watch a woman of seventy read four conversations at once off the wall with her forearm laid along it, eyes shut, correcting her grandchildren's grammar by feel.

Hair is shaved or bound flat under a skullcap of oiled sift-skin, because loose hair in a twenty-knot updraught is a torment, and because braid hunt by sound and a bound head is a quiet head.

Everything they carry is on a lanyard. Everything. Knife, ember-basket, water gourd, the spool, the child. There is no dropping something here and picking it up; there is only dropping something and listening for it to hit, two miles down, if you are high, and often not hearing it at all. An untethered person — a person who has taken off their lines and is sitting on a ledge with nothing between them and the floor — is a person in trouble, and will be approached the way you would approach someone standing on a bridge rail. Occasionally it means something ceremonial. Mostly it does not.

## Names, and the fact that they are drummed

A person's name is a rhythm. Three to seven beats, with a shape to the spacing; you learn your children's from before they can walk, by tapping it on their sternum. Names are not words and cannot be pronounced, only performed, and everyone can recognise perhaps two hundred of them the way you recognise footsteps in a corridor.

Because voices are for grief and for very close conversation, a spoken name carries only two meanings. Either the named person is dead, or the speaker's mouth is within a hand's breadth of their ear. There is no third case. Consider what this does to a marriage. Consider what it does to a funeral, where for the first and last time a name is said aloud at volume, over and over, by everyone who knew it, into the roar — the only occasion in this world when people shout, and they shout a name, and the range does not notice.

## The work, which is all one work

Everything they do is a consequence of the growth rate.

The stalk puts on a man's height a day at the tip, and everything below the tip rides up with it. Therefore every structure, every trail, every stored thing, every marked place, is climbing away from where it should be at a rate of roughly a hundred fathoms a year. Therefore the entire material culture is an answer to a single question: *how do you build something you will have to move by the length of your own body, tomorrow, and again the day after, forever?*

The answer they arrived at, in a hurry, in the first five years, in conditions of considerable panic:

**Nothing is fixed. Everything is hung, and everything that is hung is hung on a running line.** A dwelling is a basket of lashed rib-plate with a floor of woven fibre, slung from four lines that go up to anchors bitten into the wall. Each morning — "morning" being a matter of drum consensus rather than light — someone pays out the lines by an arm's length. It takes four minutes. A child can do it and usually does. An unpaid household is a household where something has gone wrong, and neighbours read this off the wall within a day, by the angle of the taps.

**Anchors are consumable.** Gum-set fibre pegs driven into drilled seats; they ride up, they get too high, they are abandoned. An old stalk wall above a long-occupied zone is a vertical ruin-field of peg stubs and rope-burn, a strip thousands of feet tall recording exactly how long people have been there and how many of them. This is the closest thing to a written history anyone has.

**Hearths are baskets.** A gum-sealed pot of embers on its own line, lowered daily, fed on dried climber dung and fibre, never allowed out. There is no ore and no flint. Fire came here with the people and has not been re-lit since; every hearth in the region is a descendant by contact of one of about sixty fires that survived the first winter, and people can tell you which. Two households whose fire-lines cross are related, in a sense that matters for marriage.

**Trails go down.** A route is cut into the inner wall as a stair of gum-and-peg, and it is cut downward continuously, at the growth rate, by whoever uses it, so that a well-travelled stair is being extended at the bottom every day and dying at the top every day and is always the same length. The stair is a standing wave. No part of it is the part that was there a year ago. This is a common figure in their talk about the self.

## The re-cutters

The one profession that is entirely about memory.

You cannot keep a record here. Carve something into the wall and it leaves — a hundred fathoms a year, straight up, out of the light-tissue zone, into the rain band, into the rime, gone. The people's response is a small guild of men and women whose whole work is copying: every year, the carved post, the tally-face, the marriage-marks, the map of a stalk's seams and burrows, all of it re-cut a hundred fathoms lower by hand, faithfully, from the original, before the original goes too high to reach.

They are very serious and they are not trusted very much. Everybody knows what copying does. Everybody has seen a tally-face whose bottom third is crisp and whose top third has gone strange — a mark that was once a climber-count now clearly, to the modern eye, a bird; a marriage-line that has acquired a fork it did not have. The oldest continuously re-cut record in the region is thirty-one years old and has been copied thirty-one times and no one alive can read the first six feet of it, though four separate families will tell you, at length, and differently, what it says.

Above the re-cutters' reach, on the wall of every inhabited stalk, there is a zone of original inscriptions rising forever into the ice: the true text, unreadable, unreachable, going up at a hundred fathoms a year with the dead.

## The two populations of one people

Down at the base, on the warm steaming mats where the sinkers graze on what has fallen, there are the floor-people: herders, mostly, and net-hangers, and the renderers who boil sift down for oil. It is rich there. It is also under the shaft, and twice an hour a slab of feather-ice the size of a cart comes down out of two miles of dark and detonates. Floor-people live under lashed rib-plate roofs pitched like shields and they listen constantly and they die young in a particular way that everyone recognises. They are the wealthy ones. They eat meat daily. They are pitied.

Up on the walls, between about eight hundred and six thousand feet, there are the wall-people, thinner, poorer, safer, working the light-tissue for climbers and for the bitter green scrape that they eat when there is nothing else and which brings on a mild, reliable, unpleasant clarity that they use for decision-making and for grief.

The two argue endlessly. They intermarry constantly. A wall-daughter marrying down to the floor is a well-known song, usually a sad one.

## The ones who remember the plain

There are perhaps six hundred people alive who were adults thirty years ago, when this was a flat black grassless plain with a horizon on it, and they herded sinkers across it, and the sky went all the way to the ground on every side.

They are a separate nation now, inside the people, and they know it.

They remember distance as a thing you could see across rather than fall through. They remember weather that came from somewhere else. They remember the first spring: the plain splitting in a scatter across three hundred miles, and the shoots coming up at the height of a man a day, and how for the first month it was a marvel, and for the second month it was a wonder, and by the end of the first year it was a wall across the world and the herds could not be walked to water anymore because the range had made its own weather and the water was now falling inside, on a wall, going down while the air went up.

They are the ones who worked out, by trial, at enormous cost, in roughly six years, everything above: the lanyard, the running line, the ember-basket, the tap-grammar, burial-by-abandonment. They know they invented it. This is doctrinally important to them and they say it out loud, which is to say they say it very close to your ear: *we made this up, and it is not old, and it may be wrong.* Their grandchildren find this embarrassing and a little frightening, the way any child is frightened to hear a parent admit to improvisation, and are already smoothing it into something that sounds inherited.

They also refuse, most of them, to die on the wall. The old plain-born walk out, when it is time, onto the flat between stalks, four or nine miles of open ground where nothing crosses and nothing lives, and sit down on the level and let the cold have them. It is the one place in this world where a body will stay where it is put.

The wall-born think this is horrifying. To lie down on flat ground at the bottom of everything and simply *remain* — not to go up, not to join the long white gallery, not to keep gaining a man's height a day forever — strikes them as a kind of annihilation, and they will not do it, and their grandparents will not do anything else.

## What they will not talk about on the wall

Three things, and they will only discuss them mouth-to-ear, which is to say in the register of grief:

That the plain is not ground. There is no rock here. The flat between the stalks is warm underfoot, and it drums, and when you cut it, it weeps clear sap that stiffens in an hour. Everyone knows this. Nobody likes the conclusion, which is that there are not many thousand stalks, and the crossing between them is not a journey between two things.

That nobody knows what is burning down there. Something in the root mass is being consumed, hugely and slowly, to drive the draught. In thirty years the fires have not slackened. There is a great deal of it, whatever it is.

That no stalk has flowered.

This last is the shape of their whole future and they have no tradition for it, because they have no tradition older than thirty years, and the old plain-born will tell you frankly, close to your ear, that a thing that grows like this grows *toward* something. They have watched the seasons of grass all their lives. They know the sequence. Spear, stem, head, seed, and then the field is dead standing and the whole business begins again from the ground.

They have got to *stem*.

## What actually lives here

The climbers live on the light-tissue. The braid live on the climbers. The sift are lifted by the draught, and feed on spore-fog on the way up, and mate at the freezing line, and fall out of the top dead, and are eaten as they fall. The sinkers live on what fell. The people live on the sinkers, and the sift, and the climbers, and the plate the climbers shed, and the fibre of the wall, and the gum of the wall, and the heat of the wall, and the light of the wall, and the water on the wall, and they move their houses down it a man's height a day so as not to be carried away.

Ask them who lives here and they will not name themselves first. They will lay a hand flat on the rind, palm down, the way you would rest your hand on the flank of a large animal you are standing beside in the dark — to know it is still breathing, and to be sure it knows you are there.

They are a film on a surface. So is everything else.

The thing they are a film on has not yet done the thing it is doing.
