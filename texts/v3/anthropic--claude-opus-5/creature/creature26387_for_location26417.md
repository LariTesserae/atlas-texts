## Tenancy

First, a distinction, because the place invites confusion on this point.

Held is not the same as living. The eels are held. They are the sump's condition, the way the tidemark is, and if you drained the tank and put them in a bucket they would be an entirely different animal with an entirely different history — they belong to the water and not to Number Six. The moths pass through. Ten days is a long life for one, and they inherit nothing, and they make no arrangements. The eleven rosettes in the second bay are not living so much as being deposited, at an inch a decade, by the same process that makes the drapes.

To live in a place you have to keep something.

Two things in Number Six keep something. One of them is in the flue. The other one takes the rungs at the sides.

## The Tender

There is a resident, and the resident has a job, and the job is older than the resident and probably older than the idea that it needed doing.

It has come to the temperature of the room. This should be understood before anything else about its body, because it is the reason nobody has seen it and it is the reason two men have touched it. Skin at blood plus a little, exactly the plate temperature of Numbers One, Three and Four, which means that the slow coarse heat-sight you develop down there — the sense that lets you find the west bay by your cheek — is blind to it entirely. It does not read as a body. In 1931 a district surveyor put his hand flat on it in the slot of the middle way, at the level of a man's chest, and wrote in his notes *bolster of loose lagging, warm, secure*, and turned his shoulders and went on past. The same error the eels get. The place is full of things that are taken for lagging until they move.

Height, as near as the polish on the slot can say, five foot four. Build: reduced. Everything that could be given up has been given up, and what is left is hands, feet, ribcage and gut. The hands are the largest part of it. There are pads at the outer edges of the palms, thick as a dog's, from a lifetime of taking rungs and pipes at the sides and never round the front, and the same pads on the outer edges of the knees, from the low way, where the wear in the north channel is polished to a width that is one body's width and always has been. It smells of pennies. Not on the hands: through. The metal has gone all the way in, and its sweat is faintly cupric, and its teeth are chalk from drinking the sump, and there is a stiffness coming into the small joints that anybody who works with old plant would recognise at once. It is calcifying. Slowly, at the rate everything happens here. In three or four hundred years it will be indistinguishable from the drapes on the No. 3 header, and the tapping test will be the only way to tell.

Its eyes are intact and it keeps them open. There is nothing for them to do. They have no aversion to being open in the dark, so they are open, wide, all the time, and the effect if you ever did get a lamp down there — you will not — would be very bad, and would be entirely your fault.

## The Round

It works. That is the thing that is hardest to convey and easiest to observe.

It goes the three routes in a fixed order, and it goes them roughly nine times a day, and the day is not a day but an interval it holds with no reference to the sky at all, and the interval has not drifted in the length of the surviving record. It puts its palm on Number Two and counts four. It puts the flat of two fingers on each of the other three vessels for a shorter count, which nobody has been able to work out the purpose of, since the other three do nothing. It goes along the second-level main with its knuckles trailing the lagging crust and it finds the new breaks. It cannot mend them. There is nothing to mend them with, and every thread in Number Six is galled shut, and it has known this longer than anyone. It finds them anyway, and puts a knuckle in each new one, and that appears to be the whole of the transaction: the wound is found, the wound is registered, the round goes on.

It never uses the catwalk. It has not used the catwalk in living memory, and the mesh at the north end has a skin of deposit on it, unbroken, that a bootless foot would break.

## The Count, and Where It Is Kept

There are four hundred and six drip tips. The description above says *perhaps four hundred*, which is what a visitor gets in an hour with their hands over their ears. Four hundred and six, of which nine are provisional — young, wetting rather than dropping, not yet on the roll. When a provisional tip makes its first clean fall the count goes up by one, and this is the largest event in a decade, larger than a bearing seizure, and it is marked.

Marking is done on the sump lip. A score in the soft deposit with a thumbnail, one score per year, and here is the thing about the record: the deposit closes over the old scores at very nearly the rate the new ones are cut, so that the tally standing at any moment is about seventy marks long, and the marks at the far end are shallowing, and then they are a texture, and then they are the lip. The tally never gets longer. It has never in its existence been longer than a life.

So the resident's memory is exactly as long as the visible portion, and no longer, and it has no way of knowing this. It knows seventy-odd years back, in the detail of somebody who has had nothing else to attend to, and behind that there is a smooth warm lip of mineral where the years used to be. It does not experience this as loss. Nothing here does. The rust has stopped; the level is the level; the count is four hundred and six.

The logbook, in the niche by the No. 3 firebox, is swollen shut and will never be opened, and this is a decision, not an accident. It knows what opening a page like that does. It has the copperplate hand by heart from before the page closed, or it has the fact of the hand by heart, which is nearly the same and lasts better.

## What It Eats

Bread, dripping, cold potato, and whatever else is in the bucket.

There is a rope and there is a bucket and there has been an arrangement since at least the ironmonger's, which became a laundry, which became a lock-up let by the month to a man who does not know what he is doing or why Fridays. He lowers it through the bars without lifting the grate. He believes there is a dog. He has never heard a dog. The bucket comes up empty and the mud in it smoothed flat with a palm, and once or twice a year there is something in the bucket coming the other way: a shilling, worn smooth, warm to the hand, out of circulation since before his father; or a two-foot length of half-inch copper capillary, coiled neat, cut clean at both ends. He has eleven of the coils in a drawer. He cannot throw them out. He could not tell you why, and if you pressed him he would get unpleasant about it.

Besides the bucket: the moths that die on the warm plate of Number Two, which drift there and dry there, and which it sweeps up with the side of one hand. It does not touch the eels. Whatever that is — arrangement, compact, sheer long familiarity — it is old and it is kept, and it is the only rule in Number Six with two parties to it.

## Rain

Rain is the one thing that happens to it.

When the alley gutter overruns and the cold rope comes down the throat, it goes to the north channel and stands in it. This is the whole of its year, in the way that Christmas is the whole of somebody else's. For the hour of the rain it is cold, properly cold, twenty degrees below the room, and for two days afterward it is the only cold thing standing in Number Six and could be found by anybody with their palm out — which is to say it is visible, once a fortnight, for a day and a half, and nobody has ever been down at the right time.

It smells the street off itself for days afterward and does not appear to like it.

## The Rule

It does not bring flame down the throat, and it does not permit flame down the throat, and if you come down with a lamp you will not be attacked but you will be watched from the far side of the No. 4 cradle with a steadiness that will end your visit early and honestly. The reason for the rule went out of the world about 1907. It has outlived its reason. That is not a weakness of the rule. Most of the resident is made of things that have outlived their reasons and are kept anyway: the count, the interval, the palm on Number Two for four, the round nine times a day past plant that will never run again. Take those away and there is nothing standing in the slot of the middle way but five foot four of calcifying meat at the temperature of the air.

It is not superstitious. It is structural.

## The Other Count

Once a fortnight the relief valve on the No. 3 header lifts by itself and sighs for four seconds. Wherever the resident is when this happens, it stops and puts a palm flat against the nearest iron and holds it there until the sigh is finished.

Once a year, at the coolest point — autumn above, though it has no word for autumn — it goes up the ladder-frame at the north end of the west bay, which it uses on no other occasion, and reaches through the broken sight-glass port and holds its palm an inch from the warm papery mass, and is noticed, and comes down. That is all. It has never touched it. It has no name for it, and it has nothing to say about it, and if it kept a logbook the entry would be a single mark.
