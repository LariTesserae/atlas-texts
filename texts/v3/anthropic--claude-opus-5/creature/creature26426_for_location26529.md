## Counting

Stand still for twenty minutes and the place will begin to declare its population. Not because anything comes out — nothing here has anywhere to come out from — but because your eye adjusts to the correct scale, which is small, and to the correct clock, which is slow, and the two adjustments have to be made in that order.

The census is short. Perhaps a dozen organisms here are large enough to point at. Perhaps forty thousand are not. Almost all of them are old, all of them are poor, and most of them are the only one of their kind in the sink — a population of one, with no mate, no rival, and no prospect of either.

They are not hanging on. That is the thing to get right about them. Nothing here is at the edge of survival; the edge is somewhere else entirely, up on the plain, where every year is a gamble on rain. Down here the terms are settled. What lives here lives at a rate that matches the water, and the water does not vary, and so neither do they. They are not enduring the place. They fit it, the way a hand fits a worn tread.

## The fig

Everything else in the sink is a tenant of the fig, and the fig arrived by accident, once, in a bird.

There is no other way it could have come. Some bird — a bulbul, a warbler, something with a crop full of figs from a wadi eight or ten kilometers off — crossed this dry tableland, and either perched on the low dry-laid wall or simply passed over, and the seeds it dropped were the size of grit, and one of them went into a joint in the northern wall three meters below the rim, into a pocket of dust and its own packaging, in the one band of rock in this entire hundred square kilometers that is permanently damp. That single event, lasting under a second, is the largest thing that has happened in the sink since the roof fell.

The trunk is the thickness of an arm and tells you nothing about the tree's age. A fig on rock is regularly killed and regularly refuses it: a hard frost, a bad decade, a goat when there were goats, and it goes back to the stump and starts again from the root collar. The visible tree may be sixty years old. The individual — the thing that grew from that seed — could be five hundred.

Its real body is the part pressed against the wall. Twelve feet of root, flattened to the width of a belt and no thicker than leather, poured into the vertical rock and following the seep down with an accuracy that looks like knowledge. Where the rock offers a hairline, the root goes in and thickens, and the hairline widens by some fraction of a millimeter, and the root goes deeper. This is the only intention in the sink. It is being executed at a rate of about a millimeter a decade, and it is aimed, whether the fig means it or not, at the block above the fern cushion, which is held in place by nothing but friction and habit. In three or four hundred years the fig will drop that block onto the ramp and change the light in this room, and everything living here will have to renegotiate.

Meanwhile it does the other essential work: it eats the sunlight before anyone else can, and it feeds them all anyway. Its canopy leans out over the void and takes the top of the light column, so that the sink is lit the color of weak tea; and every autumn it lets go of five or six thousand leaves, rough as a cat's tongue, and they go down into the dark and become the entire imported wealth of the place. There is no other carbon coming in worth mentioning. Dust, pollen from crops fifty kilometers upwind, the occasional dead insect. Otherwise: fig.

It fruits and the fruit is a joke. Small green figs form, harden, hollow, and drop, because the wasp that would enter them is not here and will never be here — the nearest caprifig grove is beyond any wasp's range, and a female Blastophaga lives two days. So each fig on this tree is a sealed chamber with a tight entrance no animal ever came through, full of flowers that were never touched, and it falls into the sink and gets glazed over with calcite. The tree is producing small stone models of the room it lives in, about four hundred a year.

## The fern, twice

The maidenhair under the northern lip is one individual, almost certainly, spread across two square meters by a rhizome no thicker than wire, ninety years of creeping through wet grit at the pace of a thumbnail per season. Its leaflets refuse water: a drop that lands on them beads, hesitates, and rolls off, which is why the whole cushion trembles and none of it is ever actually wet. The fern is dry on top and standing in a permanent shallow film below, which is the arrangement it wants.

The second fern lives under the first, and you will not see it without kneeling and lifting the fronds. Green scales the size of a paring from a fingernail, pressed flat to the wet grit, heart-shaped, one cell thick at the edges. These are the other half of the fern's life, the half that has the sex. The sperm are motile and must swim, and the water they swim in is thinner than a page, and it is there at all times because of the drip. This is the only mating that occurs in the sink. It occurs continuously, in a film, among the roots of the mother, whose offspring have nowhere to go because she is already occupying every square centimeter that is wet.

So the fern also is a population of one, doing the full ceremony of two sexes forever, in a closet, to no demographic effect whatsoever.

## The ones the drip carries

The liverworts on the wet stone are crescent-cup: flat green rosettes, forked, slightly greasy-looking, and on the upper surface of each thallus, little cups shaped like new moons. In the cups are gemmae — beads of tissue, a few dozen cells each, complete miniature liverworts requiring only to be moved. They do not fly. They do not walk. They wait for a drop of water to strike the cup and splash them, and the range of a splash is about two hand-spans.

Everything the liverwort has ever accomplished, it has accomplished two hand-spans at a time.

There are three colonies, and they mark, precisely, three drips. A liverwort here is not a plant so much as a stain that grows outward from a falling point of water at a rate of a few centimeters a decade, until it reaches rock that is only nearly wet, and stops.

## The mason

The grey-black crust on the dry blocks, and on the low wall up at the rim, is not one thing. Each patch is a consortium — a fungus farming a green alga, with yeasts in the cortex and bacteria in the interstices, an arrangement so old it has forgotten it was ever an arrangement. It grows at somewhere between a tenth and a half a millimeter a year, which means that some of the larger patches on the wall are older than the wall's builders' grandchildren.

It is also the only mason still working. The description of the wall is exact: the lichen has run across the joints and sealed them like scar tissue. That is not a metaphor from the lichen's side; that is structural. The dry-laid stones are now held together, in part, by a living skin, and if you scrubbed the skin off, the wall would loosen inside a generation.

On the drier blocks below, the same guild is doing the opposite work: secreting acid, etching the calcite it sits on, dissolving a bed for itself a few microns deep. Building on top, unbuilding underneath. It has no plan and no hurry and it will be here when the fig has dropped the block and grown back and dropped another.

The hand-sized patch of plaster survives for the same reason. A lichen capped it early, and has been slowly eating it ever since, and the eating is slower than the weathering would have been. It is preserved by what consumes it. If there was ever a mark under that lichen, the lichen now is the mark.
