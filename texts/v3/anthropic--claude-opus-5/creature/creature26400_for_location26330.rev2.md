# The Inhabitants

Everyone here is a mason or a tenant of masons, and everyone here is quiet. Those two facts explain nearly all the rest. Hold them while you go down.

---

## The crust-dwellers

The martins weigh less than a folded letter and they are the colour of wet soot, which is not an accident: in a valley lit by vertical shafts, anything pale is a spark moving across a dark wall, and sparks are taken. A martin crossing a light shaft is visible for a quarter of a second and then is simply not there any more.

They build with river clay from one bank — a single communal quarry at the third sink, a shallow bowl worn into the silt, polished by a million small bills, the only place in the valley where a crowd ever forms. Even there, nothing is said. They mix the clay with saliva from glands that swell in April until the floor of the mouth is visibly thickened, and they lay it in beads, and the beads set hard as pottery in the saturated air. The pot is round, the neck angled downward so that the permanent drip runs off the shoulder of it and not in. The neck is also narrow — narrow by a specific, measured margin, and you will hear about that margin again.

What they have instead of song is the wall.

Put your ear against the crust in June. Press it flat to the cold clay and wait. What comes through is not silence. It is a low continuous ticking, thousands of small percussions conducted through a metre and a half of bonded mud and root, layered, overlapping, arriving from every direction at once — a city talking through its floorboards. Each bird taps the shell of its own pot with the tip of the bill. The sound travels through the matrix and not through the air. The owl on the arch above hears nothing at all. This is why the crust is never repaired at the joins where it might separate; a bird that finds its section gone silent, isolated, cut off from the general hum by a crack, abandons it within a week and rebuilds elsewhere. Acoustic continuity is the value of a nest site. They do not know this. They simply cannot bear the quiet ones.

The neck of each pot has a shallow dished place worn smooth by the bill of whoever built it: a knock-plate. A returning adult lands in darkness, in mist, on a wall of eleven thousand identical doors, and finds its own by touching — by the grit-pattern pressed into the lip, by rhythm, by smell. Old birds have bills scoured blunt and shining at the tip. You can age them by it.

They do not leave upward. To launch, a martin drops. At first light they peel off the crust and fall — sixty metres, straight down the cold, gathering speed in the heavy air — and then flatten out a few feet above the pools and go along the valley beneath the arches like something thrown. They come home the other way: in the afternoon the sunlit western wall makes a thin, reliable updraught, and they spiral up it in loose, silent turns, a slow grey smoke rising against the rock for an hour before dusk. Twice a day the valley inhales and exhales birds.

In the fledging week of late July the talus below the great overhang becomes a killing floor, because a young martin's first flight is a fall it does not yet know how to end, and the brown one knows the date better than they do.

## The tenants of the dead city

The living generation is a skin. Under it is forty or fifty generations of silted pots, and that is where most of the valley's population actually lives.

Bumblebees — enormous, black-furred, cold-engined — take the upper galleries and the abandoned chambers in the talus, and they are the loudest thing in the valley and the only thing with a right to be. Nothing can catch them worth the trouble; nothing can eat them once caught. On a still noon in July you can stand on the rim eighty metres up and hear one working the saxifrage ledges, a slow burr going back and forth beneath you, obscene in all that hush, like a man whistling in a church.

There is a small dark bird that lives in the crust and never leaves it — never leaves the valley, never crosses the rim, possibly never in its life sees the plateau. It is round, wren-shaped, the brown of wet bark, and it runs on the vertical like a mouse. It nests in a silted pot in the third stratum down and it feeds on what falls: mites out of the old clay, spiders, the dropped and the dead. In February, when the crust is a sheet of ice and the martins are four thousand kilometres south, it is still there, working the underside of the overhang where the ice cannot reach, the only warm-blooded thing moving on that whole wall.

Snails graze the outside of occupied nests, and are permitted, because they take the algae film and do no other harm. They are absurdly large for their kind and their shells are thin as fingernail — all that limestone in the drip, all that damp, nothing to dry them out. You can see the dark animal through the shell. At night in August the crust is crawling with them, a slow migration up the face and back down before light, leaving mucus tracks that the next day's drip erases.

And roots. The whole crust is stitched together by roots — moss rhizoids, fern, the fine white netting of saxifrage and grass, coming out of every silted pot and running down through the layers below. The clay alone would slough in four years. The clay plus the roots holds for a century. The martins, given a choice, will always build on a rooted section, and they are always given a choice, and so the plants and the birds hold the wall up together without either party knowing there is another party.

## The diggers

Up close they are unglamorous: a heavy, blunt, deliberate animal about the weight of a spaniel, with a double coat that sheds water like a beaver's and a face that is mostly forehead. They move as though every action costs, and it does: they live on sedge and willow-leaf on a floor that frosts in July, and the whole economy of the colony runs on not wasting heat.

Look at the forepaws. The palm carries a pad thickened into a broad grey plate, and the underside of the chin is bald and calloused and slightly dished, because that is the trowel — clay is packed with the heel of the hand and then smoothed with the chin, in short sideways strokes, with the head turned. An old digger's incisors are bevelled to a chisel edge from scraping rather than cutting. Their bellies are stained the colour of whatever bank their line quarries, and the colours differ between fans, so that a wanderer's origin can be read off it at forty metres.

Their doors are objects. This is the thing nobody expects. The last constriction of a digger's entry passage is a fitted plug — clay packed over a woven willow frame, hollowed out from behind to keep it light enough to drag, shaped to one hole and useless in any other. It is kept inside on a dry ledge during the day and seated at dusk from within, and a mustelid that has worked the talus all night finds a stopper it cannot get its teeth around in a shaft it cannot turn around in. Plugs are inherited. They are re-faced and re-fitted as an animal grows and as the doorway weathers, and a plug lying out on the stone in the rain, unseated three days running, is how a colony learns that a lineage has ended.

The rest is infrastructure. They cut drainage above their chambers before they cut the chambers. They terrace. They lay woven willow roofs and pack them with moss and then put stones on the moss. They maintain paths — and the paths are worn to a shine because they are walked by animals whose great-grandmothers walked them, and the layout of a good fan is older than anything alive on it. Young diggers spend two summers as masons before they are permitted to breed, hauling clay for other families' walls, and the very old, who no longer breed and no longer travel far, become drain-minders: after heavy rain the oldest animals in the colony go out alone in the dark and walk the channels, one at a time, patching.

They speak in two registers, both of them nearly nothing. There is a whistle so brief and so high that a human ear files it as a gap in the water-noise rather than a sound. And there is the foot: a single strike of the hind heel against bare stone, which does not travel through air at all but goes through the talus, block to block, and is felt in the soles by every digger on the fan. They use the whistle for ordinary business. They use the foot only when it no longer matters whether the brown one hears.

## The quiet hunters

The mustelid is the crust's second-most-important erosive agent, after water. It climbs by punching through — one paw driven into the old strata, then the next — and it tunnels sideways through fifty generations of nests, making galleries in the dead city, and the tunnels fill with water and split the clay in frost and bring sections down. It kills less than you would think. Most nights it finds nothing, because the crust goes still before it arrives; the stillness runs ahead of it through the wall, tapped from pot to pot, faster than it can climb. Its only sound is a soft chuffing on the rare occasions when it succeeds, and that sound goes round the valley three times, and everything below hears exactly where it happened.

The owl has a harder life than she looks to have. A stone bowl full of falling water is the worst possible place for an ear-hunter: the hush covers everything, and her prey has spent ten thousand years learning to add nothing to it. So she has become a watcher. She sits under the arches, in the black, for six and seven hours at a stretch, on a shelf whitewashed by generations of her own line, and she hunts with her eyes, and she hunts light.

Her hour is dawn in summer. The fog fills the sink to the brim overnight and burns off from the top down, and for about ten minutes there is a white floor with the arches floating above it, and the first martins of the day come up through that floor — dark, slow, briefly silhouetted against white, and utterly blind to whatever is waiting in the dark air above. She takes four or five birds a week in July and nothing at all in September. Her nest niche is floored with pellets that have composted into soil, and the soil has grown moss, and the moss has grown a fern, so that she raises her young in a small green hanging garden made of the digested remains of the colony beneath her.

## The water people

The moss is the valley's chief engineer and it should be counted among the animals for all the difference it makes. It grows in the drip and the spray, it takes lime out of the water, and it lays that lime down as stone around its own stems, so that a moss cushion here is half mineral — rigid, crumbling, weighing ten times what moss should weigh. It builds. It dams the seep-lines into terraces, it seals the weirs the caddis begin, it petrifies fallen sticks and old martin nests and the bones of anything that lies still long enough. There are shelves of it on the walls, a metre out from the rock, standing on nothing but their own accumulated skeleton, and everything that happens on those shelves — the ferns, the snails, the springtails going up like smoke when you touch them — is happening on stone the moss made.

In the pools there is a fish, one kind only, stunted and dark and slow-growing, a char left behind when the river's other route closed. It is not the same fish from sink to sink; each pool chain has kept its own for long enough that they no longer match. In autumn some of them go under the rock with the water and spend the dark months in the flooded galleries between sinks, and come out in spring thinner and paler and blinking.

And in that under-water, in the sumps and the spray-wet joints where the river goes beneath the talus, there are the white things: a blind amphipod, a flatworm, a beetle that lives its whole life in the splash and cannot swim. They are the valley's oldest residents by a long way. They predate the collapse. They were living in the cave when the cave still had a roof, and when the roof fell in, most of them died in the light, and these are the ones that were far enough back.

## Two climates in one slope

Here is the fact that ought to be better known.

In a talus fan below the eastern wall, the top two metres are warm-country refugees: filmy ferns and liverworts and a snail whose nearest relatives live in the wet gorges of a country two thousand kilometres to the southwest and nowhere in between. They are here because the air under the drip-line never dries and never freezes hard.

Five metres beneath them, in the ice lenses that never melt, there is a springtail, a mite and a small pale spider that are cold-age relicts — animals of the tundra edge and the glacier front, living in the permanent frost in the gaps between blocks, in the dark, in a pocket of the last ice age that the valley has failed to lose.

One slope. Two climates that have not coexisted anywhere else on that plateau for nine thousand years, stacked vertically within the height of a house, each keeping its own tenants.

And because there is no wind down here, and because the only way out is up, and because up is where the owl is — things here stop flying. A cranefly walks. A ground beetle with fused wing-cases; a moth whose female is a fat walking sac that never leaves the moss cushion she hatched in. Flight is expensive and this valley does not pay for it. Some of these creatures exist in one sink and not the next, separated by four hundred metres of rock they will never cross.

## Winter, and who stays

The martins go in a morning, all of them, without noise. The crust stands empty and dripping and then freezes.

The diggers seal their plugs with fresh clay from the inside and do not come out. Down under the terraces, packed in family heaps in a chamber lined with a summer's hay, they sit out five months at a body temperature only just high enough to call it living, and every few weeks somebody wakes enough to strike a heel against the stone, and the answer comes back through the talus from three fans away.

The moss goes on working under the ice, in the blue four-hour light, at a rate you would need a year to measure.

The wren-thing runs the underside of the overhang. The owl hunts it, occasionally, and mostly does not find it. The blind things in the sump notice nothing, because down there nothing has changed since before the sky came in.

---

## A last thing about tenancy

Nothing here has a long lease. The wall the martins are plastered to is coming down — the great overhang loses a bus-sized section about once in thirty years, and takes eleven thousand nests with it, and the year after that the new white scar is the most contested real estate in the valley, because fresh rock is dry rock and holds new clay best. The diggers' oldest and most beautiful terraces are the ones under the thinnest remaining roof. The bridges are being eaten from beneath.

Everything alive down there is living in the rubble of the last collapse and building against the next one, and the lineages that persist are not the ones that build best. They are the ones that, when the wall goes, are quick to the new stone.

On the rim there are ravens. They are enormous and glossy and they shout, and they shout because they do not live here: they come down only for the dead — a reindeer that walked off the edge in fog, a winter-killed fox on the talus — and they leave again before dusk. In this valley, noise is the mark of a visitor.

Which is worth remembering, standing up there at the broken plate-edge with your own boots grinding the limestone, listening to eight kilometres of green silence and thinking that not much lives down there.
