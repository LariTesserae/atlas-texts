## Who lives in the Bell

Count the people first, because the number is small and it surprises everyone. Forty-one, most years. Forty-four at the last census of the old regime, thirty-eight after the fever year, forty-one now, plus the nest crews in season, who do not count and would not wish to. Forty-one people inside a hole the size of a market town, distributed along six kilometres of catwalk in a chain of orange bubbles, each of them out of earshot of every other one permanently and by the nature of the universe.

They are called, on the plateau, *the works people*, and they call themselves that too, with a slight emphasis that the plateau does not hear.

---

**Sort them by what they have done with the noise.**

The young ones still have their hearing, and it is a nuisance to them and to everybody else. They flinch. They come off shift with their jaws aching from clenching, they sleep badly in the bunkhouse on stilts, and at meals they keep turning their heads towards the fall as though it were about to say something. They are kept out in the sixth and seventh galleries where a man can still shout, and they shout, and the old hands watch them shout with the particular patience of people watching a foreigner mispronounce a word for the third time.

At about eleven years of service the hearing goes properly and the man becomes useful. This is not a euphemism the works people use; they say it flatly. A deaf hand can work the near stations — the intake screens, the strainer pits, the rope-stages in the spray, everything inside the four-hundred-metre line where the noise stops being a sound and becomes a physical circumstance, like weather or slope. He can stand there for a full shift without his attention being eaten. He is worth roughly twice a hearing man and knows it. Promotion in the Bell is a medical event, and it arrives, and everyone congratulates him, and there is a small thing done with tobacco.

So the hierarchy inverts what you would expect. At the top of it, closest to the water, are the ones who cannot hear it at all.

---

Because of this, the working language of the Bell is signed, and the deaf are its native speakers and its grammarians, and the hearing juniors are the illiterates. It is a big language. Every sign is made at arm's scale, from the shoulder, because it has to be legible in silhouette at eight metres — at the edge of a bulb's sphere, against a black backdrop, through moving air full of drip. Fine finger-work is useless here; the Bell has no whisper. A works man discussing pipe pressures looks, from a distance, like a man semaphoring for rescue.

There are consequences. Nothing said on the catwalks can be said privately. Gossip is broadcast by definition; an argument between two men at a junction is visible to anyone in the next two lamp-spheres, and quarrels in the Bell tend therefore to be brief, formal and conducted with a certain theatrical economy, since everyone is watching and everyone has seen it before.

Which leaves the dark between the lamps. Eighty-odd metres of unlit grating between one orange sun and the next: that is the only private room in the entire system. Everything that must not be seen happens there, hand on forearm, in the close tactile dialect they call *grip* — courtships, confessions, complaints about the foreman, the settling of debts, the whole interior life of forty-one people, transacted by touch in the gaps of the lighting circuit. There is a phrase for something being said in earnest: *between the bulbs.*

---

The pipes carry sound. Six hundred metres of iron on trestles, lagged in rotting felt, and a strike with a spanner runs the length of it and arrives at the far end as a clean bright knock that cuts straight through the fall. Every junction has a hammer hung on a wire. Every worker has a call-sign in taps.

This is the Bell's telegraph, its bell-pull, its roll-call and its obituary service. Three slow and two quick is the pump house wanting a man. Five and five is water rising. A long roll of rapid strokes from anywhere in the system means someone has gone off the grating, and everything stops, and the linesman starts walking.

The linesman is the only person who knows the whole Bell. He walks the cable — six kilometres of it, out and back, with a satchel of bulbs, porcelain insulators, tape and a lamp of his own — and he spends more of his life in the dark between the bulbs than in the light of them, which the others find slightly unwholesome. He is deaf as a stone, he is the best signer in the works because he has nothing else, and his call-sign is three and a half: three strokes and a glancing fourth that nobody else can produce cleanly. When he dies the half stroke will go out of use, because it would be bad manners.

---

Now the bodies.

Nothing here is dry, and so nothing here has a horny surface. Their feet are the feet of people who have been in a bath for thirty years — white, ridged, without toenails to speak of, and prone to a fungus the plateau doctors have a Latin name for and the works people call *Bell foot*, treated with a rendered grease that smells frankly awful and works. Everyone has the same rash in the same three places: belt line, collar, boot top, wherever cloth holds water against skin. Everyone is pale in the particular way of the perpetually damp, which is not the pallor of the indoors but something more like the underside of a stone. Sent up to the plateau, they burn in forty minutes and squint for two days, and they misjudge distance badly, because eyes trained on eight-metre spheres of weak orange do not know what to do with a horizon.

They do not look at your face when you speak. They look at your hands. Plateau people find this shifty and the works people find the plateau's staring at mouths obscene, like watching someone chew.

And they are wonderful at low contrast. A works woman can read a wet slate at a light level under which you would not find your own boots, and can tell a sound pipe from a failing one by the way the drip beads on it, and can pick out a fallen tool from two chapels'-worth of black breakdown blocks by a difference in wetness that does not survive description.

---

**Nothing touches the floor.** This is the third rule of the Bell and the first one taught, because the guano slopes flow, and what flows on them will process anything organic left at ground level with an efficiency that has to be seen. A leather glove set down at the start of a shift is a lace doily by the end of it. Therefore: hooks. Hooks on every trestle, every stanchion, every rafter of the roofless bathhouse, hundreds of them, so that the works look permanently half-packed. Boots hang. Food hangs in wire baskets. Bedding hangs. The bunkhouse on stilts is not a building so much as a hanging system with walls around it.

And therefore, too, the dead are not buried. They are laid in a cut on the lower guano slope with the proper words signed over them, and in nineteen days there is nothing at all, which the works people consider clean and decent and the plateau parishes consider an abomination they have stopped arguing about. Two of the forty-one are on the slope's register as *carried up* instead, at family insistence; it is a two-day haul, it is spoken of as an extravagance, and their names are said with a small pity.

---

The great luxury of the Bell, its currency, its medal and its instrument of discipline, is dryness.

The pump house is brick with a tin roof, absurd, a building inside a cave, and the two vertical engines make it the only warm dry air in a hundred and sixty vertical metres. Each worker is allotted twenty minutes a week beside the flue. This is the *dry hour*, which is not an hour, and it is taken seriously: a man will save his up for a fortnight and take forty minutes and come out looking as though he has been on holiday. Behind the engines is the dry box, a heated cabinet where the works keeps what cannot be permitted to rot — the pay ledger, the concession papers, one photograph per person, tobacco, the sewing needles, and a stack of letters from outside that were already going soft when they arrived. Space in the box is allocated by the foreman and is the subject of the works' only real politics.

And the punishment for drunkenness, theft or dropping a tool off the catwalk is not a beating or a fine. It is docked dry time. A man on half-dry for a month becomes, visibly, over the weeks, a slower and sadder animal.

---

What pays for all of it is the birds, which is worth saying plainly, because the works people are epiphytes. Four million swiftlets above them making nests worth money, and the accumulated centuries of what the swiftlets and the bats let fall, and the entire brick-and-iron apparatus of pump and tram and dynamo is a small human callus grown on the underside of a bird colony that has no idea they are there. The birds do not acknowledge them. Not fear, not habituation — genuine absence of relation, four million animals for whom the works and their lamps and their forty-one people are simply a feature of the floor. The works people find this restful. They speak of the birds the way farmers speak of weather that has been good to them, and they do not like the nest crews at all.

The nest crews come for eleven weeks. They are contracted from three valleys east, they are aerial specialists, they go up the dome on rattan and rope with poles and headlamps and take the cups off the ceiling at a rate that makes the guano hands go quiet, and they are paid perhaps four times as much. They are not deaf; they are in and out too fast for that. They shout at each other, on the catwalks, at the tables, uselessly and constantly, and this is the thing the works people cannot forgive — not the money, the shouting. Eleven weeks of men bellowing into a roar because they have not yet understood where they are. They are called, in sign, *the loud*, which in the Bell's grammar is not far off *the unborn*.

---

And then the board.

Every month, at the same hour, the board-keeper goes to the second bench with a slate and a bent brass rule and takes the level, and chalks it under eighty-one years of previous readings in eleven hands, of which four are her own family's. She is sixty-one. She has taken four hundred and some readings. The whole column of them, end to end, records a fall of about eight centimetres.

This is her life's subject and she is entirely serious about it. The water is going down. As it goes down it hands up new chambers into the air, a metre of floor at a time, a few times a century, and each time it does the finder's name goes on the board in full instead of initials — and there are men in the bunkhouse who have forty years in and nothing but initials, and who will tell you, at length, without being asked, about a side-passage floor that broke the surface in their nineteenth year and what it smelled like.

They know how it ends. They say it the way other people describe a grandchild's future. The fall will dig its basin and go quiet; the drapes will grow; the shaft will widen and let itself down; in some unimaginable afternoon there will be a green valley here with a stream and a road, and the birds gone elsewhere, and the noise over. They speak of this as a bereavement that will happen to strangers.

Meanwhile they are the only quick things in the place. The rock is a week old and the water is an infant and the colony is a weather system, and in among all that are forty-one warm, rotting, short-lived, hook-hanging people signing at each other across eight metres of lamplight, tending circles of moss the width of a dinner plate that will die when the bulb is moved, and keeping, in chalk, on a wet board, the minutes of a meeting that will last a hundred thousand years.
