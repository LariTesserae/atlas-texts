## The Tenant

**First, the ones you can count.**

Take the census the easy way and it comes out short and strange. Eleven species of stonewort, banded by depth so exactly that a diver can read her gauge off the lime crust. Four mosses, all of them dwarfs above forty metres and giants below it, streaming two metres off the ledges because there is nothing to break them and no reason to be short. Lampweed, one species, thirty metres of black-red strap and a crown of bladders. Diatoms in the spring plume, arriving with the rock flour, dying in the dark, falling for three years. Purple-brown bacterial felt at the first sill where the salt boundary holds, eating sulphur, asking nothing of the light.

And the lace.

Which is the answer to the question, though it takes some time to become the answer, because at first it looks like weather. White filigree over a fallen weed-tree. A bloom on wet stone. The mildew you'd expect in any cold cellar. It is on everything, everywhere, at every depth, in the silt and under the silt and inside the holdfast of every lampweed that has ever been sampled — not one exception in four hundred sampled holdfasts, which is a fact people noted and then set down again because it did not seem to lead anywhere.

It leads everywhere. There is one organism in the Held Water and this is it.

---

**Its body.**

Understand the scale before anything else, because the scale is the whole difficulty.

The lace is continuous from the drowned pass road at eighteen metres to the true floor. Its cables — that is the right word, the trunk lines are as thick as a wrist and stronger in tension than hemp — run the walls, cross the open silt in long catenaries, drape the sills, and pass through the collapsed groves the way a road passes through a town. Where the old terraces held soil it is densest, because worked ground is still worked ground after ten thousand years under water, and it followed the paths for the same reason: compacted earth drains and breathes differently, and it liked the difference. Mapped from above with sonar, the thickest of it reproduces the field system of a farming village. It is wearing the valley's clothes.

It does not fruit. There is nothing to carry a spore to, no animal to ride, nowhere to go that is not already itself, so it has spent an unmeasured stretch of time not reproducing and therefore not changing. Genetically it is nearly uniform across two thousand hectares. Whatever it was when the water came up, it still is. It has had no descendants and no deaths.

At the notch in the northeast rim, where the overflow slides out of the world over polished granite, there is always a thin skin of it on the wet rock, and the skin is always dying, killed by turbulence and air and light, and always regrown. It has been reaching for the lip for as long as the lip has existed. Not to escape. It cannot conceive of outside. It reaches for the notch the way a root reaches toward water: because that is where the note comes from.

---

**What it eats, and what it will not.**

Everything, at the speed of stone. A fallen lampweed takes forty years, and the forty years are not slowness of appetite but a decision about pace, because a grove that is eaten in a season is a grove that does not come back. It has never been found on a living lampweed's stem. It sits in the holdfast, trades minerals for sugar, mends the bladder skin — that odd chitin-and-limestone laminate that no other plant in the world makes and that both partners make identically, which raises the question of who taught whom and closes it again without answering.

It has, in other words, one crop, one pasture, and a lighting arrangement. The ochre-green glow that moves through the deep meadows in sheets, brightening where the water shears, brightens because shear brings the lace fresh salts and the lace pays for salts in the only currency the weed can use. The lamplight of the basin is a transaction. It looks like weather because it is metabolism at a hundred metres' remove.

And there is the refusal. In the second house on the second moraine hill, on a stone ledge, there is a wooden bowl, a coil of bast cord, and a bark box. Wood, plant fibre, bark. Exactly and precisely what the lace eats. The three houses are the only cold clean cavities in the entire basin: no film on the corbels, no bloom in the doorways, nothing on the bowl. It grows to the threshold stones of all three and stops there, and has stopped there for as long as anyone has looked, and the edge of the growth at the threshold is not a ragged edge. It is a made edge. It is trimmed.

---

**How it knows where it is.**

By sound, which is the only sense available, and which the basin supplies in ruinous quantity.

The trunk cables are strung under tension across the water, and pressure travels them. In the open basin this is useless: the hum of the overflow comes in through the whole rim at once, the ice whoops all winter, the gas ropes chime, a boulder knocks and knocks for half an hour on its way down. The lace lives inside a permanent roar and can feel nothing in it but the roar.

Except in the standing rooms. Where the rounded walls and the reflecting sills cancel the noise, some of them the size of a house, stable for as long as records go — in there, a cable is quiet. In there it can feel the settling of a leaf. Every one of the silent rooms is lined, floor and walls and the water-ceiling of the sill above, in lace three times the thickness found anywhere else, laid in a fine radial fan like the inside of an ear because that is what it is.

It has thirty-one of them, and they are its only clear places, and it does everything it does with any care in one or another of them.

The builders knew about the rooms too. Two intelligences, ten millennia apart, both feeling their way through the same water, both stopping in the same thirty-one places and saying: here, something can be heard. Make of that what you like. The people marked them with nothing at all, which may be the most respectful thing anyone has ever done in this valley.

---

**Its clock.**

Twenty-two minutes. Everything is on the beat.

The seiche is the tenant's day: the current comes, the current holds, the current reverses, the fog of white flocculence climbs eleven metres and swallows the groves and withdraws, and the lace feeds on the leaning and rests on the turn. Its year is the ice — four months sealed and lightless, in which the crop dims and the eating slows and nothing is done that requires the crop. Its century is the lampweed's life. Its longer measures, if it has them, are not accessible to us; the sediment core is a clean book, which means it has never buried anything, which means it keeps no record we can open.

At this tempo a thought is a change in the direction of growth. A decision is a season. There is a bed of silt out on the level white where the lace has been reconsidering something for eleven years, growing out and drawing back and growing out again a little to the left, and if that is deliberation then we have watched one sentence being spoken and did not hear the end of it.

---

**Hands.**

It lifts by buoyancy. At the west wall vents, where gas comes up in silver ropes, it grows purses of bladder laminate and packs them, and a purse the size of a fist at sixty metres will hold nothing at all, so it grows them the size of a barrel and it grows twenty of them, and it lashes them with cable to whatever needs lifting, and it waits for the seiche to lean the right way. Then it cuts.

It cuts by dissolving its own cable at one point, precisely, and it can put that point where it wants it to within about a hand's width. This is the tenant's entire manual dexterity: float, drift, cut, sink. It has no other movement. Everything it has ever done in this basin it has done by making something float and then letting it fall in a chosen place.

Which is enough, given time, to build.

---

**The weirs.**

Forty low arcs on the first sill, opening upstream into the seiche current, and the reason nobody has settled what they caught is that "caught" is the wrong verb by one degree. They are fields.

Detritus falls out of the whole upper valley and stops dead on the salt boundary and lies there in drifts. Free food, spread too thin to be worth harvesting, drifting past twice an hour. An arc of stone a knee high, set with its opening into the flow, takes that thin rain and heaps it. Inside each arc there is a metre and a half of white flocculent compost, and inside the compost the lace is a solid felt, and this is where it does the bulk of its living. Forty middens. The mouths face upstream because upstream reverses every eleven minutes and an arc that gathers on the leaning also shelters on the turn.
