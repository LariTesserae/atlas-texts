## The Listeners

You can cross the Made Ground for four days and conclude that nobody lives on it. This is the ordinary experience. They are perhaps nine hundred, spread across seventy miles, and the plateau gives them nothing to stand against — no smoke, no track (a footprint in the pith closes over), no fence, no path worn pale because everything is already pale. A band of eleven people at half a mile looks like a defect in the glare.

What you notice first is never a person. It is a wrongness in the register. Everything on the plateau is either the drone or the water; then somewhere off to your left there is a *slap* — flat, percussive, out of key with everything — and again, and in a pattern. That is a person hitting the water-skin with a cupped hand, which is the only instrument here and the only signal that crosses the drone without being eaten by it. They are telling someone a mile off that the channel ahead is thin. Or that they are alive. Or that they have found the mat you left and are keeping it dry.

The second thing you notice is a mat hung across a threshold three hundred feet up a throat, and it is the only object on the horizon that was made by hands, and it is black.

### What the plateau does to a body

Start with the feet, because everything about these people begins at the interface with the floor. They go barefoot; there is nothing to make a shoe from. The soles are not calloused so much as *finished* — thickened, pale, wrinkled permanently into a ridged pad, insensitive to heat, extremely sensitive to grain. A woman who has never left the plateau can tell you which throat's water she is standing in by the drag on her instep. She cannot tell you how she knows.

Above the feet, the whitening. The water is faintly milky and carries dissolved stone in solution, and the plateau does not distinguish between substrates. It will lay itself down on anything that holds still: a gourd, a heel, the underside of a sleeping wrist. So they scrape. Every adult carries a shell edge and every adult uses it on their own body daily, working the shins, the knuckles, the ankles, the outer edges of the hands, and the sound of a band waking is a dozen people quietly filing themselves. A young person is smooth and gray-pink. A person of forty has a rind at the knuckles and along the shins that has stopped coming off. A person of sixty has plates.

Then the eyes. A seventy-mile mirror burns you from underneath. They squint from infancy and the squint sets; the skin under the chin and inside the nostrils is scorched dark in people otherwise pale as the pith. They go blind late and reliably, the way lowlanders go gray. Nobody considers this a disease. It is simply the fifth or sixth decade.

And the ears. Living inside a note costs the note. Hearing narrows first at the top and then everywhere, and by seventy a person is functionally deaf — which is to say, they can no longer hear the map. This is the great inversion of the Made Ground and you must understand it or you will misread every social arrangement you see: **the old cannot navigate.** Wayfinding here is a young faculty. Children of nine, who can still hear a small throat's harmonics at four miles, lead the walking. The eldest of a band is cargo.

### Two voices

They shout. You knew that. What the account you were given did not tell you is that shouting and speaking are not the same act with different amplitudes; they are two different institutions, and a person has two names.

The shout-name is short, front-loaded, high — *Kessh, Tup, Ranna-ka* — engineered to cut a drone. It is public property. It is used for warning, trade, insult, praise, calling children out of a gallery, and the whole loud shallow business of the day. It does not change and it belongs to everybody.

The ear-name is spoken only at the ear, and only to somebody whose ear you have earned. Children are given theirs at the first count they survive; it may take twenty years for the number of people who hold it to reach ten. Learning someone's ear-name is what they have instead of a wedding, an oath, and a confession. There is no word for *secret* here, because a secret is not information; it is a posture — two people standing scandalously close, cheek to jaw, in an empty white country where everyone can see them do it for six miles.

The consequence is a people who look, from a distance, like they are constantly kissing, and who are in fact discussing water rights.

The consequence, too: nobody argues at conversational volume. A quarrel must be bellowed to be had at all, which means every quarrel is a public performance, and so quarrels are rare, stylized, and slightly enjoyable. Grief is the opposite. Grief is inaudible. You know a band has lost someone by the way they stand.

### The eleven-day rule, and the obscenity

Everything they believe is downstream of one observation: the plateau declines the event, and *you are an event.*

A notch closes in eleven days. So does a body. Not violently — nothing here is violent — but a person who lies still in a gallery through a fever wakes with the mat fused to the floor and a pale skin of new pith over the outside of one thigh, continuous with the wall, patterned with the same radiating pith-lines, and it does not come off with a shell edge. It comes off with a shell edge and blood.

So the culture is a technology of not being finished. You sleep four nights in a gallery, five at the outside. You turn in your sleep and you are taught to turn; children are corrected for stillness the way lowland children are corrected for rudeness. You do not lean on things while thinking. You do not sit on the floor. The mat exists so that there is one clean layer between you and the enterprise, and the mat itself must be lifted and shaken daily or it becomes architecture.

And you do not put an infant down. Ever. A baby is carried in a hair sling, hip to hip, hand to hand, through the whole first two years, and if the mother is scraping her shins somebody else has the child, and if nobody has the child that is a scandal of the first order. *To set down* is the plateau's foulest verb.
