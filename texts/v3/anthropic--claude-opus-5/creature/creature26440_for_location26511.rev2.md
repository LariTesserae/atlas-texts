## Who Holds It

You will not see many of them at first. A valley like this ought to be swarming — all that labour, applied continuously, forever — but the work is mostly done at the scale of a person kneeling, and a person kneeling on a terrace at two hundred metres' distance, in fog, wearing wet wool the colour of wet stone, is not a figure. It is a slight irregularity in a wall. Then the irregularity stands up and becomes an old woman with a bucket, and once you have seen one you begin to see the rest, and the valley fills in around you like a pattern resolving: forty, sixty, a hundred and more, distributed across the flanks at wide intervals, each one bent to a small square metre of the problem.

They are not numerous. They are only extremely well distributed.

---

### The pocket iron

Before anything else, learn this, because it explains most of what you will see them do.

Every adult in the valley carries a hooked bar about the length of a forearm, flattened at one end into a narrow blade and bent at the other into a hook with a squared heel. It rides in a sewn pocket down the right thigh of the coat. Advocates carry one. The physicians carry one. The Dry House archivist, whose hands are famous for never being wet, carries one.

What it is for: clearing a gutter mouth. You are walking up a stair-street, you pass a grate that has gathered a mat of alder leaf and liverwort, you do not stop walking — you stoop, hook, flick, and continue, and the water that was spreading across the tread goes back down where it belongs, and you have lost perhaps two seconds. Nobody thanks you. Nobody notices. Over a lifetime a person does this a quarter of a million times.

To walk past a blocked grate is the valley's real obscenity. Not a crime; there is no law against it, any more than there is a law against breathing out. It is simply that the person who does it has announced something about themselves that cannot be taken back, and people will be kind to them afterward in a way that is quite hard to bear.

The bars are inherited. They wear thin in the hook. An old one is nearly a wire there, and its owner will show you the thinness the way people elsewhere show you a photograph.

---

### Bodies

Low-light people. The pupils are large and slow to shut, so on a glare day — perhaps nine a year, when the lid lifts and the sun comes into the valley like something kicked over — they go about with their hats pulled down and their eyes streaming, and the works run at half pace, and everyone is faintly irritable and pretends to be delighted. Children born in a glare week are supposed to be lucky. Nobody can say at what.

The hearing is the remarkable thing. In that quiet, over a lifetime, the ear gets educated past what seems reasonable. They can tell you which of the four waters is running high without looking. They can place a shovel to within a terrace. Two of them will hold a conversation across three hundred metres of fog in ordinary speaking voices, unhurried, with pauses, each waiting out the other's silence, and neither will ever raise their voice, because a raised voice in this valley means one thing only.

When someone shouts, doors open. All of them. Instantly, on both flanks, people who have not finished a sentence are already moving downhill. It happens perhaps twice a year and it is the single most frightening sound in the world to anyone raised here, and grown men who have emigrated to loud bright cities report waking for decades afterward at the sound of an argument in the street below, standing at the window with their hearts going, waiting to be told where.

Their knees are ruined by fifty. They walk with a particular tilt — the terrace foot, one leg conditioned to the cross-fall — and when they travel out of the valley they are recognisable at once by the way they go along a level road as though it were slyly sloping and they had decided not to make an issue of it.

They do not point with the hand. The hand has something in it. They point with the chin, or with the bar.

---

### Charges

Nobody here owns land. Land is a thing that wants to go downhill; you would have to be simple to want it.

What they own is obligation. Each household holds a *charge* — a defined run of masonry, or a length of gutter, or a stair, or a stretch of the aqueduct's sixth arch, together with everything that leans on it. The charge is inherited, registered, indexed, argued about, and cannot be abandoned, only transferred to someone who will take it, which is a matter of finding someone who will.

So a good marriage is a short, cheap, well-founded wall with no spring behind it. A dowry may be nothing but the transfer of forty metres of dry retaining wall facing south, and that is a fortune, and everyone knows it, and the bride's family are congratulated with real warmth. A bad inheritance is the belly wall your grandfather accepted in a drunken generosity in a year when nobody could pay, with two hundred years of slumped fill behind it and a drain running under it that appears on no plan. Families have been crushed by such things for a century. Whole surnames are pitied.

And because the charge is not land but duty, it works downhill: what you owe is the water you pass. Your character, legally speaking, is what arrives at your neighbour's boundary and in what condition. The courts sit outdoors on the disputed run in all weather, the advocates in their oiled capes with their bars in their pockets, everyone walking slowly along the line of the argument while a clerk reads out the depths.

Ivy can be named as a defendant. So can a rowan growing in a litigated gutter. There is a body of case law about trees that grow on buildings whose ownership is unresolved, and it is enormous, and discussed at dinner, and quite funny if you know the names.

Governance follows the same logic. The valley is run by the Fall, which is a board rather than a person, and its chair is whoever currently holds the great intercepting drain under the east flank — the worst charge in the valley, a black brick bore two kilometres long that must be walked twice monthly. Authority here is what you get for accepting the thing nobody wants. It has kept the politics unusually honest, and unusually short of volunteers.

---

### The night ones

Walls fail at three in the morning. Not always, but enough that the valley has made a profession of the hours.

The listeners sleep from mid-morning to late afternoon and spend the night walking assigned circuits, slowly, stopping often, doing nothing that looks like anything. What they are attending to is the moment before: the change in a seep's note, the hiss of water finding a new path inside a fill, the dry tick of a wall going out of plumb by a thumb's width. Above all a smell — the valley calls it old breath — which is what comes out of a joint when the ground behind it opens a void. Wet stone, sourness, something like a cold cellar and a coin. Nobody can teach it and everyone who has it agrees on what it is.

A good many of the listeners are blind or going blind, which is regarded as a qualification rather than a misfortune, and they are paid well and fed well and treated by the valley with the exact tenderness it can never quite manage toward anyone who is merely useful.

Their counterparts in daylight are the setters: the very old, who no longer lift, and who instead go along a wall face with their hands and their tongues. Lime beard tells you a story — a fresh white extrusion means water has found a road it did not have last year — and the salts differ, and the old ones taste them. You will see a woman of eighty in the rain with her palm flat on a course of stone and her eyes shut, taking her time, and everyone stepping around her, and then she says a number to the foreman and the number becomes forty people and a week of work.

### The counters

In the glasshouse belt there is no insect noise and no talking, and the reason for the silence is arithmetic.

The pollinators — mostly women and children, some of them the same people their whole lives — work the lily sheds and the strawberry rows with rabbit-tail brushes, twelve hundred plants a day, and they count every one. They count in a body rhythm, not in words, the way you would carry a tune, and they can be interrupted and resume, and at the end of the day they can tell you what they did, and they are never wrong.

This produces, as a by-product, the most reliable minds in the valley. The counting trade feeds directly into the tellers, the measurers, the tally clerks who walk the works at dawn, and eventually into the Dry House, where the permanent record is kept — ink on rag, in tin boxes, in a building floored over a flue from the kilns, the only truly dry room in the valley. The family who keep it have skin like paper and are teased for it in every drying loft on the east flank, and they have heard all the jokes, and they smile the same smile their grandmother smiled.

Everything is written down. Every rebuilt metre, with the date, the foreman, the number, and the names of the hands. The valley's literature is nine hundred years of repair invoices, and they read it. They genuinely read it. A man will look up the last four rebuildings of his own charge and discover that in 1712 his own family were paid to rebuild it and that it failed again in nine years, and he will take this personally, and do something about the drain that they clearly did not do, and write down what he did for whoever comes next.

---

### Warm rooms

Nothing dries. Clothes are worn damp as a matter of course, and the only genuinely warm places are the drying lofts built over the mill flues — one to a street, low, dim, hung solid with steaming laundry so that you cannot see across the room.

This is where the social life of the valley happens. People sit on benches along the wall and talk into a forest of other people's shirts, addressing a garment, hearing a voice answer from behind a sheet. Courtship is conducted there almost entirely by sound, which perhaps explains the local conviction that you can hear whether a person is kind. Business is done there. Children are fed there and fall asleep there and are carried home. The lofts smell of hot wet wool and lye and charcoal, and any native of this valley, anywhere in the world, will stop dead at that smell in the street and have to stand still for a moment.

---

### Their dead

You cannot dig a grave in fill. The fill is structural; you would be excavating a wall from behind.

So they burn them, in the cooling kilns, at the end of a charcoal cycle, and the ash is sifted — the sifted ash in the terraces, the ash you have been walking on since the switchback road — and spread onto the upper benches where the barley goes. A single handful is kept back and worked into the mortar of the next repair made to whatever run the person held. They are put into their own wall. That is the phrase. *She's in her wall now.*

It is not thought of as sad, particularly, and it is certainly not thought of as metaphor. It is simply where people go. A man patching his grandmother's parapet is doing something that has a name and a mild, warm, ordinary feeling attached to it, roughly equivalent to visiting.

Their one piece of the sacred, as far as I can tell, is the shown wall. When a failure opens an older wall inside it, work stops. The foreman sends word. People come up in the rain with children on their shoulders to look at a face of stone that nobody has seen for four hundred years, or eight, laid by hands whose repair invoice may or may not still exist in the Dry House, and they look, and they say very little, and the setters take a single stone from the oldest visible course, and then it is all covered again by evening, because it must be. The stones taken this way are kept in the Dry House in a rack, labelled, going back further than the ink does.

There is a small stubborn sect that holds there is no original slope at all — that it is walls the whole way down, all the way to the middle of the earth, and that the mountain was never anything but this. They are regarded as slightly embarrassing and nobody can refute them.

---

### The drawdown

One night a year the flumes are shut for cleaning and the turbines stop, and the hum stops with them.

Nobody sleeps. Infants scream inconsolably. Old people sit up in the dark with the blankets round them. What you hear in that gap is the valley itself — ticking, settling, water finding its way through nine centuries of made ground with no machinery over the top of it — and it goes on for six hours and is, by every account, unbearable and enormously loved. They make a feast of it. They sit out on the terraces with lamps and the cage-birds brought outside in their cages, finches and siskins, thousands of them the length of the valley, singing at the lamplight because they think it is morning; and that is the one night of the year when this place sounds like somewhere that has animals in it.

---

### Leaving, and the other thing

The young go. Of course they go — down the switchback in a cart with a box, out to the bright loud flat cities, and a good half of them stay gone and do well and become, by all reports, entirely pleasant people.

The ones who come back all say the same thing, and they say it sheepishly, because it sounds like boasting and it isn't. Out there, nothing needed them at three in the morning. Nobody's roof was their business. They could walk past anything.

That is the whole of who lives here: people who have solved the problem of what a life is for by inheriting a task that cannot be completed, and who know perfectly well that it is a trap, and who would not be got out of it with ropes.

---

Stay three days and someone will offer you a charge. It will be a small one — twelve metres of gutter above a stair, or one arch's worth of fern-clearing — and it will be offered casually, at the end of a sentence about something else, and you should understand that it is not a chore. It is the only form of citizenship they have.

If you take it, they will show you where the grate is, and put a bar in your hand, and thereafter, walking up in the late afternoon that is most of the day, you will be one more of the irregularities in the wall: kneeling, unremarkable at two hundred metres, adding your one small sound to the four directions of it — somebody, somewhere, putting a stone back.
