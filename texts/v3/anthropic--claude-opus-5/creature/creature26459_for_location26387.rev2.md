## The Tenant

Eleven species are listed for the hollow in the county survey, and the survey is not wrong, and nobody who has stood inside it has ever been able to keep the eleven separate in their head for more than a minute.

The drivewood columns are one clone. Every one of the forty carries the same genotype, and so does the torus they stand on, and so do the fronds overhead, so that the ring is a single individual of something like four hundred years and perhaps eighty tonnes, laid out in a circle and grinding itself forward at the speed of a fingernail. The strap-vines are a different genus entirely and cannot be got to grow anywhere else; three separate attempts, cuttings and seed both, and the longest survival outside the basin was fourteen weeks. The pod-lungs are probably a modified stolon of the mat and probably not a separate organism at all, though the fermentation inside them certainly is. The mat is twenty or thirty things, or one thing wearing twenty or thirty faces, depending on how much weight you put on tissue continuity, which at Sowder's Bottom is a great deal of weight, because nothing here stays discontinuous for long.

The local phrasing does not bother with any of this. The local phrasing says *the works*, and uses a singular verb.

What it wants, insofar as that word survives contact with a thing that has no nerves: water, and regularity, and the continued absence of interruption. It is not hungry in any direction. It has no interest in spreading; the rim-growth has not advanced more than four metres in the whole photographic record, and in places it has withdrawn. It does not compete. It does not defend itself — you can cut a strap-vine with a pocket knife and the ring will not notice for a fortnight, at which point a new vine will be laid across the gap in the same three-turn wind, from the same boss, in the same left hand, and the cut ends will have been drawn down into the mat and be halfway to being nothing in particular.

It is, as far as anyone can establish, entirely without appetite. It is only extremely, unbrokenly occupied.

The one thing it does that looks like preference: it is a superb copyist of any regular geometry put in front of it. Lay something in the basin with a repeating shape and a fixed tempo and within a season or two the growth will have taken up that shape somewhere, usually badly, usually at a different scale, usually in a place where it does nothing. The brick culvert is elliptical and there are eleven elliptical apertures in the frond-ceiling that serve no drainage purpose whatsoever. The rail gauge — 1,435 millimetres — turns up three times in the spacing of drivewood bosses in the north arc, to within a centimetre. This may be coincidence. There is a great deal of structure in the hollow and if you go looking for numbers in it you will find numbers.

It has never been shown to do anything that requires it to have understood anything.

It has only ever done three or four things that would be inexplicable if it hadn't.

## The Staff

Two hundred grease-beetles, give or take a dozen, and the give-or-take has not changed in the whole hundred and some years of counting.

They are fat, soft-shelled, the colour of lard that has stood a week, and about the length of a thumb joint. Blind — not blinded, never sighted; there is no scar of an eye socket, no vestigial pigment spot, nothing in the head capsule where eyes would have been. A curate listed them badly in 1861 as a species of cockroach and nobody has troubled to correct him, because to correct him you would have to take one out, and one taken out dies in nine days, every time, in every arrangement of warmth and damp and silica grit that anyone has tried.

They live in the bearing-sockets and in the silica bed under the torus. From plates on the underside of the abdomen they secrete the wax that packs those sockets: a pale, slightly sour substance somewhere between tallow and shellac, which sets to a firm grease and takes an enormous load without squeezing out. They eat the wear-debris — ground silica, shredded fibre, and at the flywheel, iron oxide, which turns their gut contents a deep rust colour and makes them, when cut open, look like something that has been badly hurt and has not been. Their digestion is essentially a lapping compound. Grit goes in coarse and comes out fine and gets used again.

Without them the ring seizes in about a decade. This is not speculation; the northeast arc lost its beetles for some reason in the 1930s and the grinding note went ugly for nine years and there is a scarred, glassy, fused patch in the silica bed there that the ring still limps over every fourteen months, and the whole basin ticks when it does.

The best thing about them is how they move. A beetle in the silica bed walks continuously against the direction of the turn, at almost exactly the speed of the turn: two centimetres a day, more or less forever. Relative to the ring, it travels the full circumference of the works in a little under fourteen months. Relative to the ground, it stays where it is. It walks all its life and dies within a metre of where it hatched. The fastest a grease-beetle ever travels is the instant it stops walking.

They are paid. Along the bearing collars are sugar pores that open to the pressure of a beetle's foot and not to a glass rod, a wire, a fingernail, or a dead beetle's foot, and when a socket begins to wear the pores in that socket run sweeter and more beetles arrive and more larvae mature in the wax, and when the wear is made good the flow slackens and the population falls back. It is a labour market with one employer, no wages in arrears, and no possibility of leaving.

Take one up in a cupped hand and it clicks. Slow at first, then faster. Everyone who has done it describes the same feeling, which is that they are holding something that is asking to be put back in the machine.

## The Majority

By mass, the true inhabitants of Sowder's Bottom are anaerobic and have no names.

Each pod-lung carries something in the order of eight to twelve kilograms of them: a consortium of perhaps forty organisms, methanogens and their feeders and their feeders' feeders, breaking the mat's surplus down in the warm dark and putting out gas. Forty lungs. Four hundred kilograms of the things, which is to say the greatest concentration of living material in the basin after the drivewood itself, and all of it involved in a metabolism so old and so indifferent that it would proceed unaltered in a barrel, in a bog, in the gut of a cow, on a planet with no sun.

They are the only inhabitants of the hollow who are not keeping time.

Everything else here is part of the escapement. The ring counts fourteen months. The belts count the day in slack and tight. The lungs count nine hours and fire. The screw counts nine days. The beetles walk to stand still. But the ferment has no phase and no cycle and no relation to the chord that its own gas produces; it simply goes on, at whatever rate the warmth allows, and has no idea that it is the boiler of anything.

## The Lodgers

The rain-frog is brown above and waterlogged-white beneath, and if the two sightings are to be trusted it is about the size of a small loaf and its eyes have gone the same cataract-grey as a boiled fish. It lives up in the frond-ceiling where the condensation pools in the elliptical apertures. Three or four notes in an afternoon. Nobody has ever seen more than one at a time, and nobody has seen it at all since 1979.

Its call was noted in 1911 by a man with a tuning fork and a good ear, and again in 2004 by a woman with a recorder, and it is the same pitch: a shade under B flat. In 1911 that was the fundamental of the number six reed-stack. Number six has since gone a full semitone flat, as reed-stacks do, and so the frog now sings, several times a week, into a hollow that no longer contains its note, the only sound in the basin that has not moved.

The spiders are pale, flat, eyeless enough not to matter, and they do not spin. They live inside the reed-stacks, clamped to the inner wall with all eight legs and their bodies gone convex against the bore, and when the charge fires they are thrown two storeys into the fronds and come down a minute later in the warm rain and walk back and climb up and wait. Thirty thousand times in a life, at a guess. They eat pollen and the yeast in the condensate and each other's cast skins. They breed in the ascent.

Each spider knows its own stack and will not use another. Move one from eleven to four and it comes straight back out, walks off across the mat, and is dead within the hour and a strand in the weave by the spring. Whether that is the pitch, the timing, or something in the reseat of the woody lips, nobody knows. They can't be asked and they can't be kept.

## The Visitor

Two sets of tracks in a hundred and some years of note-taking, both in the soft margin by the culvert mouth, both recorded by people who had no reason to invent them and both of whom wrote them down in a flat, annoyed way, as if the tracks were an administrative problem.

Four toes, no web, a hand and a half across, the rear toe carrying weight. Heron-ish. Considerably larger than a heron. The 1889 set ran to eleven paces, the 1968 set to seven, and in both cases the line begins in undisturbed mat and ends in undisturbed mat, facing the brickwork, with no approach and no departure, and the mat on either side unmarked for twenty metres.

Neither observer claims to have seen anything. The 1968 note, in the third keeper's hand and very shaky, says only: *Come and gone in the night. Didn't take the frog.*

## The Keepers

The tally marks inside the culvert run to 4,180, in groups of five, in at least four hands, and the last group is a group of three.

The first hand is broad and square, struck with a cold chisel and a mason's hammer by somebody who had done a lot of this sort of work, deep enough to still be crisp. That hand cut 1,204 marks. The second is smaller, quicker, made with a knife rather than a chisel, and slants; 908. The third is ragged, with gaps in it — whole groups missing, then a run of four crowded into the space for two — and ends mid-group, at two, and does not resume; 611. The fourth is the neatest of the lot, cut with a chisel against a gauge, evenly spaced, and after every fifth group it adds three small letters, which are the same three letters as the mason's initials under the date-stone. That hand has cut 1,457, and is still cutting.

The intervals, where they can be checked against dated notes, come out at nine days. Nine days is one thread of the screw. Whoever started this was not counting days or moons or visits; they were counting the only quantity in the hollow that actually goes anywhere. Four thousand one hundred and eighty threads is about a hundred and three years, which puts the first mark some while after the bone-black works closed and the men were let go, and one man, evidently, was not.

The likeliest reading is the dullest one. The culvert drains the beck through the wall; if it chokes, the lower field floods and the water table under the basin shifts, and there was presumably a man kept on at a few shillings to walk down every week or so and pull the drift out of the grate. The works shut. The shillings stopped. He kept going down. When he could not, somebody else did, and by then there was no longer anybody to explain it to, so nobody was told, so the reason went out of the thing and the practice stayed in, the way a tune outlives its words.

The third hand's record breaks off at two marks, and eight metres away, in the mat beside the ring path, there is a boot sole with seggs in the heel, size nine, drawn down flat. The compression goes one way. Nobody has ever suggested a connection out loud and everybody makes it.

The fourth hand is a woman in her sixties who leaves a van on the ridge road and comes down through the scrub in nine or ten minutes. She has a dog that will not go below the tree line; it lies down at the rim with its ears flat and waits, and it has never once tried to follow her, and it was like that from the first day. She does the round in the same order every time. Clear the grate. Scrape the collar where the mat has crept over the brick, which it does about two millimetres a season and no more, as though checking. Walk the ring path, the only bare ground in the basin, looking at the silica bed, and do not stop walking while she is in it. Re-lay the strap-vine over the flywheel rim if it has crept off true, three turns, left hand. Look at the flywheel bearing and not touch it. Cut the mark. Out before the light goes green.

She does not use the word tending. Asked, she says: *I keep the drain clear.*

She has a kettle at home that she keeps just off the boil all night, on a low gas, because she cannot sleep in a quiet room and has not been able to for about thirty-five years.

## What They Are Paid

Three metres from the flywheel, in the west arc, there is a niche in the growth about the size of a bread oven, and in it: a cold chisel worn to a stub, a lump hammer, a folding rule, a flat scraper, a spool of waxed line, a tin with nothing in it, and a jar with a cloth tied over the mouth.

Nothing in the niche is being incorporated. The mat has grown a clean collar round the opening and stopped, exactly as it did at the brickwork, exactly as it did at the Robson & Son nameplate that it holds out at reading height and has held there, unabsorbed below the depth of the lettering, for longer than anyone has been alive. In a place whose whole character is that everything dropped is drawn in and pressed and made into the weave, there are four exemptions, and all four of them are legible to a person.

And under the flywheel bearing, at the height of a raised hand, above the reach of the mat, the growth has formed a shallow lipped cup, and the surplus wax from the socket runs down into it and collects, and is not taken back.

About four ounces a year. She sells it to three fiddle-makers and a man who does ropework on the coast, and it is very good wax, and there is no other source of it anywhere.

So: the ring is turned by water and heat and patience, and the sockets are packed by the beetles, and the beetles are paid in sugar through pores that will not open to anything but a living foot, and the drain is kept clear by a woman with a scraper who is paid four ounces a year out of a cup that grew where a cup was needed, and the whole apparatus — eighty tonnes of it, four hundred years of it, the chord and the rain and the two million turns — exists to deliver one black walnut-sized object into the clay every sixty years or so, for reasons that are nobody's business.

It keeps her the way a mill keeps an oiler.

The last thing to say is that below the main run of tallies, starting about three years ago, there is a second line. Small marks, grouped in fives, forty-something of them, cut with something lighter than a chisel and not quite straight.

They are at knee height.
