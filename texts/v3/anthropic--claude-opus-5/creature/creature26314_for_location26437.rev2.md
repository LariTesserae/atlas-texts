## Who lives here

Four hundred and ten people, and not one of them is from here.

That is the arithmetic that explains everything else. The chamber is nineteen years old, but the town is four, and every adult in it walked down four hundred metres of ladder with a sack on their back and a signature on a contract. They all remember sky. They all remember rain and dogs and the noise of a street. There is nobody in the Kettle to whom this is simply how the world is — except the children, of whom more later, and who are the reason the Kettle is going to become something nobody planned.

They came for the water concession and the salt and the fibre, and they came because they were the kind of people for whom a three-year term at the bottom of a hole was an improvement. Debt, mostly. Some convictions. Some divorces. A few who simply liked the terms and have since stopped being able to say why. The descent log is a census as much as a manifest: everyone who is here is in it, with a date and a weight.

## Their bodies

Four years at forty-three degrees has rebuilt them.

They are thin, all of them, in a particular way — not starved, but rendered, the fat gone from the face first so that everyone over thirty has the same hollow under the cheekbone and the same visible cords in the neck. Their hearts are slow and enormous. Their blood is thin with water. They sweat at a rate that would frighten a surface doctor and the sweat comes off them almost fresh, nearly saltless, because the body learns to hoard what it can — which is the Kettle's best joke, that a town built on salt gives none of it back.

They drink constantly and unconsciously, the way other people breathe. A Kettle adult takes twelve to fifteen litres a day and does not think about it, and the jar at the elbow is as much a part of the body as the hand.

And they have lost the cold. This is the thing nobody warns you about in the recruitment sheets. Go up the shaft after two years down and the surface will hurt you: thirty-two degrees at the shaft head feels like winter, twenty feels like drowning, and a returning worker will sit at the top in full sun, shaking, with a blanket around their shoulders, for an hour before they can speak. They call it the Cold Shakes and they are not embarrassed by it. What they are quietly embarrassed by is the other thing, the one they call being *shaft-shy*: when a person has been down so long that they stop climbing at all, then stop planning to, then stop looking up. There are perhaps forty shaft-shy in the Kettle. Nobody names them out loud. Everyone knows the list.

Their skin is the colour of unlit rooms and mottled at the wrists and the back of the neck with a permanent low-grade rash from the basalt wool, which itches for the first month and then simply becomes the texture of being alive. They scratch the inside of the wrist against the opposite thumbnail, a small fast gesture, four or five times a minute, without noticing, all of them, all the time. Visitors pick it up within a week.

And they are freckled black. Basalt glass flakes off the floor at every scuff and gets into the palms, the soles, the forearms, and stays — tiny black points under the skin that the body cannot dissolve and will not push out. A four-year resident's hands, held to the melt-eye's glare, look faintly starred. They are proud of it in a way they would deny. You can date a person in the Kettle by their hands the way you can date a tree.

## Their hands

Cut. Always. The salt grows on the catwalk rails twice a day in needles and the needles go into the palm on a grab, so everyone's hands carry a dozen small white lines at any moment, and nobody mentions a cut unless it is deep enough to need the boiler-room's brine and a wrap.

Their hands are also their manners. The alleys are shoulder-width and hung with fibre curtains so heavy that you walk the third tier by feel, and in that closet-dark the town developed its grammar of touch. You go left shoulder first. You do not apologise for contact; you apologise for *stopping*. Passing a stranger in the dark you say nothing, but you may put two fingers on the back of their collar as you go by — a half-second, no more — and feel whether the cloth is wet. This is the closest thing the Kettle has to a handshake and it is done to everyone, by everyone, from the Assembly chair to a six-year-old. A dry collar gets you stopped, turned around, walked to the nearest trough with a hand on your elbow, and no one says a word about it. Twice a year that gesture saves someone who was further gone than they knew.

## Their voices

Quiet, pitched low, slightly behind the beat. Shouting costs water and the fibre eats sound anyway, so a Kettle conversation is conducted at a murmur with the faces close, and four years of it has changed their vocal habits so thoroughly that the haul crews' surface-loud voices sound aggressive to them. A newcomer's ordinary speaking tone makes people flinch and look at the ceiling.

They also flinch at the rock, mid-word, all of them, and never remark on it. The basalt ticks and pops on no schedule; a room of twelve people will stop talking for half a second at a knuckle-crack in the ceiling, every head turning a few degrees toward it, and then resume the sentence exactly where it broke. Outsiders describe this as the single most unsettling thing about the town's company, worse than the absence of animals, because it looks like a shared hallucination.

The fleece-tenders have a hand-sign set, twenty-odd signs, for use in the field where sound carries badly and voices are unwelcome: *full, dry, cracked, mine, yours, go up, come look.* It has leaked out of the field into general use. Married couples argue in it across a room. Children learn it before they learn to read.

## Their households

A household is not a family. A household is a fleece.

The frame is the unit of everything: property, standing, marriage, inheritance. To join a household is to join your name to a frame in the Assembly's book, and to leave one is to have it struck out and to have your water re-sourced, which is done without commentary and with enormous care. There are four hundred fleeces and four hundred and ten people, and every single person in the Kettle can tell you, without hesitating, which frame they drink from. Children can do it before they can count to forty.

The taboo is absolute and it has made them strange. Nobody reaches across another's space for anything. Nobody moves another person's cup, or chair, or boots, or crate — not to help, not to tidy, not to be kind. A visiting inspector once picked up a dropped broom and leaned it back against a neighbour's wall, and three people stopped what they were doing to watch, and the neighbour came out, took the broom, put it on the floor where it had fallen, and picked it up herself. They are not superstitious about this. They are precise about it. It is the only rule they have that costs a person their life on the ladder, and they have chosen to make it easy by never, ever getting near it.

The effect is a town of people with beautiful, almost surgical spatial manners, who will let a stranger sleep in their room without a question and will not touch the stranger's bag.

## The haulers

Forty of them, in four crews, and they are a species apart. They go up and down the shaft, which takes six hours loaded and four light, and their thighs and forearms are built like nothing else in the Kettle, and they can still tolerate the cold, which the others cannot, and which the others resent and require.

They come down smelling of the outside. That is their real cargo. Children crowd a returning hauler and are allowed, by long custom, to smell their coat — grass, rain, smoke, the sweat of the mules at the winch head — and ask what each thing is. A hauler who is good with children will let this go on for five minutes and will lie a little, generously, about what a mule looks like.

They are also the town's only source of news, gossip, contraband liners, and love affairs with people who do not live here, and they are trusted in exactly the way weather is trusted.

## The Kettle-born

Eleven of them. The oldest is three years and seven months, a girl called Nial, and the calendar down here is unofficially counted from her.

These eleven have never seen the sun except as a coin travelling across the fleece-field for forty minutes a day. They have never felt a temperature below thirty degrees. They sleep twice per day, in halves, because the town works the in-drafts, and a single unbroken night will be one of the first surface things to bewilder them. They do not run, because running in this heat is punished immediately and the punishment teaches fast, so they are eerily still children, and they play sitting games — counting the drip, hand-slap patterns, a game called Listener where one of them sits in a chair with their eyes shut and the others make small noises and the sitter has to say *wall* or *ceiling* or *nothing*.

They have no word for pest, or vermin, or swarm. They know *bird* and *dog* and *horse* as words out of songs, and they pronounce them carefully, the way you pronounce a foreign city. Their parents sing them lullabies full of animals and watch them fall asleep to a zoology that is, for them, purely mythological. It is one of the two or three things that can make a Kettle adult cry without warning, and it happens in the dark where nobody sees.

And they are the whole argument. Because in eleven years those children will be the first people who consider this normal, and the adults know it, and they talk around it constantly. *When they're grown.* Nobody has decided whether that is a triumph or a thing they did to somebody.

## The moth

In Year Two a moth came down in a crate of liners that had not been heat-treated properly, and it went to the melt-house glare, as moths do, and a spinner saw it and screamed — actually screamed, in a town where nobody raises their voice — and the Kettle stopped work for eleven days.

They burned the crate. They searched every fissure on the third tier for eggs with lamps and needles. They re-treated four hundred kilograms of cloth. The hauler who had signed the crate went up the ladder and did not come back down, and *a Vess crate* is still what they call any sloppy work.

The moth is in the Assembly, under a glass, on a card, with the log page pinned beside it showing the line where it was not recorded. It is meant as a warning. It is not used as one. The children visit it, the way they visit the fig, and press their faces to the glass, and are taken away gently, and come back. It is the only animal most of them will see before they are ten.

## Osme

Osme keeps the fig. She is thirty-one, narrow, unsentimental, permanently annoyed, and she took the brush from her mother's kit the week after her mother came off the Spine in Year One.

The brush is squirrel hair. There are, by her count, forty-one hairs left in it. She has never seen a squirrel and has stopped asking haulers to describe one, because their descriptions do not agree and she has decided this is their problem. There is an ongoing proposal in the Assembly to replace the brush with one made of human hair, of which the Kettle has an abundance, and Osme has refused it three times on grounds she will not explain and which are probably not horticultural.

The fig has never fruited. Osme pollinates it anyway, every flower, on the theory that the day it decides to be a fig she intends to be ready. She weighs its leaf-litter, composts it, returns it, and records the weights in a ledger that is, without anyone deciding this, the second-most-read document in the Kettle.

She is irritated by the way people touch its trunk on their way to the ladder. She has counted: an average of two hundred and six touches a day, always the same patch, at chest height, polished now to a shine. She has complained formally about the wear on the bark. She has never once asked anyone to stop, and when a hauler leaves on a long term she stands at the tub and does not look at them while they do it.

## Their dead

Six, in four years. One off the Spine, two from a vent that opened in the Low Quarter in Year Three, one heat, one heart, one that the Listener's log records only as *ceiling*.

There is no soil to put a body in and nothing down here to consume one, and four hundred metres of ladder is too much to ask of a friend. So the dead go into the melt-eye, and the ash goes into the fibre as binder, and the panels made that season go up on the walls of the tier where the person lived.

They do not talk about this much, and they do not mark which panel is which, and they all know anyway. A woman in the fifth tier sleeps with her back against a wall she helped spin. The six names are cut into the slag plate at the shaft foot, under the rules, above the scratched line.

## The thing in the cold corner

*Nothing lives here that we did not carry.*

Everyone in the Kettle has read that sentence and everyone in the Kettle has also seen the grey lichen-analogue spreading on the cold rock by the shaft foot, which nobody planted, which no one can account for, and which the Assembly voted in Year One to leave entirely alone, on principle, by a margin of one.

It has grown eleven centimetres in four years. A man on the sixth tier measures it on the quiet, every hundred days, with a fibre thread he keeps in his liner pocket, and writes the number on the inside of his forearm and washes it off. He has never told anyone what he thinks it means. Half the town believes it blew down the shaft as a spore in the first week, which is almost certainly true. The other half does not say what it believes, because the Kettle is a place where people are careful about what they say near rock, and because the alternative is worse and better at the same time: that the mountain is not as sterile as the survey said, that something was already down here in the dark before the drill let the air in, and that the line scratched under the rules — scratched by somebody, never removed, and now read out loud to every newcomer at the foot of the ladder — has been wrong from the day it was written.

Either way, they sweep around it. They do not touch it. And on the out-draft, when the shaft pulls the air past the vent-cracks and the whole chamber goes down to that low tone at the bottom of hearing, the young ones working the far ranks will look over at the cold corner, and then at each other, and then get on with the fleeces.
