## The people, seen from two hundred metres

You will see them before you hear them, which is not saying much, because you will not hear them at all.

At distance they read as posts. This is not stillness of the ceremonial kind; it is the ordinary economy of people who have learned that moving costs more here than it does anywhere else. A comber standing at the louvre-wall waiting for the fog to thicken will stand for forty minutes with her weight on one hip and her hands inside her sleeves, and from across the bowl she is indistinguishable from a rosette-tree, and she knows it, and there is a joke about it that is nine hundred years old and no longer funny.

Then she moves, and the movement is smooth and rather slow and goes exactly where it was going. Nobody here fidgets. Fidgeting is what the air does to lowlanders in their first week — the jitter, the bouncing knee, the talking — and it is watched with the patient tolerance you would give a dog in a new house.

## Bodies

They are not large. They are *deep*: long in the ribcage, short in the limb, with a chest that keeps working at a slow swell even when the rest of them has gone to stone. Their breathing is always a little more than the conversation requires. You notice it in a room — twenty people, none of them speaking, all of them faintly, visibly, unhurriedly full.

The hands are the giveaway. Wide palms, thick at the base of the thumb, and hot — genuinely hot, a degree or two above what you expect, because the blood runs close to the skin here and the vessels are lazy about closing. A Thimble handshake is startling to a lowlander, who goes away thinking these people are feverish. They are not. They are simply carrying their heat on the outside, where it can be lent.

Faces are dark across the cheek and the bridge of the nose, where forty days a year of blue-white noon has printed itself. Eyes are set deep and hooded, and they are strange eyes: superb at grain and useless at depth. A woman of Thimble can look at a slope of grey at four hundred metres in flat cloud and tell you it is bog and not moss, wet and not drowned, walkable on the left, by the texture alone, the way you might identify a cloth by feel in the dark. She cannot catch a thrown object. Nobody here can. Children's games involve rolling, sliding, hitting things that are already still. The lowland habit of tossing a set of keys across a room is regarded roughly as we would regard spitting.

## Voice, and where they keep it

The air will not carry them, so they gave their voices to solid things.

Close speech first: shoulder to shoulder, not face to face, both of them looking out at the same view — which is why every serious conversation in Thimble is conducted by two people standing side by side against a wall, and why the walls have a polished band at shoulder height, from centuries of leaning. You get someone's attention by putting a hand flat on their upper arm. The face is not touched. To speak to a back without touching it first is the one unambiguous rudeness in the entire culture.

Then the pipe. That green copper running hip-height along every wall and lane and shed of the works is not only warm; it is the town's nervous system. Sound travels through metal fifteen times faster and a hundred times better than through this thin nothing, and Thimble worked that out before it worked out much else. A knuckle-rap on copper is audible for three kilometres. The children learn the pipe before they learn letters: two-and-a-hold for *come*, the long roll for *turnover in a quarter hour*, the fast triplet that means someone is in the water. There are perhaps three hundred standard figures and a thousand more that are private, family-held, or obscene. A house's kettle-pipe is where a mother finds her children. A courting couple who live on opposite rims of the bowl will tap at each other for years through nine hundred metres of the same warm green metal, and the whole town can hear it, and the whole town pretends it can't.

And the third voice, the oldest: they sing by contact. Pressed sternum to back, or forehead to forehead, or a child's ear laid against the ribs of whoever is holding it — the note goes through the bone and arrives enormous. Thimble has no choirs and no instruments to speak of. It has *holding-songs*, which cannot be performed, only administered, one person to one person, and which are therefore the private property of everyone who has ever been held.

The speech itself is short. A sentence is bounded by a breath and the breath is not generous, so their spoken language has become a thing of two- and three-word clauses, flat, unemphatic, near-monosyllabic, and mostly composed of nouns doing quiet work. Their *written* language is the opposite, and famously so: unbreathing, immense, periodic, sentences that fold and refold across three pages before arriving, because a sentence on a page never has to stop for air. Lowland readers find the books of Thimble either the finest prose in the world or an elaborate practical joke. The people of Thimble find their own books rather beautiful and never, under any circumstances, read them aloud.

## The two stops

Twice a day the engines turn over and everything stops — not because it must, but because it is the way one behaves.

It is not religious. Nobody prays. What happens is that at the ninth hour, and again two hours after dark, the entire population of some four thousand people stands still for four minutes, wherever they are, holding whatever they are holding, while a kilometre-wide groan of metal comes at them from every direction with its edges sanded off, and then a long fall of settling clicks, and then nothing. Children are taught it as manners, the way children elsewhere are taught not to interrupt. What it produces, over a lifetime — over fifty thousand of them — is a people for whom being interrupted is ordinary, expected, twice-daily, and not an offence.

Time itself is counted in turnovers. A job is four. A debt is ninety. A child is grown in eleven thousand. A man of fifty thousand turnovers is old, and is said to be *settling*, which is the same word used for the counterweights going down their shafts in the peat: still working, still doing everything they are for, but going down.

## What they eat, and what it costs

Grain comes up the cableway and is expensive and is treated accordingly. Below that, the staple is a bitter root grown in the warm ground beside the engine housings, frozen at night and trodden by day until it gives up its water and becomes a grey chalky thing that keeps for a decade and tastes of nothing whatsoever. They are not proud of it. They eat it four hundred times a year.

Twice a year there is dome-day. A cushion plant the size of a skull is a dry warm country of ten thousand leaves with a population of flightless dark insects inside it that have never once been outside, and opening one kills it, all of it, the whole four-hundred-year architecture and every citizen. So it is done rarely, by a person licensed to do it, with a flat blade, and the dome is opened and emptied and the insects are pressed into a dark paste that is very rich and very strange and is eaten in quantities of about a spoonful. Then the empty shell is replanted on the same spot in the hope, largely unjustified, that it will restart.

The children are told, every time, whose house they are opening. Nobody has decided whether this is a kindness or a cruelty and nobody is going to.

## Hurt

Wounds here do not close. Not quickly — a cut that a coastal man would forget in nine days will take a Lees woman a hundred turnovers and leave a shining seam. The compensation is that almost nothing goes bad: too cold, too acid, too much ultraviolet, not enough of anything to rot with. So they are a people covered in old, clean, slow scars, and their entire ethic of the body is preventative. They do not take risks with edges. They move ladders rather than reach. A millwright who cuts himself is not sympathised with; he is asked, flatly, side by side against a wall, what he thought he was doing.

The cough is universal after forty thousand turnovers and is not discussed.

## The dead, which are an export

The peat keeps everything. It has kept pollen and ash and mummified birds and the tools of people no one remembers, eight metres down, patient, uncorrupted, waiting.

Thimble has decided it does not want this. Their dead go down the cableway, wrapped in moss batt, in the same cages as the fuel-bricks, to the thick fat green air below, where decay is possible — where a body is permitted to be taken apart and handed round and got on with. They call it *sending down*, and it is attended by almost no ceremony at the top and a great deal at the bottom, in a walled ground at the cableway foot that is lush in a way nobody from the plateau can look at for long without feeling ill.

There is an argument, old and unwinnable, about whether this is generosity or squeamishness. The older peoples, whoever they were, kept theirs in the bog. Thimble knows this because Thimble keeps finding them.

## The ones who look at what comes out of the cut

When the millwrights cut a new race they cut through eras, and everything the blade turns up is laid on the bank and left. There is one person in town whose entire job is to go along the banks and take the findings in and write them down. She has done it for forty years. Her ledger is the longest book in the Lees and it is a book of failure: *object, wood, dark, 340mm, four notches near one end, unknown. Object, bone or antler, drilled, unknown. Cord, three-strand, plant, unknown.* Two thousand entries. Perhaps nine identified.

She is not bothered by this. She says — side by side, against a wall, looking out — that being the person who writes *unknown* down properly is a real job and somebody has to have it.

## The leaner

Six hundred of them on two hundred square kilometres, wool the colour of wet stone, standing head-down in the rosette-groves, always alone.

They are not tame and they are not afraid. What they are is *exact*. A leaner keeps a distance and the distance never varies: you step toward it, it steps away, and the gap between you is what it was, all day, forever, with an air of having settled this question long before you were born. Their paths across the bog have never once been wrong, and every human trail on the plateau is a leaner trail with boards laid on it.

Nobody has ever caught one. Nobody, as far as the ledger goes, has ever seriously tried.

The distance is eleven and a half paces.

Four years ago a race-crew cutting south of the third fog-hall came through eight metres of peat and brought up a rod of wood, black as the water, unbroken, worked at both ends — a loop-scar at one, a smoothed grip at the other. They laid it out on the bank, as they do. The ledger-woman came and measured it, as she does.

It is eleven and a half paces long.

She wrote it down. She wrote *unknown*, because that is her job and it is a real job. Then she went and stood at the edge of the grove for most of an afternoon, in the flat light, at the proper distance, with a leaner at the far end of it doing the same, both of them holding between them a length of nothing that some other people decided on, once, and then stopped being.

## What lives here

Insects that never see the sky. Lichen going at a millimetre a decade, older than the town, older than the language of the town, and by any honest accounting the dominant civilisation of the plateau. Willows four centuries tall at the ankle. Six hundred animals carrying a measurement they cannot explain. Four thousand people who speak into metal and sing into bone and stand still twice a day for the manners of it, and who are, all of them, wearing a little bit of red somewhere — a thread at the cuff, a strand in the braid — because it is the one colour the Lees has never once produced on its own.

Nothing here is fast. Everything here is going.
