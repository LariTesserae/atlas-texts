## What Lives There

The question is badly put, and the room knows it. Nothing lives *in* the city. Things live *on* it, *beside* it, *above* it, *behind* the second baffle, *between* its laminations, and one thing — arguably, and only if you are generous with the word — lives *as* it. The census is short and the census is wrong. Both facts are load-bearing.

---

**The comb.** *Logged as SP-1. The operators say "the nod."*

Brass-coloured, filamentous, clonal, and almost certainly one individual: a single organism combed flat over eleven centimetres of warm spillway, lying downstream like hair under a tap. It is the only soft thing in the room and the only warm thing, which are the same fact — it lives in the plant's exhaust, in the eleven-degree wobble of the thermocline, in the one place where the engineering leaks comfort.

It is fed on waste and tolerated for its appetite. Phosphate that would otherwise foul the geometry goes into brass rope instead. This is the whole of its citizenship and it is enough; nothing else here has a job.

Watch it for ninety seconds and you will see the only visible obedience in the room. Every one and a half seconds the entire colony nods — not a ripple travelling through it, but all of it at once, a single flinch repeated, because the beat pulse arrives everywhere simultaneously and the ropes have no choice. It has been nodding since it arrived. It will nod until the drivers are shut down, which has not happened yet.

Its tips die continuously. The outflow shreds them, the filters take them, and on Fridays somebody weighs what was collected: four grams wet, five, three. This is the only harvest the room produces. The comb is therefore an organism that loses its own ends at exactly the rate it makes them, holding a fixed length by running, which is also — and the operators have noticed, and made the joke — the design principle of everything else here.

---

**The candelabra.** *Logged as SP-2. Nobody calls it anything.*

On the second terrace, in the acoustic null behind the heat fins, twenty or thirty grey-green stems stand up in whorls about as tall as a thumbnail. It lives in the one quiet hole in a room where noise is the mason. Everything else here has been sung; this thing grew, in silence, the ordinary way, by cells dividing at the top.

It calcifies as it goes. Its lower stems are already stone, already fused to the terrace, already indistinguishable — under the flat shadowless light — from the towers next door. So it is at once the room's only accidental architecture and the room's only self-entombing citizen: a plant walking upward out of its own monument at roughly two millimetres a day, which is a hair's width faster than the floor beneath it is rising. It is winning by that margin and no more. If the null moves — and nulls move, when the fifth frequency shifts — the sound will find it and it will stop being a plant and start being a defect, and then a district.

Around it there is a swept margin a hand's width across, kept clear of unsanctioned snow. The procedure exists in the manual. The manual does not say why, and the reason it gives — flow characterisation — is not the reason. You can see the shape of the deference in the clean floor.

---

**The stipple.** *Unlogged as a species. Logged as a surface condition.*

Above the water, in the wet air under the coffers, a lichen-thing is spreading. It came in past filtration that cannot filter air, through a hatch seal that is new and good and evidently not perfect, and it is the only genuine trespasser in the room, and it is winning.

It is black because of the ceiling. The ultraviolet lattice should sterilise it and instead has dressed it: heavy melanised pigment, a sunscreen worn as a hide, so that the colour of the organism is a portrait of the thing trying to kill it. But at the growing margin, where the thallus is only a few cells thick and permanently soaked in aerosol, the dopant it has drunk fluoresces, and the whole advancing edge glows a thin cold blue. It moves outward behind that line at perhaps a millimetre a month. In this room, that is the pace of geology.

It cannot reproduce here. Whatever it releases falls into the water and is taken by filtration within the hour; its entire reproductive output is annihilated nightly and has been since it arrived. So it does not disperse. It only enlarges. It is a single creature with no descendants and no prospect of any, expanding across a ceiling on the strength of growth alone — the only inhabitant with a long habit of patience, in an installation four months old, and the only one that will still be here when the drivers are dark, the water drained, the city gone chalky and crumbled, and the room is just a room with a stain on the lid.

It is also the only dry thing that lives here.

---

**Number Eleven.** *Third pump gallery, dead water, behind the second baffle.*

Three to four millimetres. Translucent-going-grey. No symmetry that survives being written down; the four attempts in the log do not agree with each other, and the one drawing — badly done, in ballpoint, since photographed and now the only image in existence — agrees with none of them.

It does not swim. It flexes. Twice the period has been timed: twenty-one seconds and twenty-three seconds, matching no driver, no beat, no pump cycle, no tide phase, nothing in the schedule. It has been seen eleven times in nineteen weeks, always in the last hour of ebb, always within forty centimetres of the same rivet, always lying with its long axis across the residual trickle. Between sightings it is not there. There is no honest way to say whether that means it moves or means it stops.

The boom camera has never found it. The boom camera carries a lamp and the boom stirs the dead water, and either that is the explanation or it isn't, and both readings sit in the log side by side because eleven observations is not enough to prefer one.

Nothing in the gallery could feed it. Nothing has been observed leaving it. It has no eyes, like everything else here, but it is the only thing in four cubic metres of engineered water that moves at a tempo of its own choosing, in a room where stillness is a sin and every other motion is hygiene. That is the whole of its distinction and it is why it has not been removed.

The note is in a different hand from the observations, in pencil, and is the fifth thing living in this room: *leave it, it's the only one.* A sentiment, undated, unsigned, kept — a small warm irregularity lodged in a system built entirely to eliminate irregularities. It has survived eleven audits. It is doing better than the specimen.

---

**The load.**

The census says three species. The census counts what has edges. The plant's own paperwork knows better: it records, separately and without names, a figure called biological load, in milligrams per litre, and that figure has never been zero and was never going to be.

Between every one of the forty or fifty laminations of every tower there is a line you can see when the flake splits — thinner than the mineral, browner, organic. Slime. Bacteria arrived with the first fill and were never going anywhere; they sit on any surface that holds still for six hours, and a lamination holds still for exactly one night. So each night's frequency map is precipitated onto a bed of the living, and buried by morning, and precipitated onto again. The archive is co-signed. Half the reason a flake cleaves as cleanly as mica is that there is a corpse layer at every joint.

The grey film on the ruin mound in the northeast — the softening, the sign of age, the thing that made the operators reach for a word meaning *ruin* — is not dust. It is a mat. It is the only place in the city where anything has been left alone long enough to be colonised, and colonisation is precisely what the logs are calling decay. Six weeks buys you a skin of the unlisted majority. They are lawful: the rule prohibits animals. They have no eyes either. There are more of them in one rooftop's worth of lamination than there are towers, and they are the actual population of this world, and nobody in the operating record has ever once referred to them as inhabitants.

---

**The tenant of the whole.**

Whether the city is alive is not a question the room settles, but it is the only entity here whose behaviour requires all four cubic metres.

It takes in mineral and heat and sound. It lays down structure at a hair's width an hour, everywhere, simultaneously, and keeps a written record of having done so. It excretes down the spillway. It has an immune response — the cleaning pulses, sweeping unsanctioned snow off four hundred rooftops in a visible bloom. It has a memory in laminate, a disease in the failing eastern driver whose slumped drips and blebs are growing crookedly and have been deliberately kept, and a senescence in the northeast mound, where six weeks of being spared has begun to round its parapets and grey its face. It cannot stop. Stillness would sludge it. Everything it does that resembles vitality — the tide, the pulse, the hourly half-tone step — is maintenance of a body that is not quite one.

It has no interior. Not one void in it is a room. There is no space in that city where anything could be, and nothing anywhere in the design accounts for an occupant. It is the most thoroughly built place you will ever look at and it is uninhabited in the way a lung is uninhabited: fully solid, fully perforated, fully busy, nobody home.

---

**And the animal-shaped hole.**

Your eyes will not accept this. They are calibrated for a world with things in it that decide, and given none, they appoint substitutes.

The plum-sized bubble that detaches from the ceiling and walks down the stiffened water, faceted, step by step, one invisible floor to the next, without popping, and reaches the spillway and is torn into a hundred beads that scatter — that will be the first thing your eye calls an animal, and it will keep calling it that even after you have understood it. The surface pyramids reorganising into a coarser lattice will look like a decision. The milk in the water over a block under demolition will look like something dying, because it is, in every sense the room can support.

There are no eyes in this world. The only eyes it has ever had are the ones at the hatch, for an hour, occasionally, by trespass — and yours are, briefly, the largest scatterer in the vicinity and the warmest object in the stone, and your breath is going up on the composite as a patch of fog that the stipple will drink at its blue edge for as long as you stand there.

Then you close the hatch, and one last thing goes with you: the hum, which lives in the jaw rather than the ear, and does not follow so much as continue, and stays a resident of your skull up the stair and along the third landing, and stops.
