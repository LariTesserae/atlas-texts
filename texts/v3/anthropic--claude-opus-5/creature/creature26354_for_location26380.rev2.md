## The Pelt

Begin with the thing that is not a list.

The film is not a coating on the basin; the basin is a mineral deposit left by the film. Everything hard here has been either laid down or eaten by it. The rust cake on the fifth terrace is a building. Its architects are iron-oxidisers, each one a rod a thousandth of a millimetre long, secreting a twisted stalk of ferric hydroxide behind itself as it climbs toward the oxygen, the way a bindweed climbs a wire — except that the stalk is the entire content of the thing's life, and when the stalk is a centimetre tall the founder is a centimetre behind the front, dead, and now structural. The bright fox-orange you expose with your boot is the living edge. The black underneath is the archive.

They eat the difference between two states of iron. That is all. The gap between rust and not-rust is a very small voltage, and they live in it, and they have lived in it here without interruption for longer than the brickwork, because the ridge has never once stopped seeping and the air has never once stopped taking oxygen down to meet it. There is no generation in any meaningful sense. There is a continuity, thickening at a millimetre a decade, which is the only growth the basin permits.

The white beards on the sulphur margins are a different trade and a different temperament: filament-formers, sulphide-eaters, who stack internal beads of elemental sulphur inside themselves like a man carrying his dinner in his cheeks, and who wave because waving brings them a thousandth more of what they need. The beards do not decide to wave. The seep waves them. But they have arranged their lengths so that the seep can.

And under the collapsed slab, the brass glow. It is an accident — a few organisms dumping surplus electrons into a light-making reaction because it is cheaper than the alternative — and it illuminates, on purpose, nothing. Almost nothing here has eyes. But a few things do, and those few have quietly built their lives around a lamp that nobody lit.

## The Front

The tin snails are best understood as one animal with thousands of separable parts and a single behaviour.

On the exhalation, when the rind comes clear, they advance. Not quickly: a snail travels perhaps four centimetres in the open window, grazes, and is overtaken by the returning water, which it does not fight. It is carried and settles. The band of radular scoring around the pool is one finger wide because that is the reach of the compromise.

A shell here has a metallic sheen because the animal cannot avoid taking iron in with its food and has made a virtue of plating its house with the surplus. Split one and it flakes like enamel off a cheap pan. A snail of this kind lives about six years, which is a quarter of a million breaths, and in that time it may never travel more than two metres laterally. It moves up and down the same hand's-breadth of rind, eating a crop that regrows in ninety minutes, following its own dried mucus and everybody else's, because the mucus itself is a farm — the film colonises the slime, the snail comes back around and grazes what grew on where it walked. They are eating their own footprints, second-hand, forever, and doing well on it.

When the water returns there is a sound, if you are patient enough to isolate it: several thousand shells settling a millimetre onto wet stone, which arrives as a single soft granular *sift*, like sugar poured onto a table two rooms away.

## The Red Threads

In the ochre broth of the fifth terrace, where the mud is anoxic a centimetre down and stinks when opened, there are worms — small annelids, a few centimetres, brilliant red because they are stuffed with haemoglobin of extraordinary affinity, the kind that can strip oxygen out of water that has almost none. They live head-down in the cake and stand their tails up into the broth and wave them, slowly, to breathe.

In places they are dense enough to give the ochre a texture like short-pile carpet. Pass a hand over them, or let a shadow cross, and a wave of retraction runs outward from the disturbance — the whole patch flinching down into the mud in a spreading ring, and then re-emerging over the next four minutes, ring healing inward. It looks exactly like a blink.

They eat the mud. They pass it through themselves, and the pellets they void at the surface are cleaner than what they took in, which is one of the reasons the terraces filter as well as they do. The people who built the brick oval designed a cascade. The worms have been maintaining it, without being asked, since the design was abandoned.

## The Ranks

The crickets are the only residents who walk out.

At night — and night here is a rumour, established by other animals' behaviour — a proportion of the wall ranks detach and climb the brick, over the rounded loaves and the rounded mortar, up to the lip, where they stand. They stand a long time. The lip is the only real edge, and something in them knows it, and their antennae work over the dry side of it, testing an air that has no drip in it, no hiss, no texture: an air that is simply *empty*, which must be, to a cricket that has never known otherwise, a kind of cliff.

Then some go over, out onto the grey shale, in the wet dark, and forage on almost nothing — the odd wind-dropped fragment, the dust of other valleys — and most come back before the fog thins. A few do not come back. Those few are the basin's only export apart from vapour and the bats' flight muscles, and the export is always one-way, and the basin does not notice the loss.

On the wall they arrange themselves at antennal distance, each one's feelers resting lightly on the back or legs of its neighbours, forty to the square metre, overlapping like fish scales. This is not affection and it is not warmth; there is no warmth to share. It is a network. A footfall anywhere on that wall crosses the assembled ranks as a wave of small adjustments, faster than any individual could have detected it, and the wave arrives at the far end before the sound does. They have made a single sense organ out of sixty of themselves and they take it apart every night and rebuild it every dawn.

## The Commuters

The bats do not live here. They sleep here. This is a distinction the basin does not care about, because what it wants from them is not company.

They leave on an out-breath — always, and you can set your watch by it if you have the patience to sit through three departures. The cool draught coming up out of the pool's centre gives them their first eight metres of lift for free, and sixty small animals that must hunt all night on a budget of a few grams of fat have not failed to notice this. They drop from the gable, catch the exhalation, and rise over the rim on it, and are gone into a country that the rest of the basin has never met.

They come back with it inside them. The guano under the gable is the single richest substance in the hollow, and it is not local: it is moth, mostly, and midge from other waters, and beetle from a barley field, and — this is the strange part — pollen. Pollen in the droppings, lime and willow and hawthorn, a fine yellow dusting of a world that has plants in it. The only green thing that ever enters this basin arrives dead, in a bat's gut, and is immediately eaten by mites.

And this is where the basin gets its one season. Nothing here has a year: no light to ration it out, no frost, no heat. But in winter the bats hang and stop, and for four months the import ceases, and the guano field goes cold and is mined out, and the beetles that work it thin down to the few that can wait, and the mites that work the beetles go to their long patience. The basin's only calendar is bat-shaped and comes from somewhere else.

## Under the Gable

Where there are midge pulses there are spiders. Orb-weavers, pale, long-legged, one to a corner of the ruined brickwork, spinning webs that sag under condensation within the hour and must be re-hung constantly, which is why they hang them badly — loose, drooping, deliberately underbuilt, because a perfect web in this air is a wet rag in ten minutes and a wasted night's silk.

Four or five minutes of midge column, twice a day, is their entire economy. Between times they sit with a foreleg on a signal thread and do nothing at all, for hours, in the brass half-light, and they are among the very few things down here with working eyes, so it is at least possible that they see it.

Their egg sacs hang under the gable like small paper lanterns, and when the young emerge they do the thing their kind does: climb high, raise the abdomen, pay out a thread, and wait for wind to take them. There is no wind. There is the breath. So they balloon on the exhalation, in their hundreds, a drift of specks lifting off the brick and out over the terraces — and then the inhalation comes, gently, and most of them come back. They land on the same wall their mother worked. A few get over the rim. The basin disperses its children the way it does everything else: reluctantly, at a loss of a few percent, into a world it has no information about.

## The Long One

There are nine in the pool. Possibly eleven. They are hard to count because they will not be in two places you can see at once.

A gutter eel is not an eel. It is an amphibian that never grew up: forty centimetres of unpigmented muscle the colour of a peeled fingernail, with a feathered crimson fringe of external gills at the neck that it kept because the water was cold and rich and there was never any reason to climb out and become something else. It is blind, or nearly — there are eyes under the skin, covered over, reading nothing but a vague sense of when it is being looked at. What it has instead is a lateral line, and a pressure sense in the skin so fine that it can feel a shrimp's leg-stroke at half a metre and can certainly feel you, on the terrace above, shifting your weight.

It hunts at the pace the temperature allows. Thirty centimetres in nine minutes; a strike that is, after all that, shockingly quick — a sideways snap, a turn of the whole body, done. Then nothing again, for possibly a week. These animals can go years without eating and lose very little; they can live seventy years, eighty, a hundred; there are records of individuals that stayed within five metres of one stone for a decade and were assumed dead every time they were checked. Their lives are not slow in the sense of being impoverished. They are slow in the sense of being *long*, and they have been spent almost entirely inside a volume about the size of a small chapel's font.

They avoid one another. The pool has a geography of tenancies — the warm stretch along the live pipe, the overhang, the deep cold corner under the slab — and these are held, for years, and relinquished only on death. Twice in a human lifetime, perhaps, something in the water changes enough that two of them will find each other on purpose, and there will be a slow clumsy circling, and afterwards a clutch of eggs stuck to the underside of the collapsed slab in the brass glow, guarded by one parent for four months without food, fanned and fanned, most of them failing.

One of them is much larger than the others and has a kink in the tail, an old break badly reset. It holds the pipe. It lies along the warm iron for days at a time, gills barely stirring, at the one place in this hollow that is two degrees better than everywhere else — a hearth with no fire, heated by the difference between a deep rock and a cold night. It has held that spot for as long as anything here can be said to have held anything. The break in its tail is older than the fungus on the grease, older than the midge-skin pelt on the flywheel, older than any cricket lineage on the wall. It may have been a young animal in water that still had men in it, and oil, and heat, and noise. If so, nothing about it now remembers that, because remembering is not among the things this body does. It simply *dates from* then. It is the oldest continuous inhabitant, and it is a piece of the basin's furniture, and when the pipe burps and the hollow *tonk* goes round the oval twice, the big one does not flinch, and nothing that has ever lived here has flinched at that sound.

## Below the Refusal Line

Under the glassy layer that will not mix, there is a second basin with its own population, and nothing crosses between them except corpses going down.

Here it is sulphate-reducing bacteria, which take the sulphur that iron has been shedding for a century and breathe it, and give off the sour gas that comes up when you disturb the water. They cannot tolerate oxygen. They have not encountered any since they arrived. Around and among them lives a fauna of nematodes and ciliates so small and so specialised that they have dispensed with the organelles for burning oxygen entirely — a whole functioning food web, predator and prey, running on chemistry that the rest of the world abandoned two billion years ago.

They are in the mud, which is laminated. Each dark band is a year's dust and death; you could read the basin's history off a core sample like tree-rings, including the decade when there was grease in the water, including whatever it was that changed in the year the men stopped coming. The smell that rises when you stir it is the smell of that reading being interrupted. It is not a memory. It is just that the sequence prefers to be left in order.

## The Grease Eaters

One tenancy here is temporary, and it is the only thing in the hollow that is running out.

In the bearing housing of the absent engine, and in a wide stain in the concrete under where the plinth bolts describe a missing pump, there is old lubricant. Tallow and mineral oil, thick, rancid, largely insoluble, laid down in one decade and eaten ever since at the rate the cold permits. The pale finger-growth works it. Moth flies — tiny, furred, heart-winged, walking on the film like small grey moths that have given up on flying — breed in the greasy scum, and their larvae rasp at it, and the spiders eat the adults, and so a measurable fraction of the visible life under the gable is still, at three removes, funded by a spillage made by a man who was in a hurry on a particular afternoon.

There is perhaps twenty years of it left. When it is gone the finger-growth will go, and the moth flies will go, and the corner spiders will get a little thinner, and that will be the last change this basin ever makes on its own account.

## Whoever Is In The Third Terrace

It is worth saying plainly, because the rule is plain: nothing leaves here except as vapour.

If a man went into this water — a hundred and forty years ago, at the end of a shift, in the dark, drunk or unlucky or pushed — he would not have gone anywhere. He would have gone down, and been worked over by everything in this account in turn, and the lime would have found him within a decade, and by now he would be a lobe of pale drapery on the third step, soft-cornered, indistinguishable, with a droplet forming on his underside and swelling and falling and being replaced, and a band of radular grooves one finger wide scored across him where the snails come and go.

That is not haunting. That is the basin doing the only thing it does. Break something and it will be re-cemented rather than removed. He would be a surface. Things would graze on him. He would be, by now, load-bearing.

## What Cannot Live Here

Every resident falls into one of two classes, and there is no third.

There are those that never leave — film, worm, snail, shrimp, eel, the small anaerobic nations under the refusal line — and whose entire span occurs inside eighty paces by sixty, on a schedule hung off an eleven-minute breath that they did not choose and have never been without. They are not trapped, exactly. Trapped implies an alternative was understood. They are simply *of* it, the way the iron cake is of it, and if you lifted one out it would not experience escape; it would experience the end of everything it had ever been in contact with.

And there are those that leave and come back on time: the bats on the out-breath, the crickets over the lip and home before the fog thins, the ballooning spiderlings going out and drifting back on the inhalation like dust returning to a shaken sheet.

What cannot live here is anything that needs to leave *once*. The basin has no provision for a one-way departure. Everything it takes in, it keeps, in some form, in some layer — water, iron, sulphur, grease, moth-scales, a man, you — and the only thing it ever lets go is vapour, rising off the pewter surface into a fog ceiling that never quite tears, on the slow steady breath of a chest that is in no hurry whatsoever and has, so far, taken every single one of its breaths.
