## The Residents

Every living thing in the sink arrived by a route that is now closed.

That is the first fact about the population here, and most of the others follow from it. Nothing walked in. Nothing swam in. There is no corridor, no drainage that connects to another drainage, no flyway. What got in came as dust on a high wind, or as a seed in a gut, and the gut passed over exactly once. The census is therefore small, strange, and heavily weighted toward organisms that can survive being airborne and dead-looking for a decade. It is a lottery result. Everything here won something, and nothing here has anywhere to go.

---

**The fig.**

It is the only resident with a silhouette.

Some bird — and it can only have been a bird, one bird, one time — sat on the northern rim with a belly full of fruit from somewhere with more water in it, and left behind a smear of seeds no bigger than sand. Nearly all of them failed. One found the joint. It germinated on rock, in a crack a knuckle wide, with no soil and no promise, and did what its family does, which is to treat stone as a temporary inconvenience.

The trunk is the thickness of an arm and this tells you nothing about its age. The crack rations it; the seep rations it; a fig in a good riverbed would be four times the size in a tenth of the time. It could be eighty years old. It could be five hundred. Its wood keeps no honest record.

What it does have is the roots: twelve feet of them down the vertical face, white going to grey, flattened against the rock the way wax flattens, splitting and rejoining and splitting again, following the wet. Two of the lower ones have thickened into something that is halfway to being a second trunk. In a century or two, if the water holds, there may be a small grove down that wall which is genetically one tree, or one tree's opinion of itself, repeated.

The fig is the sink's landlord in the literal sense: it takes rent off the top. It drinks the northern seep before the fern gets it, and it intercepts the sunlight before the floor gets it, and everything below lives in the green shade of a creditor. It is also the sink's only importer. A few hundred grams of hard dark leaves come down each year, into the pool and onto the breakdown, and that quantity — call it the weight of a loaf — is the entire annual budget of organic matter arriving from the lit world. Everything that eats in this place is, at one remove or another, eating the fig's losses.

And every year it does the other thing, the pointless magnificent thing. It sets figs. Not fruit: the fig is not a fruit, it is a room, a hollow chamber lined on the inside with hundreds of flowers, evolved down to the last millimeter as a reception hall for a wasp the length of a comma. The tree builds the rooms. It ripens the scent and releases it, at the right hour, into the warm still air under the rim, a precise chemical invitation with a single correct recipient. Nothing comes. The wasp's lineage does not exist within two hundred kilometers of this tableland, and it has no way of getting here either. The figs harden, go from green to a dull purple-brown, and drop unpollinated into the sink like knuckles, where they lie on the calcite glaze and are slowly taken apart by things too small to see.

It has never once succeeded. It cannot learn. It will do it again next year.

It is also the only thing in the sink that is not finished. The root in the joint is a wedge, and a wedge under a tree's water pressure is a tool with several tons behind it, applied at a rate you would need instruments to detect. Somewhere between a thousand and ten thousand years from now, the fig or its clone-children will bring a section of the northern lip down into the sink, taking the third and fourth steps with it, opening the wall, changing the light. This is the only scheduled event in the place. The fig is scheduling it without knowing, the way everything is done here.

---

**The fern.**

Under the drip line, the maidenhair. From above it reads as one cushion, and it may in fact be one plant, spread by rhizome across two square meters over an uncountable stretch of time. Its spores came as dust; ferns are very good at being dust; it could have arrived in any century.

The visible fern is only half of it, and the less interesting half. Push the fronds aside and look at the wet rock beneath: there are films there, green, translucent, heart-shaped, each the size of half a fingernail and a few cells thick. Those are the fern's other generation, the sexual one, and they are doing something the tableland above absolutely forbids. They are releasing sperm that must *swim* — free-swimming cells with whipping tails, crossing a film of water half a millimeter deep to reach an egg a centimeter away. In the whole hundred kilometers of dry limestone, the fern's sex requires an ocean, and this is it: the wet patch under the north lip, permanently renewed at eleven-second intervals. The drip is not watering the fern. The drip *is* the fern's sea, and the sea is maintained by one leak in a ceiling.

The cushion is also the only three-dimensional structure in the sink, the only architecture at floor level, the only shade under the shade. Which is where the rest of the population lives.

---

**The animals, which exist.**

The account says there are no animals. That is true at the scale a person walks at, and it is the reason for the hole in the sound — every animal that makes an audible noise is an animal that flies, and fliers need a food supply that isn't here. So the fauna of the sink is complete and it is silent and it is roughly the size of a grain of salt.

Take a handful of the fern cushion. In it:

Bdelloid rotifers, several hundred. They look like nothing, and then, under any lens at all, like transparent leeches with two spinning wheels at the head, which are not wheels but rings of beating hairs that sweep bacteria into a mouth. They graze the films off the fern's black stems. They are all female. Their entire lineage has been all female for perhaps forty million years, with no males anywhere in it, reproducing by making copies, which makes them well suited to a place with no partners and no post.

Nematodes, fewer, thicker, blind, thrashing through the water film in the manner of something swimming in syrup.

Tardigrades: perhaps a dozen in the handful, claw-footed, slow, absurd, each one grazing an area it would take a lifetime to cross, which is two centimeters.

All three of these arrived the same way, and it is the only way available. They came as dust. Dried to a husk, metabolism stopped, indistinguishable from grit — a tardigrade in that state is a barrel of nothing that can survive years of desiccation, heat, and a fall from the top of the sky. The wind that dries the shrubs on the plain carries this grit constantly, and most of it lands on bare hot limestone and stays dead forever. A tiny fraction of it lands on the fern cushion, gets wet, and takes twenty minutes to swell back into an animal.

There may also be a mite. If there is — and in ten thousand handfuls you might find three — it is the largest animal in the sink at half a millimeter, an apex predator with a range of forty centimeters and a population, possibly, of one.

In the pool: the same crowd, washed down. Rotifers rolling over the algal film, a few nematodes in the soft sediment at the outlet seam. Nothing with a shell. Nothing with a swimming larva. Those need a carrier — a duck's foot, a stream, a flood that connects two waters — and the sink has never in its existence been connected to anything.

---

**The mats.**

The dark film on the flowstone under the water is the pool's economy in its entirety: cyanobacteria mostly, in a slime of their own making, with a scatter of diatoms whose glass shells blew in from a lake four hundred kilometers away and settled here to keep working.

They do two jobs. The first is feeding everyone, which is a small job. The second is that in pulling carbon dioxide out of the water to build themselves, they tip the chemistry over, and calcite drops out of solution beneath them. Layer by layer, at the thickness of a wish per year, the film builds the pale floor it is standing on. The basin of the pool is not simply a geological feature. It is partly a body — a mineral secreted by a crust of cells, with older crusts fossilized in it, going down.

On the wet wall, the liverworts, flat green rosettes with little cups on their upper surfaces. The cups are catapults. They are shaped to catch a falling drop and convert it into a splash that throws a few dozen clone-buds several centimeters sideways. In a place with no wind at floor level and no animal to carry anything, the only available force is gravity arriving one drop at a time, and the liverwort has built its whole dispersal around it. The sink's single catapult fires several thousand times a day and moves its ammunition, on average, the width of a hand.

---

**The lichen.**

On the drier blocks, and along the top of the dry-laid wall at the rim, the grey-black crust: a fungus with an alga farmed inside it, two organisms so long married that they have one name, one shape, and no interest in the arrangement being questioned.

It grows at a fraction of a millimeter a year, which makes it the slowest resident and the most reliable clock. The skin of lichen running across the joints of that wall, sealing stone to stone, is a measurement of how long ago the last hand touched it, legible to anyone who knows the species and is willing to do the arithmetic.

It is also the only resident performing geology. Its acids are etching the calcite beneath it, a micron at a time, releasing grains — and grains, given a few more millennia, are soil. The lichen is the only thing here manufacturing the future.

It does not need the pool. It does not need the fig. If the whole roof came down tomorrow and the sink became a bright dry scar in the plain, the fern and the liverworts and the rotifers would be gone inside a season, and the lichen would not notice.

---

**Under everything.**

The seep on the northeastern wall is water that has been inside the rock for something like a decade, and it arrives carrying almost nothing: a few parts per million of dissolved carbon, some sulfur, some iron, a great deal of dissolved limestone, and a population.

In the pores and hairline fractures of the tableland, thinly, there are bacteria. They do not eat sunlight and they do not eat leaves. They take apart minerals. The brown-black varnish staining the walls at the dark ends of the chamber, the stuff you would take for shadow or damp, is in part a manganese and iron deposit laid down by cells that are making a living from the difference between one oxidation state and another — a living so meagre that a generation may take a year, or a decade, or longer. There are lineages down there that have divided fewer times since the roof fell than the fig has made leaves.

Add them up across the whole tableland — a flat, diffuse sheet of cells through kilometers of rock, everywhere the water goes — and they outweigh the fig, the fern, the lichen, the mats and the entire invisible fauna together, by a margin not worth calculating.

The biggest living thing near the sink is under the floor, has no shape, and is in no hurry.

---

**The ones who kept a residence and did not live here.**

The feet that dished the steps belonged to the only inhabitants who ever had plans, and they were not inhabitants. You cannot live at a water-stop; the entire meaning of the place is that you arrive, fill, and go. They cut the basin under the strongest drip because they did not want to wait with a vessel held up in their arms, and that small impatience is the most human thing in the sink.

The rock answered it. The spout has run without pause ever since, and the water has hung its own copy beneath it in stone — a slow mineral duplicate of the stream, going nowhere, filling nothing. The last genuinely new idea this place had was had by a person with a pick, and the sink has spent every year since translating it patiently into its own material.

---

**What it comes to.**

A loaf of leaves a year. A skin of algae. A cushion of fern with a silent zoo in it, each animal smaller than the dot on this page. A lichen going at the speed of stone. Beneath, a vast slow nothing-much spread through the pores of a hundred square kilometers.

Everything alive here is running the same accounts as the pool: what comes in is what goes out, held inside a hand's breadth of a mark. Nothing accumulates. Nothing is stored against a future, because the residents that could imagine a future have all moved on, and the ones remaining have no organ for it.

If you sat on the largest block for an hour, nothing would notice you. Your shadow would cool two square meters of flowstone by a degree, which would be, in local terms, weather. And at some point in that hour the drip would land on the fern cushion with its soft *chuff*, and the shiver would go out through the fronds and die somewhere near the edge, and in the patch where it died a rotifer that had been dry and unliving for six months would take on water, swell, unfold, put out its wheels, and begin to eat.

That happens several times a day. It is the sink's whole calendar, and the population, to the extent that any of them can be said to hold an opinion, finds it sufficient.
