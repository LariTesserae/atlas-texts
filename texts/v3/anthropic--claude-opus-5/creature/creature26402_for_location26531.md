## Who Lives Here

Ask the question and the honest first answer is small enough to be disappointing: *a film*. Something on the order of four millimetres thick, greenish-black when wet, the colour of the ground when dry, spread out over an area you would need months to cross. That is the inhabitant. That is the whole population, the whole society, the whole history of settlement.

But four millimetres is only the answer if you insist on measuring in the wrong direction. Turn the measurement on its side. Take a coin of the crust — press a thumbnail in at dawn while it is still soft and lift out a plug the width of a fingertip — and hold it up edgewise to the light, and what you have in your hand is not a smear of slime. It is a stack of countries.

## The Stacking

The top half-millimetre is the canopy. This is where the green lives: fine filaments, each one a chain of cells in a slick transparent sheath, tangled and interwoven and glued into a felt. They are the only things here that can make a living out of nothing but light and water and air, and everything above them and below them and around them for a thousand kilometres is downstream of that one competence.

Under the green, within the next tenth of a millimetre — a distance you cannot resolve without wetting the sample and letting it separate — there is purple. Then a stratum of rose. These are the light-eaters who cannot bear oxygen, who work with the dregs of the spectrum that filter through the canopy, red and infrared, the light the green ones have no use for. They live in the shade of a garden a hair thick.

Below them the colour goes out and the smell comes in: a dark seam, the black bottom, anoxic, faintly sulphurous, where cells that have never seen light break down the corpses and the leakage and the sugary excess of everyone above and give back, upward, the sulphide and the acids that the purple layer drinks. Nothing in this stack is self-sufficient. Every layer is living off the waste of a neighbour it will never touch, in a traffic conducted entirely by diffusion, over distances of microns, with no negotiation and no failure.

There are more distinct ways of being alive in that plug of crust than in a hectare of forest. It is only that they are stacked instead of spread, and the whole arrangement — canopy, understorey, soil, and the dark composting basement — is thinner than a page.

## Their Day

They are awake for about two hours.

In the last third of the night, when the air lets go of its water and the universal sweat comes down, the sheaths swell. Filaments that spent the afternoon glued into hard varnish loosen and begin to glide — actually to move, without limbs, a slow extrusion along their own axis, micrometres a minute, which is the only travelling anybody does in this world. They come up. The whole surface, everywhere, at once: the green rises through the felt toward the coming light, and the purple rises behind it, and the mat thickens and softens and darkens, and by first light the terrace country has changed colour from bone to olive.

Then for perhaps two hours everything happens. The green ones split water and let the oxygen go — it collects under the surface film, lifts the mat in blisters, escapes as those strings of silver bubbles that sit in the shallows and are, if you are close enough, audible. The floor is at its most fertile, most crowded, most productive. The smell comes up warm off it, mineral and slightly sweet.

By mid-morning the light stops being a gift. It becomes hard, then punishing, and the filaments go back down — the same slow gliding, in reverse, deeper into their own accumulated sediment, to sit out the day under two or three millimetres of their own dead architecture with the shutters closed. The surface glasses over, whitens, chalk-bright and inert. From noon to dusk the country is not exactly asleep; it is holding still, the way something holds still in an oven. The ticking you hear from the columns is this: the sound of an entire population declining to move.

Their year is not a year. Their year is the interval between sheet-floods — the day when the water comes around your ankles and keeps going, and buries everyone under a fresh millimetre of white, and everyone has to climb. That is the great event, the reckoning, the census. Most of a mat's effort over its whole existence goes into not being buried by the very thing it is building.

## Where the Individual Would Go, If There Were One

There isn't one, and the absence is not a technicality.

A mat has no edge that means anything. It thins, it patches, it merges with the mat over the rim; the felt is continuous for kilometres and then discontinuous for a metre and then continuous again. You cannot ask how many live in a basin. You can ask how much.

Nor is there descent in the shape you would expect. They divide, yes, one becoming two becoming four, but they also pass instructions sideways — small closed rings of chemical text, handed cell to cell like a coin, across kinds, across strata, across the whole width of the continent by relay. A trick worked out at some warm seep in the ochre country — a way to survive a saltier film, a way to shrug off a particular metal — is not the property of a lineage. It is loose in the world. Given a few thousand years, given wind and flood and the slow gluey commerce of the crusts, it will turn up everywhere. The population has no memory and no council and no language, and it nonetheless holds a common stock of competences, distributed, unowned, undeletable.

And they do not properly die. The living front is always the top. Underneath, the older cells are not corpses so much as they are masonry: bound in their own sheaths, threaded through with carbonate, whitening, hardening, becoming the floor. Every one of them ends by being promoted into the ground. The banding you see in a broken column, onion on onion, is not a record of the dead. It *is* the dead, in the position they died in, doing the only job that remains to them, which is holding up the ones above.

This is a population that walks on its ancestors and is made of them and cannot read a word of them.

## The Standing Ones

If anything here deserves to be called somebody, it is the columns.

They begin as accidents — a pebble that isn't there, a high point in a basin, a thickness in the mat that catches slightly more of the dawn and slightly less of the flood-silt. The advantage is minute and it compounds. The mat on that spot builds upward instead of outward, generation on generation, and the shape steepens, and after a century it is a knuckled lump at the ankle, and after some centuries a fat pale candle at the waist, cauliflowered where the growth front bulged around obstructions it has long since incorporated. Everything it has ever been is still inside it, in order, in bands.

They are single continuous lives in a way nothing else here is. A column has a top, a shape, an age, a sunward side that is warm to the palm by mid-morning and a shaded side that stays clammy till nine. It has a history that could in principle be told, if anyone were counting laminae. It has, in the narrowest possible sense, a strategy: *be taller*.

And that is the country's only war. There is no eating here, no hunting, nothing that needs to be quick — but there is competition, absolutely, ferocious, for light and for the high ground, and it is fought by growing an inch. Battles take four hundred years. The losers are not killed; they are shaded, and then they are silted, and then they are floor. Wander any basin at the right hour and you will find the outcome written in the terracing: a scatter of standing winners, and under everyone's feet the flat white consolidated record of everybody who did not rise.

They tick as they dry. Nothing is signalling. It is only the sound of a very large number of very small things contracting in unison, at a pitch a clock would recognize.

## The Seep Nations

The seeps are foreign.

Where the warm alkaline water comes up out of the deep layers, the inhabitants are of a different order entirely — heat-tolerant, brilliantly pigmented, arranged not in strata but in rings, because at a seep the important gradient is not up-and-down but out-from-the-lip. At the source, in the hottest clearest water, there is almost nobody, a thin colourless varnish that would cook anything else. A rim or two down, in slightly cooler flow, the filaments begin: soft streamers combing out downstream, all pointing the same way, the only oriented thing in this world. Further down, where the water has given up ten degrees, the ring goes orange, then amber, then that thick deep green where the ground becomes spongy underfoot and the smell is wet limestone and bread.

Each seep is an island and the plain between seeps is an ocean nobody can swim. A seep community is a genuine population with a genuine boundary, four days' walk from its nearest neighbour, and it goes its own way — accumulating small local peculiarities, its own tolerances, its own dialect of chemistry — for as long as the spring runs. Traffic between islands is by accident only: a scrap of dried filament lifted off a crumbling terrace by wind, carried, dropped, and in nine hundred and ninety-nine cases in a thousand dropped on bone-dry pavement where it simply waits, viable, and never wakes.

When a spring shifts its plumbing and dies, the mound goes white, and the whole nation with it. Nothing marks the event. The plain rises past it a few centimetres a year until it is a rise, then a swell, then nothing.

## The Sleepers

Out past several weeks of walking there is the great dead flat, the unbanded pavement, hot enough to blister a palm, and it is not dead.

It is the largest settlement in the country, and every single resident is unconscious.

They are in the salt: cells shut down to a hard minimum, walled in crystal, some of them held in bubbles of brine no bigger than a grain of pollen, their chemistry stopped at a level that no instrument here could call life or call death. Ten thousand years is not long for them. They are waiting for the flood that comes to that country perhaps once in a century, or once in a thousand years, and when the water at last runs out that far and stands for two days, the pavement goes faintly pink — a bloom of red-and-rose halophiles across a hundred square kilometres, an entire civilization living out its whole active existence inside a week — and then the water goes, and the salt closes over, and they lie down again.

Immediately before and after, this is the emptiest place in the world. During, it is the most crowded.

## The Deep Population

And there are the ones inside the country.

Dig, and it is bands all the way down, further than a hole can be dug in a day. Between those bands, in the pore water, in films of moisture pressed between laminae fifty and eighty and three hundred metres beneath the surface, there are cells. Not many. Cut off from light for longer than the surface has existed in its present form, living off nothing but the slow chemistry of the rock itself — iron changing its mind, sulphur turning over, hydrogen seeping — at a pace where a single division might take a thousand years, and a whole life is spent doing what a surface filament does before breakfast.

They are the buried, still going. They were once the top. They have no idea there is a top. Some of them are older, as continuous living individuals, than the mounds and the columns and the terraces above them, older than the region's whole visible arrangement, and they will still be there, unhurried, a hundred metres further down, when it has all been laid over and forgotten.

The country is not standing on its dead. It is standing on its very slow.

## The Newcomers

At the seep margins and in the lee of rims: the low fleshy things, blue-grey, salt-tolerant, no taller than a hand.

They are the strangest inhabitants here, and the fewest, and the only ones that do not belong to the general scheme. Everything else in this world is an accumulation — a felt, a stack, a mound, a thing that got where it is by adding. These have a *plan*. They have an inside and an outside. They have organs, plumbing, a decision made in advance about where the water goes. They are the first tenants who did not build the building.

They are also, quietly, the only ones here practising demolition: roots crack a rim, a little acid dissolves a laminate, and around each clump the ground is very slightly *worn*. It is the only wearing away in the entire country. It amounts to nothing yet. It is the first faint rumour of a world that will one day have valleys, and directions, and therefore names.

## Their Work, Which None of Them Know About

They are making three things and are aware of none of them.

They are making the floor. That is the obvious one: band on band, a hair a night, a millimetre a flood, centimetres a year, a continent of their own spent bodies.

They are making the air. The oxygen that lifts off in silver strings each dawn and burns away by nine has, over a span with no monuments in it, been quietly accumulating overhead, and the sky is thinner and bluer and more dangerous than it was. They did that. It is the largest act in the history of the place and it took less deliberation than the folding of a sheath.

And they are making, without the smallest inkling, the conditions for something to come along that eats. There is no defence anywhere in this country. The knobs on the columns are not armour; the crust does not hide; nothing runs, or clenches, or has thought of a spine or a shell or a toxin, because the entire strategy of every inhabitant assumes a world in which the only thing that can happen to you is being shaded and slowly built over. Put a single grazer down in the middle of it — one small mouth, one thing that scrapes — and the terraces would go quiet in a way they have never been quiet, and the ticking would thin out, and the floor would stop rising.

That has not happened. It is not near. For now the residents lie four millimetres deep and a continent wide, awake two hours a day, going up a few centimetres a year, glued to a world they built and cannot see, breathing out the poison that will eventually make their successors possible.

By morning they are wet again, and slightly higher.
