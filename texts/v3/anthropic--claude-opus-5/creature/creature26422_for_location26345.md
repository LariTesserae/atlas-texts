## Who is here

Take the census honestly and it comes out at one.

Everything else on the Thatch is a tenant of the fibre — living in it, off it, inside the slow arithmetic of its balance — and everything else knows this in the way that a mite on a beetle's back knows about the beetle: not as a fact but as a direction. Down is the landlord. Up is weather. The tenants are numerous, and some of them are people, and the people are the least of them by mass and the most of them by opinion.

## The tenants of the Weft

**The grazers** are the substrate of everything. Hand-sized, pale as boiled root, eyeless, with a rasping tongue-plate that takes the inner wall of a gallery down about a millimetre a season and keeps the vessels from occluding. They move in sheets — a wall will look wet and then you understand the wetness is travelling. Ten thousand tongue-plates working at once make a sound that anybody who has ever been to the rim recognises instantly and irrationally as rain. Weft-born children learn what rain is from grazers, and are disappointed by the real thing, which is violent and does not come from the ceiling in an orderly way.

**The askers** have too many legs and no eyes and an apologetic manner. They are scavengers, waist-high, and they attach themselves to any party carrying food, following at nine or ten metres and closing in during sleep to take crumbs, gut-scrapings, hair, the softened ends of ropes. When noticed they perform something no one has adequately explained: they stop, lower the front third of the body, tap the ground twice, and back away. Then they come again. It is impossible to be angry at them for long, which may be the whole strategy. Households do not feed askers and do not drive them off, and the galleries an asker-line frequents are noticeably cleaner, and nobody has ever eaten one.

**The rope-snakes** live in the ceiling lattice and hunt downward. They are the reason every sleeping platform in the Weft is roofed with a cane grid at a hand's spacing, and the reason a mother counts children by touch four times a night, and the reason there is a whole small vocabulary for the sound a lattice makes when something is moving in it that weighs more than the lattice expected.

**The trumpeters** are the road. Three metres at the shoulder, loose-hided, blind in any useful sense, they lever their way through the lower galleries and by levering they keep them open: a trumpeter road is two and a half metres of clear passage, polished, with the crossings snapped out and the slumps trodden into ramps. Every human route in the deep Weft is a trumpeter road, and the people's relationship with them is not domestication and not hunting, but something closer to a treaty maintained by gift. The gift is salt. A family that meets a herd puts out its salt — actual salt, bought up from the plains at the day-two markets at a price that would appal you — and lets the herd lick, and does not take a calf, and in exchange has a road. Trumpeter meat is eaten, but only from a beast that has already died, and the eating is heavy with etiquette. A trumpeter's hide gives a household its water-bags for two generations. Its call, coming up through a vent-mound at night as a bass blast that makes the nap ripple, is not frightening to anyone born here. It is the sound of the road being kept.

**The choir** is the clock.

The nymphs are packed into their cells in the mid-galleries by the hundred million, layer on layer, forty years of them, stridulating from the day they hatch — the gravel-down-a-chute noise that is the whole texture of a Thatch night. Then, in a season that comes once in roughly forty years, they stop. All of them. Over about nine days the ratcheting thins, patch by patch, and the Weft fills up with a hush that has no precedent in any individual life, and then the imagos come up through every vent-mound and every crack in the nap for a fortnight, in a mass that turns the ochre plain silver-grey and then black with the ones that have died, and everything on the Thatch eats until it is sick.

The people count their lives in choirs. Two choirs is a long life. A person says *I was born a year before the choir* the way you would say a date, and everybody in earshot knows within a year how old that makes them, and also knows what it means about the emergence they were carried through as an infant and the one they will probably not see. Marriages are contracted in emergence years in numbers that make the following decade lopsided. So do feuds. There is no calendar on the Thatch except this one and the Dew Hour, and no historian, and nothing anyone would call a date; but every family can tell you which choir their grandmother's casting came up in, and be right.

## The people

They are not many. They will not add up to a civilisation, and they know it, and it does not appear to trouble them.

Look at one. Broad splayed feet with long prehensile toes for gripping cane-tips; the nap gives a hand's width and hands you forward, and a Thatch stride is a rolling, energy-recovering thing that is beautiful on the surface and pitiful anywhere else. On hard ground they limp within an hour and are lame within a day. This is called rim-legs and it is why nobody has ever gone back down and stayed. The Thatch keeps no prisoners; it has simply arranged the door so that it works better in one direction.

Barrel chest, because at noon the air pounds and you learn very young to breathe against a pressure. Bad distance vision in about half of them, superb dark adaptation in nearly all. Two complexions, running as a gradient and as a politics: the surface-working lines burnt to a deep ochre-brown, cracked at the knuckles, and the deep lines — the ones for whom the surface is a place you visit, three or four days a year, squinting — pale to a colour like the inside of a stem, with a permanent green cast at the eyelids from lantern-light. The two intermarry, and there is a joke about it, and the joke is not kind in either direction.

And the ears.

The muscle that in you does nothing at all is, in them, a thing they can clench like a fist. They shut at the third hour of light and open at dusk, and they do it without thinking about it, and the shutting has a sensation with a name, and a child who cannot yet shut is not permitted above at all. Held too long, the clench gives a headache like a bruise behind the eye; a person who forgets to shut is called by the same word used for a cistern that seeps, and it is a mild insult about carelessness generally. They wear glass beads on a neck-string, graded, greenish, cane-ash-fluxed, drilled and lipped: measured plugs for measured noise. A tapper's bead-string is a resume. The big ones at the top are for the deep roar at midsummer over a fat vessel; the little ones near the clasp are for night-work, for the choir, for a room where somebody is dying and the family wants to hear it. You can read a stranger's trade, seniority, and history of ruptures off their string before they have said a word, and they know you are doing it, and turn slightly to make it easier.

## How they talk

Three registers, and everyone is fluent in all three, and none of them is primary.

**Mouth**, for dawn, dusk, night, and the Weft. It is a low language, pitched under the choir, heavy on vowels and nasals, with almost no sibilants at all, because a sibilant dies instantly in fibre. To an outsider it sounds like men mumbling into barrels. It is capable of poetry.

**Touch**, for the Draw. Not a formal sign language — signs get lost in shimmer past twenty metres — but a system of pressures on the body: forearm, back of the hand, the sternum. The sternum especially, because at noon the sternum is already ringing and a thumb pressed there is felt inside the same cavity the roar is in. Two people at noon on the open nap will stand shoulder to shoulder and hold each other's forearms and hold a conversation of considerable subtlety for an hour, staring in different directions. Strangers find the intimacy of it hard. Thatch people find the plains habit of speaking at distance to be a kind of shouting into the void, and slightly obscene, like relieving yourself in company.

**Knock**, for distance. A heel or a mallet on the mat puts a pulse into the vessels, and the vessels carry it, and it arrives kilometres away not as a sound exactly but as a change in the texture of the roar — a stitch in it. It is slow, it is only good for about forty distinct messages, and it is how everything important travels: a hollowing found, a tap gone bad, a birth, a herd's direction, a death, come, don't come, we have salt. A person's name is a knock before it is ever a word. The mouth-name is a transliteration of the knock, and often an awkward one, and children are teased for their parents' bad taste in rhythm.

## Household

A household is a *string*: two to nine adults, however related, plus whatever children, plus their tap-rights and their lantern-lines. They say *we are three strings and a horn* to mean a small alliance with one good tapper in it.

They own: horn spigots, gut hose, glass, wooden mallets, rope in enormous quantity, cane-fibre cloth that is closer to matting than to weaving, cisterns, floats, beads, a mapped set of galleries memorised by hand and tongue.

By hand, because a Weft walker always keeps one palm on the wall, and that palm — the wall hand — is calloused differently from the other, and reads texture the way you read a page. By tongue, because condensate on a gallery wall carries the flavour of what has rotted above it, and a licked wall says where you are with a precision that no map on this plateau has ever matched. Deep-line children are taught geography as a series of tastes, and homesickness among them is specifically a taste, and this is the single hardest thing about them for an outsider to believe.

Their agriculture, such as it is, is the lantern-lines: nodes deliberately inoculated with the gall fungus by needle and spit, in rows, along a family's galleries, tended for decades, glowing that flat sourceless yellow-green. Wealth is measured in metres of lit gallery. Inheritance disputes are almost always about light.

Their great craft is the tap, and here is the bitter part. Tapping means hearing water eighty centimetres inside a living stem while standing inside a noise that would rupture you. Skill takes forty years to build. The ear does not last forty years. So the best tappers on the Thatch are always the ones going deaf, working faster and better each season on a diminishing instrument, and the very best is always someone in the last two or three years of hearing anything at all, and everyone knows it, and the apprentices watch that person's mouth for the moment they mishear a simple thing.

## The silent

When hearing goes altogether, a person becomes something else.

They can work at noon indefinitely. They can sleep through the choir. They stop wearing beads and give the string away, which is a ceremony, and they take a new name-knock that is a rhythm anyone can feel through the mat with a bare foot, because they will need to be summoned by tremor now. And they are called by the word for silence, which is the word for death, and which is not spoken to their face because they could not hear it anyway, and this is understood to be a courtesy and also a fiction that everyone maintains.
