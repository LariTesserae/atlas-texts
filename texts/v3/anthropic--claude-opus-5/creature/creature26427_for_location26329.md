### The Tenant

Something lives here, and the description is right that the place is not waiting for anyone: it isn't, because it is already occupied.

You will not see it. There is nothing to see, in the sense of a body standing in the light coming off the floor. But the Sill is a record, and it records at a fingernail per year, and it is the wrong kind of place to keep secrets. The rain dissolves the travertine everywhere, evenly, fairly, according to the local rainfall and the local carbonate and nothing else. Look at the stair treads. Look at the three flutes worn into the left-hand jamb. Those are unfair. Something is being dissolved faster in particular places, in a pattern, and the pattern has a shoulder in it, and a heel, and a hand that trails along kerbs.

The rain rounds everything equally. The tenant rounds things unequally. That difference is its body, and there is no other part of it anywhere.

### Dimensions

You can take its measurements, and they will not help you.

From the dishing of a tread: a foot, arriving at the middle-front, weight rolling slightly outward, no hurry in it. From the flutes on the jamb: a shoulder at about the height of your own, passing on the left, always the left, some thousands upon thousands of times. From the well-hair, which hangs in the crack of every kerb and moves in a place with no wind: a hand let down along the running edge of things, the way you might touch a fence you have touched all your life.

Then you find the same doorway in the hall quarter at eleven metres, with the same three flutes worn by the same shoulder, and the same doorway in the culvert at nine centimetres, with the same three flutes worn by the same shoulder.

So it has proportions and no size. It is not large or small; it is the same, larger and smaller. And there is exactly one door in the Sill, encountered at three magnifications by a tenant whose entire address is *the door*, and whose city has one of everything it touches and an indefinite number of everything it does not.

### Number

That is the unstated rule, and it is a rule about occupancy.

Towers are indefinite because nothing goes up them. Wells are indefinite because nothing draws from them — no rope-groove, remember, not one. The steps of the long stair are indefinite because the tenant descends without counting; you do not count stairs you have used forever.

Doors are definite because they must be passed through, one at a time, and passing through is a thing that happens a specific number of times. Thresholds are definite for the same reason. The tree is definite because it is arrived at.

So: how many tenants? Ask the question standing on a rooftop, among the eleven or fourteen towers, and the question has no answer; it is that kind of quantity. Ask it in a doorway, with the flutes under your palm, and the answer is one. It has never been counted from anywhere it wasn't, and it has never been anywhere but here. Sound dies within fifty metres in this air. It has spent its whole existence inside a small sphere of audibility, alone in it, and so has never had the occasion to be two.

It is not lonely. Loneliness requires having been two once.

### The Round

Its schedule is the water table. Twice a day every canal rises and falls by a handspan, and the tenant goes with the handspan; that is the whole of its calendar and it has never needed another.

You can read the route off the floor, because time in the Sill pools where water pools, and the tenant does not fight the local rate. Across the dry plates of the mud flats it goes very fast — hours pass between two steps out there, and it crosses the flats the way you cross a cold room, all at once, arriving. In the arcades it slows. At the shining old cisterns it stops entirely, and sits at the rim in the third hour of the afternoon, and watches a drop of condensation gather on the underside of a lip for most of a morning, and watches it let go.

That is not idleness. There is nothing else on the schedule. It has no work. It carries nothing — hence no broken pot, no dropped nail, no artefact of any kind in a fully drained and paved and guttered town. It repairs nothing, because the city repairs itself at very nearly the rate it fails. It closes nothing; there are no doors to close. Its labour, the entire content of its tenancy, is arrival: to have come down the long stair again, to have passed through the door on the left again, to have got to the cistern by the third hour.

And it does not go back. This is why the geometry does not close. Walk a block and return, and the city has rotated about you, because the Sill is fitted to a walk that has no last step and no starting point to be returned to. It is a city built to the shape of a round that never comes back round. Nobody minds. The tenant has never once needed the two ends of a day to meet.

### What Grew Where It Stood

The ecology is not its body. The ecology is what accumulated in the places it repeats.

The **blacksalt** is the floor it walks on and the only frank life here, and it respires hourly, in slow visible pulses, threads of bubbles going up through the shining water. It is the closest thing in the Sill to company: something that breathes near you at an interval not your own. The tenant walks on it and it squeaks and smells of hot iron, and neither of them has ever acknowledged the other, and they have done this for a very long time.

The **well-hair** is where the hand trails. Nothing more. Fine, pale, wet, moving slightly, in the crack of every single kerb — that is how many times.

The **glasswort** is harder. Fleshy, bruise-coloured, upright, standing one to every ten or twelve metres in the flooded courts, posted, obviously posted. These are the places where the tenant stopped and stood still long enough for something to grow out of the standing. Not rest: it does not tire. Waiting. Each stem is one waiting, and the spacing is simply the distance at which two waitings do not touch each other, and the courts are full of them, widely spaced, patient, in the middle of enormous empty plazas.

There is no record anywhere in the Sill of anything ever having arrived at one of those appointments. The tenant does not appear to hold this against the place. It is not waiting for anyone, in the sense the Sill is not waiting for anyone: the waiting has simply become one of the things it does, the way the seep and the bloom and the dissolution are things the landscape does, and it runs at its own rate and stays in balance, because balance is what happens here to everything, including grief, if that is what that is.

### The Court With the Tree

This is where it becomes a person, briefly, and only here.

The court is bowl-shaped. Note that: dished, not by walking through, but by standing in one place — the tread-wear of a single spot, over centuries, until the floor sags towards its own middle. And at the middle, the one tree: black-barked, leafless, alive, and *definite*, the third definite thing in the Sill after the doors and the thresholds.

Two definite things in a city of uncountable towers, facing each other in a white bowl. It comes here at the end of the round. It stands. The tree fruits.

The fruit is warm and cannot be eaten and cannot be carried out of the court, and everyone who has tried has felt it go to stone in the palm and then to water and then to nothing halfway up the stairs. This is not a trick played on visitors. The fruit is the only object in the Sill that was *made* rather than worn, and it is the only thing here that is finished, and finished things in the Sill exist exactly where they are received and nowhere else. It is not for you. It is not for elsewhere. It is for the standing, and the standing happens here.

### Its Manner Towards You

It will not meet you. It will accommodate you, which is the local form of courtesy, and it is unnerving in a way meeting would not be.

Every surface you touch is already wet with your own breath given back. Your footsteps are louder than they should be and arrive slightly late, because something in the fabric is repeating them a beat behind, learning the weight of you. And your footprints are ahead of you in the blacksalt, wet, faintly glowing, going where you were about to go, exactly your size — because the tenant has no size of its own and takes the size of whoever is present, and its one long habit, the whole grammar of it, is *return*. It gives you back your breath, your step, your route.

If you go somewhere else on purpose, the prints it laid for you stay a few hours and dissolve. They are the only litter in the Sill: unused hospitality, softening.

Stay long enough and it will take you up. Not eat you — there is no mouth in the whole city except the look of a doorway lit at the threshold and black at the lintel. It will simply add you. Your route will begin to show as a bias in the smoothing. Somewhere a jamb will start to flute at your shoulder height, on your side. And a season after you have gone or stopped, there will still be prints going on ahead down the flooded avenue at the turn of the water table, twice a day, at your size, in your gait, keeping to a round that does not close, in the dark, in the heat, in the quiet, for as long as the water keeps coming up.
