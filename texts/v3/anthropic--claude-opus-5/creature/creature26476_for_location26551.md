## The Tenants

They call themselves nothing in particular. Outside, they are Cantish, and you can pick one out on any foreign dock inside a minute, because they list.

It is the walk. Ground that has been rising under you since birth teaches the body a correction, and the correction does not switch off. Put a Cantish woman on a flat quay in a flat country and she will drift half a step leftward every ten paces and stop, and adjust, and drift again, and her hips will keep making a small allowance for a slope that isn't there. They know this about themselves. They find it funny in the way they find most things funny, which is silently, with the eyebrows. Sailors say you can hire a Cant crew and never hear them and never lose one overboard, because they trust no floor anywhere and are always, at some level below thought, holding on.

Look closer and there is more. The right thumb is broad and flattened at the pad, from pressing; nearly everyone presses something at some point in a life. The forearms carry fine white hatching where the cutting wire has skipped. Damp-pale, most of them, whatever they came from originally — and they came from everywhere, because the ore drew people the way spilled sugar draws ants — but the pallor is not sickliness, it is simply the complexion of a people who live inside a cloud and work under felt hoods. Their hearing is extraordinary and their hearing fails early, and both facts matter.

They drink the country. Fog-water comes off the fences so pure it is almost hungry, and would strip a body over years, so every cistern in Cant is dosed with a scoop of milled loaf, stirred in, settled out. It gives the water a taste of struck flint and a faint slipperiness. It leaves a grey ring on every kettle. It means that everyone in Cant has some measurable fraction of the new ground inside their bones, and they know this, and they are not sentimental about it, and they mention it at funerals.

---

Speech is conducted at a distance of about a hand.

They lean in, cheek almost at cheek, and speak on the outbreath, and the other hand — the free one, the one not carrying — works at chest height in a low economical shorthand that is not a separate language but is not optional either. The signs come out of the trades. *Yes* is the thumb pressed forward as into soft stone. *No* is the flat drop of a plumb-line. *Later* is a quarter-turn of an invisible screw. *I don't know* is the two-handed gesture of a river running out into gravel and stopping. There is a sign for *the ground gave that*, made with the palm turned away from the body, and it is considered slightly indecent to make it in front of children, though everyone does.

Visitors find the closeness unbearable, then erotic, then unbearable again. Newcomers are issued, on registration, a small uncased handbell on a cord, and must wear it a full year. If it sounds, they are fined. If it sounds three times they are sent up to the Stales to work the archive for a season, which is the local equivalent of exile, and the archivists are glad of the help.

Laughter is a shudder in the shoulders and a hiss through the teeth. Weeping is silent and is done into a cupped hand — you see people in doorways with a hand over the whole lower face, and you learn to walk past. Grief, real grief, the kind with sound in it, goes down to the water.

That is the one licence. At the tide-scarp, facing out, you may shout. Everyone does, eventually. Children are taken down at seven or eight and made to do it, standing on raw stone with a parent's hand on the back of the neck, and most of them cannot manage it the first time, and then they manage it, and hear their own voice for the first time in their lives at full extension, and are frightened of it, and cry, silently, on the way home. They call it going down to say it. Bereavement, ruin, rage, a birth: you go down and say it to the water, which is leaving anyway and takes it with it.

---

Their trades have made them into shapes.

The **canters** go house to house with a dial-gauge in a felt bag and a long spanner across the shoulders, and they let themselves in, and they go down into your cellar and take up the jacking-screws a quarter-turn, and come up, and check the plumb-line in your stairwell, and go. They do not knock. It is not thought rude. They are the only people in Cant who have been in every cellar, and they consequently know everything about everybody, and are famously, structurally discreet: a canter who gossips loses the round. They walk with the spanner-stoop, one shoulder lower. Old canters can feel a half-degree through the soles of their boots and will stop mid-sentence in the street, frowning at a wall, and go and find whoever owns it.

The **tuners** are worse and stranger. They carry a felt-wrapped bar and their own mouths, and they go out through the new districts humming — a long, low, sliding hum, up and down — with a hand flat against the fresh stone, feeling for where the note lives. Every district in Cant has a note, filed at the office, and no machine may be licensed in that district whose bearings or belts or gear-trains sit anywhere near it. Tuners are half musicians and half inspectors of bearings, and the inspection of bearings is the most feared function in the town, because of the bandsaw, and the wedding. A tuner may condemn a mill on the strength of a sound nobody else can hear, and be obeyed. They are usually thin, usually solitary, often unwell in some vague way, and they die deaf like everybody else, and when a tuner goes deaf they hand over the bar and go and sit at the pouring floors, where the noise is.

Because that is what happens to the deaf. Deafness in Cant is not a loss of place, it is a change of place. It comes early — the hearing that lets you notice a drip three streets away is not built to last — and when it comes, the smelters take you. The pouring floors are sunk in pits and lined with dead cracked Stales-stone that can't be made to ring, and they are the loudest room in the country, and they are staffed almost entirely by the old, who work there in a roar they cannot hear, in felt hoods, signing at each other across the light of the metal. Cant's elders are not put out to grass. They are put down among the copper and they are very hard to argue with.

The **fencemen** live out on the seaward flats among the thread-combs, mending, in oilcloth so wet it shines like a beetle. They are considered feral. They come into town for tobacco and stand too close even by local standards, and their hands are wrecked, and they are the only Cantish who sing out loud, out there, in the middle of the hiss where nothing can hear them. The **rollers** move buildings. The **gaugers** measure the swell of the country with instruments calibrated to a hair, and publish, and are believed with reservations. The **finders** walk the newest mud with a spade and a sack and pick up copper as if collecting eggs, and are lucky, and are drunks.

---

They do not count in years. Ask a woman her age and she will tell you where the water was when she was born: *I'm nine thousand paces*, meaning the tide has gone that far out since, meaning old, meaning old enough to have watched her birth-street go dry, uphill, quiet, and start to green. To be old in Cant is to be older than the hill you are standing on, and they will say so, and it is a genuine boast, and it never gets less strange.

Marriages are counted the same way — not in years but in moves. *We have moved five times together.* Every household in Cant moves seaward through a life, chasing the lease line, *while the water is within four thousand paces*, and every move is a purge, because you own only what you can carry down two streets. So their possessions are few and superb. A thimble. A gauge. A good knife. The plumb-bob out of the stairwell, brass, worn smooth, inherited, hung with the rings of the dead. And, in almost every house, one small badly made object of pale stone: the thing you pressed when you were nine, out of your own allotted lump, in your own nine days, before you knew what you were doing. A lopsided cup. A bird. A hand holding a hand. They are kept forever and shown to no one but family.

And the dead are pressed.

There is a trade for it, and it is quiet and well-paid and hereditary, and the clock is the same nine days. What comes out is not a headstone; it is a block, a good one, load-bearing, with the person somewhere in the middle of it, and it goes into a wall. Not a monument wall. A working wall — a mill gable, a cistern housing, the corner of a stair. The town is built of its people in the most literal available sense, and this is why nobody in Cant is much troubled by the fact that everything they make is temporary: they are not building for keeps, they are building a slow procession inland.

Which means the Stales are the graveyard. All eighty years of it, roofless, root-broken, filled to the sills with dwarf birch, and every standing gable in it somebody's grandmother. So there is the inland walk. People go up in the pearl light with a flask and sit in the ferns in a house with no roof, and put a hand on a particular block, and say very little, because up there the silence is not a rule and that unnerves them. Foxes den in the counting-houses. Everyone thinks this is proper. The archivist lives out there alone with the ledgers in the dry-under, and comes down twice a year, and speaks too loudly, and is forgiven.

---

They have no theory. It has to be said again, because outsiders keep trying to hand them one.

When a shelf comes up with a stair of eleven treads in it, worn, going down into rock that was seafloor last spring, the Cantish put a bench at the top and a lamp at the bottom. When a void appears in a cliff the exact shape of a seated person, someone will go, once, alone, generally at dusk, and sit in it, and come back, and not say. When the north main rose already holed at the right bore along the surveyors' unbuilt line, they laid the pipe in the holes and finished a fortnight early and the office noted the saving.

If you push them on it — and you will, everyone does — you get the palm turned away. And then, if they like you, something close to an answer, said at a hand's distance, on the outbreath, in the drip and the wide thin hiss of the fences:

That the world here is not finished. That it is being made in the same direction they are going, slightly ahead. That they have decided to take this as courtesy. That the stone is always listening for its note and it is bad manners to make it wait.

Then they will get up off the bench at the top of the stairs that go nowhere, and start down the hill toward the water, correcting, correcting, at four thousand paces and closing.
