## Who answers the door

A woman of about fifty, in a quilted feather-coat gone grey at the cuffs, with lime dust in the creases of her knuckles and a fine white bloom along the outer edge of each thumbnail. She opens the door and immediately shouts a greeting at you in a level, cheerful, enormous voice, and then leans in closer than you would like, and watches your mouth.

She is short and very deep through the chest. Her hands are large for her frame and the skin of them is thickened and slightly translucent at the fingertips, like candle-wax; her nails are magnificent, hard as shell, and ridged. Her teeth are extraordinary — white, dense, unworn, the teeth of someone who has drunk water saturated with the stuff teeth are made of since the day she was weaned. She has three small drilled beads on a cord around her neck, brownish, irregular, and she will tell you about them at the slightest encouragement, because they came out of her own body and she is proud of them.

She is standing in what her grandmother built as a parlour and which is now a half-cellar with the window sills at pavement height. She apologises for it. She is not embarrassed.

## Bodies

They are highland people, and it shows in the ordinary ways: barrel chests, thick blood, fingers that go purple in cold air, an unfair talent for going up stairs. Newcomers from the lowlands spend their first month asleep.

The rest of it is local. Everyone above thirty has the same notch in their hearing, at the same frequencies — a hole bitten out of the middle of the range by sixty years of standing under a colony. Everyone above fifty is frankly deaf in that band and compensates by lip-reading without knowing they are doing it. It is why they stand too close. It is why, abroad, they are thought rude, and hectoring, and slightly stupid, and why they think everyone abroad is mumbling on purpose.

The lungs are the real reckoning. Quicklime is the Shelf's wealth and quicklime is a fine white dust that sets in the wet, and the wet is inside you. Kiln-workers cough for forty years and then stop. There are whole streets in the lime districts where the men are dead at fifty-five and the widows run the guild, and this is not considered a tragedy so much as an arrangement, which is worse.

And there are the stones. Alkaline water, hard water, water that will cement a kettle: it will also, patiently, build small rough pebbles in a kidney. Almost everyone gets one eventually. There is a word for the pain — it is not a polite word — and a well-developed folk repertoire for enduring it, and a guild of cutters who are, by the oldest usage, the same profession as masons, distinguished only by which side of the skin they work. When the stone comes out it is washed, dried, drilled, dated, and worn. A person with five on the cord is treated with a certain deference. The Shelf's own joke about itself: *even our insides aggrade.*

## Speech

The colony roar is not featureless. It has a gap — a band, up above the general surf, where a hundred million filter-feeders happen not to put much energy. Every human thing on the Shelf lives in that gap.

Their language has almost no fricatives; an *s* dies within four metres. It is built of hard stops and nasals and a small set of pitched vowels that cut, and it is spoken flat and loud with the mouth wide, so that it can be read as well as heard. Their music is thin, high, and piercing, and sounds to foreigners like a workshop accident. Their emergency whistles are pitched into the same gap, which is why a rim-breach alarm can be heard across nine kilometres of screaming birds and why lowland visitors, who cannot hear it at all, must be led out by the hand.

Alongside the speech there is the hand-talk, which the eggers and the rimwardens developed and everyone else half-learned. Nobody thinks of it as a separate language. Grandmothers use it across a crowded room. Lovers use it. It has a sign for *the ground has moved again* that is also, by long slippage, the sign for *what did you expect*.

Shelf people abroad cannot sleep. They buy tin trays and set them under a slow drip, or they pay a boy to sit outside the door and rustle gravel in a sack, and they explain this to their hosts too loudly, and are not believed.

## What they eat

Eggs. Eggs beyond counting, taken under quota, from colonies whose harvest is written into law in units of clutches-per-thousand-rims and has not been meaningfully revised in six hundred years. Eggs boiled, pickled in brine-pan salt, dried into sheets, whipped into a pale foam and baked in geothermal ovens into a thing with the texture of pumice, which they love and no one else can be persuaded to finish. Shrimp, in paste, in cake, in a fermented sauce that is the national condiment and smells like a low tide in a warm room. Fly-flour from the runnels, sieved and pressed, high in fat, faintly nutty, used the way other peoples use grain. Bird, of course — old bird, tough bird, bird stewed for a day and a half in a copper pot fed off a steam main. And mat: cut in bricks, soaked, scraped of its black underlayer, fermented for a month, sliced thin. It is black-green, sour, elastic, and tastes of iron and pond. Children are made to eat it. Adults, away from home, find that they want it.

Grain is a luxury import and bread is a delicacy served at weddings in slices the width of a finger. Fruit is a story. There is a spirit distilled off the mat ferment which is clear and vicious and tastes of eggs, and they are not ashamed of it either.

## Work

Everything anyone does here is one of four things: making the water go where it should, taking what the water leaves, feeding the birds' surplus into the world below, and keeping the city from being swallowed by its own floor.

The **rimwardens** walk the terrace lips with a bag of lime putty and a trowel, all day, forever, mending. A rim is a wall a hand high holding back a warm shallow lake with a hundred thousand nesting birds standing in it; a breach drains a field in an afternoon, kills a season's hatch, and takes eighty years to rebuild. Rimwardens are laconic, weatherbeaten, crusted white to mid-shin, and hold a social rank out of all proportion to their pay. They are, functionally, the fire brigade of a place with nothing to burn.

The **eggers** work the colony fields in oiled hoods and ear-plates, moving in files, signing. They come home half-deaf at thirty and rich at forty and are the only people on the Shelf who habitually wear colour.

The **Rinse** wash the streets. Every street, every day, with wire brooms and hot acid-water off the tanks — because a street that is not washed for a season is a street with a lump in it, and a street not washed for a decade is a ramp. Two thousand of them in Aggrade alone. They have their own hymn-shouting, their own bad reputation, and their own quiet certainty that they are the only thing standing between the city and geology.

The **Measure** are surveyors, and they are the closest thing the Shelf has to a priesthood. They keep the benchmark net — iron studs sunk deep into old rock across a thousand kilometres — and they re-level it, ward by ward, on a rolling twelve-year cycle, and publish the rise. Four point one millimetres in the Ombrine ward. Eight point nine down in the pans. Their tables are the basis of every contract, every lease, every inheritance and civic plan, and to falsify a level is the one crime here that has never been commuted, ever, on the grounds that it is a lie told to people not yet born.

And the **quarry-historians**, who mine lime out of the fill and, in doing so, cut open the buried streets: shopfronts, gutters, a bollard, a dog's print, a child's dropped bead, all of it turned to stone and perfect. They work with hammers in one hand and a ledger in the other. They are paid by the ton and cited in the journals.

## Houses, courses, debts

A family is identified by how deep it is. A house's *course* is the layer of the city its street was on when the house was raised; a family three courses down is old, and says so, and a family whose ground floor is still at pavement level is *surface* — new money, or newcomers, or fools. Kitchens are on the second floor. The best rooms are on the third. Outside staircases are added by the generation, each a slightly different stone, so that you can read a building's age off its flank like a road-cut. Beside every important door is the column of dated marks, cut by a licensed marker, one per decade, each one a little lower than the last.

Debts here are quoted in millimetres. A loan of two hundred millimetres is a loan due when the Measure certifies that the ward has risen that much — call it thirty-five years, but the point is that it is not thirty-five years, it is *when the ground says so*, and if the rise slows, the debt sleeps. Marriage contracts, water rights, mining leases and apprenticeships are all written the same way. The calendar has a unit for the time a doorstep takes to become a step, and it is the unit in which people say things like *I will love you*, and mean it as a measurement.

Nobody here is in a hurry, and this drives foreign merchants to despair, and it should be understood that it is not serenity. It is arithmetic. A Shelf person who wants something in three years is thought childish. A Shelf person who wants something in three hundred is a respectable citizen with a plan and probably a grandchild already assigned to it.

## Children

Loud from birth, and no one minds. They learn to read a road-cut before they read letters — the game is to guess a layer's date from its lamination and be corrected by an uncle. They are given, at about six, a small object of their own choosing to hang in a warm runnel, and they visit it, and it fattens and whitens with a skin of stone, and at fifteen they take it out and it has become an unrecognisable lump with their thing somewhere inside it, and this is considered a complete education in the local metaphysics.

They swim in the warm pools, which they are not supposed to do. They are all slightly poisoned by the water for the first year of life and it makes their bones dense and their teeth immortal. They can identify eleven kinds of bird by the sound alone through a wall.

## The dead

There is no soil. There has never been soil. So the dead are *set*: laid out on a lattice in a walled seep-house, in the warm bitter water, and left. In fourteen months a body is a white form with the drape of the cloth still showing. In forty years it is rock. The families visit; the crust thickens; the features go. At some point the seep-house is full and is sealed and becomes, simply, a piece of the ground, and in three hundred years someone with a hammer and a ledger will cut through it on the way to a lime seam and note it in the margin and go on.

They know this. It is the arrangement. When a quarry-historian breaks into a set-house the work stops for the day and the crew stands in it and shouts, once, all together — the old high note, the one that goes through the roar — and then they take the stone, because the stone is what the city is made of, and their own great-grandchildren will take them.

Ask them whether that troubles them and they will look at you the way you would look at someone distressed that rain falls downward.

## The blush

When the rust wave comes over the eastern scarp and begins its four-month crawl, they take three days off.

There is drinking, and terrible high music, and the eggers wear their colours in the streets, and there is a great deal of eating of things dyed the same red as the mats, badly. And on the last night, at the hour of the tear, when the cloud lid rips and ten thousand pools go gold at once, every household stands out on its roof or its stair, and each person says aloud, into a roar so total that nobody can hear anybody, one thing they intend to have finished before the ground comes up another hand's-breadth.

Then they wash the doorstep, re-cut the mark, and go back to work.

They do not know why the wave keeps its appointment. They have papers about it. They are not troubled: they live in a place where the floor is patiently turning their house into a museum of itself, where the water builds stones in their kidneys and beads in their walls, where their voices are shaped by the gap in a noise older than their language — and something out there in all that dumb chemistry keeps time better than they do, and comes when it said it would, and is beautiful for a month.

They are, when you stand back far enough, one more layer: a thin warm film over half a million square kilometres of wet rock, taking in mineral and light and putting out pattern, a few millimetres a year, never stopping, never quite a season, and getting slowly, honestly, further from the bottom.
