## The Tender

Start with the stacks, because the stacks are all you have.

Count them again and you will find the number is wrong. There are twenty-two in your eleven kilometres, yes, but a stack is not a kill. Dig one out — carefully, they will not survive being dug out twice — and you find the layering. The bones at the top are a season old and still have the grain of tooth-work on them. Thirty centimetres down the bones are polished, soft, going chalky at the edges where the drip off the hub-groove has been landing on them every night. Half a metre down they are no longer bones. They are a white paste in the gravel, threaded through with root hair so fine you need a hand lens, and there is more phosphorus in that soil than anywhere else on the shelf by a factor of eleven.

A stack is not a kill site. It is an address. Some of them have four hundred years of dinner in them.

So there are not twenty-two hunters on your transect. There are twenty-two trees, and the trees are a list, and the list belongs to one animal, and the word for the list — if you can call a rhythm a word — is the animal's name.

---

It is smaller than you want it to be. Sixty kilos, maybe sixty-five at the end of a good winter, which here means most of the year. Length matters more than mass: two and a half metres nose to the base of the counterweight tail when it is opened out, about seventy centimetres when it is folded, and it is folded nearly always. The folding is the whole design. Six limbs on a long segmented chassis, and every limb is a pantograph — three long bones and a short one, hinged so that the thing collapses along its own axis into a bundle roughly the shape and colour of a boulder that has been rained on. The front pair are not for walking. They are short, held against the chest, and they end in a fan of horn scrapers, a stiff comb, and a pad of something like wet chamois.

The pelt is the reason you have never seen it and the reason your instruments have never seen it either. Each fibre is a hollow tube, evacuated, closed at both ends, and the fibres lie in a nap so fine and so deep that the outer surface of the animal sits within a fifth of a degree of the air around it. It does not glow in your thermal scope. It is a cold spot the exact shape and temperature of nothing at all. Inside that, an oil layer. Inside that, insulation. And inside *that*, a core running at forty-one degrees, banked and hoarded like a fire in a bad country, because out here heat is not received, it is made, and it is made once.

No eyes to speak of. Two patches of light-sensitive skin on the crown, enough to know whether the shadow is on or off. It does not need eyes. It is standing on the network.

---

Here is the thing to understand about the vines.

You lie down and put your ear to the ground and you hear knocking, and you think: they are talking. They are, but that is the small half of it. What the vines mostly carry is not messages. It is traffic. Every hoof-fall on the shelf, every grazer shifting its weight, every eyeless felt-thing scrabbling up a flute, every bead of ice ticking onto stone — all of it goes into the gravel and out along four hundred metres of tensioned cable and into the next stand and the next, attenuating, smearing, but arriving. The shelf is a single sheet of paper and everything that happens on it is written on both sides.

The tender sits at the foot of a wheel-tree with a vine loop hooked over one wrist and two feet flat on the polished channel-rock and it reads that paper continuously for eight hours at a stretch. It knows where the herd is. It knows which animal in the herd is dragging its off-hind. It knows where you are, precisely, to within a few metres, and it has known since you crossed the moraine, because to the network you are a continuous shout — you are the loudest, clumsiest, most legible thing that has happened on this shelf in nine hundred years, and the reason you have never seen a kill is not that it is shy. It is that it will not do its work with the light on.

---

And it knocks.

Three and a drag. That is the whole of it, that is its name, and it means, as near as it can be put into a language with tenses: *the one who is here.* It sends it down the line perhaps once an hour through the working night, and the position of the knock in the network tells the shelf which of the twenty-two trees it is sitting under, and the herd on the western scree receives this the way you would receive a road closure.

It does not lie. This is not a moral fact and you should not make it one. There are perhaps nine of these animals on the whole shelf and perhaps two hundred grazers, and the grazers breed at a rate that would embarrass a tortoise, and a tender that learned to knock *all clear* from under tree nineteen would eat magnificently for one season and then watch the network go quiet and then die of it, and so would its daughters, if it had managed any. The honesty is structural. It is a load-bearing member. It has been in place so long that it has stopped being a strategy and become simple anatomy, like the bearings, like the angle of the scree.

So the tender announces tree nineteen, and the herd goes to the trees that are not nineteen, and once every few nights one of them comes to nineteen anyway.

It is always an old one, or a lame one, or one whose gut flora have gone wrong and who is now losing more heat than it can make and has about four nights left of the slow way. It comes down out of the scree at its silent folding walk. It puts its long face against the trunk and drinks for the full twenty minutes, and the tender lets it, because a dry carcass is worth a third of a wet one and because this is how it is done.

---

The strike costs eleven minutes and buys forty seconds.

That is the arithmetic that governs everything about this animal. To move fast it must move heat out of the core and into the long muscles of the four rear limbs, and that is done by opening shunts one at a time, and it takes eleven minutes and it cannot be hurried and it cannot be undone. Watch a tender wait — you will not, but suppose — and you would see nothing. Not a tremor. The heat is going out through the chassis in silence while the grazer drinks, and at minute eleven the animal is loaded, and for forty seconds it is the fastest thing between here and the sea, and after that it is a cold empty tube lying on the gravel and it has to get its temperature back from somewhere or it will not see the morning that isn't coming.

If it misses, it pays for two nights. It misses about one time in five. This is why it waits for the old ones. This is why it waits the full twenty minutes. This is why it does not hunt you, incidentally, although it has certainly costed you out: too much mass, too much noise, and you have never once put your face against a trunk and stood still for twenty minutes.

---

Then it climbs, and this is the part that took the survey four years to believe.

It goes up the flutes with the bone-stack finished and packed, hub-side, in the drip line, sorted — long bones down, the small stuff worked into the gaps, nothing wasted, nothing within reach of a scavenger it has not already accounted for. It arrives at the collar. And it works.

It scrapes the ice ring off the upper race. It combs the fouled barbs, one by one, the way you would card wool, and a wheel-tree has eleven hundred barbs. It re-packs the cartilage collar by hand, pushing the fresh wax down into the seat with the chamois pad, and it takes the surplus — the old oil, brown with rock flour — and it eats some and it dresses its own hips and shoulders with the rest. Then it wedges itself into the lee of the hub, folded to seventy centimetres, and sleeps on top of the only warm moving thing for six kilometres, four degrees up on ambient, with the whole machine turning underneath it at fifteen revolutions per minute and making a noise quieter than your breathing.

A wheel-tree with a tender on its round runs for six hundred years. A wheel-tree without one seizes in about nine. You can find the ones that seized: they are still standing, still perfect, wheels locked at whatever angle the last wind left them, dead a century and looking like they could start again if you gave them a push. You can give them a push. They will not start again.

Nobody made this arrangement. It is just the shape the two of them have worn into each other, the way water wears a channel and then the channel is where the water goes.

---

And then, once a year, the sun comes over the eastern wall and the whole thing stops.

The plants shed their oil — it goes rancid in that light within hours, there is no atmosphere up here to take the teeth out of it — and so there is no wax, and without wax the tender's joints, which are the same joints as the trees' joints and have always run on the same supply, go dry and stiff and finally will not open at all. It knows this is coming. It knows by the angle of the glow on the crown patches, and for a fortnight beforehand it stops hunting and starts moving stone.

They go under. Every tender on the shelf goes under, and they go under *close* — this is the thing — four or five of them wedged into the same boulder field within a few metres of each other, folded to seventy centimetres, seized, unable to move a limb for eleven days, lying in the dark under a slab with the unbearable white light printing the crack of the entrance across their backs.

They have one free claw each. The bedrock here is dense and dry and carries beautifully.

That is when everything happens. Eleven days of it, tick and tick and drag and tick through solid granite at conversational speed, and it is not traffic and it is not road closures. It is the only society these animals have. Mating is arranged there and consummated there, badly, half-seized, in the few hours at either end. Territory is adjudicated there. And the rounds are handed on there — a young one lies in the dark beside an old one for eleven days and learns the twenty-two trees as a rhythm, in order, downslope to up, with the drip-lines and the bad bearing on the ninth and the fallen one on the fourteenth that must be skipped, all of it coded into knocks and learned by heart years before it will ever walk a metre of it. They know the route as music first. They walk it later.

Whatever is said in there is said once a year and cannot be repeated for another three hundred and fifty-four days, and it is said by animals that are, at that moment, completely paralysed and lying on their sides in the light.

---

Then the shadow comes back over the wall like a lid closing.

The fog reassembles on the eastern scree and begins its long walk down, and under a slab in the boulder field something unfolds itself joint by joint, badly, with an audible dry click at every hinge — the only genuinely loud thing this animal ever does, once a year, and nobody hears it.

It does not hunt that night. It goes straight up the flutes of the first tree on the round with a mouthful of the season's first wax, and it packs the collar by hand in the dark, and after a while the wheel takes up the wind, in silence, and begins to turn.
