## Nine, probably

They have never agreed on the number. Counting requires assembly, and assembly is hard here: the ribbon weed closes behind a body without noise, the water forgets a swimmer inside two lengths, and a crew of nine is entirely invisible to itself except at the rim, at the heaters, at the meal. Somebody once counted eleven and was not believed, because eleven is the number of the pumps and the number of the bells and therefore a suspicious number. Somebody once counted seven and was told they had missed the two in the lower gallery. The working figure is nine. It is carried the way the census is carried: as a belief with a decimal point in it.

They have no name for themselves. A name for the group would have to be said in air, and in air their voices come out wrong — flat, mispitched, too loud or not loud enough, the voices of people who have not heard themselves speak in a long time. In the grip there is a sign that means *all of us*: the whole forearm taken in two hands, from elbow to knuckles, and held. It is the same sign as *the basin*. No one has ever needed to separate them.

## What they look like

Smooth. Not shaved — hair fouls, and the ones who kept theirs spent their lives drawing hornwort out of it with their fingers, and stopped, and now smoothness is simply the shape a person is. Skin the colour of the light they live in, which is green, so that at the rim, in the clean cold sunlight, they look faintly wrong, like something brought up from a cellar. Under the nails and in the cuticles and at the inner corners of the eyes, a permanent tinge that will not scrub out. Their palms and soles are white and ridged and never entirely dry.

Their fingertips have gone shallow. Not callused — nothing abrades here — but softened, the whorls swollen flat by a life at thirty-two degrees, so that the print of a hand is no longer a thing any of them can reliably leave. On Terrace Four the tacky epoxy holds a fingerprint sharper than any living finger in the basin can now produce. It is visited. One of them claims it, and the claim is not quite possible: the print is a size smaller than that hand. Nobody contests it. It is the oldest legible thing here that a person made by accident.

They are exquisite at temperature. A keeper crossing the second halocline in total dark knows it by the belly, by half a degree, and can tell you which layer they are in without opening their eyes. They are correspondingly poor at hearing. In air they flinch at wind. The bells, if the bells could ring, would probably hurt them.

On the upper terraces they work on breath, heads breaking into the air every minute or so, the surface bulging and resealing around the neck without spray. For the lower galleries there is a hose from a small pump, and a second person to tend it. The tender feels a tug at the wrist every hundred beats, which is not a signal in any emergency sense but a word — the oldest word, probably, older than the grammar — meaning *I am still here*. It is given by the one below and answered by the one above. A shift on the hose is four hours of saying nothing else.

They sleep in the water. On the lower halocline, where the density steps and a body simply will not sink, they lie in the drifted leaf litter three metres above the floor, strapped by one wrist to a bolt so as not to drift into the weed, and the meadow takes their weight like a mattress of wet paper. Above them, the green. Below them, through the litter, the dull orange of the filament heaters in their furred sleeves. Their dreams are silent. All of them report this; none of them has anything to compare it to.

## The grip

It was invented in a month, by people who had just discovered that the fluid ate their voices, and it has never been written down, and it has outlasted every hand that made it.

The forearm is the sentence. The wrist is *now*. Toward the elbow is the past, and the soft inside of the elbow is the deep past, the place you touch to say *before I was here*. The back of the hand is the conditional: everything wished for, feared, or refused happens on the back of the hand. Pressure is emphasis. Two fingers laid flat is a question. The thumb hooked under the heel of the other's palm is a negation, and it is rude, and there is no polite form of it.

The commonest utterance has no name because it is not thought of as speech: contact held and nothing said. Two keepers working the same tray will keep a wrist against a wrist for an hour and exchange no word at all. It means what it appears to mean.

Names are touches — a place on the arm and a pressure. This produces the one real limitation of the language: you cannot speak of an absent person except by performing their name on the body of a third party, which is a loan, and which the third party feels. Gossip requires a volunteer. As a result there is very little gossip, and what there is has a formal, almost liturgical quality, like an accusation read aloud with the accused in the room.

The strongest word they have is *bell*. It is made by striking a soft fist into the other's open palm, which in the hushwater produces nothing whatsoever. It means a wanting that cannot be had. They use it for the dead, for the road below the cloud, for weather, for the noise a thing would make.

## The gauge house, and the two hands

Above the tide-marks and the chalked pump logs sits the jar.

Someone visits it twice a day. This is not a duty on any list; it simply happens, and it happens more often than twice. They check the meniscus. Once a turnover, someone swims down to Terrace Five and brings back a hundred millilitres in a stoppered tube, which is the single most ceremonious errand in the basin, and the only one for which a person will put on a garment.

There are two hands on the card. The first wrote the date, the initials, the word CENSUS, and the count. The second hand amended it, twice.

The keepers are not one thing about the jar. There are those for whom it is the only cathedral available: seven nematodes and a rotifer, thrashing in a fist of water, the entire animal kingdom, the proof that the basin is a world and not merely a machine that is very good at being wet. And there are those for whom it is an unexploded device — a vector, a courier, the precise thing the whole flora was selected to do without, sitting on a shelf above the pump logs like a lit fuse in a paper cup. Both parties visit. Both parties top it up. The argument is never settled because the argument is conducted in the grip, and the grip has no tense for *forever*, only the back of the hand.

They cannot tell the animals apart. The count is therefore a doctrine rather than an observation. It has been eight, and seven, and eight again. Nobody knows which.

## The other populations

They regard the vegetation the way people elsewhere regard weather: constant, enormous, not company. It is cut back, cropped, parted, eaten. Terrace One is fern rhizome and a starch panicle; the cycad seeds on the rim are soaked for eleven days and baked into a flat grey cake on the glass sleeve of a heater. They eat in air because eating cannot be done below, and so the meal is the only moment when all of them — nine, probably — are in one place and can see each other. They sit apart anyway. Hands are for talking.

The machines are closer to being neighbours. The eleven pumps are named, and the names are name-touches, the same grammar used for persons, which nobody finds odd. Number Six runs warm. Number Nine has a crank you can feel through the plate of the floor if you lie down on it, once every fifteen seconds, and keepers who cannot sleep go and lie on Nine. The combs on Terrace Two are the closest thing the basin has to a pulse: every eleven minutes the ceramic tines turn and touch each panicle in sequence and shake loose a little pollen for an insect that does not exist. Some of them time their breathing to it. All of them know, without checking, how long it is until the next pass. The spares bin for the tines still holds something over four thousand pieces, kiln-marked, identical, and the level in the bin has dropped by less than a hand's width.

## The throat

Once a turnover somebody goes down inside the column.

The cage is a wet warm hour in the dark, and at the bottom the air is thick and tastes of kettles. Here are the sinter-heads: pale knobbed masses the size of a chair, wet-looking, seated in ranks in the falling water, drawing the carbonate out of it and laying it down in rings at six centimetres a year. They were manufactured. Each carries a stamped serial plate, and the plates have gone illegible in the way that a thing under a millimetre of its own product goes illegible.

They have been dividing for a long time. A daughter head buds off the flank of the mother and carries the mother's serial, because the serial is a physical plate and gets overgrown, not reissued. The numbers are therefore no longer unique. There are four hundred and some heads down there and perhaps thirty distinct numbers among them, which means that the only thing in the entire basin that reproduces by cloning and also carries a manufacturer's stamp is the thing actually building the place.

The keeper who goes down puts a hand on one. It is warm, and slightly gritty, and it is the only gritty thing above the cloud deck. They come back up and say so, on the wrist, and the others say *yes*, which is pressure, held.

## What lives here

There is no archive. The pump logs are chalk, and chalk is erased at every turnover, so the institution's memory is nineteen days deep and has been nineteen days deep since the beginning. What persists is accidental: a fingerprint in epoxy; two hands on a card; the founder's mark on eleven brass bells facing outward for the benefit of nobody; and inside one stacked cedar formwork board, in pencil, a name, a date, and a complaint about the rain below — rain that no living keeper has been in, on a road that half of them consider a story, delivered by a truck that the other half consider a story too.

So the count of inhabitants depends entirely on what you are willing to call a life.

The flora is the biomass and does not care. The machines are the metabolism and do not care either. The seven-and-one in the jar are the only animals, and are worshipped and feared and watered by hand. The heads at the bottom of the throat are the only thing growing on purpose, and they are lifting the whole arrangement — water, weed, pumps, bells, jar, and nine soft green people — into the cold clean light at six centimetres a year, and they will still be doing it long after the last hand goes off the last wrist.

But if you ask what the basin actually keeps alive — what it has managed to carry, unwritten, undamaged, unabraded, through however many thousand years of chalk erased and chalk rewritten — the answer is the grip. Invented in a month. Transmitted only body to body, never recorded, never needing a courier beyond the courier it already is. It has outlived its inventors, its vocabulary has drifted, its sign for *bell* has spread to cover every impossible thing, and it propagates exactly the way the rest of the flora here propagates: by contact, by division, without sound, and without any animal to carry it but the one it is riding.
