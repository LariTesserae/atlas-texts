## The Worn

They are the only things in the basin that show wear.

Stand a mason next to the wall she has just finished and the difference is obscene. The wall has its burr on. She does not. Her palms are saucered like the ninth step, glossed at the heel of the thumb, ridged where the mallet handle has taught the flesh its shape. Her knees are polished. The pads of her fingers have gone smooth and slightly translucent, so that in wickmoss light you can see the darker line of the bone. Her spine has begun to carry a slight forward set, a plumb-bob hung a half-degree off. She is thirty-one thousand drops old, which is nothing, which is a hand's width of the cistern wall, and yet she is the oldest object in the room.

Nobody here has a creased face. This is the first thing you notice and the last thing you understand. They age only in the parts that touch the work — hands, knees, soles, shoulder-joint, the two vertebrae that take a hod — and everything above the collarbone stays as new as a fresh sill. You cannot tell a first-course worker from one who has been laying since before the Fourth Quarter was pegged out. You have to ask to see their hands, and asking is polite, and they give them over the way you would hand someone a tally-cord: flat, palms up, unhurried, a little proud.

And you may not sand them smooth. That is the rule and it is not a metaphor. Nails are allowed to thicken and split and yellow and are never trimmed, only broken back by work. Calluses are never pared. A crack in a palm is dressed with mortar-clay and left to close on its own crooked line, and the line stays, and it means something, and its meaning is *I was here for that.* Their bodies are the only archive the country permits. There is no writing beyond maker's marks and tally-scratches; there are no books, no records, no histories; there is a knotted eel-skin cord hanging in each horn-brace that says how many drops have fallen since somebody decided to start counting, and there is a mason's hand.

---

They are short, most of them, and wide across the back, and hairless in the way the drays are hairless — not shaved, simply without. Skin the grey-tan of unfired clay, dusted paler by brick flour, sluiced clean twice a shift by the Breath, so that they go through the day in a cycle of chalking over and washing down, chalking over and washing down. Eyes very large and pale-irised, near-useless past twenty paces, extraordinary at texture. A mason can put her thumb to a fresh brick and tell you which kiln in the western sink coughed it out, and whether the pug was over-worked, and whether it will weep true in the Breath or sweat blotchy. They read the country the way you read a face.

By their second year they are all half-deaf. This is expected and nobody grieves it. Close speech is a mutter directly into the ear, lips almost touching the lobe, one hand cupped — from a distance it looks like the whole basin is telling each other secrets, and it is not, it is people saying *pass the float.* At any range beyond arm's length they stamp. The heel-language is small, maybe two hundred figures, and every one of them is practical: *stop, load coming, water rising, wall's soft, come here, don't, someone's hurt, someone's hurt badly, all clear.* Names are stamped figures too. A person's name is three or four heel-beats on brick, and it goes into the floor, and it arrives underneath everyone at once, which is why they say a name is *the only true thing you can say in this country*, since sound through the floor is the present and everything spoken through air is already the past.

The word for a single person, one alone, out of a crew, is the same word as for a brick that has been trimmed and not laid. A spare. A loose one. It is not a nice word. They will not use it to your face.

---

The crew is eleven. Always eleven, because eleven settles a vote. Two setters, two trimmers, a board (who mixes and slings mortar and is the one everybody shouts at), two on the cage-wheel counting aloud, a line-hand with the chalk and the reed-level, a hod, a lamp-tender who renders the eel fat and guards the copper lamp with a seriousness that outsiders find funny for about a week, and an under-listener.

Above the crews there are two authorities and they do not like each other.

The **line-setters** decide where the country goes next. They walk the stubble at the front of a quarter with a bob and a cord and they chalk the day's line, and once that line is chalked it is not argued with, and the districts advance across the basin because eleven people in soft copper armbands said *here.* They are calm and dry and slightly insufferable.

The **cistern-readers** decide where the water will be. Each shift they go down into the sunk courts with a marked staff and a slate and they come up and post the waterline, and their reckoning is why nobody drowns, and their reckoning is also frequently wrong, which they will admit only among themselves. They are respected in the way weather is respected. They have the best food. They are permitted to be strange.

Between them, the **horn crews**, who hold the count, and who are neither liked nor disliked but treated as furniture, which is the highest form of trust available here.

And apart from all of them, the **unmakers**, who take down finished wall. Somebody has to; nothing may be built on new ground; the total is always the same and only the arrangement changes. Unmakers work the rear of a quarter, breaking back rooms that were laid a hundred drops ago by people they know, pounding the courses to grit for the kiln-fields. It is not shameful work but it is quiet work, and unmaking crews are quiet crews, and they have a courtesy that no one made them adopt: given the choice, an unmaker takes down her own wall first. They carry tokens — a chip of glazed lip, a fragment with a mark on it — in a wrist-pouch, and they do not talk about the pouch, and when the pouch is full they empty it into the pug pit themselves, by hand, at the end of a shift, alone.

---

They sleep on the work front, in the courts they finished that day, which have walls and no roof and will never have a roof, because the quarter will have moved on before roofing is scheduled and roofing is always the thing that gets deferred. This means nobody sleeps twice in the same room. Nobody has a house. The word they use for *home* is a direction — it means *the way the line is going* — and if you ask a mason where she is from she will not name a place, she will tell you a bearing and a number of courses, which is to say she will tell you her age.

They eat cistern eel, boiled to a grey rope, and a mash made from the sweet inner scurf of wickmoss that has to be scraped, soaked, and pressed three times to stop being poison, and they eat astonishingly little of it, less than bodies that work like theirs should need. This has never struck anyone as odd. There is no one to compare themselves to.

Tenderness they keep small and hidden, and they have exactly one rite for it. Two people who want each other press their palms flat into the same bed of wet mortar, side by side, and then the brick goes on top, and it is set, and it is plumbed, and it is buried. There is no witness, no word, no announcement. The pair are said to be **coursed**. It lasts as long as it lasts and there is no ceremony for its ending either; you simply stop laying the same course. Somewhere in the walls of the Laid Country there are perhaps three hundred thousand pairs of palm-prints, sealed face-in, in mortar that never fully cures, and every one of them is soft, and every one of them would smudge if you found it, and no one will ever find it.

---

There are no children.

This is the part of the description that people from outside cannot leave alone, and the masons find the fixation baffling and then tiresome. New workers arrive out of the dark past the rim wall, in ones and twos, never in numbers, walking in with a mallet already in hand and their palms already faintly grooved. They know the trade. They do not know their names. They get headaches for a month. Someone stamps a figure at them, three or four beats, and after a while they answer to it, and that is that; they are of the current course. The word for such a person means *the stain that shines*, borrowed from the wickmoss, and is affectionate.

Nobody has found a small one. Nobody has found a small eel either. This parallel is well known and is considered either a joke or a doctrine depending on the district.

And people go the same way. A mason will finish a shift, hand off her lamp, walk out past the work front into the greater dark toward the sound of mallets, and not come back. It is not called death and it is not called desertion. It is called **going forward**. Her crew does not mourn. They post the vacancy at the horn-brace and get a stain that shines within a hundred drops, and if that seems cold to you, consider that it has never once failed to work.

Death is the other thing, and death is loud and public and warm and is the only occasion on which this country stops working.

When a mason dies her crew carries her to the kiln-field at the western sink themselves, over their shoulders, in shifts, stamping her name figure the whole way so the floor keeps saying it. Her bones go in as grog: fired, ground, thrown into the pug and worked through until they are everywhere in the batch at the finest possible dilution. Then the batch is pressed and stamped with her mark — her mark, not the maker's — and the bricks go up in a course, one course, all the way across whatever wall the line-setters have chalked, and that course is laid slow, by hand, by people who knew her, and after that nobody points it out again. To be **laid** is the good end. It is the name of the country. When they rub a thumb on a fresh brick and say *there's a little dray in this one*, they are making a joke, and the joke is that it isn't only dray, and everybody knows, and that is why it's funny, and that is why it's said quietly.

---

Then there are the **Come-Ups**.

Eleven of them, from the Sixth Shaft. They do not work. They are fed and housed at the front of whatever quarter they attach themselves to, and no one grudges it, and no one asks them anything, and the reason no one asks is that early on some people did ask and the answers were not usable.

What the Come-Ups do is sweep.

They sweep constantly, with bundles of eel-skin lash bound to a stick, in a country that has no dust worth the name. They sweep floors that were laid yesterday. They sweep the same six feet of arcade for an hour. They sweep the stubble behind the unmakers. When the Breath comes on and the walls silver and the water runs, they stand still and wait for the drying and then they sweep the chalky bloom off, patiently, in long overlapping strokes, and they get the corners.

They are sweeping toward a floor of glazed tile, three hundred and forty courses down, swept clean, still faintly warm.

---

And there are the **under-listeners**, one to a crew, who lie down at the start of each shift with their ear flat to the brick and their eyes shut, and call the voids, and call the soft ground, and are why the collapses are rare. They hear the present. They will tell you, if you get them alone and mutter it into their ear, that under every crew there is another crew, mirrored, laying the course beneath yours upside-down, with your own mark on their bricks; that when you knock, you are being answered by yourself; and that the reason the floor is warm at the bottom of the Sixth Shaft is that the sweeping never stopped. Most masons treat this as the harmless religion of a specialised trade, and leave a single drop of Breath-water in a fresh joint before setting, the way you'd nod to someone in a corridor.

What the diggers of the Sixth Shaft found, on the way down — before the tile, before the warmth — was courses of grey-green brick, mortared with a clay that had never fully cured, stamped with maker's marks.

Some of the marks belonged to masons who were, at that moment, standing at the top of the shaft holding the rope.

That is why they came up and did not go back, and why they sweep, and why nobody has articulated the last rule, which is not a rule but the fact everyone works inside of: that they are not building this country. It is already built, out to no edge and down to no floor. They are only catching up to it. They lay the courses that are already there into the places where they already are, and their hands wear down doing it, and their faces stay new, and when they are finished being hands they become the wall, evenly, at the finest possible dilution — and beyond the rim, in the greater dark, arriving late and doubled and out of the floor, the mallets keep the count.
