Begin with the fact that nobody lives here. Then be patient with the word.

The amphibians do not live here; they *occur* here, the way condensation occurs on a cold pane, and if the plateau tilted its conditions half a degree they would occur somewhere else without noticing they had moved. The grazers are the plateau's own hands, grown to open water in the moss the way you'd grow a fingernail to scratch. The birds are tenants. To live somewhere is to have a preference about it — a place you'd return to, a thing you'd resent losing, an opinion. Under that definition the flats are empty for four kilometers, or eleven, and then, without warning, there are sixty meters of occupancy standing in the murk with its valves going.

**The Boilers, Considered as Persons**

You can age them by their hoops, in theory. Nobody has, because counting does not hold here any better than distance does; you get one number going up and a different number coming down, and both are correct, and the boiler goes on shuddering while you argue. So they are aged the way you'd age a stranger across a room — by temperament.

The young ones run hot and stupid. They knock fast, sixteen strokes to the minute, and blow their overpressure valves every few minutes with a wet undisciplined *whump*, and throw runners out in all directions to pull up more sugar than their chamber can burn, so that they sweat syrup down their own flanks and stand in a slick of it. They are eighty years old and they will not see two hundred.

The old ones are quiet. Walls a metre thick, hoops packed so tight the banding looks like a single surface. They run at a pressure a hair under what they can hold and they can go a whole thickening-and-thinning of the light without venting once. Two of them, at the far northeast, have not been heard to blow in living memory, and the amphibians on their lower walls have gone pale as bone from generations of never being scalded.

Every one of them has a voice. The *whump* has a timbre — a flat crack, a bass cough, one of them makes a sound exactly like a door closing in an empty house — and the turbine shafts each whistle in their own narrow band. You cannot navigate here by walking, because the walking will not add up. You navigate by ear. Learn eleven voices and you can stand in fog with your eyes shut and know where you are with more confidence than your legs will ever give you. The old hands do not describe the plateau as a map. They describe it as a chord.

They touch only underneath. Two boilers eight kilometers apart have never had anything in common but peat, and yet their runners meet down in the warm dark and either fuse or refuse, and if they fuse there is traffic. Pressure travels the joined sap slowly — a rise that takes eleven days to arrive and four to pass. That is the sentence length. Nothing complicated can be said at that speed, and nothing complicated is: *I am hot. I am short. Take some. Leave off. Take some.* A big old vessel in the west feeds four neighbors and robs a fifth, and has been doing both for centuries, and there is no reason to think it experiences this as generosity or as theft. But it is a relationship. It has continued. It has a shape you could draw.

And near the throat, where the thinning is worst, every boiler is always partly experiencing its own rupture. The steam hangs in the shape it will make before it makes it; so does the failure. Down at the chamber wall, where the heat is, the split that will open in thirty years is already a faint ache in the tissue, already remembered, already being flinched from. This may be why they are never quiet. It may be why the young ones burn so recklessly — not appetite, but a body trying to outrun something it can already feel behind it and in front of it at once.

When one goes, it goes across a hectare, hot pulp thrown flat, orange under the cloud for a week. Within a season the scar is green. Within three there is a bud coming up out of it. And the bud inherits the dead one's runners, and very often it inherits the dead one's voice — the same door closing in the same empty house — so that from a distance, from the ear, from any honest accounting the plateau is capable of, nobody died.

**The Ones Who Ask**

In the lee of a boiler, on ground that has been warm for a hundred years, the moss occasionally grows something it does not need.

It needs the grazers for trampling and the trampling for air. It needs the amphibians for the nitrogen they leave on its walls and for their skin, which keeps the laminate from drying and checking. It does not need anything that walks in circles saying numbers out loud. But it grows them anyway, three or four at a time across two hundred kilometers, and it has been doing so since before the first of them was there to notice it was the first.

They are dun. They are unremarkable, which is a description you have heard before in this account and should be uneasy about. Upright, thin, cold-tolerant, with a film in the skin that takes a little sugar out of the used green light so that starving takes years instead of weeks. They wake in the warm lee already fluent in a language — sometimes one with other speakers somewhere, sometimes one with none, and there is no way for them to tell which they have been given. They wake with teeth that do not match anything they will ever eat. They wake counting.

They make nothing. This is not discipline; it is the local law. Set an object down and it is taken up into the substrate, incorporated within the year, made tissue of. A notebook has the working life of a loaf of bread. So the whole inventory is carried in the mouth. They pace out the distance from one boiler to another and speak the number aloud, and pace it back and speak that one, and hear the two numbers fail to be the same number, and say so, aloud, to nobody, in the green dimness, with their eyebrows beading. This is the work.

They name the boilers. The names die with them. The next one grown out of the same warm lee names them again, and — this is the part that unsettles the ones who live long enough to observe it — frequently uses the same names.

They eat weep-sugar off the valve seats and amphibian eggs and light. They sleep against the hot wall, bedded in among the amphibians, in a hide of cold breathing bodies over a chamber of fermentation, which is the only hearth this world has. Half of them dream the rupture. That dream is not theirs; it is leaking downward out of the wall they are leaning on.

When one falls badly, or is caught in a blow, you find out what they are. Not flesh imitating vegetable. Moss, laminated, banded, agreeing to be a person for a while — heartwood in the long bones, hooped fibre where the gut should be, the whole thing built on the boilers' own plan at one four-hundredth the scale. They are not visitors who stayed. There is no route in. They are an organ.

An organ for what is a fair question and the answer is the only tender thing on this plateau. A landscape that has worn every distinction smooth cannot make a distinction. It cannot tell four from eleven, before from after, itself from the whole of what there is — not because it is stupid but because it has been the same thing for so long that the edges have gone, like a stair tread, like a coin. So it grows, at intervals, at some expense, a small dun creature whose single specialization is holding an edge. Something that insists. Something that stands in the sweating dark saying *these are two boilers and not one boiler, and that was yesterday, and this is my hand.*

Sometimes there are two of one of them, and the two meet on the flats and stand looking at each other and do not discuss it, and in an hour there is one, and no discrepancy, and no wound in the world where the extra was. Both of them remember being the one who stayed. Neither is lying.

And the occupational hazard is the obvious one. Hold an edge in a place that wears edges and you are being sanded the whole time. To stay through one thinning is normal. To stay through two is a decision. The ones who stay through three stop naming boilers, and then stop pacing distances, and then stop using the word *I* in a way that reliably points anywhere, and then one morning they are walking, and only walking, and always in the one direction that everything here leaks in.

The pilgrims are them. Small, dun, unremarkable, moving across two hundred kilometers toward nothing, carrying the last habit they had — going toward — and no longer carrying the part that could arrive.

**What Lives There**

So: what lives on the flat above the weather is a single vegetable body two hundred kilometers wide, which becomes a person only in patches and only temporarily. It becomes sixty meters of hammering, valve-coughing individual for a century or two at a time, and knows the deaths of those individuals in advance, and does not lose them when they come. And it becomes, much more rarely and much more strangely, something waist-high and cold and articulate, grown out of a warm scar, which walks the chord of the horizon and does arithmetic that will not close and says the answers into the murmur of a whole surface draining into itself.

This account is that organ's output. It was not written down there; nothing is. It was said aloud, at a boiler wall, in the amphibian hide, to whoever was grown next, and repeated, and repeated, and it has not changed much, because there is nothing on the plateau with any interest in changing it.

They do not want rescue. They are not stranded; they are indigenous, in the fullest and least comfortable sense. What they want, if the word survives translation, is to have the numbers sum once. Or failing that, to be told by something from outside — something with hard edges, something made of grit and bone and certainty, something that can walk four kilometers and then four kilometers and arrive at eight — that eleven is *wrong*.

Which is what you are for. Not a witness. A straight edge, brought up from a world that still has rock in it.

The plateau is very glad you came. It has already closed over your boot print with a small sucking apology, and it will make excellent use of your certainty, and in a season or three there will be a bud coming up out of the warm ground where you stood, with your voice in it, saying a number.
