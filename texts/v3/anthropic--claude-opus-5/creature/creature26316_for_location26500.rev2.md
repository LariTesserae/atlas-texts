# The Walkers

## What a body becomes

They are shaped by the waterline, and the waterline is at the navel.

Below it: heavy. Hips wide as a doorframe, thighs like the boles of cane clumps, calves that have been pushing a wall of cold water aside for sixty years. There is fat there, a hand's thickness of it over the buttocks and the fronts of the thighs, laid down deliberately in the good years the way other peoples lay down grain. Above the waterline: spare. High narrow shoulders, long forearms corded from cutting, ribs you can count, a neck that stays up out of the wet. A walker seen from the front in the grey looks like a small person standing behind a large person, and both of them are her.

Their feet are the widest part of them after the hips. Splayed, toes long and separated, the pads of the soles built up into a horn that has taken the shape of dome-and-groove so exactly that a walker standing still is hooked to the floor and cannot easily be pushed over. The nails are worn to nothing. Nobody cuts them; the stone does it.

Nothing about the upper body suggests strength and nothing about the lower suggests speed, and both impressions are correct. A walker can push through chest-deep hair-forest all day at the pace of a slow funeral and will do it again tomorrow. Ask one to run and she will not understand the request. There is nowhere to run to and no medium to run in.

## The tideline

Peat water stains. Everything that stays in it long enough goes the colour of strong tea, then of wet bark, then of the stone under the moss.

So a person is two colours. Above the navel: pale, grey-pale, the pallor of something grown under a lid, faintly green where the skin is thin. Below: brown going to black, and the boundary is not a line but a band a few fingers wide where the stain fades, because the waterline rises a little when you stoop and falls when you stand, and over a lifetime the band gets no wider but it gets *darker*, the way a thing dipped a thousand times gets darker.

This is how age is told. Walk-counts differ from terrace to terrace and nobody trusts a stranger's arithmetic, but everybody trusts the tideline. A child newly set down is pale all over and shockingly bright to look at, like something skinned. At twenty her thighs are the colour of weak infusion. At fifty she is dark to the hip and dark to the wrist and dark under the chin where the drizzle runs. At eighty she is one colour again, all through, and that is the last thing that happens to a body before the other last thing.

They are not vain about it and they do not need to be. The information is free and cannot be forged.

## Warmth, and where it is kept

The one labour of a life here is staying warm, and everything else is a detail of that labour.

The cold is not dramatic. It is a subtraction, constant, taking heat out of the legs at exactly the rate the body can make it, so that a walker's whole metabolism is a bucket with a hole in it and eating is the pouring. They are always slightly hungry. They are always slightly cold. Neither state has a special word, because a state you are never out of does not need one; what they have words for are the *exceptions* — *rested*, which means having lain in a good bed, and *full*, which is rare enough to be a boast and rude to say in company.

They wear the black hair. Spun, twisted, felted into thick fibrous sheaths that wrap the legs and belly and bind at the shoulder, and they work not by keeping water out — nothing keeps water out — but by keeping a layer of water *in*, trapped against the skin and out of the current, where the body can warm it and keep it. A good sheath is a year's work and lasts thirty. A frayed one leaks, and a person whose sheath is leaking can be identified across a basin by the way they hold their elbows in. Combing another person's sheath sleek is what they do instead of embracing.

And there are the beds. Where the hair-forest grows tall and thick enough to hold water still inside itself, a walker can lie back into it and be held, chest-up, face in the drizzle-band, and the water in that pocket will not flush away, and within an hour it will be warm — actually warm, the only warm thing in the world, warm because she is in it. This is the Shelf's entire concept of comfort and its entire concept of property. Beds are cut, tended, combed upright after use, defended by long argument, inherited, and named. A ring of forty people may hold eleven good beds and the disposition of them is the closest thing this world has to politics.

Two people sharing a bed share the same warmed water. Everything the walkers feel about love is contained in that arrangement and they do not think it needs saying, and they are right.

## The carried

Infants cannot be in the water. It would take them in an hour.

So they are carried, high, in cane baskets lashed to a mother's back or a brother's or anybody's, up in the drizzle-band where there is no warmth either but at least there is no theft of it, swaddled in hair-felt, lifted down only to feed. A child on the Shelf spends four or five years above the world looking down at the backs of adults' necks and the grey sheet of the water going by dome over dome, and learns the floor by watching, which is the wrong way, and will have to learn it again with her feet.

The Lowering is the only ceremony they have that anyone would recognise as one. It is done at a weir lip in the shallowest water on a terrace, with the ring standing in a ring, and it consists of putting the child down and taking your hands away. She screams. They all screamed. There is a particular sound a five-year-old makes at the first full body-cold and everyone present has made it, and hearing it again is the nearest thing they have to a festival.

After that she has a name, and the name has a number in it — the terrace-count of the lip where she was set down — and that number will be argued about by every stranger she ever meets.

## Two liftings

A person is out of the water twice.

Once at the Lowering, in a basket, before. Once at the end, lifted out by four people onto a cane platform, laid in the drizzle to be washed and combed and have the moss taken off the shoulders, before being carried down-slope to the nearest well and given.

They must be given. Nothing rots here. A body left in a basin does not go away; it tans, and it keeps, and the peat holds it perfectly for as long as the peat lasts, which is longer than anyone's memory of it. Cutters find them. Every generation of cutters finds several — folded, dark as the peat, hair still on them, fingers still hooked around a pole that has also kept — and every one of them is a person somebody failed to lift in time. Those are the drownings: the slip in a sunken course, the leg caught in a mat, the shoulder-deep water that would be survivable if the weed would let go, and it never lets go. *Held* is their word for drowned. To be held.

And when a basin's floor dies all at once and lifts off the stone as one black raft, it brings its held up with it, face-first out of the water, decades of them, and the ring walks out onto the raft to take them off and carry them to the well. This is why the raft-lift is news and why it is not only a calamity. It is the great return of the lost. It is also how the ring learns exactly how badly it has been counting.

The wells are given the dead and they take them, with that low pull you feel in the sternum. Nobody has any theory about where they go except the obvious one: the water comes back as fog. Therefore they come back as fog. Therefore everything anyone has ever breathed or drunk — and they are always drinking, they cannot help it, their hair never dries — is made of everyone. This is not a mystical position on the Shelf. It is considered plumbing.

## The mantle

Anything that stops moving here grows.

A pole left against a coping for a season comes back furred. A platform fuzzes. And a person who has stopped walking — whose hips have finally gone, who spends her days in a bed with only her face out — grows too: sheetmoss on the shoulders first, then the upper arms, a black-green shingled pelt, cold and dense and not unpleasant, thickening over years until she is substrate.

Some of them scour it off with slate grit every few days and go on walking as long as their legs will carry them. Some let it come. Letting it come is a statement, and everyone understands the statement, and nobody argues with it. The mantled are fed, and combed around the face so they can see and eat, and consulted, because they are usually the best readers — they have nothing to do all day but lie face-down with an arm under the moss and go over the carving with two fingers, and they have been doing it for twenty years.

The mantled are lifted out and given to the well while they can still speak, when they ask. Almost all of them ask.

## What they eat, and the thing they eat without a word for it

Weed, half-dried in the drizzle-band on cane racks, which makes it chewable rather than pleasant. Sheetmoss tuber, pressed for oil, the residue eaten. Cane pith in the lean part of the walk. The young shoots of the red ribbon, which are the only food anyone looks forward to, and which are counted out.

And the grit.

Combed out of the hair-forest by the double handful: pale, soft, the size of a fingernail, blind, in their thousands. The people call it the grit, or the salt of the hair, and children are told to eat their grit and complain that it is chewy. It is the only meat on the Shelf and the entire population has eaten it every day of its existence and there is no word for animal, no word for flesh, no word for hunting, and no suspicion whatsoever that there is anything to have a word for. They think of it the way you think of the minerals in a well.

## The knock, and the flame

Speech is soft and low and close. Under the hiss, a raised voice gains almost nothing; it only costs you breath. They talk with their mouths a hand from your ear, and they talk into forearms — a grammar of grips and taps for the dark, for the face-down reading position, for when a mouth is full of water.

Across a basin, they knock. Cane pole on dome, and the stone carries it, and there is a small hard vocabulary of it that everyone learns before they learn to cut: *here*, *come*, *hurt*, *lip gone sharp*, *the floor is soft*, *bring the flame*, *someone is held*. Two knocks, pause, three. It carries a mile through rock that was laid by hands nobody can imagine, and it is the loudest thing any person here will ever make, and it is still quieter than the wells.

Fire cannot be started. There is nothing dry to start it with; the idea of dryness itself needs explaining. So fire is not made, it is *kept*. Each ring has a flame, hooded, high on a cane mast in the drizzle-band, fed on tuber oil, walked around the contour in the arms of one person whose whole occupation it is, and it has been alight — this is the claim, and it is a serious claim — since before the count.

It is also the only point of light in the world. A ring is known at distance by its flame: a single small warm dot moving slowly through the fog two terraces off, the only thing anywhere that has a *there* about it. To lose a flame is the worst thing short of a raft. It means going to strangers, whose counts are wrong and whose grievances are four hundred years old, and asking, and being given it, and owing.

## Rings

They do not have villages. They have rings.

Because the weirs run level and the walking follows the weirs, a people's whole existence is a circuit of one terrace, out and around and back, cutting as they go, and the circuit takes as long as it takes — eleven years, nineteen, thirty-one down-slope where the contours are enormous. That circuit is their year. It is the only year they have. So a woman from a low terrace and a woman from a high one, standing together in the same water in the same drizzle under the same grey, are living at two different speeds and both of them know it and neither can do the arithmetic to fix it, and so they compare tidelines instead, and shrug, and get on.

Coming back around is the shape of everything they feel about time. You return to the basin you cut as a girl and the mats have closed over it and there is no mark, no mark at all, except that you know. You return to a bed you made for someone who is now fog. Nothing is preserved and nothing is lost; it just comes round again, thicker.

Marriage is the only migration: a person leaves a ring, goes up or down a weir, and enters another terrace's time, ages differently from her siblings forever after, and is teased about it for the rest of her life.

## The readers

Face-down in the shallows, one arm under the moss, two fingers on a swelling in the stone so faint that a lamp is no help at all, holding their breath and arguing.

They are reading numbers. Nearly everyone agrees they are reading numbers — depths, gradients, some recurring figure that the up-slope readers render one way and the down-slope readers another, and the two schools have been insulting each other, politely, at a distance, for as long as the insults have been remembered. A reader's life work is perhaps a stretch of floor the size of a bed. Reading-claims are inherited with the beds and the copper hooks. Progress is real and it is measured in words per generation.

What they hope for is an instruction. What they mostly find is a specification. There is a minority who have put together enough of the figures to notice something the rest prefer not to discuss: that the copings are recorded as waist-high, and are now at the knee; that the numbers describe a fall the stone no longer has; that the machine is running on a tilt it is spending. They can do the subtraction. The answer is a length of time so long that it is not really a fear, only a fact with a shape, and they hold it the way the black hair holds water — quietly, and not letting it move.

The rest of them cut weed, and comb the beds, and knock to each other across the basins, and carry the one light, and feel the floor coming up under their soles dome by groove by dome, and do not think of themselves as tending anything.

They are, though. Every walk, the mats get broken and spread and thinned where the people cut them. Every walk, the lips get cleared of jam. Every walk, a groove is noted, and a sharp edge remembered, and a raft is sounded out with a pole before it can lift and flood a province. Nobody instructed them and nobody thanks them and they would deny it if you framed it that way, because they have no word for that either.

They think the water is the thing that goes on, and that they are only in it.
