## Who Is Here

### The deed-holders

The oldest residents are the mats, and they are not really residents. They are what the water is doing.

Filaments a hundredth of a millimetre thick, wound in left-handed coils, each cell an unpunctuated sentence three billion years long: they have no strategy beyond arithmetic. Warm, alkaline, high in dissolved carbon, no grazers larger than a fly larva — this is not a marginal habitat for them, it is the best address in the world, and they respond by doubling. In a good week a lagoon goes from clear liquor to jade to the opacity of pea soup, and the crop is measured not in cells but in centimetres of thickness against the shore. Then the wind shifts, or the temperature climbs two degrees, and the whole population dies at once in windrows a kilometre long and goes leathery, and peels, and stinks, and the arithmetic starts again from whatever survived in a crack.

Deeper into the concentrated brine, where even they will not go, the water turns the colour of watered blood. That is archaea — older still, single-celled, packed with carotenoid to keep the light from taking them apart, living in liquor that is chemically closer to a degreasing bath than to anything you would call water. They ask for nothing. They are not doing badly.

Everything else in the basin, every fly and shrimp and fish and bird and man, is downstream of these two and would be gone within a season if they stopped.

### The stills

The brine flies do not endure the chemistry; they use it. Each larva grazes the mats in water it could not survive if it were built like other insects, and each is fitted with a small organ whose only job is to pump the mineral back out — a still inside a still, in a basin that is itself a still. Their empty pupal cases wash into drifts along the tidemark, ankle-deep, dry as chaff, and grind down into the crust, so that part of the ground you are walking on is made of them.

They have no interest in you whatsoever. This is difficult to convey to anyone who has not stood in it. You walk into a stratum of a hundred million animals and it opens and closes around you and not one lands, not one bites, not one registers your existence, and you come out the far side entirely unattended and obscurely insulted.

### Powder

There is a generation of brine shrimp in the dust. Their eggs — cysts, properly — sit dormant in the dry crust for years, metabolically switched off, indifferent to sixty-five degrees, and they are lifted with the alkaline dust and blown north under the lid and breathed in by everybody. When the flood comes they hatch in their millions within thirty hours and turn the shallows pink from below.

So a good part of the basin's fauna spends most of its life as something you would sweep off a windowsill.

### Each pool a nation

The fish are the aristocracy, and the most precarious thing here.

Palm-length cichlids, silver going to slate, in the outflow pools of the hot springs, holding station in current at thirty-eight, thirty-nine, forty degrees — within two or three degrees of the temperature that would cook them. They cannot rest in the warm inflow and they cannot stay in the cooler margin where the oxygen crashes, so they oscillate, all day, all night, along a gradient a few metres long. They are, in the strict sense, never comfortable.

Because ammonia will not diffuse out of a body into water at pH ten and a half, they have given it up. They convert their nitrogen to urea instead and carry it in the blood, which is what a shark does, and a camel, and you — a physiology no ordinary freshwater fish has any use for, arrived at here because the alternative was poisoning. At dawn, when the pool's oxygen bottoms out, they come up and gulp air at the surface in a soft continuous rain of small sounds, and any bird that has learned this comes and stands in the shallows and waits.

Each pool is separated from every other pool by kilometres of lethal ground. Nothing crosses. So each pool has been alone with itself for ten thousand years, and holds a population of four hundred, nine hundred, two thousand individuals that exists nowhere else, in a body of water a man could bail out with a bucket before lunch.

In 1998 a slurry line was rerouted forty metres and Pool 14 went dry in eleven days. The chemist has two of its inhabitants in a jar of formalin on the shelf above her desk. They were never described, never named, never compared with anything, and there is nothing left to compare them with. They are the type specimens of nothing.

### The pink line

Between a hundred thousand and a million and a half flamingos, and not one of them lives here.

They are the basin's commuters and its opportunists. The lesser ones — the small deep-pink millions — carry in the upper bill a filter of fine lamellae that strains coiled cyanobacteria out of the liquor at a rate of something like twenty beakfuls a second, and they track blooms across a thousand kilometres of Rift by a means nobody has established, arriving at a new one within days, at night, having been somewhere else entirely on Tuesday. Among them, in hundreds rather than thousands, the greater ones: taller, paler, wading deeper, taking shrimp and larvae, standing apart with the slight social separation of a different trade.

They cannot drink what they feed in. So the whole flock is tethered to the spring lips, and you will see them standing in outflow at seventy degrees with their bills at the surface, taking water hot enough to scald a hand, because it is the only water there is.

The young pay for the place. Soda accretes on the legs of chicks in wet years and sets into anklets of hard mineral, and a bird that has been anklets-bound is a bird that will be walked down by a marabou. This is not rare and it is not remarked on.

Mostly they do not breed here — they eat here, and rest here, and once in a decade something in the water is right and a few thousand of them build low mud cones out on an island of crust and stand over them, and half the time the flood comes at the wrong moment and takes the lot.

They follow the cutter-heads. The mine makes the lagoons, the lagoons make the bloom, the bloom makes the line on the horizon, and no bird in the basin knows that any of it depends on one running calciner and a market for glass.

### The ones with an opinion

Marabou storks, several hundred, at the settling ponds and the outfall and the back of the canteen: bare-headed, pouched, standing with their wings open and their shoulders up like men waiting to be told bad news. They eat what the pan kills, and the pan kills constantly and pushes it ashore. They have learned the plant's timetable better than some of the shift crews. They know which door the kitchen scraps come out of and at what hour; they know that the dredger's wash brings up dead things; they know a vehicle from a walker at four hundred metres.

The marabou is the only animal in the basin that has an opinion about the humans. It watches you the whole way across the apron, turning its head, and it does not move aside.

At the rim, thirty kilometres out where the crust meets the thorn, jackals work the tidemark at dusk and hyenas work it from midnight.
