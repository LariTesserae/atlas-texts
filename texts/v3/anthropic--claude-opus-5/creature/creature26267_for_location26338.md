## The Inhabitants

There are six hundred and forty-one of them on the mapped part of the plateau, and there is nothing else. Not "nothing else living" — nothing else at all, unless you count the mountains, which are also technically in motion.

Call them engines. *Tree* fails, and it fails for a specific reason: a tree is a thing in a hurry. A tree is a bid, a gamble against weather and appetite and the calendar, and every part of it is shaped by the need to get somewhere before something happens. Nothing is going to happen here. These things are built to the tolerances of objects that have never once been rushed.

## One of them, in detail

Take the individual standing about four kilometers in from the eastern lip, in the long fixed shade-triangle of a spire, so that its canopy is half sunlit and half not and it has solved for both.

It is thirty-one meters to the top wafer. The trunk is a left-laid bundle of one hundred and forty-four aluminum strands, and if you follow the bundle down with your hand you will feel that it is not perfectly straight. There is a slow sinuosity in it, an S with a wavelength of about nine meters, so gentle that the eye reads the trunk as vertical and the palm does not.

That curve is a record. The plateau is being tilted by the ranges as they extrude, a few millimeters a century, and the tilt does not always go the same way. The engine is a plumb-bob that has to keep re-deriving vertical, and it has been re-deriving vertical since before there were mountains here worth the name. The growth ring at the base — still working, still laying down fiber at the rate of a fingernail's thickness per human generation — writes the correction into the lay of the strands as it goes. The whole trunk is a strip chart. Twelve million years of orogeny, plotted in drawn metal, in a medium that cannot fade, blur, or be overwritten, standing in the open, unread.

Every one of the six hundred and forty-one carries the same chart, from its own position, and the charts agree.

## What the ticking is

You will have wondered why the leaves adjust at all. The sun does not move. The dry irregular tick of a petiole cranking a fraction of a degree seems like an animal twitching in its sleep, a nervous system with nothing to be nervous about.

They are tracking the mountains.

The sun is fixed; the horizon is not. The western blades are coming up at their few millimeters a century, and the reflected sheet of white light they throw down onto the floor changes its geometry as they rise, and the plateau tips, and the engine's own mound grows and alters the albedo underfoot, and a wafer that was optimal in the Miocene is now costing a measurable fraction of a percent. So the petiole ticks. That sound is the only sound in the world that is about anything, and what it is about is the growth of a mountain range.

Their clock is geology. It is the only clock available, and they read it to a fraction of a degree.

## The walking

Here is what nobody notices on the first day, because the floor is bright and the mounds are dramatic and the eye does not want to look at faint discolorations.

Leading away from every mound, there is a track. A band of compacted conductive slag one to three meters wide, matte grey-black against the glass, running for hundreds of meters or several kilometers, sometimes straight, more often not: long shallow curves, a hesitation, a doubling-back, a place where the track thickens as though something stood still for a hundred thousand years and then thought better of it.

They walked.

Not in the way an animal walks. They grew asymmetrically, extruding new mat on one side and abandoning it on the other, hauling the whole standing engine across the pavement at a speed of perhaps a meter per century. As they went they laid down wire from the nozzles at the whorls, spinning conductive line onto the rock behind them so they could keep drawing current from the ground they had already reduced. Road-building. The wire was infrastructure.

And they walked away from each other. Each engine's root mat holds several hundred volts against the rock, and it can feel a neighbor's counter-field through the crust as a pressure, an interference, a shortfall in the ore it was counting on. The tracks record the negotiation. You can see near-collisions. You can see one individual turn aside from a patch of good feldspar because something four kilometers away was already pulling on it. You can see the long converging spirals of a dozen engines settling toward a spacing that satisfied all of them, and then the tracks stop, all of them, within a stratigraphic hair of each other, and the hexagonal lattice is complete.

The mounds are what happened next. The nozzles did not stop. They cannot stop; the sun does not stop, the electrons have to go somewhere, and the wire is the exhaust of a photochemical engine that has no way to refuse light. So the road went on being made, straight up, into a cone, for ten million years.

Each of them is standing on an enormous quantity of road that does not go anywhere. The wire is superb — dead-consistent gauge, no inclusions, better than any factory's — because nothing has ever needed it, and so nothing has ever compromised it.

## Who they know

Six neighbors. Never seen, never touched, felt continuously as a mild balanced pressure in the mat, from a distance of two and a half kilometers, for the entire duration of their adult lives.

That is the whole society. It is a stalemate of mutual repulsion, and it is also the only thing holding any of them in place. Take one away and the others would eventually lean, and then walk.

## The two that touched

Fourteen kilometers northwest there is a double. Two engines that came at each other during the walking phase on a converging line and did not resolve it in time, and their mats fused, and after that neither could push the other away because they were drawing on the same ground.

They are still there. Both alive, both stunted, each about two-thirds the canopy of a normal individual, standing eleven meters apart with their branches interleaved and their trunks leaning slightly away from one another in a permanent, futile, million-year attempt at politeness. Their wire is thin and full of dendritic flaws. Their mound is single and shared and lopsided.

It is the only intimacy on the plateau, it was an error, and neither of them died of it.

## The one that may be dead

Near the middle of the lattice stands an individual with a full and perfect canopy, iridescent, correctly angled, and no hum.

Put your ear to the trunk and there is nothing. The nozzles are dry, capped with a hard plug of their own last extrusion. The mound is intact and its top layer is oxidized to a shade very slightly different from the others', by an amount that could mean ten thousand years or ten million.

It cannot decay. Nothing here can. Its leaves will hold their angle until the mountains come down, which they will not. Its mat is still a sheet of metal on rock and still shorts out the local field, so its neighbors still keep their distance from a pressure that may no longer be generated by anything, and the lattice still counts it. Its place is not vacant. Its place is occupied by the possibility that it is merely being patient.

This is the central fact about the inhabitants, and it is not a metaphor: **in a world without decay, patience and death have the same appearance.** They are indistinguishable from outside, and none of them has any means of checking on another beyond the pressure in the ground.

## In the mounds

Sort through the coils near the base of any mound and you will find, at a rate of perhaps forty per cubic meter, objects that are not product. They are wound spheres the size of a plum: graphite core, tight aluminum overwrap, a coat of sulfide, and — sealed inside, under the wrap — a bead of water.

That is where the thimble went. The engines vent water vapor as a byproduct they have no use for; some of it went to the cold traps in the eastern shade, and some of it went into these. A dormant mat needs a molecule of electrolyte to strike its first current against the rock. Without water, no start.

There are perhaps two million of these on the plateau, and none of them has ever started, because there is nowhere to start. The lattice is solved; every square meter is inside somebody's field. So they sit in the wire, in perfect preservation, and you cannot tell whether they are seeds or fossils, for exactly the same reason you cannot tell about the quiet one in the middle.

## What is coming

The plateau is in permanent mid-afternoon that is very slowly becoming late afternoon.

The western blades are rising. In something like forty million years they will stand above forty degrees and the plateau will go into permanent shade, and the engines will not be able to hold their voltage, and everything here will stop while remaining exactly where it is and looking exactly as it does now.

They know. It is the only thing they know. The four westernmost individuals, deepest under the coming ridge, have leaf angles at the extreme of their range, canopies cranked over hard, wire gone thin and dirty, hum dropped in pitch. And on the eastern side of each of their mounds there is a faint new discoloration on the glass, two meters long, three centimeters thick.

They have started walking again.

## If you spoke to them

You cannot, but you should understand the shape of the failure. Their fastest voluntary act is a petiole tick, and the decision to make it is arrived at over decades. A single exchange would outlast your species.

But do not mistake that for insensitivity. Stand on a root mat for an afternoon and your shadow is a load: a measurable shortfall in a canopy that has been reading its own light budget to five decimal places since the Miocene. The engine will not notice you. It will notice the anomaly. It will spend eight hundred years compensating for it, and the compensation will be laid into the trunk by the growth ring as a hairline irregularity in the lay of the strands, indistinguishable from a small earthquake.

That is the arrangement. You will leave your scuff marks bright on the glass, unblurred, permanent, and one of the inhabitants will have written you down without ever having known you were there.
