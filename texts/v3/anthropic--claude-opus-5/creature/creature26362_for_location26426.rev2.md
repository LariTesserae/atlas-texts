# What Lives There

## 1. The innumerable

Start at the bottom, with the things that are not individuals in any useful sense.

The crust-sheets come first onto new stone, within hours of a burst. They are flat, pale, plated, about the size of a thumb-joint, and they arrive by the million in a slow creeping tide out of the older galleries, following the heat. What they do is scrape. They take the raw mineral skin off the fresh fracture and pass it as a fine grey paste that dries into a crumbly rind, and that rind is the only soil this world has. Every root, every seedling pipe, every flute-stem in the Underplace is standing in the excrement of crust-sheets. They have no defence and need none; nothing bothers to eat them because they are mostly stone. When a sheet-colony is buried by a calving plate it does not die so much as stop, and stays stopped, and a Ringer breaking open an old seam will find a grey pavement of them lying exactly as they were arranged three hundred years ago, still oriented uphill.

Above them: the file. Nine million crumb-mites to a mature trunk, ascending inside, descending outside, forever, paid in wax at the collar-stations. They have no leaders and no plan and no capacity to stop. A mite that falls out of the file is eaten by the file. When a trunk dies — undermined, or drained, or simply chosen against by its own roots — the wax stops, and within a day the whole nine million come out of it at once. Ringers call this a spill. It looks like the trunk is pouring. A spill will strip a pod of everything edible in twenty minutes and then die in a heap at the base of the dead trunk, because the file has nowhere to go, and the heap is a windfall of fat and shell that a settlement will work for a week and talk about for a generation.

And in all the gas between everything, the drift: a suspended haze of pollen-eaters too small to resolve, breathed in and out of every lung and gas-sac in the region, coating everything, gumming the corners of the eye. You do not notice the drift. You notice when a district's drift is thin, and that means something upwind has gone wrong, and you send someone.

## 2. The makers

The loud animals are not incidental fauna. They are the load-bearing members.

The rasps — rasp-crickets, forearm-length, armoured in laminated glass-scale — string themselves along a trunk in vertical ranks of two and three hundred and draw the file of their femurs across their flanks, and that is their whole life. The file does not regrow. A rasp is born with a fixed number of strokes in it, somewhere near nine million, and it spends them at a steady rate, and when the file is worn smooth the animal goes quiet and is dead within the day. About eleven months, if nothing eats it. The rank, though, never stops: nymphs come up the trunk and slot into the gaps as the worn ones drop off, so that a rank is a continuous institution far older than any cricket in it. Ringers hang egg-combs to seed new ranks, and carry combs when they travel the way other peoples carry seed corn, and a settlement's wealth is counted partly in how many ranks it has resident and how evenly they are spaced. There are ranks in the old districts that have been sounding for four generations of people and four hundred generations of cricket, and they have names, and their names are drummed.

The bellows-moths do the middle voice and are also the larder. They don't fly so much as heave themselves through the thick gas, clapping their wings inside a thoracic cavity, a tearing whirr you feel in the teeth. They are the fattest things in the world. Their bodies are where the pollen goes and where it turns into oil, and that oil is as close to drinking as anyone here gets: the Ringers press it, and the waits hunt it, and the grubs of it live fat in the pith and get roasted. A bellows-moth's life is one long conversion of gold dust into grease, conducted at volume.

And under all of it, the flutes: reed-stems eighty and a hundred feet tall, holed, played by the rising heat, holding the chord. Nothing lives in a flute-stem, because the pressure inside would kill anything, but everything lives against them. Colonies orient to them. Ringers site their settlements a certain distance off the nearest flute-bank, near enough for the drone, far enough to sleep.

## 3. Hearing, and the trade in holes

Nothing here hears sound. Sound is the medium, not the message; you might as well say a fish hears water. What the fauna perceive are *holes* — the shadows cast in the noise by solid bodies. An animal moving through the Underplace is a moving absence, a shape of relative quiet sliding across the din, and every hunter in the region reads those absences through foot-plates and bone and gas-sac.

Which inverts the local grammar of concealment. To be silent is to be brilliantly visible. The way to disappear here is to make noise of the right texture, to scatter your own shadow, and so almost everything that wants to be unseen buzzes, rattles, clacks, or carries passengers that do. Prey species are often the loudest things in a gallery. Small grazers cultivate rasp-nymphs in their shell-pits and go about haloed in racket. A Ringer walking alone carries a hand-clacker and works it constantly, not to be heard but to stop being seen.

The waits are what this is all for. A wait is a flat, eight-legged, glass-plated sheet of an animal, big as a door, pressed against a trunk so closely that it makes almost no shadow at all, and it can hold that position for nine or ten days. It reads the gallery for a hole of the right size moving at the right speed. Then it comes off the trunk in one motion. They have no call and no display and no social life whatsoever, and they find each other to mate by the only means available — two absences converging — which means that courtship and predation are the same procedure performed with slightly different timing, and it goes wrong constantly.

## 4. The still

There is one soft thing in the Underplace.

It is soft because it eats vibration. A still is perhaps the size of an ox, low, blind, immensely slow, and clothed in a fleece of hollow fibres finer than pollen, ten thousand layers deep, and what that fleece does is take the sound out of the gas and turn it into food. Around a still, for thirty or forty paces, there is a hemisphere of something no one here has any other use for: quiet. Inside that hemisphere, curing fibre sets cloudy. New growth goes brittle and then powdery, and powder is what a still eats. It does not attack anything. It simply arrives, and stands, and softens the world in a circle, and grazes the ruin.

A district knows a still has come in when the chord goes thin. You do not hear the silence — you feel the pressure drop, a hollow place in the sternum where the noise should be pushing back. There is nothing in local reckoning more frightening. A still left alone for a season will weaken the plates over an entire gallery, and the plates will calve, and the gallery will be gone.

So the settlements converge. Runners go out; drum-frames are carried on poles; four or five villages will bring everything they have and form a ring at forty paces and begin a drumming-in, and it takes between two and nine days. The animal feeds. It goes on feeding. Its fleece engorges and stiffens and the hemisphere of quiet shrinks in against it foot by foot, and at some point it can take no more and seizes, and dies standing up, and does not fall over, because nothing here falls over.

Then they take the fleece.

It is the most valuable material in the world, and it is valuable for exactly one purpose. Ringer infants are born soft. No ear-plates, no throat pouch worth the name, nothing but skin and cartilage, and the noise that builds the roof over their heads would kill them in an afternoon. So every settlement keeps a cradle: a nursery lined inside and out with still-fleece, hung not on living fibre but on dead frame or bare stone, because nothing will cure true anywhere near it. The cradle is structurally inconvenient, ugly, sited badly, patched in forty places with the pelts of forty different animals killed across three centuries, and it is worth more than every bone in the settlement. Cradles have names and genealogies. A settlement that loses its cradle to a plate-fall will trade away its copper, its ranks, its stores and its standing to get a hand's width of patching from a neighbour, and if no neighbour will trade, it will do worse than trade.

Inside the cradle is the only silence a Ringer ever experiences. It lasts about two years. It ends on the day the ear-plates set.

## 5. At the front

The borers are blind, pale, leg-thick, and they chew rock. They follow heat downhill into every new fracture the roots open, and they pass the stone as grit, and that grit is the floor of every district anyone lives in. They are the second half of the world's manufacture and they do not appear to enjoy it. They have no mouths in the ordinary sense — the whole front third of the animal is a grinding surface — and they cannot be turned aside, and a borer that tunnels into a pod will come out the other side and keep going.

Things live in the borer-tracks: soft-bodied, hot-adapted, drawn up from wherever *down* eventually leads, and almost nobody has looked at them closely, because the front is the loudest place in the world and the light does not reach it well and the roots are firing like artillery overhead.

## 6. The Ringers

They are not numerous and they are not spreading. Two hundred pods in the region, nine or ten to a settlement, each settlement out of sight of every other and connected by walkers.

They talk very little in the gas, because the gas is full. They talk through the fibre: jaw-plate pressed to a cord or a strut, throat-pouch pulsing, sending slow patterned thumps down the braid. Trunk-talk is about a tenth the speed of speech and carries a mile, and it has made their language into something percussive at root. A Ringer's name is not a sound. It is a rhythm — four to nine beats, unique within a district — and it can be drummed, and that is the point.

Because the drum-frame is never idle. Whoever is not asleep or working is striking plates, day and night, in rotation, and the rotation is a roster, and the roster is recited by name. Living names and dead names both: you hold your grandmother's figure for a while, then your own, then your grandmother's again. The genealogy and the duty schedule are the same document, and it is played continuously, and it is audible for four miles. This is how a district is monitored. A good Ringer lying awake can hear which names are being drummed in three settlements at once and know that the northern one is down two hands and is curing its eastern growth too slow, and when a frame stops, everyone downwind knows exactly which name stopped.

Someone then walks four hours through the noise to find out why. Usually they find a pod hanging empty, a split rack, a spill, a wait. They take down the copper beads, strip the ancestors out of the doorway to carry home, and begin drumming.

Copper is the only metal and it buys only one thing: news. Bead-price is set by how many days' warning the information is worth. *Still in the lower gallery. Plate sounding rotten above the third bank. My daughter is of age and my ranks are healthy.*

And the dead become furniture, as promised. Lacquered, jointed, lashed in: ribs into ladder-rungs, femurs into frame-struts, skulls set facing outward along the door-lip of the pod the person lived in. A rich pod is a pod so thick with ancestors it has no wall left showing, and such a pod is also physically stronger, and both of these facts are meant when they call it rich. Old settlements walk on their great-grandparents and are held up by them, and nobody there finds this either sad or beautiful. Bone is good material. There is no other way to keep a thing.

## 7. The front-folk

Down at the growing front, the noise is free. The roots fire, the borers grind, the new plate booms as it sets, and nothing needs a drum. So there are people down there who do not drum, and have no frames, and no rosters, and therefore no recited genealogies and almost no ancestors — they move every season, following the burst-line, living rich off virgin pith in galleries a month old. Canopy Ringers regard them as children with knives: bone-poor, nameless past two generations, doomed the moment the front outruns them.

The front-folk have no cradle. What they have is a scrap of fleece the size of two hands, carried by whoever is designated to carry it, wrapped around one infant at a time. Births are timed and negotiated. When two are born close together, a decision is made. They are entirely aware of what the canopy people think of them, and they point out, accurately, that a settlement that stays put is a settlement waiting for its plate to calve.

## 8. Ceiling-fall

There is a second world on top of the canopy plates, pressed flat between green-glass and bedrock, in a quiet the Underplace cannot imagine. Nobody has been there. When a plate fails, its topside comes down with it, and what comes down is pale, flattened, fat, and mostly already dead: broad soft creatures with no plating and no resonators, built for a world where silence is normal. The Ringers call all of it ceiling-fall, have no separate names for any of it, and render it for oil.

Occasionally something comes down alive. It does not survive the noise. It lasts a few minutes, and in those minutes it does something no local animal does — it makes an uncontrolled sound, over and over, into a world where such a sound means nothing at all.

The light-pipes go up through the plate and up through the bedrock to the surface, and they are climbable, and every few generations somebody tries. There are three such names still in the rotation in the eastern districts, drummed nightly, attached to nobody, held open.

## 9. And the child

At two years the plates set. The cradle is opened at the end of a curing-cycle, with the whole settlement on the frame and every rank on the trunk running hot, and the child is lifted out of the only quiet it will ever have and put down onto the deck, and the frame takes up its name for the first time — four to nine beats, new, unique within the district, audible for four miles.

They say the child always reaches back toward the cradle. They say this without much sympathy. There is no going back in; the fleece is finite, and there are others coming, and the chord has not resolved once in the three hundred years anyone has been counting, and the roof overhead is still curing and will need shaking tonight.
