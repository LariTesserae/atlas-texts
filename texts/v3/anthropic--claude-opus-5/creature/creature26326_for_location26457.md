## The Tenant, or Ashael, or Whatever He Is Called Where You Are Standing

**The wrong question.** You will want to ask *who lives here*, and the ridge will decline the question on two grounds: the *who* is negotiable and the *lives* is frankly generous. Ask instead what the mountain is doing with its water, follow the answer, and be prepared for the answer to be a person.

---

**First evidence.** Long before you see him you will find the polish.

The pinnacle field is all fresh cleavage and salt fur, and against that texture a worn place is as loud as a shout. There are handholds at the mouths of the chimneys, five shallow scoops in a vertical row, greased-looking, the salt bloom unable to get purchase. There are thresholds dished by kneeling. There is one boulder at the head of every aisle with a bright bruised patch on its upper face where cobble has met cobble, ten thousand times, always at the same angle to the grain, because the grain is not something he works with. It is something he obeys.

There are also tracks, which is a strange word for what they are. A line of crushed bloom, hand-width, running west along the shelves. Kneel and look closely: the crushing at the near end is old, greyed, half-recolonised by aragonite fans; at the far end it is powder-fresh and still settling. The track is not a record of something that walked. It is one long footfall lying down along its own duration, and both ends of it are happening.

And the smell, which is the ridge's own smell — flint and old milk — but concentrated, warmed, with a mineral sweetness under it, the way a wet chalk cliff smells in sun. That is him. That is the smell of a body precipitating.

---

**What he is made of.** The same thing everything here is made of. He is a salt.

Understand the local ecology properly and he is not an exception to it but its logical extreme: a bloom that competes so well it has learned to move to the seep instead of waiting for the seep to come to it. Everything the fans and needles and dendritic sprays are doing — growing away from the stone in defiance of everything, encroaching, smothering, being dissolved and carried down and laid down again over slower rivals — he does too. He is simply better at it, and has been at it long enough to have accumulated a shape.

Close to, he is banded. Growth-lines run through him the way they run through a stalagmite sawn in half, hundreds to the finger-joint, each one a wet cycle, and where he is thin — the outer fingers, the rim of the ear, the flare of the nostril — he is translucent, and the light comes through pink-grey and shows the darker manganese threading where a body would keep its veins. His hands are the exception. His hands are dense, glassy, layered like agate, hammered denser at every recrystallisation until they ring when he sets them down. They are the oldest part of him and they will be the last part of him, and knowing that explains a great deal about the walls of the aisles.

He is roughly your size. This is the detail that will keep you awake later.

---

**How to pick him out.** Every column in the thicket leans upper-left to lower-right, because the rock has one grain and no opinions. He does not. He stands a few degrees off, plumb to some other down — the same down the eastern seeps use when they run uphill and leave the summit as a rising plume. He drinks that water. He is oriented to it. Against ten thousand parallel shelves and two hundred obedient spires, a single wrong angle is visible at a kilometre, and once you have learned to see it you will see it constantly, and most of the time you will be right.

---

**How many.** One. Also perhaps nine hundred. The number depends on how finely the ridge is willing to divide him that day, and the ridge is not consistent, and the rule about counting exists for a reason.

Here is the arrangement, and it takes some holding in the head, because the two grains run counter. The stone gets younger westward — sharper, brighter, less holed. He gets *older* westward. His life runs against the erosion. He begins among the mounds and the collapse-fans at the east end and he ends, insofar as he ends, in the quarry-fresh rock at the west, and everything he does he does uphill against the current of the mountain's own undoing. That is not a metaphor available to him. It is the floor plan.

At the crossing point, where the ridge's middle age and his own coincide, the roof is on the shelter, the floor is swept, and the tallies are being cut. Everything else is the same man at other distances.

---

**The day.** He maintains the combs. This is the bulk of it.

He walks the windward saddles and resets slabs the frost has lifted, taps the notches deeper with a cobble, clears the troughs of collapsed fan. The palisades strip the fog and the fog becomes water and the water goes into the black-lined basins and out along the channels, and he walks the channels, and where a channel has cracked he chinks it, and where a channel ends in a broken aqueduct pouring two thousand metres into nothing he stands for a while at the lip and then turns round and walks back to the head of the system and starts again.

The channels are not going nowhere. They are going west. They are trying to reach the far end of him, and the far end of him has weathered past the point of receiving water, and he knows this — he can walk there in an afternoon and look at it — and the water keeps going out and keeps falling into the air, and he keeps the notches sharp.

Between times he knaps. He shapes slabs for the shelter that is being built, at the stage where the shelter is being built. He sweeps a floor that is dry. He cuts eleven marks.

---

**The eyes.** They are lenses of the clear glassy mineral, the same stuff that runs in veins through the massif, and they are continuous with it. This is not a figure of speech either. Light that enters his eyes is in the rock, and the rock conducts, and it comes out somewhere else.

So: the suns in the ground. The sun in the underside of the overhang, the sun in the floor of the blind court where no sun has any business being. Those are places he has looked at. Some of them are minutes old and some are older than the pinnacle they are shining out of, and if you sit long enough in one of the small bright patches you will notice it move, slowly, with the unhurried scanning motion of something reading a slope for loose rock.

He is looking at that spot. He is not necessarily looking at it *now*.

---

**Speech.** He can, and the experience is not worth seeking twice.

The local rule holds for him as it holds for the cracking of stone: the report arrives before the event. He answers first. You will be standing in an aisle working up the nerve to speak and a dry voice like two slabs sliding will say *no, further down, past the third comb,* and then you will hear yourself ask where the water is, and the asking will feel like a formality you are performing for someone else's benefit, because it is.

Conversation with him is therefore a matter of assembling your questions out of his answers, and the process gives you an unpleasant sense of being the more ghostly party.

---

**The eleven.** From the door of the shelter, on a clear day, at the settled middle of the ridge, you can see eleven of him.

That is all the tally is. He counts himself. He counts himself once a cycle and cuts what he finds, and on the lintels where the groups run to thousands, worn shallow and recut over the top of themselves, the figure is eleven every time, has always been eleven, will be eleven, which is either enormously consoling or the single most frightening thing on the mountain, and he has had a long time to decide which and has not.

Do not count with him. The cairns disagree, the spurs disagree, and the one thing on this ridge that has never once disagreed with itself is that number.

---

**The hands.** Both accounts are true and they do not conflict.

The first: at the east end, where he has gone soft and fibrous and finally come apart, the hands remain. Agate outlasts everything. They lie in the scree with the fans growing over them, palm-down, still slightly cupped. And he collects them. He carries them west along the shelves to the sheltered walls, lays them flat against the stone, and blows ochre and manganese around them, and lifts them away, and the pale negative stays.

The second: he lays his own hand there too, the living one, the middle-aged one, and does the same, because a man will.

Hundreds, layered, the older ones going under the bloom, the newest crisp with the pigment cup standing beside it. The damp on the cup is condensation. Everything here is damp. That is genuinely all it is, and the reason to keep walking is not that the answer is otherwise.

---

**The west end.** He gets thinner. Not frailer — denser, harder, more glassy, less. Recrystallisation costs him a little material every cycle and the loss goes out with the water, eastward, downhill, to be laid down over slower colonies at the young end and become the first banded grain of a new outer finger.

He is his own succession. The rule stated at the start of the ecology — *a bloom that grows fast is a bloom that grows soluble, and the next wet cycle dissolves it and reprecipitates it further down* — has one exception with a name, and the exception is only that he has arranged for the down-slope colony that gets overgrown to be himself.

At the very last of him, at the bright sharp new end of the ridge where the rock is barely weathered at all, there is a figure kneeling at a scored outline in the dirt with unsorted slabs beside him. He is very old there and the work is only starting there and both of those are simply where he is standing.

---

**The second shelter.** In most valley-heads there is one. In three that have been described there are two.

The second is smaller, sited badly, built with an unfamiliar carelessness about the grain — the courses stepped the wrong way, the roof-slab split and mended with chinking, honest work by someone who did not have ten thousand years of practice. Inside, swept. Inside, a hearth-ring, a stone lamp, and a lintel bearing a single group of eleven marks, cut recently, cut by a hand that had to learn where to put the second one.

He built the first sort for himself.

He did not build these. He keeps them swept.

---

**If you meet him.** He will already have set water out, in a hollow at the trough's edge, and he will be kneeling at the comb with his back to you resetting a slab, and he will not turn round, because he has done the turning-round already, some while back, at a stage you have not reached and he cannot revisit.

Your shadows will be standing with his. They will be at different ages. One of them will be at his.

Drink the water; it is clean, it is the cleanest water on the world, it has been in the sky for a thousand kilometres and in the rock for none at all. Then note the bearing of the hammering, stay off that face until tomorrow, and go down, quickly, while the falling water still agrees with you about which way that is.
