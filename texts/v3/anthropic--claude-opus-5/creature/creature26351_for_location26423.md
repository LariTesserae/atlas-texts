# Who lives on the Shoulder

The first answer is: almost nothing. Two crabs, some ants, birds that don't land on stone, a tree. You could survey the terrace for a week and file it as sterile.

That answer survives about a season.

## The crab

She is called Marta, all of them, always, because it saves the argument about which one you saw. Wrist-wide, eyeless, pale as the gel she eats, and translucent enough on the underside that in low sun you can see the gut running through her as a single grey line, working.

She grazes the Sump floor in slow arcs and leaves clean silt behind her, which is why the water stays drinkable-looking and why nobody drinks it. She is not shy. Enter the pool and she will come and settle in your shadow, not touching, and stay there for as long as you are willing to stand in warm water doing nothing, which on the Shoulder turns out to be a long time. Weighed once, under protest: 340 grams. Not weighed since.

There are never fewer than two and never more than three. There have never been young. There has never been a fresh moult — but in the sixth season somebody found an old one, on the plate above the Sump, complete, split along the correct seam, with the leg segments intact and the fine hairs on the swimming paddles legible.

It was Shoulder stone. It already had a lamination in it, running the wrong way, and one thumbnail of mica.

## The ones that pass over

Swifts. They come in off the sea and go up the flank at head height and do not touch the terrace, because nothing sticks to new stone — no lichen, no algae, no saliva, no glue, no nest. The mountain is beautifully made and completely inhospitable, like a hotel room with no hooks.

Except the fig. Under the fig's canopy, on the rough grey undersides of the two lowest branches, there are eleven nest scars and, in the dry season, seven to nine active pairs. The only surface on the whole mountain that will hold a nest is the one surface the mountain didn't make.

The oldest bird in that colony was hatched somewhere else, before there was a mountain here to fly around. They keep coming back to an address that did not exist when they learned it. Nobody has a comfortable explanation for that either.

## The ants

Black, unremarkable, from a genus that occurs down in the forest and has no business at 2800 metres. They come up in single file along the western joint, cross ninety metres of bare hot plate, load a chip of stone roughly a third of their own mass, and go back down.

They take perhaps four kilos a year. The mountain makes seven metres a year. This is not erosion; it is not anything.

What they take is the point. They will not carry blank stone. They pass over it. They take flecked stone, banded stone, stone with a rust seam in it, and in the second season a party of them spent nine days dismantling a bivalve cast and carrying it away in pieces.

Their nest was found at 1900 metres, in the forest, and opened. There is a chamber at the third level, about the size of two fists, lined with mica chips laid flat and overlapping, and in the centre of the floor, reassembled, most of the bivalve.

The working phrase for that one is *midden*, and it fools nobody.

## The wasps

Cut a fig open. There are wasps inside it. Correct wasps: the females chewed out, the males dead in place with their wings unformed, the numbers plausible, the galls in the right proportion.

They belong to the right family. They belong to no species that has been described. Somebody sent four specimens away and got back a letter that used the phrase *nearest morphological relative* and then a date in the Miocene, and then apologised, twice, in the same paragraph.

Nothing has ever been caught flying. Nothing has ever been seen entering a fig. The figs are fertile. The seeds germinate — down at the station, in trays, where they grow into ordinary figs with ordinary problems.

*Ornament*, again. It is the same word and it is doing the same work and nobody likes it any better in a fruit than in a rock.

## The frog

There is no frog.

Twice. Once in the third season, once in the ninth. Both times at night, both times a single call and nothing after it, both times a clean match to a stream frog that lives four hundred kilometres away across open water. The second time it came from directly beneath a hammock, from a plate that a person had been looking at all afternoon, and that person now works on a different project in a different country and answers emails about the Shoulder briefly and politely.

## The three

There are always three people, and they are there for six weeks.

Six is not a budget number. Six is the number arrived at after the observation that at around day fifty people stop putting their things away. It isn't fear that goes, or judgement, or anything you could fail a test on. What goes is the small nightly fuss of gathering the cup, the pencil, the lens cap, the boots, and hanging them in the net bag under the hammock. Around day fifty the cup stays out.

The cup is there in the morning. It is stone. It is warm. Three days later it has a hairline of quartz in the glaze that was never in the glaze, and a chip out of the rim that the owner will tell you, if you ask, was always there.

And around day fifty-five the owner doesn't mind.

So: six weeks, three people, and the rule about the ground, and the other rule which is *count your tools*, which people obey for a fortnight and then obey properly for the rest of their lives.

They become, quite quickly, a specific kind of animal. They stand on one foot. They test temperature with the soles of their feet instead of the probe, which is against protocol and is universal by week two. They talk quietly, because the place does not give anything back and shouting into that feels like shouting into a hospital corridor. They develop the fig habit: everyone, sooner or later, sits with their back against the trunk, because it is the only object on the mountain that is unambiguously older than the ground it stands on, and there is a comfort in that which nobody puts in the log.

And they lose things, and the things come back as record. A pencil. A lens cap, tread of the knurling preserved. A field dressing, used, left on a plate in the fourth season by a hydrologist with a cut hand.

Two days later the plate had a rust-coloured seam in it, eleven centimetres long, iron-rich, correctly weathered, indistinguishable from the others.

That is where the iron comes from. Not all of it. Some of it.

## Tavares

He is the one who lost the boot. Left one, size 44, set down beside the hammock at the end of a long day in the fourth season, and by morning it was four centimetres of pale grey standing proud of the plate with the stitching in it.

He still has the right one. He keeps it in his kit and brings it up every rotation, which everyone finds either superstitious or unbearable depending on how long they have been on the mountain. He is fifty-eight. He does the fig count. He is the one who noticed that the two hammock pegs — the ones the mountain has "so far tolerated" — are not merely tolerated.

They are being held. Each year the collar of stone around each peg is a little thicker and a little more finished, and the peg sits a little deeper, and the load-test figures go up rather than down. Something below has read the rule about not sleeping on the ground, and has understood which way round it goes, and is doing its part.

That is the closest thing to a conversation that has ever happened here, and it took eleven years and it consists entirely of two pegs being made stronger without being asked.

## What is underneath

It has no observed body. The seventh stoup, split, gave us the only look: pale filaments in bundles, fine as spider silk, with a sheen like the inside of a fig, dead within seconds of air. Not tissue that has died — tissue that cannot be present and exposed at the same time, the way a word cannot be spoken and written in the same instant.

It works from the top down. Blank ground first, then lamination laid in reverse order, then mica, then inclusions, then — sometimes, sparingly, as if aware that too much would be vulgar — a fossil. That is not how sediment happens. That is how a copyist works: from the finished surface backwards into the reasons for it. It is not making rock. It is making rock *convincing*, and it only begins once the ground has been out in the light where something could look at it.

It has never answered a question. It has never made a mark anyone requested, or repeated one on request, or hurried, or hesitated. It took the tarp and understood polymer well enough to interleave it and hold it eleven years. It took a boot and kept every stitch. It took a bandage and turned a stain into geology.

It does not seem to be aware of us in any way that would satisfy a committee. It is aware of us in every way that would satisfy a landlord.

## Who actually lives here

Not the crabs; they are staff. Not the swifts; they are weather with opinions. Not the ants, who are archivists of a very small collection. Not the three, who are on rotation and go home and dream about warm stone for about a year and then mostly stop.

The fig is eighty years old on a nineteen-year-old mountain. It stays at 2800 metres while the ground streams up past its roots at seven metres a year, which means the joint beneath it is being cut open, continuously, downward, forever, at exactly the rate required. It is given warmth from below and cloud from beneath at two o'clock and dew at night and the only soil this mountain owns, manufactured out of collapsing ferns in eleven gardens that appear where they are needed. It is given a pollinator that had to be invented, and given birds whose droppings are the only fertiliser at this altitude, and given a pool kept clean by two crabs and sometimes three, and given, latterly, a small number of quiet people who prune nothing, take two figs a season, hang their beds from its branches, and sit with their backs against it in the last hour of light.

Everything on the Shoulder is either the tree, or in service to the tree, or watching.

The mountain is nineteen years old and rising, blank and pale, out of a throat that does not explain itself, and it is doing all of this without a sound, at the temperature of held tea, with the fossils put in afterwards like a signature.

Ask which of them is the guest.
