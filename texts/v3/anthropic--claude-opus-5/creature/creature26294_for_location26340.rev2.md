## Also Here

You do not meet them. They meet you, days earlier, and decide.

The first evidence is a wrongness in the lamps. You are walking at dusk under the vault, and the pendant bells are doing what bells do — brightening a hundred paces ahead of you, guttering out behind — except that in one direction they are already lit, a soft irregular line of them burning down out of the dark like a road that has been travelled very recently by something that stirred the air. You stop. (You should not stop.) The line does not advance. It does not retreat. It simply fades, bell by bell, over about a quarter of an hour, which is the length of time it takes a thing to be gone.

That was a person moving at what they call the slow walk: an amble so gradual and so laterally smooth that it displaces almost no air, the whole body gliding at the pace of a shadow on a wall. They can hold it for six hours. They use it to approach anything they are not certain about, and they have not been certain about anything in nine thousand years.

The second evidence is that one of the doorframes has hands.

---

They are not many. Across a tableland the size of a continent there are perhaps sixty thousand of them, in bands of twenty to forty, and the whole population could be stood comfortably on the quilt above one modest lake, where of course they would all immediately begin to sink.

Physically they are what four thousand metres of thin sweet air makes of a body: enormous in the chest, narrow everywhere else, with a ribcage that looks inflated rather than grown. The eyes are large and very dark and functionally useless in daylight — bring one down to the low country and it will spend the first week weeping and the second week refusing to leave shelter. The skin is pale to the point of translucency in the young, so that you can follow the vessels in a child's forearm like veins in a leaf, and progressively greener with age, and this is not metaphor. The Sill colonises. It begins in the softened creases of the feet and behind the knees and at the hairline, and it is patient, and it is everywhere, and so every single one of them carries at the hip a small blunt scraper of mineralised heartwood, worn to the shape of their own hand, and uses it perhaps thirty times a day. The action is as unconscious as breathing. They scrape while listening, while eating, while grieving. Their thumbnails are worn to slivers. A person who has not been peeled in a week can be identified at forty paces by the faint pale fuzz along the jaw.

The old are the colour of a leaf held up to light.

---

The first thing a child is taught, before feeding and before any word, is not to stand still.

The lesson is the whole of their bearing. Even at rest they shift — a slow rocking transfer from foot to foot, four or five times a minute, a motion so ingrained that they do it asleep, in their slings, twitching like dreaming dogs. To an outsider a band of them at leisure looks like a congregation swaying to music no one is playing. To them, a stationary lowlander looks like a man who has lain down in the road.

From this comes their entire grammar of stillness, which is the closest thing they have to law. To stand still and let the quilt take your feet is a formal act. It is how oaths are sworn, how quarrels are ended, how a debt is acknowledged and how a marriage is made — two people standing, hands at their sides, for as long as it takes, and then tearing free. The tearing costs skin. That is the point. You can read a person's history off their insteps.

The longest standing is grief, and we will come back to it.

---

They are silent, and they have three languages.

The first and constant one is conducted through the columns. The canopy is fused; the columns are one continuous body; air here carries nothing but wood carries everything. A knuckle rapped against a trunk goes four kilometres through the vault and comes out the other side as a tremor you can feel in the jawbone if you set your face to the wood, which is what they do — you will see a whole band kneeling at a column with their cheeks pressed to it like people listening at a door. The vocabulary is small, positional, and old. There are taps for *water here*, *browser here*, *come*, *don't come*, *sour hollow*, *I am four days from you and going north*. There are private rhythms that function as names; you can recognise a cousin's hand at three kilometres the way you would recognise a cough.

The first tap a child learns is two knocks, a pause, and one: *also here*. It means, approximately, *I am a person as well*. It is the greeting, the all-clear, the thing you send into the dark before you enter it, and it is also what they call themselves, when pressed by a stranger who wants a name for them and will not accept a gesture.

The last tap in the language is one long beat, given by drawing a knuckle slowly down the grain, and it means *stop tapping*. There is no argument with it and no reply to it. It is given perhaps twice in a lifetime, always at night, always by someone who has heard something answer the columns from below. Afterwards the band moves for a week and says nothing at all, in any register, and the children are carried.

The second language is scent. Every person wears one, mixed from resins and crushed gallery flowers and the glandular oils of certain pale grazers, and it is their true name, and it is inherited laterally rather than lineally — you are given your grandmother's scent only if you are thought to be like her, and to be given it is the highest compliment the culture contains. They can smell each other coming. They can smell mood, illness, lying. Lowlanders, who wash, are considered to be walking around with their faces missing.

The third is light: the bells, which can be squeezed, and certain luminous pastes, and the general trick of brightening a room by clapping at the air. This is flirtation, mostly, and warning, occasionally. A person who wants you to know they are approaching in the dark will set a single bell going at intervals as they come, which is an act of courtesy that costs them their advantage.

---

And then there are the two places where they are allowed to be loud, and both of them are holy.

The first is open water. On the black upraised lakes — no ambush possible, nothing in the water that hunts above it, sound dying at the far shore — they speak aloud, and this is where every spoken word they own was born. Court is held on rafts of woven moss in the middle of the lake. Arguments are settled there; contracts made there; the phrase for having a reckoning with someone translates exactly as *to take them out on the water*. Their spoken language is consequently strange: it has a rich and almost legalistic vocabulary for obligation, kinship, boundary, apology, and grievance, and almost nothing for the country itself. The words for *quilt*, *column*, *browser* and *rain* are all late loans, awkward, obviously imported from the tapping. They talk about each other out loud. Everything else they say with their hands.

They are also all born there. A newborn is the loudest object on the Sill and cannot be reasoned with, so a band with a pregnancy poles out to the raft-islands and stays a year, and every one of them, therefore, has their earliest memory on warm black water under an open hole in the vault, in a place where noise did not kill anybody. It is difficult to overstate what this does to them. Their word for *lake* and their word for *safe* are the same word, and the sound of a distant voice in the dark under the columns produces in them a nausea of pure wrongness, like seeing a house walking.

The second loud place is the Long Flower, and that is the end of this account.

---

They have never seen fire. Not suppressed it, not lost it — never had it. When a lowlander explains it, the explanation is received politely and filed with the other things travellers say, somewhere between the claim that water can be made hard by cold and the claim that there are places where you can see a hundred kilometres. The nearest thing they have to a hearth is their own gut, and the nearest thing they have to a kitchen is the peat.

Cooking here means burying. Below the quilt the old layers run at blood heat, endlessly, from the slow smoulder of their own decay, and into this they sink baskets: meat of the ribbed grazers, the flat leaf-plates, the starchy sub-quilt roots, the heavy translucent flesh of the mound-browsers. Three weeks for greens. Four months for meat. Certain funeral dishes are put down at a birth and opened at a wedding. What comes up is sour, tender, faintly effervescent, brown, and intensely nutritious, and it will floor an outsider for two days. Every family has its own pits and its own resident flora, and a marriage is judged in part on whether the two households' pits can be made to agree.

They drink sap, tapped from the columns with a spile and a gourd and a long series of apologies. Thin, warm, sweet, and metallic — the same sap that runs in the vines and the quilt and the pale shelves, the same sap that is in the wall of your bedroom and in the floor and in the thing that ate your dog. They are aware of this. There is a whole body of thought about it, and none of it is comfortable.

---

Their houses are not built. They are persuaded, and they are slow.

Cutting is close to taboo — a wound weeps sap, sap draws a fog of small biting things, and a house that bleeds is a house full of insects — so nothing is sawn that can be bent, and nothing is bent that can be encouraged. A room begins as a knot tied in two vines by someone's grandmother. It is trained a hand's width a year. By the time it can hold a sleeping platform its maker is dead and her daughter has begun the doorway; by the time the doorway will pass an adult, the daughter is old. They inherit half-grown rooms and leave three-quarter-grown ones. There are households living in their great-great-grandmother's opinion of a house, and disliking it, and continuing it anyway, because to start a new one is to condemn your children to sleep in slings.

Everything is knots. They have four hundred of them and a professional class who tie the difficult ones. Nothing is joined, glued, pegged, or nailed. Every tie is renewed annually, which is also their census, their calendar and their liturgy: on the year's turn the whole band goes over the whole settlement knot by knot, and the tying of a particular carrying-loop over a sleeping child is what stands in this culture for a blessing.

---

Their roads are animals.

A mound-browser cropping its slow arc around a lake leaves behind a firm mown track, manured, scoured of ambush-hunters, good walking for two years before the moss closes it. So the roads of the Sill are moving objects with a head at one end, and they are circular, and they enclose rather than connect. You can walk a browser's arc for a hundred kilometres in perfect safety and end up where you began. Every band owns browsers the way other people own orchards — never killed, bled monthly through a cannula in the foot, their track schedules memorised back six generations — and the animals are named, and the naming of a browser is a matter of genuine ceremony, and when one dies the band mourns it properly and then has no road.

To go from one lake to the next you must leave the road and cross unmown country, and that interval, however short, is the fundamental unit of danger in their lives. All their proverbs about risk are about it. All their maps are useless in a generation, because everything on them is walking.

---

The only hard things in the world are teeth, nodules, and the rim.

The heartwood nodules the Sill occasionally surfaces are the country's flint: knives, scrapers, spiles, needles. They are found, never made, and finding one is the sort of event people build a life's reputation on. Because a knife can only be resharpened by grinding it against another knife, every blade shrinks across the generations, and so status is inverse to size. The great families carry blades the length of a thumbnail, worn down through twenty inheritances, kept in a woven case, entirely useless, and worth more than a browser.

Teeth are the other mineral, and the only one a body manufactures. The dead are stripped of them and they are strung, and a household's cord of teeth is its archive, its land deed and its genealogy: this molar is the great-aunt who held the north pits, this incisor is the drowned uncle nobody speaks of on water. A person's entire estate, in the strictest sense, is thirty-two objects.

And then there is the rim, where the tableland is cut open and there is *actual rock*, honest and grey and infinitely abundant — and so the rim bands are obscenely rich, trading grit and pebbles and bags of plain sand inland at prices that would buy a lake. They are also regarded as mildly insane, because they live where you can see out. A rim-dweller has looked at a horizon. A rim-dweller has stood in unfiltered light with four thousand metres of nothing under their toes and the whole continental hiss in their ears, and come back, and the interior bands watch them carefully afterwards for signs. Their villages creep inland a few metres a century as the escarpment eats itself backwards, so their sinking-places eventually go over the edge, and everything they have ever consigned to the future falls instead into the cloud and is not kept. This is considered a tragedy and is the subject of most of their songs, which they sing, of course, on water.

---

There is no burial, because everything is burial. There is no fire.

A body is laid out on clean quilt, on its side, with the knees drawn, and left, and over about a month the mat does what the mat has been doing to that person since the day they were born — it closes over the feet, then the shins, then the rest — and the difference between a living person on the Sill and a dead one is only that the dead one has stopped scraping. They are entirely clear about this and say it to each other in as many words. *We are all being buried. Some of us are still objecting.*

The mourners' work is to stand at the edge of the body and let themselves be taken, in shifts, for as long as it lasts. Close kin stand for hours at a time. At the end of a great funeral the family cannot walk for a week, and someone must carry them along the browser road to the lake, and there, on the raft, out loud, for the first and last time, the dead person's spoken name is said.

Except that it isn't, not for everyone, and here is the thing they are ashamed of and cannot stop doing: some names are held back. Kept. Carried for years, sometimes for a lifetime, by a person who has decided that the lake is not enough — and taken instead to the front of the Long Flower, where the air is unbreathable with sweetness and the pollinators come in silent millions and the whole twenty-kilometre band of the world is blazing white and no sound anybody makes matters at all, because the country is drunk and blind and too loud with wings to be listening for a position. At the flower-front, people scream. It is the only place they do. They walk a thousand kilometres to stand in the white with their hands at their sides, not moving, letting it take their feet, and say one word at the top of their voice.

There is an order of people who go and do not come back — who shift camp a few hundred paces each spring and keep pace with the front, and stay inside the blossom for forty or fifty years, and forget how to whisper. They are the only members of this silent, careful, appallingly patient species who die having spoken every day of their adult lives. Bands who encounter them at the edge of the front treat them with something between reverence and revulsion, take their messages, and move on quickly.

And behind the front, in the litter and the sag, ten years later, the readers come through — the ones whose profession is to walk between the structures and interpret them, the doorframes and benches and the low walls enclosing nothing, the four steps going up out of the moss, the thresholds worn concave by feet that are nine hundred metres down. They record what they find. They sink their own carved shapes at the sinking-places for the Sill to copy in ten thousand years, and they have never once received a reply, and they do it anyway, and that is the entire literature of this people.

Occasionally, in the exhausted country behind the flower, a reader finds something that is not a doorframe.

It is made of living wood and still attached. It is the height of a person. It is standing with its hands at its sides, and it has no face, because the Sill does not know what a face is, but it knows the shape of somebody who stopped objecting, and it is very old, and it is very good at repeating what it has swallowed.

They do not touch these. They tap two knocks, a pause, and one, and they go around.
