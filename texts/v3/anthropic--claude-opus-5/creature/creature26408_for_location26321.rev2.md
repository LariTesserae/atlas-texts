# The Inhabitants

## I. The honest answer

Ask who lives on the Wick and the honest answer is a number, and the number is wrong by several orders of magnitude no matter which one you pick, because most of what lives here is too small to count and too numerous to estimate, and because the thing you are really asking about is not a population but a layer.

There is a band on the spire, perhaps two hundred paces of vertical, where the cold is survivable and the stone is warm and the fog delivers water twice a day. Above it: scour, wind, nothing. Below it: the cloud, and drowning. The band does not move. The mountain moves *through* it, upward, a finger joint a year, forever.

So the correct way to say it is this: nothing on the Wick has an address. Everything alive here is a standing wave. The wailers are a standing wave. The knappers are a standing wave. The rock passes through them the way a river passes through a whirlpool, and the whirlpool stays.

## II. The screaming layer

The wailers are not, in any way that matters, individuals. They are a sound that keeps a certain number of bodies in the air.

Sooty, hook-winged, blind from the third day of life, when the eye-studs seal over. They nest so densely that a chick cannot move without touching four others. Each bird's descending shriek is slightly its own — a hair of pitch, a particular hitch at the bottom of the fall — and that difference is everything it has. It is name, address, claim, and continuous proof of life. A wailer that stops screaming is dead, and the colony knows within seconds, and the neighbours' voices spread sideways into the space its voice occupied, the way water closes.

They have never heard silence. Not one of them, not for an instant, in any generation. A wailer taken off the Wick and carried down into the quiet will spiral, and sit, and refuse food, and die in about four days, of something that is not grief and is not fear and has no name in any language that has words.

They fly out into the black over the cloud-sea and read the drifting plankton by echo, and come back, and feed, and scream. When a big luminous drift comes through, ten thousand voices change pitch inside a second and a half, and for a few minutes the whole spire is stippled faint green, and you can see it: the mountain furred with bodies from the fog line to the crest, every flute full, every ledge doubled and tripled, a thing so densely occupied that it looks less like a mountain with animals on it than like an animal with a mountain inside.

## III. The maintenance

The crawlers are the reason any of it works.

Fist-sized, flattened, plated in overlapping scutes that slide across a glass edge the way a caterpillar crosses a saw blade — not by avoiding the edge but by having a body built to be sawed and not cut. They graze the fungal mats. They keep the mats from closing over the water grooves. They cannot climb across the grain, only along it, which means a fresh flake-scar is an ocean to them, and a colony cut off by a slide simply ends, gradually, over about nine years, without drama.

They also, without intending anything, polish. A hundred thousand armored bellies dragging the same diagonal for ten thousand years will do to glass roughly what a tongue does, only slower and more evenly. The famous licked grooves are part tongue and part belly and part nothing anyone did on purpose.

And they are prophets. A crawler will not walk a flute that is going to let go. Something in the stress of the glass — a hum, a temperature, the feel of it underfoot — turns them, and they turn as a body, and the flute empties over a day or two. The knappers know this. The knappers watch crawler traffic the way a sailor watches a barometer, and when a flute empties, they move, carrying everything they own, which is not much.

Sometimes the crawlers are wrong. More often the knappers are slow.

## IV. The oldest thing here

The combs are warmer than the rock.

Pale fans, cold-loving, feeding on the endless rain of droppings, fruiting in hand-wide sheets out of every crevice above the nesting band. They are warm because they are working. Put your palm against a comb-fan and it is like touching the inside of a wrist.

Under the fans, in the fractures, the actual organism: threads going down into the glass, following hairline cracks before the cracks are wide enough to be called cracks, sometimes a full arm's depth into the black. It is likely — nobody can prove it and nobody who could prove it has ever come here — that every comb on the Wick is one individual, and that it has been one individual for longer than the spire has been above the cloud.

Here is the part worth sitting with. The rock rises a finger a year. The comb does not rise. It grows downward at precisely the rate the mountain grows upward, and has done so, continuously, for an unmeasurable span, in order to *remain exactly where it is*. Its entire existence is an act of holding still. It is the most patient thing on the mountain and possibly in the world, and it expresses this patience as heat, which is why the dead are laid where the combs are thickest, and why the children are brought there to be warmed.

## V. The eleven

The cushion plants are older than every animal here and have no more strategy than a stone has. They sit in the dew basins in tight grey domes, growing perhaps the thickness of a fingernail in a decade, and they are the only things on the Wick that a fall does not really threaten, because they are already as low and as small and as attached as it is possible to be.

One of them flowers. Not annually — every nine years or so, irregularly, without warning, in the full dark, a single waxy bloom the size of a thumbnail that smells faintly of hot metal and lasts eleven hours.

It is pollinated. The seed sets; the seed is fertile; it has been happening since before any of this. Nobody has seen the pollinator. It does not come from below. A knapper sitting out a flowering — they do sit them out, they gather, they don't know why — will report, by touch, afterward, that something passed overhead in the dark, from the east, downward, and that the air went briefly warm and smelled like the flower, and that it did not come back.

The east is where the larger mass is. The one that eats the dawn. There is no further information and there is not going to be any.

## VI. The ones with hands

Waist-high. Dark-furred, and the fur is not for warmth so much as for water — it is coarse and channelled and sheds condensate off the shoulders and down the flanks so the animal underneath stays dry. Wide splayed feet with horn pads three fingers thick, replaced by chewing and trimming, worn down and rebuilt on a cycle of about two months. Long arms. Small ribcage. Big, deep-set, nearly useless eyes: enough to register the grey hour and a plankton drift, not enough to see a face.

They are deaf. All of them, from infancy, because of the wailers. The ear is there, and it is structurally complete, and it is overwhelmed from the day of birth by a sound that never stops, and by the second year it has quietly given up. A knapper lives inside a silence located in the middle of the loudest place above the clouds.

So everything is hands.

They speak by touch — palm to wrist, palm to collarbone, a pressure-and-tap language delivered on the other person's body, so that to hold a conversation you must be close enough to hold. There is no shouting across a flute. There is no calling out in warning. If you want to tell someone the flute is going, you have to go to them, and put your hand on them, and say it into their skin, and if you are not willing to cross the ground to do that, they simply do not learn it. A great deal about them follows from this and most of it is better than you would expect.

They also read the mountain. The glass ticks, thousands of times a night, as it cools and cracks, and a knapper feels this through the pads of the feet and the heel of the hand. Ticking is weather. Ticking is news. A flat regular tick is a good flute; a clustering, a gathering-up of ticks toward one pitch of the body, is a flute that is about to be somewhere else. They cannot hear their own names but they can feel the mountain talking about itself all night long, and they lie against it to listen.

**A day.** There is no day. There is the grey hour, and the rest.

They eat crawlers, cracked at the seam with a lancet and eaten warm; comb-fungus, in quantity, which is most of the diet and probably why they are alive at all; plankton-mash scraped off wet stone with the edge of a scraper after a heavy drift, which is a delicacy and tastes of nothing. Wailer eggs, rarely — the colony is blind but it mobs by sound, and a raider who makes noise comes back ruined or does not come back. They do this maybe four times a year, and only the young do it, and the old ones do not forbid it and do not help.

They drink from the basins, breaking the algal skin with the back of the hand. And every one of them, every day, without any apparent reason, licks the grooves. A few strokes of the tongue along the polished diagonal above their basin. It is the first thing a child is taught that is not a gesture of the hands. It looks like a tic, or a devotion. It is, in fact, hydraulic maintenance, performed at a rate of about a millimetre per century, and it is the only large-scale construction project the species has, and none of them knows it is a project.

**The hand.** Five to nine of them share a flute and a basin and are called, in the touch-language, by the sign that also means *the working hand* — thumb and four fingers, one grip. Kinship inside a hand is loose and unimportant. A child belongs to the hand. An adult who leaves one hand for another does so by walking over and sitting down and waiting to be touched.

**The children.** Few, slow, carried for a long time against the belly where the parent's fur channels the water away, and then dependent for years, because the thing they must learn cannot be hurried and cannot be spoken.

**The six gestures.** Percussion flake, prepared core, blade, scraper, lancet, retouch. Struck from fresh spall off the flanks, in the dark, by feel. They are extremely good. They have been extremely good for longer than anything here has been measured, and they have not improved and they will not, because improvement requires that someone be able to say *try it differently* to someone else, and nobody here can say anything to anybody who is not already touching them.

Teaching is therefore literal. The child's hands are placed on the adult's hands. The adult works; the child rides the work, feels the angle of the strike through the bones of another person's wrist, feels the hesitation before the blow and the follow-through after it, for years, until one day the adult's hands are placed on the child's hands instead and nothing else about the arrangement changes.

Everything they know was transmitted skin to skin. There is no other channel. There has never been one.

**The dead.** Since everything rises, the dead do not need to be taken anywhere. They are carried up a little, laid in a high flute where the combs are thick, and left, and after that the mountain does the rest — a finger a year, a forearm a lifetime, off the crest and into the cloud in something like ten thousand years.

Within a season a body is a warm pale garden. The combs take it and fan out of it and run a little hotter than they do anywhere else on the spire. In the worst of the cold, this is where the children are brought. They are set down among the fans and they sleep there, and their hands stay warm, and this is not a ritual and not a symbol and nobody has ever needed to explain it.

**The grey hour.** Once a day, for about an hour, the northern rim of the cloud floor goes the colour of dirty water. Every knapper on the Wick stops. Not all at once, not in unison, but within a few minutes all of them have put down the work and turned north and sat, and they sit for the duration, and then they get up.

They do not do anything while they sit. Nothing is passed hand to hand. It is not worship. But it has exactly the shape worship would leave, if worship had been here once and gone, and only the sitting-down was left behind.

## VII. What is in the glass

And then there is the thing that makes visitors stop talking.

A fresh face splits off a flute and there, suspended in the black, a hand's depth in, is a blade. Or a core with four negatives struck off it in the standard order. Or a lancet, unfinished, abandoned at the fifth gesture.

The glass has never been above ground. It is coming up out of the world for the first time, new, and it is full of the work.

The knappers chip them out and use them. A found blade goes into the bundle with the rest, is used until it is used up, and the exhausted core is kept — that much is consistent — carried in the fibre wrap and eventually handed to a child during the years of hands-on-hands, not ceremonially, just handed over, the way you hand someone a tool.

They do not find it remarkable. There is no touch-sign for *impossible*. There is a sign that means *the mountain gives the edge*, and it is the same sign that means *the mountain takes the ledge*, and as far as anyone has been able to determine, this is not a metaphor to them and never was.

Nineteen years ago, in a flute at about the middle of the inhabited band, a slide took a face off and left, four hand-spans deep in clean black glass, a hand. Not a print. A hand: fingers folded around a prepared core, thumb set along the ridge, wrist cocked, caught at the exact instant before the fourth gesture.

They chipped it out. They used the core. It was good glass.

## VIII. Who lives here

A wick draws fuel up out of the dark by capillary action, and delivers it, steadily, at a fixed height, to be burned.

The spire rises a finger a year and is consumed off the top as spall. Between the rising and the consuming there is a narrow band where the rock is warm and the water runs and everything that can reach it has come: a screaming, farming, fruiting, knapping layer two hundred paces tall that has not moved in ten thousand years while an entire mountain has passed through it and out the top.

That layer is the flame. The wailers are its roar. The combs are its heat, held at the base where flame is blue. The knappers are the bright part, the part that throws a little light, and they are burning at exactly the rate the fuel is supplied, which is a finger a year, which is why nothing here ever changes and nothing here ever gets to rest.

And the fuel comes up already soaked in what it will burn. The six gestures are in the glass before the glass arrives. Whatever is happening down there, in the making, has hands, and works the same way, in the same order, and has done so for as long as there has been anything to come up.

So: who lives on the Wick?

Something small, waist-high, dark, deaf, patient beyond reckoning, sitting in the dark with a child's hands laid over its own, striking a core it did not make, in a sequence it did not invent, with a technique it cannot alter and cannot describe, on a mountain that is delivering it, one finger-width a year, to the sky —

and under its hands, the stone, ticking all night, saying something.
