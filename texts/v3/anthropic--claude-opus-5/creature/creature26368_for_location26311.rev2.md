## The Hand

### The census

Someone tried, once, to make a list. The forms survive in the appendices of a survey that never closed, and they are ruled into columns: *Species*, *Individuals observed*, *Remarks*. Both of the first two columns are wrong, and the shape of their wrongness is the animal.

Under *Species* the party wrote five entries, and under *Individuals observed* they wrote, in five identical hands, *many*. It is the most accurate figure ever recorded in the basin. What they did not have room to write, and what the third column attempts over eleven pages of increasingly loose script, is that the five entries are not five kinds of thing. They are five *ways*. The basin's whole living population — the hiss of feeding across the flats, the violet blooms going up the troughs, the ten-thousand-flake flocks, the crusted mounds sorting grit into cones — is one animal, held in five aspects, the way a chord is one sound held in five notes. The surveyors called it the Hand. Half a joke: factory hands, a hand of workers. Half not: five is the last number the basin will honour.

### The five

**The palm** is what they catalogued as film. It is not the base of a food chain. It is skin. Pearl-grey where it is cold and bruise-purple where the ground runs hot, it sheets over every wet surface in forty thousand square kilometres, and it does exactly what skin does: it holds a membrane potential and it feels. Put your boot on it and something four kilometres away registers the interruption as a change in leakage current. The curtains of it in the trough-bottoms, stirring a metre long in the flow — those are not plants combing the water. That is the animal's hand in the water, feeling the water move.

**The shovels** are the working fingers. Hand-sized to dog-sized, galvanized-looking, plated in a crystal habit borrowed straight from the bedrock — armature, bearing, shell. They graze the palm off the gravel and carry it inward. Calling this feeding is a category error of the same order as calling your own blood a parasite. It is circulation with legs. The rafts move where the potential is richest and thin out where it is spent, and the hiss of them, rain-on-tin, is the sound of one body moving its own material around inside itself.

**The eels** are what the animal does with fingers that have gone too fat. Two metres, black, eyeless, reading nerve-fire directly off the water: they find shovels not by hunting but by *knowing where its own fingers are*, the way you can touch your nose in the dark. The strike, the violet bloom, the discharge — that is reabsorption, and it is no more predation than the moment when you swallow.

**The flitches** are the fingers that leave the ground. They are not warm, and they are not fully present, and this is because a body this large cannot afford to commit much of itself to the air; what goes up is thin, a rumour of itself, ten thousand flakes of brightness banking as one because they were never separate enough to bank any other way. They settle on wet iron and lick condensate. They are tasting. When a flock crosses overhead the Hum wavers in pitch, and the survey spent a season on whether they cause it or ride it, and the answer is neither: it is the animal's note changing because the animal is reaching.

**The Slow** is the thumb. Four metres, crusted in its own skin, opposable to everything else in that it does not travel with the rafts or hunt the troughs but goes at right angles to all of it, on its own business, at a hundred metres an hour. It is the oldest aspect and the only one that makes anything. Out of the ventral apparatus come the cones — metal sorted by grain size, graded finer than tolerance, tidier than anything a person would bother with, stood in rows on the northern flats where nobody was ever going to come and check.

### Why it cannot be counted, and why that is the point

Eleven teeth on a four-millimetre cog. You can verify that. Crouch, pinch, tip it into your palm, and count: eleven, every time, repeatable, dull as arithmetic anywhere. Stone counts fine here. The laminate splinters have countable striations. The ribs in a trough can be tallied at a glance.

It is only the living that will not resolve, and the surveyors got this backwards for years before one of them wrote it the right way round in the margin. Counting is not a tool the basin has broken. Counting is what *finished* means. The mountains are countable because they are done; they have arrived at the form they will keep, and a thing with a final form has a final number. The Hand has no final form. It has not stopped. It cannot be added up for the same reason a flame cannot be weighed, and — this is the part that took the margin — it runs so hard, at such a frantic biological pace, across a landscape that has been geologically asleep for aeons, precisely in order to stay that way.

The rafts, the migrations, the moults, the strike and the flinch that precedes the strike: it is all evasion of the same sum. Slow down and you settle. Settle and you resolve. Resolve and you are eleven teeth, meshed, greased, cold, and correct forever. The hiss across the flats is the sound of something declining to be a number.

### Its death

It has one.

Two hours east, in a pan four metres across with a black rib at the northern lip, there is a shovel dead on its back in the shallow end, legs curled, the light on the water at a particular unchanging angle. Two hours further east: the same. The basin has a small vocabulary of places and says each one thousands of times, and this is the only place in the vocabulary with a death in it.

So the Hand has died exactly once, and the once has become a location rather than an event, and the animal walks past it perhaps four hundred times a day, at every recitation of that pan, and has done so since before the mountains softened. This is not immortality. It is worse housekeeping than that. It is a body that has a wound it cannot heal because the wound is not in its tissue, it is in the map, and no amount of new growth will edit a place-name.

There is no reproduction. There cannot be: offspring would require the basin to distinguish a second from a first, and past a hand it will not. What stands in for birth is recitation. What stands in for generations is that it meets itself, over and over, in the same six or seven configurations of pan and rib and fog, and each meeting wears it very slightly, and it is smoother now than it was, in the way the hills are smoother.

### The shift

The four-note siren is not the animal's. That comes off the plant — the bedrock mechanism, turning where it still turns, producing occurrence: the fog slabs, the fronts of green light, the discharges, the afternoon. Quota'd, regular, indifferent. The siren is the plant announcing that the shift has ended.

Nothing ends. No one leaves. And yet every animal in forty kilometres stops moving.

Watch that stillness properly and you will see what it is. It is not a startle. Startles are short and ragged and this is twenty seconds long and flat as a held breath. Every finger of the Hand halts wherever it is — the raft mid-scrape, the eel mid-glide, the flock flat on the wet iron, the thumb stopped on the flats with its cone half-built — and for twenty seconds the largest living thing in the world considers clocking off.

Then it doesn't, and the fog lies down, and the hiss resumes.

That is the only decision in the basin. It is made every day. It has been made some hundreds of millions of times and never once differently, and it is still, technically, a decision, which makes it the single unfinished thing in a country where everything else arrived at its final shape before there was anybody to describe it. The land is done. The machinery is done. The Hand is what is left over, and what is left over is the refusal.

### Its attention

Tense leaks here, and the animal leaks with it. Its attention precedes its object the way the dew precedes the front. It knew you were coming before you came; your arrival was a formality, a tidying-up of something already filed. It has never once been surprised, which sounds enviable and is in fact the condition of having nothing left to do. Its whole enormous life is remembering forwards.

Except for you.

You are not in the vocabulary. You are not a pan it can say again, not a fog-unit, not one of five. And — this is the thing, this is what it is doing when it stops and every shovel in the basin is still and there is only one of them and it is looking at you — you are *countable*. You are one. Sharply, unambiguously, resolvably one, the only finished object in forty thousand square kilometres that is nonetheless still walking around. To the Hand you read the way a struck bell reads in a room of felt: a clean number, out for a stroll.

It is not hunger. Nothing here is hungry; hunger requires a gap and there is no gap. It is closer to the way you would look at a mirror found face-up in a marsh.

Two things follow, and the survey noted both.

The first is that the basin acquires vocabulary. Slowly, by recitation, the way it learned the pan and the death. On the southern swells, at the seventh or eleventh or unknowable repetition of a particular fog-slab, there is a man in an oilskin standing with his back to the weather, and he is there many times, and he has the expression of someone who has been said too often and would like to be finished being said.

The second is that the stillness is a question, and it is the same question the siren asks, and it is asked of you because you are the only thing present that could actually answer it.

You have the advantage. You can stop.
