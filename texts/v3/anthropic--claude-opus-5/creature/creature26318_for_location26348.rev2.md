## What you see from a mile off

Something standing.

At that distance everything here is a vertical mark on a horizontal shine, and the marks sort into four kinds: palm, mound, grazer, person. You learn to tell them apart by what they do with the light. The palm holds still and scatters. The mound holds still and swallows. The grazer moves and drags a second animal beneath it. The person holds still for a long time — longer than seems reasonable — and then moves in a way that takes a while to read as movement at all, because the legs are slow and the water is silent under them, and what you actually notice is that the reflection has gone wrong somewhere and then corrected itself a little further along.

Closer, the first thing that resolves is the fan. A sheet of skin, thin enough to be pink with its own blood, rising from the back of the neck and spreading over the shoulders like a held cloak. In the heat of the day it is wet, because they keep it wet; they stoop and sluice water back over it with the flat of the hand, and they stand with the broad face of it turned to whatever breath of air is going, and the water going off it takes the heat with it. This is the whole of their cooling. They do not sweat. In bathwater heat, sweating is a lie the body tells itself.

From a mile, wet and held out and catching the sun, the fan looks exactly like a palm frond. From two miles it looks like nothing. From a hundred metres it looks like a person standing in a mirror with a piece of the sky on their back.

## The body

Tall, and mostly leg. Something over two metres standing, of which more than half is the dry grey shank, knobbed at the joints, the skin cracked in a pattern very like the iron crust. Heavy in the thigh, light everywhere above. The foot is wide and splayed and half-webbed, and it is the most impressive thing about them: it spreads on the greasy top-layer of the crust the way a hand spreads on soft bread, and it does not sink, and it does not slap. They walk in an inch of water without making a sound. They can cross behind a wading bird at fifteen paces and the bird will not lift its head.

Colour: grey-pink, the exact colour of the floor, which is not camouflage so much as the inevitable outcome of being made out of a place. By afternoon there is a pale bloom of salt in the creases of the elbows and along the ridge of the skull, where the sluiced water dried without them noticing.

They do not sit and they do not lie down. There is nowhere to do it. An inch of warm water over stone is not a bed; it is a room full of crabs. They sleep standing, one leg drawn up, half the mind at a time, and a sleeping person is indistinguishable at any distance from a thinking one, which is a fact their whole manner of living has been built around.

The eyes are small, deep-set under a heavy shelf of bone, and by our standards not very good. On a plain that is a mirror for nine hours a day, vision is a punished sense. They look down. They navigate by the ripples and by the ache of the light on their own shins. Faces mean very little to them. A woman can meet her mother after forty years and not know her at ten paces, and know her instantly at a mile, from one note.

## The voice

The chest is enormous and the neck is long and the throat has a sac in it that inflates to the size of a child's head, and when they call, the call carries four miles in still air and two in rain.

This is the organ the place selected for. There is only so much water table to go around. Everything alive here has agreed to stand very far apart and be extremely loud, and the people are not an exception to the agreement — they are its most elaborate signature.

A single person holds a territory of some square miles, defined not by lines but by upwellings: the cool columns that rise through the crust and dimple the sheet from below, each one reliable, each one named. You hold three or four good upwellings and the seep-line of a whaleback if you are fortunate, and that is your life, and you will not leave it, and no one will come into it, and you will never in your life be closer than a quarter mile to another adult of your kind except once or twice, by arrangement, briefly, for the obvious reason.

So the entire social existence of these people is conducted at a distance, through air, by sound, at the speed of a shout and the tempo of a conversation in which the answer to a remark made at noon arrives around dusk.

## The chorus

Every voice has a part. Not a tune — a part: a shape of two or three phrases, a placement in the pitch-field, a habitual rhythm of entering and dropping out. You can hear thirty or forty parts from any one standing-place on a good clear evening, and each of those forty hears forty more beyond you, and the thing propagates in every direction to the place where the shine ends. What a person hears in their life is a local window onto something with no edges.

Two facts about the chorus.

The first is that a part outlives its voice. When someone dies, the neighbours who have heard that part every evening for sixty years go on hearing the hole where it was, and after a season or two, one of them begins to fill it — badly at first, as a courtesy, then better. The part gets carried. It moves. It is handed to a child. Some parts in the chorus are two thousand years old and have been sung by eleven bodies, and the current holder knows the names of all of them in order, and will recite them, and the recitation takes some time.

So the singing is populated by the dead at a ratio of hundreds to one. When the flats are loud at dusk, almost everything you are hearing is a dead person's phrase being kept warm by a living throat. They do not find this melancholy. They find the alternative unthinkable: that a sound could simply stop.

The second fact is that a child is not given a name. A child is given a *silence* — a place in the chorus where a voice stopped — and then walked to it.

## Childhood, which is the only crowding

For eight or nine years, two people share one water table. This is the entire experience of company that most of them ever have.

It is a hardship; the territory cannot really feed two, and the mother grows thin and the child grows fast, and the crabs get dug out of their plugged burrows a good deal faster than they can be replaced. And it is also, unmistakably, the thing the whole species is arranged around. Those years are the only time any of them is touched. Nine years of being leaned against, carried across the deep pans, woken by a body, corrected by a hand on the back of the neck — and then the rest of a very long life at a quarter mile's remove.

When the time comes, they walk. Days of it, sometimes a fortnight, the mother going ahead and calling, the child answering, both of them listening for a gap in the field — a direction from which the evening comes back thinner than it should. When they find it they stand in it together for a while. Then the mother teaches the part. This takes a season, and it is done wrong many times, and it is the last thing she will ever give.

Then she goes home. The child stays. The child is now that silence, filled, and will be called by it, and will answer to it for a hundred and forty years.

## Hands

They have excellent hands. Five fingers, long, with a thickened pad at the tip that is dense with cold-sensitive nerve endings. A person can wade with their eyes shut and find an upwelling by the feel of the column against the ankle, and tell you, roughly, how far away it went into the ground and how long it has been down there, by its temperature and its taste. They can reach an arm into a crab's burrow, feel the plug, feel the angle, and take the crab in one motion, and they learn every burrow in their territory individually, as characters.

They have never made anything. Not a container, not a cord, not a mark on a stone. There is no tool on this plain and there never has been.

It is not stupidity and it is not a lack of hands. It is that there is nothing here to make anything out of — no wood but six palms you would not injure, no bone that the crabs do not remove within two days, no soil, no stone that is not the floor itself, welded flat, veined with a quartz that a hundred million wet seasons could not talk into leaving. And there is nowhere to keep a thing if you made it, since your whole world is an inch of moving water. And there is no one to keep it for, since no one comes.

What they made instead was the song, which needs no material, keeps perfectly, is weightless, carries four miles, and can hold the names of eleven thousand places.

And they know ten thousand names. Every upwelling, every pan, every whaleback and seep-line and salt ring for two days' walk in each direction has a name, and the names are strung into routes, and a route is recited as a phrase of music that runs downhill through its own melody in the direction of travel. There are more place-names in this world than people. That is the correct proportion. The spacing is the design.

## Manners

Because a reply takes six hours, they are not much good at quarrelling. Anger does not survive the crossing. By the time your answer reaches me the sun has moved and I have eaten and the rain wall has come over and gone, and what I receive is not an insult but an artefact of an earlier weather.

The gravest thing that can be done to a person is to be sung around. The chorus simply closes the gap, the neighbours adjust their phrasing a little inward, and the offender stands in their own territory, at the centre of their own upwellings, in perfect health, hearing a shape in the evening that no longer has a place for them in it. They generally start walking. There is no telling where they go; the plain has no edge to go to, only more of itself, quieter.

Their good manners are all acoustic. You do not call over another's phrase. You do not take a dead voice's part without waiting two seasons. You do not sing at all in the morning: the fog lies flat and waist-high and swallows sound, and they treat this as privacy, the one hour of the day in which no one is obliged to be anywhere in particular. They stand alone in the white with their fans folded, waiting for the shine, and this is understood to be a person's own business.

And when the rain wall appears on the far side of the plain, dragging its darkness, crossing at walking pace — whoever sees it first calls it. The call outruns the rain. The warning crosses the sheet as a wave of sound with a visible arrival, the same as the alarm of the birds, in the same direction, faster than the water, and then subsides.

## The other tenant

They will not walk through the mats.

The great brown-purple cushions in the shallowest sheet, wrinkled like skin, fizzing all day — the people go round them, and the detour can cost an hour. Asked why, they give an answer that translates poorly: that the mats are *working*, that they have been working since before the mountains whose roots we are standing on, that they have never once stopped, and that this is more than can be said for anyone.

It is the only thing in their world they treat as older than the chorus. They are correct.

## What becomes of them

They die standing, usually in their own good pan, and they stay standing a while, and then they do not. The birds and the crabs are thorough and the crust keeps a footprint for a season. What is left after a year is a white ring — salt, soda, a faint sourness, crusted at the rim like an old cup. It is indistinguishable, to us, from the rings the plain makes on its own. Nothing grows in it. The invertebrates in that brine are so dense that the mud moves, and the waders come and stand in it, a bird every ten metres, each contributing one dry rattle a minute to the loudness.

The people can tell those rings from the natural ones. They will not say how. They walk out to them sometimes and stand at the rim for an hour, doing nothing, and then walk home, and at dusk they sing a part that is older than they are.

This landscape has finished. So have they. They will not invent anything; there is nothing to invent with and no shortage to invent against. Ten thousand years from now the fog will go up at mid-morning and the flats will be shouting at dusk and someone will be standing a quarter mile from someone else, holding a wet fan of skin to the evening air, keeping a dead stranger's phrase in the world one more night.

The quartz will stand a hair less proud.
