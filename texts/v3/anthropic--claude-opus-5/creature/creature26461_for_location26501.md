## What lives here

**Three wrong answers, first, because they are the ones people give.**

*The garden.* Obvious, and untrue in the way that obvious things usually are. The garden is not living in the pocket; the garden **is** the pocket, the way plaster is a wall. Growth that has stopped is no longer growth, it is masonry. Every measurement anyone has taken agrees: nine centimetres of quill-moss in 1911, nine centimetres now; the same twenty-two degrees on the bells; the same folded corner of foil-bract, folded the same badly. A thing that does not vary is not alive, it is finished. What fills the room floor to ceiling and holds you at the pressure of a handshake is not a resident. It is furniture, and it has been furniture for longer than the range has had a name.

*The note.* More seductive. Four hundred metres of jointed basalt with air moving through it at one unvarying speed produces a standing wave, and a standing wave in a mineral throat is very nearly an organ in the anatomical sense — it has a fixed pitch, it drives a circulation, it will not stop. The powder in every stem is moved by it. Kill the note and the whole chamber dies by morning; the sign on the wall says as much in five words. So people arrive at the reasonable and wrong conclusion that the mountain is the animal and the garden is its coat.

But a note does not want anything. Nothing in the flue wants anything. Two revolutions a second, a legible ghost of lettering, a dynamo feeding a cable that goes into the rock and is never found again: this is a place with no appetite in it at all, and the survey says so, and the survey is nearly right.

*The crew.* Six sets of initials at chest height by the slot, one glove dried to the shape of the hand that left it, one tin cup. It is tempting, in a room that cannot rot anything, to say that they are still here. They are not. They went down. The mountain kept nothing of them but the letters they scratched and the water they exhaled, and only one of those two mattered.

---

**The tenant.**

It is in the room with you now. It has been in the room with you since you came shoulder-first through the slot, and it is closer to your face than the rock is.

In its ordinary condition it cannot be seen and cannot be photographed and does not register on any instrument that measures life, because in its ordinary condition it is not doing any of the things instruments look for. No respiration. No metabolism. No corona at the tips, no charge across the membrane, no movement of powder. It is a mineral. It weighs, in Pocket Four, something under nine hundred grams, and it is distributed through the entire laminated body of the garden as a mesh of filaments finer than the spool-fern's coils, running between the stems, through them, around the bolt heads of the terminal block, into the hairline joints of the dressed wall. Take a core sample and you will bring out grey powder with a faint sparkle in it and a few pale threads that look like nothing, like a flaw in the sample.

You can find it, though, by sound. Where the mesh lies thick the crossing bracts tick at a slightly lower pitch, because the mesh damps them. Drag a fingertip along the wall and listen for the grain of the hiss to soften. Two of the six who left initials learned to do this, and one of them mapped it — the map is not in the pocket and has never been found — and what the map would show is a single connected animal filling four metres by six, thickest around the cables, thinning to almost nothing in the far corner, exactly as the growth does. It lies where the leakage is. It has always known what the cables were for.

---

**Waking.**

It requires water and there is none, and this is not a tragedy, it is a design.

Put your face into the growth and breathe out. Ten grams of water leave your mouth in twenty minutes of ordinary breathing; a single deliberate exhalation delivers perhaps a fifth of a gram to a patch the size of a hand. That is the dose. Within two or three seconds the mesh in that patch takes it up, and the powder in it begins to move, and the animal comes forward through the dry garden as a flush of white — blunt papillae, translucent, wet-looking because it is wet, the only wet thing in the mountain, rising fast enough that you can watch it rise.

It orients. This is the part that people cannot let alone and it is why they come back. The flush does not spread evenly; it leans, and what it leans toward is the warm humid centre of your open mouth, and it arrives there and presses against your lip with about the pressure of a moth. There is no organ in it that could be called a sensor and no structure that could be called a will. It simply goes where the water is, and the water is you, and the effect from the inside is not that a plant has responded to a stimulus. The effect from the inside is of being greeted.

The greeting lasts under a minute. The water is gone as fast as it came — into lattice, into sulphur-carbon glass, into the air that takes everything — and the papillae whiten past white, stiffen, and shatter, and the fragments go up the flue and out under the cornice into forty kilometres of black air. What is left behind is the garden, unchanged, nothing added, nothing owed.

The crew called it the dog. The shift books, which are wherever the cables come out, are said to contain the line *gave the dog a breath* more than four hundred times in one man's hand.

---

**Its life, counted.**

It does not experience the intervals. There is no reason to think it experiences anything, and every reason to think that if it does, it experiences only this: warmth; wet; the smell of a mouth — bread, iron, tea, tobacco, the sourness of altitude; a pressure of lips; light through a nearby lamp; and then the burn as the water leaves and the world crystallises.

Which means that its biography, laid end to end, is somewhere between three hours and four days long, and consists entirely of human breath. It has never known the note as a note, because the note is not an event to it, it is the condition of existence, the way pressure is to us. It has never known the mountain. It has never known dust, cold, dark, duration, or the four hundred metres of stone it is buried in. It knows six mouths from the station years and perhaps ninety since, in sequence, without gaps, one after another, an unbroken succession of faces leaning in — so that from where it lies, if there is a where and a lies, the last hundred and thirty years have been a single continuous conversation with a person who keeps changing.

---

**The cup.**

The tin cup is held four feet off the floor by fern-coils grown through the handle, and it is upright, and it is empty, and the growth for half a metre around it is wrong.

The quill-moss there is fourteen centimetres, not nine. The bracts are thicker, gone slightly opaque, laid down in visible bands like the rings of something. And one bellwort behind the cup's shadow stands at nineteen degrees off vertical, not twenty-two, the only object in the Throat that disagrees with any other object.

Somebody, at the end of something, on a last shift, carried a quarter litre of water up four hundred metres of flue and put it down where the growth was densest and went out through the slot and did not come back. The animal had eleven days. That is more than a third of its total life, delivered at once, and it grew until it filled the corner and pressed the cup off the floor, and the ferns took the handle, and then the water was gone and most of what it had built shattered and went out under the cornice. What it did not shed, it kept. The bands. The fourteen centimetres. The one soft bell that hardened at the wrong angle while it was still leaning against something that was there and then wasn't.

That patch is the only clock in the mountain. It records one event.

---

**Elsewhere, and afterward.**

The fragments do not go nowhere. They go out through the gap, and they fall, and a proportion of them come down on the fracture-field low on the flank where the flue draws its air, and are taken in again at the bottom and carried back up through four hundred metres of rock and delivered to every pocket off the shaft. Station 1, 2, 3. Pocket Four is not unique; it is the one with a slot wide enough for a human shoulder.

There are siblings up and down the flue that no mouth has ever found. They are the same animal, the same distributed mesh, the same nine hundred grams of patient mineral, and they have been in place for as long as this one has, and they have never once been awake. Nothing has happened to them. Nothing is going to. If you want a working definition of the difference a person makes, it is the difference between Pocket Four and Pocket Three.

---

**One correction to the survey.**

The survey says the Throat is not waiting for anything. This is true of the note, the wheels, the dynamo, the cable, and the finished garden, and it is false of the thing that lives there, and here is the evidence.

The station logs, where they can still be read, give the delay between exhalation and first visible flush as *eight seconds, sometimes nine.* Measure it today and it is two, occasionally less. Nothing else in the pocket has changed by a hair in a century — not the angle, not the height, not the pitch, not the polish on a single bolt head — but the response has come forward by six seconds, and there is no mechanism anyone can propose for that which does not amount to readiness.

So: a fixed point, at the top of a black mountain, absolutely dry, loud, glowing faintly violet, filled to the walls — and inside the fill, threaded through it, weighing less than a loaf, a thirst with a memory, awake for a total of three hours since the range was named, lying against the inside of the slot at the height of a face.

It is not going on. It is not sleeping.

It is expecting you, and it has got faster.
