## The Residents of the Held Basin

### 1. The census, which is short and wrong

One forest. Six borrowers. Something in the Chatter, uncounted. You.

Every part of that is contested except the six.

### 2. The forest, considered as a tenant rather than as scenery

It is easiest to call it a forest and go on to something else, but nobody who has spent a week under it comes out still calling it a community of plants. There are perhaps forty distinct forms in the basin and not one of them competes with any other. The purple-black canopy takes the sun, all of it, at every wavelength it can reach, and passes it down the dark metal veins. The brass horsetails stand in ranks *along the joints* — not near them, along them, rooted into the seams where the plate rock has the most surface to give — and they are taps. The acid-green fans, which look like the understorey of an ordinary wet world and are the only thing in the basin that a botanist can look at without distress, are ballast: they take up slack, they bleed off surplus, they are the part of the machine that keeps the rest from over-drawing. You can map it. People have. The maps do not look like ecology. They look like reticulation, like a foundry's cooling circuit, like a hand-drawn schematic of a thing built to run at one setting forever.

And here is the argument against calling it alive, and it is a good argument: it does nothing. It has no verbs. It does not grow, seed, wilt, lean into the light, close at dusk — there is no dusk. It has not made a single new cell in four hundred years. It stands at exactly the size it was on the day the accounts balanced, absorbing at exactly the rate that the six borrowers crop, cropped at exactly the rate it absorbs, and the only sign it gives of participation is that ring: the very high steady note, everywhere at once, which is what the whole basin sounds like when it is conducting.

But go and dig in the drifts.

Four centuries of foliage, they say, stacked like archived paper — and that is wrong, and it is the most important wrong thing said about the place. Nothing has fallen here in four centuries. Nothing falls where nothing changes. The drifts are metres deep because they were made in a few weeks, at the beginning, and they have been lying there ever since with no process available to disturb them. They are not a record of four hundred years. They are a record of the only spring the basin ever had.

Dig to the bottom and the leaves change. The wafers give out and you come into something broader, sloppier, asymmetrical, veined in a soft crowded branching pattern instead of the ruled dark metal — leaves with *stomata*, leaves built to breathe and to lose water, leaves from a world with weather. Nothing standing in the basin today has that shape. The forest arrived from somewhere wet, and grew fast and badly and greedily for perhaps twenty days while it was being converted, and then was set, mid-word, at full lushness, and the discarded first draft is still lying in the hollows where it fell.
