## The Inhabitants

### 1. The Landlords

Start with the ones who own everything, because nothing else here owns anything.

A grove is not a population of towers. It is one animal wearing thirty or four hundred bodies, joined below the crust by root-grafts as thick as a thigh, and the fluid in any one trunk has stood in most of the others at some point in the last few centuries. Call the whole thing a holding. A holding knows exactly one number to any real precision — the tonnage it contains — and it knows it the way you know how much air is in your chest. Everything it does is done against that number.

It does very little, and all of it is slow, and all of it is accounting. It decides, over a decade, whether it can afford a new trunk. It decides, over a century, whether to spend two litres on a daughter. It turns its upper spirals into an oncoming dust front six hours before the front arrives, because the front is where it gets its nitrogen, and this is the most sudden action it will take in a thousand years. When one of its bodies fails, it does not mourn and does not decompose it; it begins the withdrawal, and for the next ten centuries that trunk stands as a hollowing shell being politely emptied by its relatives.

The grove tone is the sound of this holding pumping itself uphill with no valves. It is not a heartbeat, whatever visitors say. It is the sound of a treasury being counted continuously and never spent.

### 2. Tenants in the Glass

Inside the rind — not on it, *in* it, in the interstitial spaces of that dark translucent material — there is a fauna, and it is numerous, and it is invisible without breaking the thing you would be looking into.

They are flat. Flatness is the whole design. Some are the size of a fingernail paring and some you would need a lens for, and they live their entire lives in a gap of half a millimetre between laminations of green glass, moving through it at a rate of centimetres per day, drinking picolitres from the vessels they nick and reseal behind them. Nothing they take is missed. They are the reason the rind is not perfectly transparent: those faint mottlings, when you hold a cut frond to the sun, are trails, some of them older than any browser alive.

They never see the outside. They are born in the glass, they breed in the glass, and when they die the holding withdraws them along with everything else. As far as I can tell they are the most numerous form of life on the continent by a factor of thousands, and they have no relationship of any kind with the sky.

### 3. The Walker

Now the one that matters, because it is the only thing here that goes anywhere on purpose.

Four hundred kilograms, low as a table, broad as a door. Six legs, short, columnar, set wide, and it moves them in a sequence that never has fewer than four down; it has no gait faster than the one it has, and no need of one. The hide is grey suede to the eye and dead to the touch — laminated, non-transpiring, and never shed, because shedding is a loss. When a plate does lift at the margin the walker works it off with a hind foot and eats it. Nothing leaves this animal except by intention.

Two things about its body will strike you before anything else.

The first is that its back is not the same colour as its sides. The dorsal shield is pale, almost white, and very smooth, and it is a radiator. In a thin dry atmosphere under an indigo sky, a good surface pointed at the zenith can dump heat straight out to space, and the walker's whole daylight posture is organised around keeping that shield square to the sky while presenting the narrowest possible edge to the sun. Cross a pan behind one and you will notice it drifting a few degrees off its line through the day, tacking against the sun's bearing, and that is what it's doing: it is standing in its own shade and pouring the day out through its back into the dark.

Which is why, at nightfall, it walks toward a grove. Not for comfort. The same shield that saves it at noon would kill it by fourth hour under a clear night sky, and so the walker spends its evenings pressed against a blood-warm trunk with its back to the canopy, drawing back some fraction of what it threw away. Every animal on this plateau is a machine for pointing at the correct sky.

The second thing is the mouth. It is a rasp, and it is also a seal. The outer lip is a soft compliant ring that goes flat against the rind and holds — genuinely holds, you can hear the small sound when it releases — and inside that seal the rasp works a shallow oval score until the vessels give. The walker then drinks by suction into a sealed gullet, and not one drop of what comes up ever meets the air. This is the entire technology of the plateau's higher life: one closed vessel docking with another. Four litres in about eleven minutes. Then the seal breaks, the walker steps back, the oval begins to close, and the ledger is amended.

It does not urinate. It does not exhale. Its nitrogen goes out as a hard pale pellet with less water in it than the crust it lands on, and it puts these down at the base of trunks with what looks a great deal like care, though I would not push that word.

### 4. The Ledger

Here is what a walker is, on the inside: a map with dates on it.

It holds perhaps thirty litres. A sixty-kilometre crossing costs it one and a half. A tower can be scored perhaps once in three years without harm, and there might be forty scoreable towers in a holding, and there are eleven holdings on the loop this particular animal walks, and the loop takes it nineteen months. It knows the last date it drank from individual trunks. Not from groves — from trunks. It knows which ones healed slowly the last time and are therefore to be left another season. It knows which pans grow soft in the season of fronts and are to be skirted at a cost of nine extra kilometres. It carries a margin sufficient for one crossing plus one, always, and it will turn away from a grove it has arrived at, having walked two days to it, if the arithmetic that arrival produces is worse than the arithmetic of going on.

This is the whole of its mind, and it is enough of a mind that I am not comfortable calling it anything less.

Its trails across the crust are the only marks any living thing has made on this continent, and they are erased by every front and redrawn within a season, and the redrawing is not memory. Two walkers who have never met, whose lineages parted a thousand years ago, will lay the same line across the same pan, because the problem has one answer. A calf follows its parent for thirty or forty years and learns nothing from her; it derives, and the derivation converges, and by the time it leaves it cannot be distinguished from having been taught. Here, teaching and solving arrive at the same place, so the continent has never needed to invent the difference.

And here is the thing the visitors' account misses, the reason the towers tolerate the dawn hour.

The dawn film is not a gift and it is not a theft. It carries gametes. A sealed plant cannot broadcast anything — release is loss, and there is no wind in a grove and nothing on a tower that flutters — so for one hour before sunrise, at the coldest, the holdings put their sex out onto their own trunks in a film thin enough to reabsorb, and the walkers stand with their mouthparts flat against it, and go on standing there for the whole hour, and then walk sixty kilometres.

That is the mail. The walkers are why holdings ninety kilometres apart are one lineage. The routes and the spacing of the groves are not two facts, they are one fact seen twice: the groves stand where the walking is cheapest, and the walking is cheapest where the groves stand. Nobody arranged it. Both parties are simply paying the same freight, in opposite directions, and have been for a hundred million years.

### 5. The Fourth Sound

The visitors count three sounds and they are wrong, because they were standing up.

The pale stone rings. Lie down on it with your ear flat and give it an hour and you will hear the plateau's only conversation: a slow irregular knocking, single strikes with long gaps, coming from very far off. A walker has a thickened boss on the underside of its fourth foot, and it taps. The air here will not carry a shout ten metres, but the shelf carries a heel-strike for thirty kilometres and more.

It is not language and it is not much. It is a position, and a state, and — I think, though this is the least defensible thing I will say — a debt. Walkers meet perhaps four times in a life for the purpose of meeting, and those meetings are arranged over months by knocking, and they consist of one animal transferring to another a sealed capsule containing gametes and about a litre of water. A dowry. Everything on this continent that reproduces does it by handing over part of the estate, and the walkers are no exception, and a walker that has given a dowry walks the next hundred kilometres with a smaller margin than it likes and knows it.

### 6. The Falling Thing

The sailer is not a bird and is not, I think, best thought of as a flier at all. It is a glider that has arranged for the ground to keep pushing it back up.

Nine metres of wing, membrane like scraped horn, and a body inside that you could hold in two hands — a keel, a gut, a beak with a cutting ridge, and almost no reserve of anything. It weighs nothing because it cannot afford to weigh anything. It lives on the permanent thermals that stand over the dark groves, and its map of the plateau is the same map the walkers carry, seen from above and inverted: not a chain of wells but a lattice of columns, forty kilometres of sinking air between one and the next, each crossing a controlled fall that ends either in a column or in the crust. Its whole existence is the management of a single continuous descent.
