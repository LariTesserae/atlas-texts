Everything alive in that room does the same work, and the work is metallurgy.

There is no other trade. Nothing here photosynthesizes, nothing ferments, nothing filters. Every animal in the room is engaged, directly or at one or two removes, in the business of taking oxygen off iron and getting paid in the difference. It is a single-industry world. The differences between its inhabitants are differences in how far from the wall you are willing to be, and what you are willing to eat instead.

---

**The crust-kind.**

Start with the ones on the east wall, because everything else is a comment on them.

An adult is about four centimeters across, roughly oval, and from above it is seamless: eleven plates the color of old nickel, overlapping back to front like roof tiles, so that the whole animal presents a single sloped surface with no visible joint. This is not decoration. The plate margins are filed — fine ridges, hundreds of them, and the rear edge of each plate rides on the ridges of the plate behind it, and when the animal shrugs, minutely, it makes one dry syllable. That is the tick. A healthy adult ticks every three or four seconds for its entire life, awake or otherwise, and stops only when it is dying, molting, or being killed.

Underneath: a ring of eighteen short legs around the rim, hardly legs at all, more like adjustable feet — and in the middle, filling most of the underside, the pad. The pad is the animal. It is a soft grey membrane, warm to nothing, and its chemistry is not describable in the vocabulary of digestion. It is closer to a cell in a battery: it is laid against the live surface and it moves current across itself and uses the movement to strip oxide, and what it leaves behind is a smear of reduced metal and a fine ash which is the reason the room smells of hot dust and old coins.

They lie on the bloom four and five deep. It is worth being precise about what depth means, because the pile is a series, not a heap. Only the bottom layer touches the wall. The second layer presses its pads against the *backs* of the first, and takes what passes through them; the third takes what passes through the second. Every animal in the pile is both a feeder and a conductor for the animals on top of it, and every layer loses something, so that a grazer on the fifth tier is living on the room's thinnest possible margin and can feel, through its pad, exactly how much better life is one body down.

So the hierarchy is vertical and it is measurable. The strong go under. They wedge in at the base, at the center, where the insulation split, and they hold that millimeter with their whole existence, and they do very well there: the plates thicken, the files coarsen, the rim spreads.

And that is how they die. A grazer grows by molting — there is no other growth — and molting requires room, and slack time, and a soft interval, and none of those is available at the bottom of the pile. The best position in the world is the one you cannot leave. An old center-holder becomes too thick to molt, and then too thick to move, and then its pad fuses to the wall it has been eating, and then it stops feeding altogether and turns to the only remaining function: it extrudes capsules from under its rim, dry hard eggs the size of a coarse grain of sand, which are pushed out into the traffic and carried off on other animals' feet, and it goes on extruding them for a long time in a fixed position while oxide creeps up over its plates.

Which means the bloom is partly ancestral. The green-white patch on the east wall, the size of a hand, this world's entire primary production — it is not alive, and it never was, but a good third of its substrate is the crusted-over bodies of animals who won.

**The eleven minutes.**

Every forty-one hours the leakage triples and for eleven minutes the potential is high enough that even the fifth tier and the far margin can eat well. What the crust does with this is not celebrate. It reshuffles.

The margins surge in — that is the roar, the applause-through-a-floor sound, tens of thousands of filed rims scraping over each other at once. But the more important motion is outward.
