## Bodies

Four hundred and ten people live in the Kettle, and seven of them were born in it. The oldest of the seven is three years and four months. The rest — four hundred and three adults and half-grown — came down the ladder on purpose, most of them within the last three years, and every one of them could give you the day and the hour.

They are thin. Not starved — the descent log runs to eleven hundred kilos of pressed food a month — but stripped, the way rope is stripped, because in forty-three degrees fat is a coat you cannot take off. The heavy ones went up in the first year. What remains is a population of long forearms and visible tendons and shoulder blades that stand out under wet fibre, and a way of standing very still with the weight on one hip, because moving costs and stillness is free.

Their skin is the colour of underlit paper, and it does not tan; it does something else, a dull matte thickening across the shoulders and the back of the neck where the radiant heat comes off the rock. In every crease — elbow, knuckle, the fold behind the knee — there is salt. It goes in and does not come out. They bathe in the Weep's hot brine, standing in the pans at the end of a watch, and then they scrape themselves down with a fibre glove and step out drier than they went in and white to the shins. The scraping is the second chorus of the Kettle, less organised than the sweeping, a wet rasping that starts in the southeast about an hour before each bell.

Nobody has a beard. Nobody has hair below the collar if they can help it. Under a permanently damp cloth in that heat, hair is a garden, and the Kettle has no interest in gardens it did not plant. Every adult carries, in a twist of fibre in a hip pocket, a flake of floor-glass — struck off the pahoehoe by a bootheel, keen enough to open a finger to the bone — and they shave with it, and cut cord with it, and lance their own salt-boils with it, and when it dulls they drop it and pick up another. The mountain hands out blades for free. It is the only thing in the Kettle that is free.

Their hands are the log of their trade. Fleece-tenders have cuts across the pads of the fingers, from the gutters and from the rails after a bloom. Melt-house crew have shiny burns on the backs of the wrists and no eyebrows and a squint they keep even in the dark. Haulers' palms are so glazed that they have to be careful shaking hands with surface people, who feel that skin and go quiet.

Two kinds of eyes, and the town half-jokes about them as if they were two nations. Fleece eyes: dark-adapted, pupils wide as a cat's, able to walk the third tier's coat-closet alleys at a jog with no lamp at all, and useless in the sun-walk — the coin comes across the floor and the fleece-tenders turn their backs to it, weeping freely, grinning. Melt eyes: scorched by the furnace glare through four years of goggles, sharp in white light, blind in orange gloom, feeling their way home along the curtains like a man in a strange house.

## The Collar

Ask anyone in the Kettle what the first thing you learn is and they will touch their throat while answering.

They re-wet at the troughs every half hour. The interval is so much the shape of their lives that it has become the unit of time: nothing is measured in minutes here, it is measured in wettings — *two wettings to the melt-house*, *she was in labour eleven wettings*. Under that runs the pumps at eleven strokes a minute, and people count strokes without knowing they are counting, and can tell you to within twenty seconds how long you have been talking to them.

They wet in their sleep. This is the thing that unnerves outsiders more than the absence of animals, because you can watch it happen: a room of hammocks in the dark, and every few minutes an arm comes out, finds the jar, presses the cloth, lays it back across the throat, and the sleeper never surfaces. Mothers do it twice — their own, then the child's — without waking, without error, in the dark, in the correct order.

The Kettle-born learn the gesture before speech. The oldest of the seven, a girl called Vett, could touch her collar at nine months and could not say a word until sixteen. Her mother is not troubled by this. Her mother says the order is right.

## Households

A household in the Kettle is not a marriage, a room, or a family. A household is a set of frames.

You are what you own of the fleece-field. The great houses of the town — and there are perhaps nine — hold twenty or twenty-two frames apiece, and their people sleep on the fifth and sixth tiers where the rock is only thirty-eight degrees, and their names are the ones on the Assembly board. The middle of the town holds three, four, six frames, and lives on the third. And then there are the Dry.

Ninety-one people in the Kettle own no water. They came down late, or they lost frames in a settlement, or they were hired down from the surface stations with a contract that pays in jars. A Dry man drinks by the terms of a written agreement, and the agreement specifies litres, and he can read it, and does, often. They work the pans and the pumps and the hauls. They are not despised — the Kettle is too small and too hot for that kind of energy — but they are counted, and they know they are counted, and among themselves they use a different word for the fleece-field: they call it the orchard, which is a joke about ownership and about fruit, and they will not explain it to a frame-holder.

To marry here is to merge your frames, and it is done in the Assembly with the descent clerk present, and the vows are about water. To part is the hardest problem in the Kettle's short law, because a fleece cannot be halved, and moving a frame a hand's width is the offence that gets you upped. There have been four partings. Three were settled by one party leaving. The fourth is still open, two years on, and the couple in question have not spoken since, and tend adjacent frames on opposite sides of the same gutter, and are said to be scrupulously fair about the sweeping.

## Watches

The town does not keep a day. It keeps a breath.

The bell rings for the in-draft, and the white column comes down the shaft, and the fog closes over the floor from the ankles up, and four hundred people are on the field within four minutes with jars and gutter-rakes, moving in a soft blind press through twenty metres of visibility, calling to each other in a flat close murmur that carries better in fog than shouting does. Two of these a day. Sleep goes in the two long troughs between, in halves, so that no one in the Kettle has slept more than five hours consecutively since they arrived, and several of them say they have never felt better, and the medic writes that down without comment.

The talk is quiet, close, at a range of about thirty centimetres, mouth near ear, because the fibre eats sound and the heat makes volume expensive. On the surface they are taken for mumblers and conspirators. Among themselves they have an extraordinary economy: whole exchanges done in eyebrow and chin, the melt-house crew reading lips through goggles, the haulers' four-note whistle code up the shaft, which is the loudest legal noise in town.

And once a day the coin crosses the floor and the whole arithmetic stops. The Kettle's one unmanaged forty minutes. People bring their children and stand them in it and step back out. Old men stand in it for a count of thirty with their eyes shut and their faces up and their hands at their sides, and step out, and go back to work, and are visibly better for hours. Nobody has ever needed to enforce the courtesy rules. It is the only thing here that nobody has needed to enforce.

## The Listeners

There is a chair at the centre of the floor and someone is in it every hour of every day, and the person in it is the most powerful individual in the Kettle for two hours and nobody at all afterwards.

Anyone can stand the watch; about sixty do. But there are eleven who are asked for, and the town knows exactly who they are, and they are strange people. They have learned the rock's vocabulary the way a shepherd learns a hillside: the knuckle-tick that means nothing, the double tick that means nothing but is worth a note, the long tearing groan in the ceiling that means write it down and sit forward. Nine calls in four years. Six wrong. Three vents. No one has ever been reprimanded for a wrong call and no one ever will be, and the Listeners still carry the six like debts.

The best of them is a woman of fifty-one from the pans, who cannot read, and who dictates her log to whoever is passing, and whose entries are the ones the melt-house crew go and look up.

Sit that chair alone through an out-draft and the chamber makes its low tone, under the floor of hearing, changing pitch as the salt shuts the cracks one at a time. Everyone agrees it is not a voice. Everyone under about twenty-five hears a word in it — two syllables, the same two syllables — and they will not write it in the log, and they will not say it to a frame-holder, and when a new young hauler comes down they wait a month and then take him to the chair on an out-draft and let him hear it and say nothing, and if he comes up saying the word they nod and that is the whole of the initiation.

## Osme

One woman in the Kettle has a job that only she does. Osme, forty-four, came down in the second year with her mother, who died in the fourth; she keeps the fig.

She waters it from its dedicated fleece, which is the only frame in the field owned by the Assembly. She weighs its leaf-litter on a pan-balance and composts it and gives it back.
