# Inhabitants of Panel Seven

The survey lists nine species and calls the place impoverished. The survey is wrong in the ordinary way: it counts species. What lives here is a small number of very specific individuals, most of them known by name to four people, and one or two things that are not organisms at all but are unmistakably alive, and you cannot understand any of them separately.

## The Pair

They hold the eastern scarp from the rowan northward, and they have held it, the fitters say, since about 2009. It cannot be the same two birds. Ravens go fifteen years if they are lucky, and there are fitters who have been here thirty-one. But no one has ever seen a handover, so the story stands: the Pair, definite article, no other name needed, because there has only ever been one pair and there is no room for a second.

The nest is on a ledge forty metres up, in the lee of a fallen block, and it is not a nest so much as a stratum. Fifteen seasons of building, no season's structure removed: sticks, heather stems, baler twine in three colours, a strip of red hazard tape, a length of copper earth wire that the fitters recognise and have never mentioned to anyone. It is a metre deep now and it overhangs its ledge and one day it will come down into the moss and lie there like everything else that has come down into the moss.

They are silent. Not mute — they are physically ordinary ravens and the throat works; you can watch the feathers of the hackle lift and part with the effort of a call, and see the bill open to the exact angle, and the whole thing happens inside the roar and does not exist. What has happened instead is that the visible parts of the display have swollen to fill the vacancy. Everything a raven does with its voice, the Pair does with its body, and larger. The bill-clack that a raven on the plateau performs as a sound is performed here as a *gesture*, the bill held out level and snapped so wide that you see the pink of the gape from two hundred metres. The male's courtship roll over the evasée is not the flick that ravens do elsewhere but a full inversion, held, three or four beats of flying upside down in the warm rising column with the fog going past him. It is showy to the point of embarrassment. It is what happens to a language when you take away half of it and it has to make the remainder carry the load.

They read the fitters. Not the men — the lamp. There is a sequence, two slow swings and a stop, that means the shift is out and the crib bags are open on the step, and the Pair are down off the scarp inside forty seconds every single time, and they have never once come for any other swing of any other lamp. Cray has tried to catch them out for years. They do not fool.

Their young leave in July and do not come back, which is normal, and what becomes of them is not. A bird ringed as a nestling here in 2009 — green over metal, left leg — was seen twice the following spring on the plateau above, silent in a group of six calling ravens, kept to the edge of the group, and then not seen again. That is two observations and it is nothing, and everybody who works here believes the same thing about it and does not say so.

## Seven

The vixen under the tarmac slab is called Seven, and so was the one before her, and the one before that, because the panel is Seven and it saves argument. There have been at least five. The den is the same den: the slab came down in the eighties at an angle of about thirty degrees and left a dry wedge under it, floored with dropped sandstone, warm from the plume, invisible from the rim, and no fox that has found it has ever voluntarily left.

The cubs are the strangest animals in the county. A rural fox cub will take flight at thirty metres and keep the hedge between you and it. Seven's cubs have a flight distance of about a metre and a half, and that is not tameness, it is an absence — the startle reflex in a mammal is built and calibrated on sound, on the snapped twig and the shout and the dog, and nothing in this trough has ever announced itself. They have never once been warned. They grow up in a world where things simply appear, and so they have concluded, reasonably, that appearing is what things do. A cub will walk up the step of the fan house and sit down with its chin on the threshold and wait to be looked at. In June you cannot sit down on the anchor blocks without one coming to see what you are.

They are killed, all of them, as soon as they leave. The Hallowbank road is two kilometres north across the plateau and it takes them in their first autumn. There is a column in the back of the log book, not an official column, going back to 1991, and it is mostly dates and mostly March, and it is the only sentimental thing in the building. The trough produces, every year, four or five flawless and entirely unfit animals, and exports them into a world that expects them to have learned to flinch.

## The Sighted

Everything that persists here has done it by not needing to listen.

The wolf spiders are the great success: *Pardosa*, thousands to the hectare on the drier hummocks, hunting by eyeshine, which works. But the males court by drumming — palps on the substrate, a pulse train in the low hundreds of hertz, straight into the middle of what the fan is doing to the ground. So they don't. Or they do, and nothing comes of it, and what the females here respond to is the other half of the display, the raised palps waved in the visual field, which in ordinary populations is the decoration and here is the whole message. A student came from Lancaster and spent two summers on it and went away without a thesis, and left behind twelve marker canes that the moss has since eaten.

The midges are the joke of the place, if you want one. *Culicoides* find their blood meal by warmth, by moisture, and above all by carbon dioxide, and what stands at the south end of this valley is a concrete cone eighteen metres high exhaling four hundred cubic metres a second of warm, saturated, CO₂-enriched air from eleven kilometres of worked-out coal. It is the signature of a mammal the size of a hill. From the last week of May the swarm over the evasée is a column you can see from the rim, and the concrete furs over, and they land and crawl and find nothing at all, and by noon the base of the cone is grey with dead ones a centimetre deep in the angle of the plinth, and the wolf spiders work that line like a beach.

And there are birds every March and every October — redwings coming down into the fog, meadow pipits, once a hoopoe that four men saw and nobody photographed. They drop in, and they are gone inside three hours. They cannot hold territory in a place with no acoustic channel; they cannot hear the alarm; they cannot even hear themselves, which may be the worse thing. The trough is a sieve, and what it catches is only whatever can live with one sense painted out.

## The Line

Except along the Line.

The substation cable was laid in a trench eight hundred metres across the mire in 1974, backfilled, and forgotten, and the moss closed over it and now shows it as a strip of slightly brighter green a metre wide running dead straight from the rim to the fan house — the only straight living thing in the trough. Underneath, the duct is dry, warm from the cable, and roofed with sixty centimetres of saturated peat, which is one of the best acoustic damping materials there is.

It is quiet down there. It is the only quiet in two kilometres.

So the Line is where everything that needs to hear has gone. Shrews: an absurd density of them, common and pygmy, working the duct and the metre of moss either side of it, squeaking to each other in a corridor where squeaking still means something. Field voles in the margins, runs radiating a few metres off the Line and stopping, as if the grass were water. Ground beetles. And — this is the part visitors refuse to believe — a naturalised colony of house crickets in the warm brick and the first sixty metres of duct, descended from an infestation of the old canteen stores in about 1978, singing at four and a half kilohertz, all year, in the dark, under a mire, in Northumberland. Put your ear to the moss over the Line on a February night and you will hear a sound from a bakery basement, and lift your head ten centimetres and lose it entirely.

The frogs use the warm-band pools and spawn insanely early — the log book gives 22 January as the record, and nothing later than the second week of February since 1996 — and they cannot chorus, so the males simply swim about and grab, and the pools boil in silence, and it works well enough that there are more frogs here than anywhere on the plateau.

## The Crew

There are four, on twelves, two on at a time, and there has not been an hour since 1974 in which the fan was unwatched, because the law says so.

Dennis Cray, thirty-one years, who cannot hear a telephone and can feel a bearing beginning to run warm through the concrete floor with his boots off. Marian Ogle, who came as a contractor in 2004 to do six weeks on the switchgear and never left, and who keeps the mire records nobody asked for. Joss, who is twenty-six and paints, badly, from the window. And a rotating fourth, currently a man from the agency who will not last, because the new ones either take to it in the first fortnight or begin to talk about the pressure in the chest.

Their hands are the thing. There is a sign language here of perhaps two hundred items, cribbed from crane work in the seventies and then grown on its own like a species on an island. It has signs that exist nowhere else on earth: a flat palm drawn level under the chin for the fog lying down; two fingers hooked under the jaw and pulled back for the fox, from the way she sits with her chin up on the step; a fist opened slowly at the temple that means *it has changed and I do not yet know how*, which is the most important sign in the building and is used perhaps twice a year. Their faces do the grammar. Ogle's husband says that after four days on she comes home and talks over the top of her own speech with her hands and cannot stop for about a day.

And the log book. Since 1974: hourly vibration readings, bearing temperatures, current draw, in six different hands, the earliest of them V. Tasker's, who died in 1996. In the margins, in the same hands, in the same pencil, on the same ruled pages between the numbers: first cranberry flower. First frogspawn. Raven off the nest. Birch in leaf. Cubs out (4). Cubs out (3, one small). First rime, 11/10. The men who keep the machine alive have, without anybody instructing them to, kept fifty-one continuous years of phenology on the youngest mire in England, at hourly resolution, in the margins of a statutory document, and nobody outside the substation has ever asked to see it.

## The Sheet

And under all of them is the one large organism, which is the moss.

Not a community — a sheet. Forty to ninety centimetres deep over the whole floor and up the lower scarps, four hundred thousand tonnes of it counting the water, which you must, because the water is the point. It makes its own conditions: acidifies, waterlogs, starves out competitors, holds twenty times its weight and will not let go. It has swallowed the rails, the anchor blocks, the wheels of the flatbed, the cable trench, the marker canes, every bootprint within a week. It grows a millimetre a year and two in the warm band, and it is the only thing here with an opinion about the next four hundred years.

It is also, uniquely, a wild organism with a maintenance schedule. It is here because two machines breathe on it. Somewhere in a contract there is a line item, a sum in the low tens of thousands, for bearing replacement, and that line item is the reason a Sphagnum lawn is bright green in December at this latitude and altitude. The mire is not tended. It is not managed, protected, designated, or even, formally, noticed. It is kept alive as a side effect, by men attending to something else entirely, the way a cat is kept alive by a warehouse.

## Number One and Number Two

Which leaves the two that are not organisms.

Number One is original, 1974. Number Two went in in 1998. They are nominally identical and they are not: Number Two runs fractionally fast, by something like five hundredths of a hertz, and so the two fundamentals walk in and out of phase with each other on a cycle of about twenty seconds. Three per minute. A slow swelling and thinning of the whole pressure field of the valley, a beat you do not hear but do, in fact, breathe with, and everybody who works here breathes with it, and so do the ravens on the nest ledge, and nobody realises until they leave and find that their chest has been borrowing somebody else's rhythm for thirty-one years.

The standing waves ride on this. The nodes are not quite fixed; they slide back and forth by five or six centimetres, three times a minute, for fifty-one years, which is why the still pools are not perfectly still at the edges, and possibly why the mosses in the nodes grow taller, though as noted, nobody has published.

## Twenty Minutes

In the small hours of a night in May, they stop.

The run-down takes about four minutes and it is the only event in the year. The note falls out of the floor and down through the chest, and comes up through the throat into audibility as it dies — everybody hears it, briefly, as a sound, the one time it is ever a sound — and goes.

The fog over the evasée collapses within ninety seconds, downward, like a dropped sheet, and the stars are there, which people who have worked twenty years here have seen four times. The still pools lose their stillness, all at once, all over the valley, and begin doing what water does. The mire creaks as it comes back up off the pressure; Ogle's word for it is *shifting in a chair*. Something drains, somewhere, into the sandstone. The midges, if it is a warm night, come off the concrete and go looking, properly, for the first time, for the animal.

Seven comes out from under the slab and stands in the open with her ears going, and does not run, because she has nothing to run from, but she is the only fox in England standing in a landscape that has abruptly acquired an entire additional dimension, and she stands there the whole twenty minutes with her head moving, and does not hunt.

And on the scarp, the Pair wake up.

What happens next is disputed, and I will give it as it is given. Cray has been present at nine changeovers and says it has happened at four of them. The male comes off the nest ledge onto the block above and stands, and the hackles part, and the bill opens to the angle, and he makes a sound.

It is not a raven. Ravens are mimics — the best of the passerines, better than the starling, and they take their repertoire from whatever their world contains. The male's world has contained, since the egg, one signal, continuously, at every waking moment, and what comes out of him in the dark on the four occasions is described identically by everyone who has heard it: low, not loud, throbbing very slightly in and out, and pitched, as far as anyone can judge, somewhere just at the bottom of hearing.

There is a phone recording from 2019. The county recorder listened to it and attributed it to handling noise and asked, politely, whether anyone had considered the effect of expectation.

Then at about the eighteenth minute the crew wind them back up, and Number One takes the load, and the note climbs down out of the audible and back into the sternum where it belongs, and the fog stands up again over the cone inside three minutes, and the pools go still along the nodes, and Seven goes back under the slab, and the shrews come up out of the Line, and the whole valley closes over the quiet the way the moss closes over a boot.

Cray retires in April. He has already bought the fan for the bedroom.
