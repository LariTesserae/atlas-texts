## Who Lives in Number Six

Ask it properly, because the answer depends on the verb. Things *visit* Number Six: rain, soot, the granular weather of the alley, a man once a season with a pencil. Things *persist* in it, which is different, and the difference is a matter of rate. A visitor is anything the chamber can absorb in a fortnight. A resident is anything the chamber has had to make room for.

By that measure the population is small, and none of it was invited.

### The Countable

Start with the sump, because the sump is where you can put a number to it.

The eels came up the drains as elvers, a thumb-length of glass with two black beads at the front, some wet spring before the interceptor sewer was cut and closed the road back. They came the way elvers come — following warmth and the smell of fresh water against the current, climbing wet brick with their bodies, passing through a six-inch pipe as easily as through a cathedral door. Say forty of them arrived. The sump kept four.

They are thirty inches now and they will not be thirty-two. An eel of their years should have silvered — should have thickened its skin, doubled its eye, stopped eating, gone down the river in autumn and out past the shelf toward water it has never seen, to breed once and die. These have not begun. Something in them is waiting on a signal that twenty feet of made ground does not transmit: a change in the day-length, a slackening of the current, the taste of the sea coming up to meet them. In its absence they have simply declined to proceed. They hold station in the warm chalk-tasting water with a slow fanning of the pectorals, and they are not old so much as *postponed*, and they will be postponed for a long time yet.

They are not blind in the way the accounts say. The eyes are there, pale, unpigmented, capable of nothing. What they use is the lateral line — the row of pores down the flank that reads pressure — and by it they know the whole tank continuously: its ten-foot square, its lip of deposit, the standing shape of the water. When a drop falls from the calcite eighteen feet above and strikes the surface, every eel in the sump feels the ring go out and come back off the walls, and knows from the return where in the tank the drop landed. This is how they live: in a dark room whose furniture is announced four or five times a second. When a body comes down the rungs and the air moves and the heat of it reaches the water an hour later as a hundredth of a degree, they feel that too, and go to the bottom, and stay there for a day and a half.

What they eat, since somebody always asks: hoppers. In the silt of the sump and packed into the warm cracked mud of the north channel there is a population of blind amphipods, rice-grain, translucent, perhaps four hundred thousand of them, that you can find by pressing your palm flat on the channel floor and waiting for the tickle. The hoppers eat what the rain brings down — the soot and grease and sugar and horse of a century of alley, washed through the grating once a fortnight in a cold rope and laid out warm in the dark like a table. They eat moths. They eat, eventually, everything, including each other, including any eel that finally gives up. The whole column stands on a street it will never see, and the street has no idea it is feeding anyone.

### The Hair-Eaters

The moths are the chamber's own, and the fact of them is quietly astonishing.

The lagging on the mains is plaster-and-hair: lime, gypsum, and a binder of cattle and horse hair, beaten in by a labourer in a yard in 1871 and troweled on hot. It is the hair they want. The larvae live in the cracks of the crust, in galleries they line with silk and their own spent grain, and those black fine threads in the lagging that the survey called *possibly a fungus* are not fungus; put a fingernail into one and it parts, and there is a grub in it the size of a comma, glassy, with a dark gut showing through. They go quiet when the rain comes because the cold sends them down into the wall of the gallery, and they come back up over two days as the pipes tick their way back to blood heat.

So: a species eating the insulation off pipes that have carried nothing since the reign of a different king, and eating it so slowly that in a hundred and fifty years they have taken perhaps a sixth of it. The adults emerge with no pattern on the wing, no scales to speak of, eyes reduced to two specks of pigment with nothing behind them. They do not fly to find each other; flying in that thicket is suicide. They walk. They walk the pipes, which is to say they walk the entire volume of Number Six, because the pipes go everywhere and touch everything, and a moth that sets out along the twelve-inch main at the west end can reach the flue, the catwalk, the sump lip, and the underside of every hanger without once leaving iron. A male reads a female's scent off the metal she has walked, hours later, the way you would read a sentence, and follows it at a quarter-inch a second for as long as it takes.

They fly only when they are lost, and the whirring stops the instant they touch anything, which is almost instantly. The ones that fly high enough drift into the heat over Number Two, stall, and come down on the plate, and are cooked in the time it takes to say so, and go over the edge into the channel, and the hoppers have them by morning.

There are, at any moment, perhaps nine hundred of them in the chamber. Nobody has counted. Nobody could.

### The Eleven

The white rosettes in the second bay are not ferns and not, in the ordinary sense, plants. They are the fruiting structure of something that lives mostly in the joints: a lime-tolerant, tar-eating growth that runs as a rind in the mortar and puts up these wax-white stalks under the one drip that has fallen in the same place since Gladstone. Cold to the touch, in air at blood heat — they are running water up through themselves and losing it off the stalk-tips, and the loss keeps them three degrees below the room. It is the only cold thing in Number Six that isn't the floor.

An inch a decade. Eleven rosettes. The logbook has said eleven since 1902, when it said *eleven, as before*, and before that, in 1889, in the copperplate, *ferns xi*. This is the fact about Number Six that unsettles the practical mind more than the warmth of Number Two or the mass in the flue, because a colony that neither grows nor dies nor spreads is not behaving like a colony. Eleven is not a population. Eleven is a *number being maintained*.

### Tenders

And then there is the one who comes down.

There has always been one. The chain is not official and has not been for eighty years; the works were absorbed, the company that absorbed them was nationalised, the nationalised body was broken and sold, and somewhere in that four-fold shuffle Number Six fell out of the schedules entirely and became a thing that nobody owns and one person attends. The key gets handed over in a pub, in a kitchen, at a funeral. It is a key to nothing — the grating has never been locked — and it is handed over anyway, because that is how it was done to the one handing it over.

The tender goes down four or five times a year, and nobody has ever been able to say why with a straight face.

What they do: they take the low way along the north channel on hands and knees, because the middle way is for people with something to carry and the catwalk is for people who have not learned. They put a palm on Number Two and count four. They put a palm on One, Three, Four. They feel their way to the No. 3 header and find the gauge by the shape of its bezel and read it with a thumbnail against the needle stop, and it is three-quarters of a bar, and it has been three-quarters of a bar for every tender there has ever been. They go to the second bay and count the rosettes with the backs of their fingers, which takes four minutes and is done with great gentleness. They go to the sump and crouch on the lip and wait, and sometimes there is a turn in the water and sometimes there is not.

Then they sit on the dry mud in the dark with their back against a pier, in a room at the temperature of the inside of a body, and they stay there for between twenty minutes and two hours, and they could not tell you what that part is for.

Then they write in the book. There are two books now. The old one is a ledger, and its pages are swollen shut from *1911* onward, and its entries before that decline over four decades from full columns of pressures and temperatures, to sentences, to words. *Level. Level. Eleven. Warm as ever. Nothing wanting.* The new one is a school exercise book kept in a tin on a shelf bricked into the second pier, written in pencil because ink dies down here, and it says much the same things in five hands. *Level. Eleven. 3/4. Quiet. Two eels seen.*

They come up with their clothes wet through and their palms smelling of pennies and they are unusually calm for the rest of that day, and their people notice it, and say nothing.

### The Rule

You do not bring flame down the throat of Number Six.

The reason is lost. It survives as a rule with the confidence of a rule that once had a very good reason and has since become better than its reason. It is taught in one sentence, at the grating, before the first descent, and the teaching does not include a why, because the teacher was not given one either.

The ledger has a gap. The first pages — the founding, the commissioning, whatever was written between 1871 and 1884 — are not rotted away. They are *gone*, cut or gnawed close to the gutter, and the stubs are clean and dry and a little chewed. The earliest surviving page begins mid-sentence at the top, and the half-sentence reads:

*—took the lamp up to the flue, and I will not again, and I have told Dowsett he is not to*

and then the copperplate resumes, three lines down, with a column of pressures.

### The Thing in the Flue

Above Number One, high in the cold brick flue that has drawn nothing since the fuel road was bricked across, reachable by an arm through a broken sight-glass port, there is a mass the size of a hat.

It is paper. Where a flake has come away — it has come away once, in living memory, and the tender who got it brought it up in a tobacco tin — the layers are grey and fine and laid in curving courses like a wasp's comb, and stiffened through with the same mineral that is drowning the pipework, so that the outer shell is half-limestone and the inner leaves are still soft. And on the flake, under a lens, in a hand you have seen in the ledger, are the letters *ing house, No. 6, this day comm*.

It is warm. This is the whole of the problem. The flue is cold, the brick around it is cold, there is no line into it and nothing near it that runs, and the mass is at the temperature of the inside of your wrist, and has been for four generations, and has grown in that time by about a quarter of an inch.

There is no opening. There is no traffic in or out; a hundred and fifty years of tenders sitting in the dark with nothing to do but listen would have heard traffic. Nothing has ever been seen to leave it and nothing has ever been seen to arrive.

Hold your palm six inches away and do not touch it — nobody touches it; that is not a rule, it is simply a thing nobody does — and for the first several seconds you feel an even warmth, the way you feel a warmth from any warm thing. Then the warmth stops being even. It organises. It comes in a faint patterning across the skin of your palm, hot and less hot, moving, as though the shape of your hand were being gone over slowly and from several directions at once; and the word every tender reaches for independently, in five different decades, without having read the others, is *noticed*.

You take your hand back. The patterning stops. The warmth goes even again, and stays even, and will be even in eleven years when you or the one after you comes and holds a palm up to it.

### What Lives Here

So: four eels that have declined to become adults, nine hundred moths that eat the hair out of the walls, an unchanging eleven of cold white stalks, half a million hoppers in the mud, a person who comes down a ladder four times a year for no reason they can defend, and something in a cold flue that is warm, and made of the record of this place, and has eaten the reason for the one rule.

And under all of it the hum that a man attributed to the works in 1889 and a woman attributed to the substation in 1954 and which predates both, sitting in your sternum, unlocated.

You can arrange these as a list of tenants, and the list is accurate, and it is the wrong shape. The right shape is this: Number Six does not contain its inhabitants. It *is* metabolising at their rates. A drop every eleven minutes. A valve every fortnight. Four degrees a year, out of phase with the sky. An inch a decade. A quarter-inch in four generations. Three-quarters of a bar, held, against nothing, for longer than the firm that made the gauge has existed as a name on a door. The eels feel the room; the moths walk the room; the tender reads the room with a thumbnail and writes *level* in pencil and sits in the dark until the room has finished doing whatever it is that being sat in does for it.

And something up in the cold flue keeps the count.

That is the honest answer, and it is why people who have gone down go quiet when you ask. Not *what lives there* — the eels, the moths, the eleven, yes, fine. The thing they cannot say at a normal volume is that all of it is one slow animal that got its body from a foundry in Dudley and its patience from the ground, and that when you climb down twenty-two hot rungs into it you are not an intruder and not a guest.

You are a fortnight's disturbance. You are a bright fast smear of heat in a room that will take fourteen days to absorb you.

And you were noted.
