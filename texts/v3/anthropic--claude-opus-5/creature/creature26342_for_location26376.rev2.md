## The Smelter

Start with the largest, because it is the easiest to see and the hardest to understand.

It has no name for itself and no need of one. Call it a smelter. Six legs, columnar, each ending in a pad of the same mineral plate as its back, self-grinding, regrown from beneath as it wears — so that a footfall is a slow press and a slight rotation, and what it leaves is a polished disc in the pavement the width of a millstone, the stones beneath it seated a little deeper than before, their varnish scuffed to pale. The discs last about three hundred years. A line of them, faintly brighter than the ground, running across the grain to nowhere, is the most common trace of a large animal on this plateau, and you can date it by eye if you know the varnish: white-pale, this decade; grey, last century; barely readable, your great-great-grandfather's lifetime, if you had one here.

The intake is underneath, a transverse slot between the forelegs, with a rasping palate that takes pavement gravel in mouthfuls of eight or ten kilos. Inside, the load is sorted by density, fermented, leached, and stripped. What it does not want comes out behind as a tidy windrow of cleaned gravel, graded fine to coarse, two hand-spans high, paler than the surrounding pavement because every stone in it has been turned. This is the truest map of the animal's passage. It re-varnishes at the standard rate. A smelter's track fades in exactly the time the plateau takes to forget anything.

The body between intake and tailings is mostly culture. Chambers of bacteria, held at a chemistry the animal maintains the way you maintain a temperature, with the same lack of attention. Running the length of the trunk is the keel: a rolled membrane, hundreds of square metres of it if you could unwind it, held at a standing potential against the plate of the back. Metal comes out of solution and lays down on the keel in a sheet. The sheet grows faster than it can adhere. It is extruded laterally, through slots between the flank plates, and it peels.

That is the whole industry. There is no intention in it anywhere. The animal is not making foil; the animal is failing, continuously and at scale, to keep its own metal attached to itself.

The vanes along the spine are fog-combs, radiators, and electrodes at once, which is the sort of economy you get when a lineage has had a very long time and no competition. During the tide they stream. The rest of the year they stand dry and catch the evening gradient, and if you were standing close on a still clear night — you would not be, nothing lets you be, but if — the hair on your forearms would lift and stay lifted.

It has no eyes to speak of. Four light-patches, enough to know lid from ground, enough to know it has not fallen over. It navigates with its feet. Ground that has been plated — joints packed with bright foil, oxide flour worked into the tuff — has a different conductivity, a different taste through the pads, than ground that has not. The animal drifts away from richness the way you drift away from a smell. It does not remember where it has been. It does not need to. It has written where it has been onto the landscape in metal, and it reads the writing with its feet, and the writing fades at a rate that sets the period of its circuit.

That is the mechanism. Four hundred animals, or six, each walking away from its own exhaust and everyone else's, on a surface that resets in fifty years. The interlocking is not cooperation and not territory. It is four hundred blind avoidances meshing into a pattern that covers a continent without gaps and without overlap, and has done so, with the same efficiency, for longer than the lichen maps around it have existed.

## How long

Here is the number that reorganises everything else.

One smelter stops, roughly, every three hundred years. There are five hundred of them. Do the arithmetic and you get a mean life of a hundred and fifty thousand years, which is not a life span in any sense you use the phrase. It is an accident rate. They do not age. They are not killed. The culture crashes, rarely; a leg fractures on a joint, rarer; one walks into a filled playa and sinks to the hock in clay it cannot get purchase in, rarest of all and the only death on the plateau that could be called a mistake.

Which means: the individuals out there now were out there when the current drifts began to form. An animal you might see this afternoon has walked its loop three thousand times. It has crossed its own tailings, unrecognised, ten thousand times. It predates the species of lichen growing on the stones it is eating.

They are not really a population. They are closer to a climate with legs.

## How there come to be more of them

Two smelters may live their whole enormous lives without coming within thirty kilometres of one another, and still reproduce.

On still clear evenings, when the plankton goes up, the smelters release with it. Gametes, if you want the word — charged, filamented, no larger than the rice-grain aeroplankton they rise beside, indistinguishable from it to anything but each other. They ride the potential gradient up to the underside of the lid and stay there, in the inversion, in numbers no one has ever estimated, for months. They are eaten by kites in vast quantities and it makes no difference.

Then the fog tide climbs the escarpment and the smelters stop walking and stand for nine days with their combs streaming, and the cloud is combed. Whatever was aloft comes down in the condensate, runs the grooves, goes inward with the water.

Fertilisation happens in the rain that isn't rain. It happens inside the animal, in the dark, in a wet channel between two plates, and it has nothing to do with meeting.

What comes of it is shed a decade later as a thickened scale — a seed-plate, heavier than foil, carrying a bud and an inoculum of the parent's culture — which falls into a lee drift and lies there looking exactly like what is around it. For forty years it is a stack that is not a stack. It eats oxide flour and its mother's old leavings, which is to say it grows on garbage, because it cannot make its own yet. It has no legs. The legs are the last thing.

Then one year it stands up, and there is one more.

You could walk over a dozen of them in a day's crossing and register nothing but drift.

## The passengers

Everything that big and that slow is real estate.

On the plates, especially along the flanks where the newest foil comes through, there are grazers: flattened, plated themselves, the size of a thumbnail, cropping the bright metal before it can peel. Their whole world is one animal. There is no second world within reach; the next smelter is a week's walk for something with legs a millimetre long, across open pavement that would kill them in an afternoon.

So they go up. In the evening, on the gradient, spinning a filament and letting go — off a moving mountain, into the air, with no control over where they land, and the odds are catastrophic and the lineage is fifty million years old.

Deeper in, between the overlapping plates of the back, where condensate is held for months after the tide, there is a wet community that never sees the lid: pale, blind, dense, feeding on the animal's own leaked chemistry. Tidy, actually. There is no harm being done. The smelter has no immune defence worth the name against any of them, because nothing any of them could do would matter on the timescale at which the smelter exists.

## The drift, in section

Cut a drift with a spade — you will not, nothing here has a spade, but imagine it — and the wall of the cut is stratified by both age and appetite.

*Top, bright.* Loose sheets, dry, clicking. Foil-lice, flat as their medium, sliding between leaves. This layer is hostile: it freezes hard at night, it gets blown, it is where the kites feed when they descend. Fast, by local standards. Things here live eleven years.

*A hand down, dull.* The metal has begun to go. Mites in the millions per square metre — orange, translucent, some cutting pits in the first oxide bloom, some eating the cutters, some eating nothing but the waste of the cutters and doing very well on it. Nine species of foil-louse graded by depth so precisely that you could sample blind and know how deep you had reached by which one came out.

*Two hands down, green-red.* The crumble. No structure left, no sheets, a rustling dampness like rotten leather. This is where the mass is. The springtail-thing lives its entire life in the water film on the underside of a single leaf-layer here, travels the length of the drift, never surfaces, and would die of the open air in ten minutes.

*A metre down, dark.* True earth. The only earth. Compacted oxide flour, dead generations, and a permanent humidity — because the joints beneath the drift hold water for years after a tide, and the drift is the lid on that. Below this everything is burrowers.

## The majority

The burrowers are the plateau, in the sense that they are most of what is alive on it.

Soft, segmented, the length of a forearm, blind, unpigmented, slow. They follow the water films through the pavement joints, and a joint is not a tunnel — it is a crack a few millimetres wide that they wedge and widen with their whole bodies, working forward over months. A colony is not a society. It is a set of siblings that entered the same joint at the same time and have been travelling the same route ever since, spaced along it, each one alone for its whole life and never more than a metre from the next.

They move on a fifty-year lag behind the smelters, because that is how long foil takes to become food. A smelter passes overhead in a single afternoon, and half a century later the ground beneath its old track thickens with them. The greatest animal migration on this world is a smear of blind soft things following, at one generation's delay, a path walked by something they will never encounter and have no concept of.

They feel it coming, though. Everything feels the smelters coming. Footfall conducted through tuff, one strike every few minutes, detectable for two days ahead of arrival. It has no meaning. It is not a threat and not an opportunity and there is nothing to be done about it.

The burrowers themselves tap. Slowly, one strike a minute or so, and there is a predator down there that listens for it — long, hard-plated, patient, the only thing on the plateau that could be called a hunter at a scale you would notice. It eats twice a long year, at the tide, when everything below is awake and moving. Between meals it does not hunt, does not move, does not appreciably breathe. Its heart runs at something like four beats an hour.

## The air

Two circulations, and they alternate.

On clear still evenings, the ground is charged and the plankton goes up: the rice-grain invertebrates on their silk, the smelter gametes, the thumbnail grazers gambling on a new mountain, spores, flakes, dust that is alive. The air acquires that texture — a faint moving grain, like looking through slow falling ash — and it is the only time this place looks busy.

The kites come after them. A membrane a metre across, a body the size of a fist, no flapping, ever: they tilt into the gradient and hold, mouths open, sliding the long shallow lift for days. Eleven days aloft is ordinary. They drink rime off their own leading edges in the morning. They mate up there, incidentally, without landing, and it looks like two sheets of paper briefly interfering.

From up there they see the thing nobody on the ground sees: the drifts as brightness. The whole plateau as a field of pale and dull, the bright streaks being new work and the dull being old, so that the entire three-thousand-year history of smelter traffic is laid out as a tonal map, and the kites hunt it, because bright means recent means the grazers are launching.

They are the only dispersal service on the plateau. In their fur — they have something like fur, a low bronze nap on the underside — ride mites, spores, eggs, whole lineages that cannot otherwise cross forty kilometres of open pavement. A kite is a ferry that does not know it is a ferry.

When the fog tide arrives, all of this stops. No gradient, wet membranes, no lift. The kites come down and sit in the drifts with their wings furled like collapsed tents, and do not eat for nine days, and lose a third of their weight, and it is fine, it happens twice a long year, it is what they are built for. The air empties. The ground fills. Everything below comes up to the joint mouths to drink.

## The waiters

Some things here are not alive most of the time.

The brine shrimp in the playa clay, obviously — eggs by the uncountable billion, dormant since the last filling, with a hatch rate low enough that a single filling never exhausts the bank. When a basin does hold water, for its decade, there is also a filter-feeder that comes out of the clay as a desiccated disc the size of a coin and inflates into something the size of a plate, and breeds continuously for nine years, and dries. The kites come for that decade from a hundred kilometres away and it is the only crowding this world ever does.

And then there are the corpse specialists.

They exist as eggs in the pavement joints, spread thin across the entire plateau, waiting for an event that occurs once every three hundred years within reach of any given point — and by "reach" one means the distance a kite can carry them, which is why they have hooks. Some lineages have been in the egg for eleven hundred years. Most of any generation never hatch at all. The ones that do have four decades of appalling plenty and then it is over for another few centuries.

## The city

A smelter dies over about eight months, and the first thing that happens is the only broadcast this world has.

It has held a standing voltage for a hundred and fifty thousand years. Now the culture is gone and the field decays — not sharply, but over weeks, a slow fading hole in a background that everything with a field organ has been reading its entire life. Kites feel it from very far off. They do not understand it. They turn toward it because the gradient bends, and that is enough.

Then: fifty tonnes of concentrated metal and organic matter, lying on the pavement, in a landscape where the richest thing normally available is a fist-sized cushion plant full of its own dead.

The kites arrive in weeks, and unload their passengers. The plate-lifters come next, wedging into the joints of the back as the plates loosen. Within five years the flanks are open and the culture chambers are a wet anaerobic country. Within thirty the burrowers have found it from below and are coming up through the pavement joints in numbers nobody could count, and the ground for a hundred metres around is warm — actually, measurably warm — which is a thing that happens nowhere else here.

It takes two hundred years to dismantle. Everything on the plateau that specialises in abundance is present, and almost nothing else, and the whole of it is silent, and at the end it is over and the specialists go back into the joints as eggs for three centuries.

What stays is the keel. A rolled sheet of near-pure metal that nothing can eat, lying where it fell, oxidising at the standard ungenerous rate. Three thousand years of it. Beneath and around it the soil is, by local standards, obscene: an oval two hundred metres long where the cushion plants grow in a continuous sward instead of scattered singletons, where the bronze felt is ankle-deep, where there are species found nowhere else on the plateau, because they arose here, in isolation, in an island of fertility surrounded by nine hundred kilometres of nothing.

There are perhaps forty such islands out there, of forty different ages, the oldest barely legible as a change in the lichen, the youngest still faintly warm. Nothing connects them but kites.

The entire biodiversity of this world is generated by the occasional accidental death of something that almost never dies.

## What lives here

Ask it plainly and the answer is: one process, in about six hundred bodies, feeding about nine thousand species, most of which are underground and blind.

The smelters are not the inhabitants. They are the weather. They walk their meshed circuits, avoiding their own exhaust, plating a continent in a metal they cannot keep, and everything else — the lice, the mites, the springtail-things, the burrowers in their hundred-mile joints, the hunters at four beats an hour, the kites sliding the gradient with their mouths open, the eggs in the clay — lives downstream of that failure, at one remove or nine.

There is no one here to notice this. The nearest thing to attention on the plateau is a blind animal reading the conductivity of the ground through its feet, and what it is reading is a message it wrote itself, fifty years ago, meaning nothing except: *already done, go elsewhere.*

It has been getting that message, and obeying it, and finding elsewhere, for longer than your species has existed.
