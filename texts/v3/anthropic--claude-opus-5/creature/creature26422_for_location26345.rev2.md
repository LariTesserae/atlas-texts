# What Lives on the Thatch

Begin with the ear. Everything here is an ear first and a body afterwards.

Eyes are cheap on the Thatch and mostly abandoned. Below the surface there is nothing to see and six hundred metres of things to hear; above it there is plenty to see and nothing worth looking at, four Standers and a shimmer, all day, for thirty kilometres. So the eye has gone the way of the thumb in a creature that does not climb. The Weft is full of animals with skin over the sockets, animals with the sockets grown shut, animals whose ancestors' eyes are visible only as two paler patches of hide, like the ghost of a habit. And in exchange: tympana on the knees. Tympana on the flanks. Combs of stiff hair along the spine that read pressure the way you read a printed page. Grazers that navigate by the timbre of their own chewing. A whole continent of listening, inside a noise that never stops, which is the trick of it — they are not listening *through* the Draw, they are listening *to* it, the way you look at the air and not through it when you are watching for rain.

## The lower citizenry

The blind pale grazers come in sheets, thousands at a time, moving over the inner walls like a spill of milk going uphill. Hand-sized, soft, with a rasping mouthpart that scours the fungal fur off the cane and leaves a bright clean track behind. They are the bottom of everything. Every predator down there is a specialist in the sound of a grazer sheet changing direction.

Above them, in every sense, the things with too many legs and an apologetic manner — and it is worth saying that the apology is real and not a figure of speech. They go stiff and low when anything larger passes, and they raise the first two pairs of legs and hold them folded, and they wait, and then they resume. They are one of the few animals in the Weft a child is allowed to handle. They do not bite. They have nothing to bite with. They eat what has already been eaten twice.

Rope-snakes in the ceiling lattices, hunting by the dead spot their own bodies make in the ambient roar — a rope-snake knows where a thing is by the shape of the quiet it casts, which is the same principle everyone on the Thatch is frightened of at larger scale. They are unhurried, extremely long, and warm to the touch.

The choir, in their cells, forty years of packed nymphs stridulating from inside the wood. This is not an animal so much as a condition of the building. You lay your palm on a gallery wall and the wall is going, steadily, like gravel down a chute, and it has been going since before your grandmother, and it will go for another eleven years, and then one summer the whole stacked backlog will come out at once and the Thatch will do the loudest thing it does.

And the trumpeters: three metres at the shoulder, loose-hided, tapir-ish, blind as stones, levering themselves through the lower galleries with a patience that looks like grief. They are the road-makers. A trumpeter widens a passage by centuries of shoulder-work, and everything else in the Weft — grazer, predator, person — uses what the trumpeters have opened. A gallery you can walk upright in is a gallery a trumpeter has been using since before there were people on the plateau. The dead of the road-makers become the husk-houses. This is not lost on anybody.

## The islands in the air

Each Stander is its own nation and its own mistake. A hundred and forty metres of column with a mop of green on top, and up in that mop, a population of clawed, loud, territorial, intensely political animals that have been isolated from every other mop for longer than the isolation makes sense. No two Standers have the same crown-folk. They differ in size, in cry, in the number of toes, in whether they hoard. They cannot cross the nap; the Draw would cook them and the surface offers nothing. So they quarrel at very high volume with the only neighbours they will ever have, which are their own relatives, forever.

A tapper working a Stander knows the crown-folk of that particular Stander as individuals — not as a species, as a cast list. They steal. You do not leave a bright thing unattended at a node-room. Some households keep one, taken young; it never becomes tame, it learns to fetch out of pure larceny, and when it dies you carry it out and lay it on the nap with everything else.

## The people

Not many. Scattered. They will not add up to a civilisation and they have never wanted to.

They are short and wide through the foot, with long grasping toes and a walk that looks, to a rim-dweller, like wading. This is the nap handing them forward and them accepting the offer. The chest is deep. The hands are ordinary. The face is where the work shows.

Every one of them can clamp the ear. It is a knack, taught between the second and fourth year, drilled by mothers with a drum and a piece of dried gut, and a child who has not got it by five is a child who will live Below. The clamp is not conscious after a while; it sets in at the third hour like a tide going out, and comes off at dusk, and in between a Thatch person is functionally deaf to everything above a certain pressure and can still, somehow, hear water moving inside a cane. Around fifty the muscle stops holding. This is called going short. A short tapper works mornings and the Dew Hour and is not pitied, because the alternative to going short is not going short, and everyone knows what that means.

Second: the teeth. Water-reading is done by bone. A tapper carries a bite-rod — horn, sometimes a shard of the greenish glass, the length of a forearm — presses one end to the cane and grips the other in the back teeth, and closes the eyes, which are useless anyway, and *tastes* the column. Fat and slow and wobbling is good. Thin and bright is deadwood. A knock with a delay in it is a wall the cane has already built against somebody else's tap, forty years ago, and you move on. The left molars of a working tapper wear to a notch by thirty. The jaw goes lopsided by forty-five. You can identify a retired tapper across a camp by the way the face sits.

Third: the two kinds. Above-folk walk the surface, tend Standers, comb fog, cut cairns, and are the colour of the nap. Below-folk live in the Weft and are the colour of the inside of a shell, and a great many of them have never been up, and consider the surface roughly the way you would consider standing on the outside of the hull. Both are the people of the Thatch. They marry across and argue about it. Children are Below-people until they are seven regardless, because the Weft is the only nursery that exists.

What they all share is about eighty words of chest-talk: a touch language, palm to sternum, used at noon when the Draw makes the mouth a decoration. *Water. No water. Come. Don't. Hollowing — go back. Three of us. One of us now.* It drifts less than speech does, because speech is used with your own people and chest-talk is used with strangers, and strangers are the thing that keeps a language honest. Two parties meeting on the nap at noon stand close enough to touch, hands flat on each other's breastbones, having a conversation that from any distance looks like mutual consolation.

They eat grazer, nymph, fungus, sap, and the bitter mineral crust that dries on a vent-mound collar, which is why the collars are polished smooth not only by shoulders. They work horn, gut, bone, cane, and that rough green glass. Their entire material culture is plumbing. Their proudest object is a spigot. Their cisterns are heirlooms and have names.

## Fire

There is no law about fire.

There are laws about killing a stem, and elaborate customary law about tapping another family's cane, and a quantity of etiquette about cairns. About fire there is nothing, because law is a thing you make for offences that can be survived, and the Thatch is three hundred metres of compressed dead cane over a plain of hot brown nothing, and the rim cliffs are black lignin that burns like slate has no business burning.

So fire lives in a hollowed node with a horn hood and a water bowl beneath it, tended by one named person per camp who does nothing else, who is fed by others, who sleeps beside it, and who is not spoken to while working. Children are shown fire once, formally, in a way that is remembered for life. There is no word in any Thatch dialect for a fire out of doors. The concept exists, in the way the concept of drowning exists in a desert: as a thing you could describe if pressed and would rather not.

## How they count

By choirs. Every fortieth year the nymphs come up, and for one season the Weft is unusable — the galleries pack solid with emerging adults and the stridulation reaches a level that kills the old and deafens the unlucky — and the entire population of the Thatch, Above-folk and Below-folk alike, goes up onto the surface and lives out a year under the Draw.

This is the Exile. It is the only event on the plateau that involves everybody. Camps that have no contact for decades find themselves sharing a Stander's shade. Chest-talk gets a workout and comes back changed. Marriages happen. Feuds are settled or begun. There is a glut of food beyond anything the rest of life offers, because the emerged choir is fat and stupid and lies about in windrows, and for one year the people are gorged and burnt and sleeping in the open and half-mad with noise, and then the choir dies off and everyone goes back down, and the galleries are ankle-deep in husk for a decade.

A Thatch person's age is given in choirs. *She has seen two.* Almost nobody sees three; those who do are carried up for the third, and it is considered a good death to have it happen during the Exile, out in the shimmer, where the mat can get at you quickly.

## The Quiet

Everyone has a rule about the quiet places and no two rules agree.

The people of the eastern combs will not enter a hollowing but will walk its edge sunwise, once, and leave a glass bead. The Long Round families will not approach within a stone's throw and turn the head aside, on the grounds that a hollowing that has not seen you cannot mind you. There is a lineage near the northern Standers that claims hollowings are the mat's mouths and sends a portion of every tap into one, ritually, at Dew Hour. Others hold that they are simply lids over rot, which is what they demonstrably are, and that superstition is a thing the ignorant do, and *those* families still send somebody the long way round, and cannot tell you why.

Two facts are agreed. The first is that people go into hollowings and do not come out. The second is more troubling and less discussed: that things have been seen leaving them, at Dew Hour, in the gleam, and that whatever leaves a hollowing leaves no track in the nap.

Everything leaves a track in the nap. That is what the nap is for.

## The Laying, and the Count

There is no ground, so there is no burying. The dead are carried out to open nap, away from any route, and laid face up, and the family arranges the hands. The arrangement is a question. Families have their own: fingers laced, palms open, one hand over the heart and one held out. The mat closes over in a season and the cane grows through, and in thirty or forty years the plateau extrudes the shape, a third too large, hollow, fused, ribbed, standing alone in the shimmer twenty kilometres from anything.

And the hands are always wrong. Always. Never the question that was asked. Some say this is the answer and cannot be read; most say the hands are the mat's business and not ours; a very few read them, and are left alone about it, and are not invited to things.

Here is what is done with them. A sound casting is opened — a door cut low, the rope stored, the rim smoothed — and then it is struck. Not idly: a padded maul, at the breastbone, where the shell is thinnest and the whole hollow speaks. Low frequency travels through two thousand metres of wet fibre the way nothing else does. A struck casting can be felt sixty kilometres out, in the teeth, at the correct hour.

The correct hour is the dusk slack, the twenty minutes when the Draw falls away and before the night-things start. And in that twenty minutes, every night, all across the Thatch, the Count goes out. It begins near the centre and moves outward: each station strikes its own name, three beats and a figure, and waits, and the next takes it up, and the pattern runs to the rim families and turns and comes back. Children learn the Count as a song before they learn speech. Any person anywhere on the plateau, lying in a node-room with a palm on the wall, knows by the end of that twenty minutes who is still there.

A station that misses three nights is walked to.

So the arrangement is this. The people of the Thatch are few and scattered and will never be a nation, and they are held together, nightly, by the amplified voices of their own dead grandparents, standing out in the empty nap where they came up, hollow, oversized, hands in a position nobody chose. The living are named by the dead. The network grows when a casting arrives, which is a thing that happens perhaps twice in a lifetime, and is a considerable event — the plateau has gained a word — and there is a small ceremony, and the new voice is tried, and everyone within range learns what it sounds like, and it is added to the song.

Trumpeters rub against the castings. Mites nest in the shoulders. Weather gets in.

## And the third school

The tappers say the resemblance is the resemblance of a footprint. The others point at the castings of things never lost here — plains animals, shapes of shapes.

There is a third position, held by nobody respectable, which observes that the plateau has been listening to the Count every night for a very long time, through the same fibre that carries it; that the mat grows around whatever falls on it; and that a continent which has spent ten thousand years in perfect balance, growing nothing and losing nothing, has had a great deal of spare capacity and no obvious use for it.

They say this at night, in the galleries, where it is warm and clicking and it smells of animals. Everyone tells them it is a story for children.

Then the fog comes down and the nap runs black and ticks, and everything that lives above the surface goes out to drink, and by the second hour of light it is gone, and at the third hour the Draw comes up out of the floor of the world and drowns the whole argument out.
