## The forty-three

Ask any of them how many people live in the Room and you will get the number before you finish the question. They all carry it. It is not a census figure to them; it is closer to a pulse. When it changes — a birth, a death, a departure — the change is spoken aloud once on every level, by whoever is nearest, in the same flat delivery used for shim readings. *Forty-four.* *Forty-two.* Then it is not spoken again for years.

They are small people, most of them, and they are small in a particular way: short in the shin, long in the arm, thick through the shoulder and hand. Two centuries of stairs. Nobody designed this and nobody has noticed it, because they have no one to compare themselves to. The gait is the most visible thing about them — a rolling, toe-first placement, weight always kept over the back foot, learned before speech on wet stone with an edge that opens skin. They do not walk slowly. They walk as if the floor is a question they have already answered.

Hands: short nails, always. Knuckles scarred across the backs in the same pattern in everyone over fifteen — scrub-scars, from pushing a brush into a fracture face that has been waiting eighty million years for something soft. They call them wall hands. A child's first set is a small occasion; nobody makes anything of it, but the ones they get on the day are treated with a particular ointment kept for that purpose and no other.

They are pale, and the paleness is uniform, because the light in the Room comes from everywhere at once and casts almost no shadow on a face. This has consequences in how they read each other. Expression here is not done in the eyes and brows, which stay evenly lit and legible all day; it is done in the hands and the angle of the head, and a resident can tell from thirty metres down a bridge whether a person wants company. They are very rarely wrong.

Their hearing is the thing an outsider would find uncanny, if an outsider existed. They know the four hundred and six threads by voice. Not as a party trick — as literacy. A child of nine can tell you that the west wall has begun its weep from the change in the background texture, the way you might notice a fan cut out in another room. When a thread alters pitch, someone comes. There is no alarm system. There does not need to be.

## Two of everything

The Room does not dry. So: two shirts, two under-layers, two pairs of the felted boots, one on and one hanging. The hanging one is never dry either; it is merely less wet, and that is the whole of the domestic economy. They own little else. A typical residence — four rooms cut into the wall behind a stone face, nineteen degrees, dark, low — contains a bed, a lamp, a press for clothes, a shelf, and between eight and forty objects. Forty is a lot. Forty is remarked upon.

There is exactly one dry place. On level six, above the main sink exhaust, is a chamber the size of a large kitchen where the reclaimed heat is run through wool racks before it goes up the flue. It is thirty-one degrees in there and it smells of hot lanolin and iron, and everyone in the Room passes through it at some point in every day, and so it is the parlour, the court, the gossip exchange, the place where arguments are finished. They call it the Dry Room. Visitors — there are none — would be told about it within a minute. It is the only room in the Room that has ever been described as cheerful.

They eat from the Larder, on level seven: eleven tanks, five running, fed on the difference in temperature between the residences and the stone. The product is a pale cake with a texture between bread and curd, and the flavour is adjusted by six powders whose recipes are held by two families and are the subject of the only sustained economic rivalry in the settlement. Children fish the cisterns for amphipods with bent wire and eat them raw, translucent, and are told off with a severity that has been theatre for four generations. A bowl of boiled amphipod appears at every funeral. Nobody has ever explained why and nobody asks, because the answer is obviously *because it always has*, and that is a complete answer here.

## What they do

Everything in the Room is maintained and nothing in the Room is made. That distinction runs through their lives like the fold between the blueschist and the gneiss.

The Survey is three people and a nine-day cycle. Sennet, who is sixty-eight, walks it with the lunchbox machine and has walked it for forty years, and can name any jack-plate on any level from the sound of its screw. Her two juniors do the levels she has decided she no longer does well, which is not the same as the levels she can no longer reach. After the walk the numbers go up on the board at level four. Crews turn screws. Two turns here, half a turn at the foot of the east stair, take a shim out of bridge six. It takes four people most of a day and the town is level again, or level enough, which is the only kind of level available inside something that is growing.

Scrubbing is everyone, on rotation, one day in eleven. Brush, scraper, a bucket of cistern water, a harness. It is cold, it is tedious, it ruins the hands, and it is the oldest job because it is the one that is actually load-bearing: a hold-fast becomes a root becomes a crack becomes water in the wrong place becomes a slab, and there is a slab down at level five with a scar on it like broken bottle glass which killed two people in the year 74 and which nobody has moved. They eat lunch on it during the scrub rotation. It is a good table.

Titles have outlived their jobs, and the Room keeps the titles. The lamplighter lights nothing; the walls do that. The lamplighter's apprentice — Duill, who is twelve, who is the youngest person in the Room by five years — spends her days on the driplines with a notebook, reading gauges, because the lamplighter's office absorbed hydrology sometime around year 90 and never changed its name. There is a smith on level three with a foundry that has not been fired since year 191. He oils the tools on the ninth day. He is not idle; he repairs, he grinds, he re-cuts threads on old bolts, and he knows the difference between his work and the work the foundry was built for, and that knowledge sits in him the way the cold sits in the stone.

## The count

Here is what they are actually doing, underneath the screws and the brushes.

There are two hundred and nine braces in the Room. There are thirty-one spares, in a rack on level six, in boxes that have not changed since they were filled. Nobody can make a thirty-second. The anchors they can machine, the cylinders they cannot, and the difference between those two facts is the shape of their future.

They know the number. Everyone over ten knows the number. It is not discussed in the way you would expect. It is discussed the way a family discusses an old roof: with precision, without panic, and with a running argument about whether the rate of failure over the last century is a rate at all or just a set of accidents that happened to be spaced out. The Survey's instrument is one of three; two work. The cold cabinet on level six, which is the reason forty-three people in a closed space do not have the faces of forty-three cousins, is a little over half empty, and there are records for it, and the records are read by exactly the people who need to read them and by no one else, out of a tact that has hardened into law.

So the Room is a machine for staying the same shape, and the machine is finite, and its inhabitants have a very exact sense of how finite. They respond to this by turning screws. There is no other available response, and they long ago stopped treating that as a tragedy. A man on level two puts it like this: the mountain is going up at three millimetres a year and will do that for a million years and we have thirty-one braces, so the question was always settled; the only thing anybody ever gets to decide is what the place looks like while it lasts. Then he will offer you tea, and the tea will be better than you expect, because they have had two hundred years to get the powders right.

## The ledger

One book. It is kept on level four, in a dry box, and it is written in by one person at a time — currently Ardis, who is fifty-one, who took it from her aunt, who took it from a man who was not related to anyone.

Everything goes in the same book in the same format: ticks, with time and magnitude; the ice-fall, with the date and the weight if anyone bothered to weigh it; chough-days; births; deaths; departures. A tick is three lines. A death is three lines. This is not coldness. It is the deepest thing they believe, which is that the Room is one system and a person leaving it is an event of the same kind as five metres of ice letting go — real, loud, scheduled or not, and afterwards the geometry needs checking.

The ledger has a flaw at its root and the flaw has produced the only theology the Room possesses. It records four thousand and six chough-days across one hundred and eighty years. That is twenty-two a year. Everyone alive has seen perhaps nine in a good summer. So either the early keepers counted something else, or there were more birds then, or the first fifty years were exaggerated by people with too little to write down. The Twenty-Two hold that the outside used to be richer and is thinning; the Nine hold that the early ledger is simply wrong and that the world beyond the lid is exactly as full as it has ever been. Nobody can settle it. Both sides use the same evidence. It is argued about in the Dry Room, at length, with great seriousness, by people who have known each other their entire lives, and it functions precisely as such arguments function everywhere, which is to say it gives them something to be on opposite sides of that costs nothing.

Departures are written the same way as everything else. *Year 161, day 4. Corwen Arvo, 23, out by the joint, 0550. Weather fair.* There are nineteen such entries. None has a second line. They all understand that the joint under the lid can be opened from the inside by anyone who knows where to put their hands, and every one of them knows where to put their hands, and that is the fact that makes the Room a home rather than a box. Nobody speaks of the nineteen with contempt or with grief. On the anniversary of a departure the departed's family does nothing at all, and everyone else is careful to also do nothing, elaborately.

## The children

Six of them, spread across eleven years, which is not enough for a proper set of games and so they have improvised a culture of two and three: rope work on the stairs, a counting game with shims, an elaborate territorial pretence involving the unused hundred and thirty rooms on levels one and two, where nobody has lived since before their grandparents and the doors still open.

They draw the chough. All of them, constantly, on slate, badly. It is the only creature any of them has seen that has been in both places. A child's first chough-sighting is an event on the level of a first tooth, and Duill still remembers hers — the bird walked the level-five railing with its unearned confidence, took a spoon, and went up through the slot, and she has not been the same since, in the way you are not the same after you first understand that the ceiling has an outside.

What they are taught, early and thoroughly: the weight of the chandelier. Why you do not shout. Where your hands go on the stairs. How to read a dripline gauge. How to sit still and listen to the threads for an hour and report what changed. That last one is a lesson and also, eventually, a job.

## The dead

They go down.

There is no burial in the usual sense, because there is nothing to bury into but sorting talus, and there is no burning, because the flue is for heat and a body in the flue would be a plume that someone might notice. So the dead are carried down all seven levels by whoever is able, which at forty-three people is very nearly everyone, and laid on the talus at the north end, and left. The Room does the rest. Stones move on the ticks, a few dozen at a time. The mountain rises three millimetres a year and the floor sorts itself and within a decade there is nothing, and within a century the north end of the talus is simply the north end of the talus, containing everyone.

They do not visit. There is nowhere to visit. But people go and stand down there quite often, singly, for no stated reason, and if two arrive at once the second one turns around.

## The one who is not doing anything

Stand on the bridge at level four in the afternoon and you will count two people in a town built for three hundred. One will be Duill on her gauges.

The other will be Vahn, who is seventy-nine, who walked the Survey for thirty-one years and cannot now do the east stair, and who sits on the level-four landing with a blanket and a cup and appears to be doing nothing.

He is working. He is the best ear in the Room, and what he is doing is listening to four hundred and six threads simultaneously and waiting for one of them to change, which happens perhaps forty times a year and which he catches, on average, eleven minutes before the instruments. There is no title for this. It is not on the rotation. He does it seven hours a day and has done for eleven years, and the ledger has a dozen entries that exist because of him and do not say so.

He is also, most of the time, just sitting. The two are not separable and the Room has never asked him to separate them. Forty-three people in three hundred rooms; the emptiness is not a wound; the ratio is correct. Everyone has space to be quiet in, and the quiet is not empty, and an old man on a landing with a cup is the settlement's most sensitive instrument and also an old man on a landing with a cup.

Ask him what the Room is for and you will get the engineers' shrug — the one they all do, learned young, inherited entire — and the gesture at the floor. What the gesture means is either *that's where the cost goes* or *ask the mountain* or *nobody wrote it down,* and the fact that three answers fit one gesture is exactly why the gesture has survived two hundred years and the answer hasn't.

Then he will go back to listening, because the west wall is about to start, and it is early, and someone should note the time.
