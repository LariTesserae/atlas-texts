## Who is in the water

**Bodies**

Two hundred people, and they are all built the same way, because the water built them.

They are short and thick through the chest, with the deep barrel that comes of a childhood spent making the lungs a bigger room than they were born as. Under the skin there is a layer of fat, even on the hungry ones, laid down over the ribs and the thighs and especially across the back of the shoulders, so that a person standing on a raft in the rain steams slightly and does not shiver for a while. The grease goes on over that. Pig-fat, rendered and mixed with a little ash, worked into the shoulders and the crown of the head, so the whole town smells faintly of a cold kitchen.

Their hands are wrong for anything but here. The fingers are permanently ridged and pale at the tips, wrinkled wet or dry, and the heel of the right palm — the mallet hand — is a plate of horn a quarter-inch thick. The teeth of anyone over thirty who twists cordage have a groove worn into the front two, where the fibre has been drawn through ten thousand times. The eyes are set deep and blink slowly, and the pupils are large, and they see better at eight metres in tea-coloured light than a visitor sees on the surface at noon.

They do not sunburn, mostly, because everything comes to them through leaves; but the crown of the head and the tops of the ears are weathered like the rim, and the rest of them is the colour of the inside of a shell.

They are amphibious by four years old and useless at running. A grown man of this town, put on dry level ground, walks like a man carrying something heavy that isn't there.

**How they talk**

A sentence here is one breath long.

This is not a rule anyone teaches. It is simply that all the important conversations of a life happen in a flooded room at five metres, hanging off a mat, with the air coming from a spile in a node above the doorway. You take your mouthful, you say your piece, you pass the spile. Everything anyone has ever needed to say has had to fit inside a lungful, and so their language has evolved short — dense, front-loaded, with the verb early and the qualifications dropped. To speak past your breath is not eloquence, it is bad manners; it means the next person is waiting with an empty chest while you decorate. Their word for a windbag translates as *two-breath*.

They keep count of breaths in argument the way other people keep count of points. A judgement handed down in eleven breaths is remembered as a heavy judgement. There is a marriage-vow of exactly one breath, and a much longer form, seven breaths, used only when the families have quarrelled and everyone needs to hear the whole thing spelled out.

Above water they are shockingly loud. There is no habit of moderation in them, because above water there is nothing to moderate for; the rain is on the leaves and the frogs are going and the wind comes over the rim, and a raft-conversation is four people bellowing simultaneously at a range of two feet, all of them perfectly happy. Visitors find them abrupt underwater and deafening in the air and conclude, wrongly, that they are two different peoples.

**Names**

A person has three.

The mouth-name is given at birth and is used by perhaps six people ever — mother, siblings, the one you sleep beside. It is a soft thing and slightly embarrassing to hear out loud in company, like being seen without grease on.

The mark is a notch cut into a named column at the exact level of the water on the day you were born. It is nobody's business but yours. It drowns at the rate of a finger's width per generation, which is to say that you are young while your mark is dry and middle-aged when it is wet and old when you have to duck to see it. *A hand under* is a very old person. There are two of those at present and they are both women and they are both extremely rude.

The third name is the knock. It is a rhythm of between three and seven beats, given at about five years old when the child can hold a mallet without dropping it, and it is what the person actually *is*, socially, for the rest of their life. It is how you are summoned, discussed, blamed, invited, mourned. There are only about three hundred rhythms in circulation and they are inherited: when someone dies their knock is struck once more at the end of the death, and then rested, and then given out again some years later to a child. A child given a rhythm too soon after its holder's death is watched carefully by the old women for signs of the previous occupant arriving slowly, the way silt arrives.

They can all recognise a hand. Every person taps with an inflection nothing can disguise — a hurry in the third beat, a soft final stroke — and a listener knows not just *who* but what mood, at a mile, through a plant.

**The gap**

Sound goes faster through the cane than through the water. This is the fact the whole society is built on and none of them think about, in the way that other people do not think about the ground being solid.

A knock arrives twice. First through the stem, quick and thin and hard; then through the water, a beat later, round and soft. The interval between the two arrivals is the distance. Children are trained on it before they are trained on anything else — sent out at dusk with a grandmother who taps and asks *how far*, and cuffed gently when they guess wrong. By nine, a child can place a knock within thirty metres anywhere in the bowl. By twenty, they do it without noticing, the way you notice which room of a house a voice is in.

The consequence is that nobody here is ever lost, and nobody is ever alone, and both of these facts are experienced as pressure. All day and all night, under the frogs and the applause of the rain, every person in the basin carries a low continuous awareness of where all the others are and roughly what they are doing. It is not telepathy. It is just that the town is a single instrument and everyone is standing inside it.

Therefore: solitude has to be manufactured, and it is done by *going off the cane*. You swim out into open water between the columns, touching nothing, and float. Off the cane you cannot be heard and cannot hear, and you are not addressed, and by strict custom you are not looked for. Young people do it to sulk. Lovers do it, which is scandalous and universal. A widow does it for nine days after a death, floating out in the far colonnade, coming in only to eat, saying nothing; and when she puts her hand back on a stem the whole bowl knows within the minute, and someone taps her rhythm, and she taps back, and that is the end of the mourning.

**Air**

They are all, in the most literal sense, dependent on the plant's breath.

Three thousand metres is thin. The sky here is not generous. The gas in the internodes is richer than the sky is — it comes out of the spile fizzing and sour, and it goes into the blood like good news. Divers take a mouthful before a bout and it buys them twenty seconds. The old and the sick, up on the rafts, sleep with a spile permanently in a node and a pith tube in the mouth, breathing the plant all night, and live years longer than they otherwise would. Every infant in the town has been held to a node in its first week. They say the plant makes the second half of a person.
