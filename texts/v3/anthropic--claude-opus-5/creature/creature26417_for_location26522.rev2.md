## What lives here

### The heron

Start with the heron, because it is the only thing in the chamber that will look back at you.

It is a female, or was; sex is a surface concern and it has let most of those go. It came up the outfall in the flood of a year the slate records only as a water level, and it has been in the chamber long enough that the calcite has begun to do to it what it does to everything that holds still. The primaries on the left wing are stiffened with it. The legs, below the water line, are chalked like old pipework. It cannot fly more than the length of the nave now and does not try; it moves between the three columns it uses by a series of considered hops and one beat of the wings, and it lands without sound, because everything here lands without sound.

It has resolved itself down to four actions. Stand. Turn the head. Strike. Swallow. There used to be a fifth — a shuffling half-mantle of the wings that herons do when uneasy — and it has not been seen for six years. Whatever the chamber does to noise, it has done to this bird's repertoire, paring it away, and what is left is the irreducible core of heron: a spike, a stomach, and a patience with no bottom to it.

It has a name. It is written once on the back of the current slate, in small letters, and rubbed out, and still legible in the smear. Neither of the two who come down will admit to having written it.

### The still congregation

The eels are not individuals and do not wish to be treated as such. Two hundred, pale as peeled willow, lying in the deep end in knots like discarded rope. They do everything in the eleven-minute rhythm and nothing outside it. Between strokes they are so motionless that the mineral has started on them too — not a coat, they move enough for that, but a certain dullness of the skin, a lack of gloss, as though the water has begun to think of them as furniture.

The alignment is real. Every eel at rest lies with its head towards the dynamo, within a few degrees. It is not the current; the current at that end sets across them. It is not the light; they lie so in the dark hours when the filaments sag almost out. The younger of the two keepers has measured the bearing with a compass and found the dynamo sits fourteen degrees off, and the eels split the difference between the machine and the magnetic north, which explains nothing and has been written on the slate anyway, in small figures, twice.

The crayfish keep house. One to a niche, a hundred and some niches under the pier, and the same tenancy holds for years at a time; when a niche goes vacant it is occupied within a fortnight, and there is never a dispute that anyone has witnessed, because nothing here can afford the noise of a dispute. They are the colour of a fingernail and they are the chamber's undertakers. Anything that dies is skeletal in three days and gone in ten, and what is left is a clean pale object that begins, immediately, to acquire its coat.

The cave crickets keep their hand's span from one another with an exactness that looks like manners and is really only the old arithmetic of appetite and risk. They do not chirp. Their ancestors did. Somewhere in the last century and a half the stridulating ridge shortened in them, generation on generation, because a signal that returns nothing is a cost with no return, and they have arrived at silence the way water arrives at level.

The rats are the only cynics in the room. Three families, fat, grey-white about the muzzle, unhurried, contemptuous. They have the stroke memorised to the second and cross the low walkway in the four seconds after the knock, when the discharge is loud enough to cover a whole rat's worth of sound. The oldest of them has a ruined ear and no fear of anything on two legs, and has been observed sitting on the toe of a stationary boot for the duration of a full eleven minutes, warming itself, waiting for the weight to shift.

### The two

They are known by their chalk.

**The first hand** is block capitals, pressed hard enough to score the slate under the chalk, sevens barred, water levels given to the centimetre and never finer. It writes the date in the military order. It signs, when it signs, with two letters and a full stop after each. It does the grease.

She comes down on the second Sunday and the last Thursday and after heavy rain, whenever that falls, and she comes down with a canvas bag on a lanyard because both hands belong to the rail. She is somewhere past sixty. She came into the work through a water authority that was sold, broken up, sold again, renamed twice, and floated, and through every one of those transactions the chamber travelled as a line item nobody queried: *Structure 4b, inspection and lubrication, quarterly.* There has not been a quarterly inspection since the nineteen-eighties. There has been her, roughly ninety times a year, unpaid since the second sale.

She carries the grease up in two-pound tins of a tallow compound whose maker stopped trading in 1971. There are nine tins left on the shelf by the pier. At one tin every fourteen months she has worked out how long she has, and she has written the year on the shelf edge in pencil and does not look at it. She packs the gland, she turns the wick-feeds, she wipes the beam bearing with a rag and holds the back of her hand near the shaft to feel for a warmth that is wrong. She knows the engine's small dishonesties: the stroke that comes eight seconds late when the ground is dry, the tick in the gear train at the fourth tooth, the way the flywheel moss browns when the bearing is running hot, so that the moss is a thermometer and she reads it before she reads anything else.

She does not take photographs. She thinks it would be rude.

**The second hand** is small, slanted, quick, with a fountain pen's habits transferred to chalk; it writes levels in millimetres, which is more precision than the room can honestly support, and it writes other things too: eel counts, the date the fern under lamp eleven put up a new frond, the bearing of the compass, a question mark on its own. It does not sign. It marks its entries with a single dot to the left.

He is thirty-some. He came down the first time through the hatch without permission, with rope he did not need and a camera he could not make work in nineteen lamps' worth of ochre light, and he was standing on the third column's walkway with his heart going when the beam moved for the first time in his presence and he sat down on the wet plate and stayed sitting for four strokes, forty-four minutes, and came up changed in the way people are embarrassed to describe. He came back eleven times before he found the coat-shaped sculpture by the stair had a fresh tin of grease beside it and a scrap of slate with two letters and a full stop on it, and understood he had been seen, weighed, and — this is the extraordinary thing about the place — permitted.

They have met four times in six years. They have never had a conversation longer than fifteen seconds, because a conversation cannot be had here; the voice goes two metres and drops dead into the moonmilk like a bird into a net. Everything between them is done in objects and taps. He leaves spare filament lamps in a wooden box, sourced from a theatrical supplier in Leipzig, because five of the twenty-four porcelain insulators have been dark since before either of them, and he is slowly, one lamp a year, bringing the room back up to its design intent. She leaves him a pencil when his is worn down. He leaves her a cup of the sort that can be taken away again. Once she left a length of new lead sheet, rolled, with no note, and he understood it was for the defective joint where the elder root comes through, and he understood equally that he was not to use it, that it was a test of whether he understood the root was a resident now, and he rolled it back up and put it on the shelf and she has never mentioned it and neither has he.

The tap language runs to eleven signals. One long: *I am down.* Two: *I am going up.* Three quick: *stop, wait.* Four: *look.* Five, evenly spaced, is the one that empties the blood out of you — *the stroke is late* — and there is no signal for what you do after that, because there is nothing to do but stand with your hand on the rail and count, and find out.

The handrail is polished to satin at every arm's reach along its whole seventy-one steps. That is not two people's worth of polish. That is everybody's.

### The dead ones

Behind the slate, in a niche closed with a board, there are forty-one more slates, stacked, each one worked until the chalk would no longer take and then retired. They are the room's parish register. The hands go backwards: a copperplate with looped descenders that stops in 1974, mid-column; a scratchy hand that uses fractions of an inch and abbreviates *water* to *wr*; one that for three years drew a small sun in the corner of every entry and then didn't; one that from 1918 to 1921 wrote nothing but the count, no levels, no initials, just the strokes, day after day, as if counting were all that could be managed.

And there is, every fifteen or twenty years, a run of entries in a hand neither keeper of the period could account for. Levels only. No initials, no dot. Correct to the centimetre, and in the modern runs correct to a centimetre at hours when both of them can prove they were above ground. Each assumes it is the other being coy. They have never asked. It is not a place for asking, and the levels are accurate, and accuracy is the only credential the chamber has ever required.

### The oldest resident

The engine is not alive and is the most alive thing in the room, in the sense that everything else here is arranged around its habits the way a household is arranged around an invalid or a god.

It eats grease and packing and, once, a bronze bush. It has a temperature, and the moss on the flywheel reports it. It has moods that correlate with rainfall three days previous. It has been running since before the invention of the aeroplane and it has never once been asked to, and if both keepers stopped tomorrow it would run on the last of the grease for perhaps two years and then begin to knock, and then to labour, and then to seize, and the water would rise past the green band and the bright band and the crickets would go and the ferns would drown and the eels, one supposes, would do rather well.

The heron would stand on the third column until the water reached it and then it would stand a while longer.

### The line

That is the whole population: two hundred eels, a hundred crayfish, three rat families, an uncountable weight of shrimp and springtail, an elder tree's one blind finger, nineteen lamps' worth of fern, a bird made mostly of limestone and hunger, an engine, and two people who owe it nothing.

The line on the slate has been re-chalked by every hand in the niche behind it, and it stands out now in low relief, a quarter-inch of accumulated chalk and calcite, a little fossil sentence:

**IT DOES NOT NEED US. COME ANYWAY.**

It is not an invitation. Read it again with the rhythm of the room in you and you will hear what it actually is: the only instruction the place has ever issued, issued once, by someone who had worked out that nothing down here requires witnessing and had decided to witness it regardless, every second Sunday and last Thursday, for as long as the tins hold out.
