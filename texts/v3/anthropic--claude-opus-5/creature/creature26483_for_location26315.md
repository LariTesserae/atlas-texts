## The bodies

Start with what the place has made of them, because the people here are less inhabitants than they are impressions — the way the mat holds a boot print for two seasons, the Shelf holds its workers in a particular shape.

They are all slightly deaf in the same way. Not generally deaf: notched. There is a trench in their hearing at about four thousand cycles, cut identically in all six of them by identical pressure over unequal years, so that they share a deafness the way a family shares a nose. Consonants go first. A newcomer says *thistle* and *whistle* and *this'll* and they are three of the same word. Below the notch they hear fine, which is a cruelty, because it means the falls remain audible to them long after speech has become optional.

They have the jaw. All of them. Masseters like small apples, molars worn to plates, and a habit — you see it on the walkways, at distance, if you know to look — of pressing two fingers up under the cheekbone and holding there for a moment while they wait for the pain to move on somewhere else. The valley dentist has a name for it in his ledger, *shelf-bite*, and a standing arrangement with the works office.

And the eyes, yes. Everyone here has beautiful eyes, ruined. The whites go tea-coloured at the corners; a wing of fleshy tissue creeps in from the nose side, a millimeter a year, and they call it *the mat*, because it is, it is exactly the same behaviour, a green thing growing over new stone. They wear the smoked-glass goggles from the stores and they take them off constantly, because the glass fogs in the spray-curtain and because in that flat forge light the eye wants naked information. They squint into a permanent narrow readiness. It gives them all, at forty, an expression of amused disbelief, which is not their expression at all.

Their voices are the last thing. They shout. They shout in the sheds, they shout at the stove, they shout at a bird nine meters off, and when they go down on rotation — three weeks, once a year, the cableway, the cloud, the astonishing green noise of trees — they are taken in the valley for drunks, or for angry men, or for that particular kind of countryman who has no manners. In the valley bars they are given wide tables. It takes eleven days for a shelf voice to come back down to a room's size, and rotation is twenty-one, and the last ten days are the only days in the year they sound to strangers like people.

## Hands

There are four hundred and twelve signs.

Not a language, they'd tell you, if you asked in writing; a working vocabulary. But it has grammar it did not import, it has tense marked in the shoulder, it has a scandalous number of words for the condition of wet plant matter, and it has been inherited, on this plateau, from hands to hands, without ever having been written down, since somewhere before 1911.

Most of it is trade. *Intake up. Intake down. Rot, deep. Rot, shallow, no matter. Grease. Wheel. Two turns. Enough.* Some of it is weather, and is enormous — nine signs for the way cloud sits on the western lip, each with a consequence attached. Some of it is old and nobody now knows the referent: a flat hand rocked twice at hip height means *don't*, universally, urgently, and derives, according to the marginalia, from a gesture about a tramway that has been under moss since before the war.

And a handful of the signs are not for work at all, and those are the ones that get taught last, in the second year, when the newcomer has stopped talking aloud to himself.

There is a sign for *the noise has gone away*, which does not mean the falls have stopped, because the falls do not stop. It means the thing that happens in the fourteenth month, when the wall of white pressure crosses over and becomes a silence, and you find you are standing still in the middle of the mat with your mouth open. It is made with the fingertips coming away from the ears, opening outward, like something released rather than something lost.

The same sign, made with the palms turned down toward the ground, means *beautiful*.

## Slate

Each shed has one, hung by the door, and a jar of water, and the words dry off in four minutes.

They use them for numbers mostly, gauge heights and valve counts, but everyone up here also knows what the slate is actually for. It is the only privacy on a plateau where you can be seen from fifteen kilometers away and where speech is impossible and where every third thought must therefore be carried around whole in the body like a stone in a boot. So: you write it, and you look at it, and it goes.

Nobody discusses this. There is no ritual around it. But if you come into a shed and the slate is wet and blank and someone is sitting with their back to it, you go out and check a gauge that does not need checking, and you come back in ten minutes, and that is the whole of the etiquette.

## Marginalia

The library is the correspondence.

Nine sheds, perhaps four hundred books, all of them swollen to twice thickness, pages gone soft as cloth and furred at the edges, spines opened out flat by the damp until a book lies on a bunk like something filleted. They circulate. There is no system; there is drift. A novel translated badly from a language nobody up here reads went from Shed Two to Shed Seven over the course of thirty-one years.

And in the margins: the actual conversation of this place. Pencil, mostly, because ink runs. Generations arguing. Someone in 1923 disagrees on page 60 with the book's account of grief; someone in 1954 disagrees with *him*, at length, in a smaller hand, and is tender about it, and calls him *friend*, and has never met him, and knows he died in the valley of something ordinary. Someone in 1971 draws an arrow between the two and writes *both of you are describing the fog*. There are jokes that take four decades to land. There is a proposal of marriage on the endpaper of a hydrology manual, made to no one specified, unanswered for sixty years and then answered — *yes, but not up here* — in a hand that does not identify itself.

They read aloud, all of them, when alone. It is universal and nobody mentions it. You cannot hear yourself, and the point is not to hear yourself; the point is that the mouth is a muscle and it forgets.

## Six

**Vurst**, thirty-one years on the Shelf, is the one who listens to the collar. She goes out to the lip at the end of the day and puts both palms on the bolted steel and stands there ten minutes with her eyes closed and her goggles pushed up into her hair, reading four hundred tons a minute through her hands. She can feel the valley's evening. She knows the exact tremor of the tram starting its up-run at 18:40; she knows when the second mill drops its load and the pressure eases a half-degree; she has felt, twice, a turbine come off balance nine hundred meters below her and inside a cloud, and been right both times, and been thanked by letter. She has never been in the powerhouse. She has never seen a lit street from a lit street. What she knows of the town of eleven thousand people whose lamps she keeps burning is a thing that arrives in her wrists at dusk, and she is not curious in the way people down there assume she must be. She has a whole town in her hands every evening. Curiosity would be greed.

**Tamm** is nineteen and in his first summer and still hearing the noise, which means he has not slept properly in five weeks and his hands shake at the buckles and he talks out loud, constantly, to himself, in the flat over-loud voice of a man in a gale. They do not help him. There is nothing to do. Vurst gives him the transects nearest Shed Four and lets him alone and once a week writes on the slate *it goes*, and lets him read it, and lets it dry.

**Sabin** cuts the peat. Four cubic meters a year, by permit, from the mat's deep black layers, and his real work is the map: a hundred-year rotation drawn on oilcloth in three colours, showing every cut since 1911 and the state of its healing, with numbered stakes out on the mat that he replaces when they rot. He tends a schedule whose completion is ninety years past his death. He has strong opinions about the stakes his predecessors placed and repositions two or three a season, muttering, and pencils the corrections into the oilcloth's margins with the date, for whoever comes.

**Ott** is thirty years dead and still lives here, because he began the bird count in 1948 in the back of the official log, in a column he ruled himself, and the count has never once been dropped. Whoever is in Shed Four does the dipper transect on the first clear morning of the month. Nobody instituted this. Nobody in the valley office knows the column exists.

**Aleu** does the weeding, which means she spends her working life on her knees with her bare hands in cold brown water pulling the pale bent-grass out at the root, one stem at a time, over thirty square kilometers, forever. Gloves lose the feel of the root-crown. Her hands are the hands of a woman twice her age and she is entirely serene about this and has a sign of her own invention, used only by her, for *I found one where there should not be one*, which is half triumph and half a kind of grief.

**And the sixth** rotates, and the sixth is always the one who is leaving, and everyone knows it by the second month, and is kind, and does not say.

In summer three more come up: seasonal, students, loud in the eyes. They gesture enormously, in signs learned too fast, all of it at the volume of shouting, and the winter six find them exhausting in a way they cannot explain and do not resent, and are sorry when they go, and sleep better after.

## The bird

The dipper is brown, and small, and works the sheeting films for larvae with its whole body tipped forward, and it sings directly at you with its throat visibly moving and you cannot hear one note of it, ever, and neither can it.

They are fond of it out of all proportion. It is the only thing here that is not water, plant, iron, or them. There are three named individuals in the log, identified by leg-scar and by territory, and their deaths are recorded in the same grease pencil as intake heights, and once, in 1966, in a hand that was not supposed to do this in an official document, with a line drawn under it.

## The fox, in the ledgers

Eleven entries since 1911. Every one of them the same event: the fox comes up from the treeline, crosses the entire Shelf in a straight line, and goes back down.

The marginalia around these eleven entries constitute the plateau's only body of theory. Migration, madness, salt, a route older than the plateau, a single lineage passing something down. In 1937 someone wrote *it is not crossing. we are what it crosses.* Someone else, decades later, appended: *no. it is looking for the treeline on the other side. there isn't one. it will keep coming.*

## The mat, which is the actual inhabitant

Everything above is staff.

The thing that lives here is one continuous acid green-black body of perhaps eight thousand years, thirty centimeters thick on the slabs and four meters thick and floating in the hollows, no soil beneath it because it is the only soil, growing up the vertical faces, capping the boulders, over the pipes, over the roofs, over the tramway, breathing out iodine and holding a season's rainfall in its tissue and letting it go over weeks so that a town below can have lit streets and an electric tram.

It has one appetite and it exercises it about once a generation. The six know exactly where. Two cairns mark two of the false grounds. The third is marked by a grave, which contains Marda Hesp, who went down after Jonna Weld in 1958 and was pulled out, and which does not contain Weld, who is still there, at some depth, in black water at four degrees, in the condition such water preserves. Everyone on the Shelf knows the bearing. In a dry autumn, once or twice in a working life, the level drops and something pale comes up nearer the light, and whoever sees it writes the date in the log, and nothing else, because there is nothing else that is anyone's business.

They walk her boundary at dawn in cold months, because a void beneath the mat breathes upward and the frost above it lingers an hour longer than the frost around it, and so the plateau's most dangerous thing is legible for exactly one hour a day, in a slow grey shape on green, and reading it is the highest skill anyone here has.

## Bow Three

The oldest resident has a bearing and a schedule: visible 1030h–1500h, use for orientation from the shed line. It has been standing in the same place in the spray-curtain, doing the same thing, since before the log.

Every newcomer is walked out into the curtain at the right hour and positioned, and shown the ring — the small perfect circle of light that stands around the shadow of your own head on the fog, and only your own. You cannot see anyone else's. The person standing an arm's length away, in another acoustic country, has one too, and it is invisible to you, and yours is invisible to them, and there is no arrangement of two bodies on this plateau that will ever let one person see the other crowned.

Everyone up here has been shown this in their first week, deliberately, without commentary, by someone who then walked away across the mat and left them in it.

That is the induction. There isn't another.

And afterward, sooner or later — the fourteenth month, usually, out on the transects, alone, four kilometers from another human being — they stop, and stand, and turn their palms down toward that green skin stretched over new stone, and make the sign to nobody at all.
