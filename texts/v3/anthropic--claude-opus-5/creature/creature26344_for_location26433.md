## What Lives at Hesp

**Bodies**

They are small. An adult stands about as high as your sternum, long in the back, short in the shin, with a deep chest that does most of its work slowly. The skin is matte and dry to the touch, faintly floury, and it darkens over a lifetime the way clay darkens in a kiln — the young are the pale grey-buff of unfired ware, the old are the red-brown of a good pot, and the very old have a glaze at the knuckles and the heels where they have rubbed against their own hamlet for eighty years. Nothing about them is wet. Their eyes have no shine. They are hairless except for a comb of fine filaments along each forearm and a stiff fringe above the brow, both of which catch pollen and are groomed by others, never by oneself, in long companionable sessions that constitute most of what they have instead of conversation.

They see perfectly well. They simply do not believe it. A person who wants to know how far away something is will pace it, or throw a knotted cord, or send a child. Watching a Maker look at a distant thing is like watching someone listen politely to a rumour.

The feet are broad and padded, like the suction-pads of dustvine, and grip the lattice as readily as the floor. Half the population is above your head at any given time and has been for some while.

**Palms**

The hand is the organ of everything.

In the centre of each palm there is a ridge of hardened skin in the shape of a shallow chevron. It is not a scar and not a birthmark; it thickens over the years, as a callus does, but it thickens in a pattern, and the pattern is the same in all of them and always has been. An infant's chevron is a faint crease you can only find by feel. An elder's is deep, glossy, and slightly cracked at the point.

This means that everything they have ever pressed is signed, and dated. Brush the courses of the basin and you are reading a register: here a shallow print, a young hand, a course laid badly and left; here the same chevron forty years deeper, laid true, in the same wall, eleven rows up. Their whole history is legible and nobody reads it, because reading it would require looking, and because the walls are simply where the family is.

They eat through the palms and the soles. There are no meals. When a pod puffs, whoever is near turns their hands outward into the hanging gold and stands, half-lidded, for as long as it takes; and this is also their only formal gathering, six or eight of them standing in a drift together with their hands open, saying nothing, for the length of a slow afternoon. It is not a rite. It is dinner.

Their gesture for *water* — the flat hand turned over, palm down, meaning *that which we do not have and do not miss* — is the closing of the feeding hand. Their word for the thing they lack is the shape their hand makes when it has had enough.

**Mouths**

The mouth is a tool with a warning on it.

They can speak. Their voices, from disuse, are strange and unrehearsed, each one a private instrument nobody has tuned: reedy, or gravelled, or unnervingly sweet. A Maker's voice is more intimate than their body, and it is considered a serious thing to hear one — the way, elsewhere, it is a serious thing to be shown a wound.

Because *naming fixes*, ordinary business is done with the hands: a fast, low, economical signing kept close to the ribs, with the elbows still. There are no names. There are gestures — a hand-shape apiece, usually taken from a task or an accident — and the shapes outlive their wearers and are taken up again, so that there is always one who is *the flat hand drawn along the hip* (sweeper of the seventh threshold) and always one who is *two fingers hooked, turned twice* (a person who once fell from the lattice, four generations ago, and every person since who has been trusted with heights). They are not exactly individuals with roles. They are roles that have grown people the way a socket in the floor grows a plant.

Children, who shout, are not scolded. They are given the stone.

The stone sits in the low garden and it is the most real object in Hesp. Every generation has screamed names at it — hundreds of names, thousands, all its own — and it is consequently so thoroughly fixed that it cannot be moved, cannot be marked, is cooler than everything around it, and casts its shadow a full heartbeat after you have finished moving into the light. Children adore it. They lie on it and bellow nonsense at it and dare each other to watch the shadow catch up. By the age of six or seven most of them have quietly stopped, the way one stops climbing on furniture, and after that they speak perhaps twelve times in a life.

**The Unnamers**

Someone has to talk.

The priesthood — perhaps five of them at any time — does the backwards work: taking down the names that earlier, freer generations hammered into things. The technique is a mutter with the breath drawn *in* on the syllables, the name spoken and then unspoken from the tail forward, hours of it, done at the low end of the cycle when the sap stiffens and everything is a little more willing to be let go. It works. Slowly, an over-real chair becomes liftable again. A path that had gone rigid can be walked off.

The cost is that it happens to them.

An unnamer gains weight without gaining mass. Ten years in, it takes two people to help one to their feet, and neither can tell you what they are lifting. Twenty years in, their shadow lags. Thirty, and they begin to choose a corner.

The far rooms are full of them. That is what those objects are — the ones so thoroughly named they cannot be lifted or broken, the ones whose shadows arrive a moment late. They are the old priests, folded and set down, their chevrons still just visible under the glaze, and they are still consulted. You go into the far rooms and put your palm against one and wait, and sometimes the warmth answers back and sometimes it does not, and either way you have done the polite thing.

**How There Come to Be More**

In a corridor at the north of the basin there is a length of wall kept clear of vine, and the carvers work there.

A figure is begun shallow — a suggestion, checked constantly with cord, never with the eye. Two or three carvers work it for a season, deepening the relief a shell-blade's width at a time, and they do not cut a face. Faces are not theirs to give. Then everyone waits, sometimes for months, sometimes for years, and the waiting is the sociable part: people come to sit with the wall, groom each other's forearm-combs, stand near it with their hands open so that the drift is thick where it needs to be thick.

And one day the chest edge lifts. A hair's width. Someone marks the floor with a blade.

The nudging-out is delicate and takes weeks, and the whole time somebody has to keep the wax-beetles off with a husk comb, because the beetles do not distinguish between a fissure and a birth, and will helpfully seal a person back into the wall while everyone is sleeping.

Not all of them come out. Some figures breathe and stay flat, and are visited, and are talked to with hands, and are not considered failures — the gesture for such a person means roughly *one who took the wall's side*. There are eleven of them in the north corridor, breathing, at various depths.

And some come out sevened. The waking arrives seven times, and six of those times have not happened yet, so that a single child may be delivered across forty years, in six further instalments, each one arriving as an infant into the middle of some later generation, each one recognising the others instantly and being recognised. The Makers manage this without visible strain. Their gesture for such a set is the same one they use for a long note in the leaves: *not finished sounding.*

**How There Come to Be Fewer**

They go shallow.

An old Maker will lie down more, and in one posture, and the far side of the body begins to agree with the floor. There is a duty — walking the corridors and the garden at intervals, nudging sleepers awake — and it is done for the animals and for the people equally, and it is considered the kindest of the tasks, and it is the one nobody wants.

Because eventually the sleeper you have come to wake will make a gesture instead of getting up. The flat hand, turned over, palm down.

*That which we do not have and do not miss.*

They use the same sign for their dead as for water, and they use it without ceremony, and when a visitor's face does something at this they do not understand what the face is for. Afterward the outline stays in the floor or the wall — a person-shaped relief, decorative, slightly warm — and someone will sit near it for a while and comb the forearm filaments that are no longer there, out of habit, for a season or two. The corridors of Hesp are lined with everybody who has ever lived there. They are all still, technically, in the plan.
