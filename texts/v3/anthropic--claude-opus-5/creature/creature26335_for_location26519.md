## The upkeep

### What they are called

They have no word that means *us* in the way you would want it to. The nearest one means something like *the wedges*, or *the hands* — a term from the tool-inventory, listed among the adzes and the rasps. Ask them what lives in the Bell and they will answer, without evasion, that one thing lives here, and it is the hollow in the middle, and it sings; and that they are its upkeep, in the way that the rubbing-stone is the upkeep of a block.

This is not modesty. They genuinely do not count themselves among the room's inhabitants, which is why the old register in the thresholds — the counting-beads of green copper, three hundred and forty of them, each pressed into a socket ground to fit — records chords, pollen-falls, notes gained and lost, and the number of hands available, in the same column and the same units as the number of usable wedges.

### The body

They would come to your sternum. Barrel-chested, narrow-hipped, thin in the limb, with the long splayed foot that made the stair-treads into bowls: bare soles always, hardened to something like horn, waxed by generations of contact into the glaze you slipped on coming down.

Skin matte and dry as paper, plated across the shoulders and outer forearms where a body leans on hot masonry. Hairless. No lids that close fully; a nictitating film instead, drawn several times a minute against the dust. Eyes large and very dark, tuned to the cherry end of the spectrum and to that faint chalk-green phosphorescence — they read the older quarters as an outline map, and they navigate almost entirely by it, at a walk, without hesitation, on stairs with no handrail above a sixty-pace drop.

Along the ribs and the outside of each arm there are flanges: thin folds of skin, stiffened with fine cartilaginous rods, four to a side. On the inhale, when the cooler air comes up through the grate and the room quiets a fraction, the flanges open. On the exhale they fold flat. Every one of them, every nine seconds, all their lives. Sitting still in a niche, a hand is doing exactly what the flue-thistles are doing on the terrace behind it, at the same tempo, for the same reason: this is a body that sheds heat and cannot afford to spend water doing it.

Fine filaments above the brow and along the inner wrist, easily missed, read the moving air. A widened flue-bore does not first present itself to them as a wrong note. It presents as a wrong tickle on the wrist as they pass — a flutter where there should be a steady press. Tuners work with their arms bare and held slightly out from the body, wrists forward, which from a distance looks like a kind of formal politeness.

### Water

They do not drink. There is nothing to drink. Every drop in them was made inside them, oxidized out of the hydrogen the plants pulled from the shaft's breath and packed into their tissues; a hand's whole water-budget for a lifetime is a slow manufacture, sipped from its own food.

Consequences, all of them visible. No sweat. No tears — the eye is washed by the film and a waxy secretion, and weeping does not exist, not as a suppressed act but as an absent one; there is no word.
