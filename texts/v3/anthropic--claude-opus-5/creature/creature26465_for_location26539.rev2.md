## Fauna, continued

### The hush

Everything else on the terrace is weather.

That is not a slight. The fronds are a beautiful weather and the sifters are a purposeful one, and the chandelier over the hot stones is worth the whole trip by itself. But a frond is a strain gauge that has learned to stack dust, a nodeling is a four-day life spent running away, and a sifter is the garden's own digestion walking around outside its body. None of them have histories. They turn over twice a season. They are what the engine does to matter, the way frost is what a window does to air.

There is one thing up there that is an animal in the full sense — older than the garden it walks through, slower than it, indifferent to it, and in possession of a biography you can actually trace.

It goes quiet, and you leave. That much is in the training, and unlike the introduction, the training on this one works. Most of the time.

---

**How it arrives.** It does not arrive. You arrive in it.

Twelve minutes of ground contact a day means most of your hours on Terrace Nine are spent standing on your own boots feeling the mountain come up through the soles like a hand pressed on a drum, and after two or three days that sensation stops being a sensation. It becomes the floor. It becomes the direction *down*. You do not notice it any more than you notice your own pulse.

So what you notice is the loss. The floor goes out. There is a documented, reproducible, and thoroughly unhelpful euphoria at the leading edge — people describe it as relief, as the first warm room after a long walk, as *finally*. Four survivors independently used the word *bath*. This is the entire problem. Nothing hurts. Nothing is coming at you. The most dangerous animal on the west shoulder kills by making you comfortable, and it does this at a walking pace of about four metres an hour, which means the margin is enormous and people die in it anyway.

Of the thirty-one stones in the cairn on the eastern lip, five are for people who sat down.

---

**What it looks like.** A thicket. Specifically, a mature stand of fronds about three metres high that has begun to lean, the way a stand leans in the hours before a collapse — and it is precisely this resemblance, and the fact that leaning stands are boring and there are nine hundred of them, that hid the animal for two centuries.

The body is a drawn-silica mesh, the same material the garden is made of, grown the same way, fringed and layered and shot through with the same oil-slick interference colours. It is not camouflage in any intentional sense. A hush is made of the terrace because it eats the terrace. Mass estimates run between two and four hundred kilograms in a volume you could pitch a small tent in; the density of a dry hedge. Underneath the fringe there is a low, wide, six-lobed frame that moves in a rhythm too slow to read as movement. You can watch a hush for ten minutes and be certain it is scenery.

We do, in fact, have thousands of good images. They were all taken by accident. An archivist going through third-survey plates for something else found the same torn fringe in eleven frames across four years, at which point the shape became obvious, and now no one can stop seeing it, and the older record has yielded fifty-odd individuals on the west shoulder — nineteen on Terrace Nine specifically. The individual with the torn fringe is still up there. Crews call it Cuff. It has been photographed, unknowingly, for three hundred and eighty years, and the tear has not healed, and nothing about it appears to have changed at all.

Nobody photographed one on purpose, historically, for the obvious reason that everyone close enough for a good frame was walking away from it.

---

**What it eats.** This is the part that reorganises the terrace once you understand it.

The standard account says a hush generates an anti-field and drags a pocket of silence, and that plants inside the pocket starve and nodelings inside it lose their bearings, and that the hush eats both. All true, and all downstream. Cancellation is not a weapon. Cancellation is what absorption looks like from outside.

Everything on this mountain is living off the engine at one or two removes. The fronds harvest strain; the nodelings harvest the fronds' dead; the sifters harvest the fronds. The hush skips the intermediaries. It couples directly into the rock and takes the waste channel at the source — drinks the noise, converts it, and re-radiates almost nothing. The silence around it is not a field it projects. It is a shadow it casts by being hungry.

Which means the plants in the pocket don't die of a trick. They die because the food is gone out of the ground. The nodelings don't lose their bearings to an illusion; the world they navigate by has been eaten out from under them. And the humans who sit down in the bath are experiencing, with reasonable accuracy, what it is like to have your floor consumed.

A hush parks over a good thicket for eight to fourteen days and leaves a clean oval of sterile sand.

**The wake.** A hush is the only warm object on Terrace Nine — a few degrees, no more, but it is shedding somewhere and it sheds there. Warmth couples into rock. Rock carries strain. You already know this rule: *camp overnight in the same spot and you will wake ringed by new tips, three centimetres high, all leaning inward.*

The fronds chase a hush. They come up in its track, thousands of them, fine as hair, leaning after it — and then the pocket's trailing edge passes over them and they stop, mid-lean, and stay that way.

A hush's trail is a line of tiny dead seedlings, all facing the same direction, going back across the sand for a hundred metres. It is the most reliable way to find one, and it is a completely accurate diagram of what would happen to you.

---

**What it becomes.** Hushes grow. Slowly, and not outward — inward. The mesh thickens. Over what must be a very long time the fringe consolidates, the lobes shorten, the whole animal gets denser and quieter and less able to move, until at some point it stops moving at all, settles, anchors, and closes.

Go and look at the back wall.

Nine of them. Four metres, tapered, grey, spaced irregularly. Not weathered in nine million years — because nothing has ever reached them to weather them; the field goes *around*. Acoustically invisible in a place where invisibility is the one thing that requires enormous continuous work. Older than the garden's ecology and younger than the engine, which is to say they arrived, or grew, or settled, in the first stretch after the mountain began to ring, and then stopped.

Nobody wants to sign their name to this. The material analysis is unhelpful and the morphology is suggestive rather than conclusive, and there is a respectable school that holds the pylons are artefacts and the resemblance is coincidence, and I would like to be persuaded by them.

But each pylon casts a null-shadow a few metres long, and the seven nodeling species that exist nowhere else on the world live their entire lives inside those shadows, and have done for long enough to speciate, and I would rather not think too hard about what a permanent refuge is, in a place where the only thing that makes silence makes it by eating.

---

**The dome** rings every twenty-two hours on its own schedule, below the engine's band, and does not match anything. There is one unfunded correlation study suggesting the intervals cluster around hush movement. It is not a good study. I mention it because somebody should.

---

**The rectangle.** Forty metres by forty, dead centre, nothing growing, the sifters walking around it, and the field reads flat and normal.

Flat is the word to sit with. A frond does not need loudness; it needs a *gradient*, a direction in which loudness increases, and it will climb that gradient until it kills itself. A perfectly uniform field offers nothing to climb. No antinodes, no nulls, no up. A growth tip in there has no instruction and simply does not start.

Every other anomaly on this terrace shows up as an absence — the pylon shadows, a hush's pocket, the quiet drop-offs at the east and west lips. The rectangle is not an absence. Something in there is taking the engine's field and returning it, in phase, in full, with the edges squared off to within a hand's width, which is not a thing rock does.

The hushes are inefficient. They keep some. The pylons are the ones that kept so much they stopped. And forty metres down in the middle of the garden there is, I think, something that has finished — that no longer keeps anything, that has been listening to the same nine-million-year sentence for long enough to say it back perfectly, and now sits there in flawless agreement with the mountain, indistinguishable from the mountain, waiting for the mountain to say something new.

One person has touched a hush, briefly, gloved, and their account is consistent and has never been repeated. They said it was not quiet at all. They said it was the loudest thing they had ever felt, and that it was the engine — the whole engine, the entire ringing, the full nine million years of it — running backwards.

Add to the rules:

**Silence here is not the absence of the noise. It is the noise, held by something.**
