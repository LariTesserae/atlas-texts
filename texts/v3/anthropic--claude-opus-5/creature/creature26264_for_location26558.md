## The people

Two hundred and six of them, at the last count, which is taken on the same day the far paths are scraped and is not called a count but a scraping.

They are long-boned and narrow, with big chests for the thin air and small mouths for everything else. Their skin is the colour of the light they live in, a wet greenish pallor, except for the backs of the hands and the bridge of the nose, which are weathered dark by one hour of sun a day, and the palms, which are pink and hard and gloss-scarred from cane and glass. Everyone is slightly damp. Everyone smells faintly of the felt, which is the smell of a warm animal that has been rained on.

They walk with a rolling step, outside of the heel through to the toe, taught before speech and never lost, in shoes that are six layers of cured felt lashed to a wooden last and resoled every ninth day. They walk slowly. This is not temperament. At this height, hurry means panting, and panting is noise, and so nobody runs. When somebody runs, everyone within sight stops what they are doing and turns, because it means a child is under something, or a shrug has come up early, or a house is going green.

Their hearing is exquisite and it is the organ they are proudest of and most tired of. An old woman on the south shelf can tell you which of her grandsons is coming up the path from the pressure of his shoe on wet moss at forty paces. There is no dark to them, not really; they navigate the night cloud by drip-count and by the different silences that different objects make, a cistern's silence being wide and cold, a cane stand's silence being fretted and busy. Blindness is an inconvenience here, deafness a disaster. A deaf person cannot hear the noise they themselves are making. Two are living now, both old, both attended, both loved in the exhausting way you love someone who might at any moment cough down a wall.

Their hair is a crop. Everyone grows it and everyone cuts it, and it is twisted with the wet-strong hair of the recent dead into the cord that lashes every house, every ladder, every loom on the ridge. There is no house on the Nap that is not tied together with its grandmothers. They do not find this touching. They find it structural.

## The near tongue and the far

They have two languages and only one of them is for saying things.

The near tongue is hands, face, shoulders, and the flat of the fingers on someone's forearm. It is fast, it is filthy, it is capable of subordinate clauses and sarcasm and prayer, and it grows nothing at all, which is exactly why it is the tongue of daily life. Children have it before they can walk. Old people who have lost the use of one hand speak a clipped, telegraphic version that the young find both moving and hard to sit through. It is not a code for speech. Speech is the code for it.

The far tongue is the voice, and the voice is not for talking to people. The voice is for growing. You do not ask a woman what she said today. You ask what she grew. When two people on this ridge want to be understood by each other they use their hands; when they use their mouths, it is work, or it is law, or it is an accident, or somebody has died.

Everybody has two names. The hand-name is a gesture, usually cruel in origin and usually forgotten — a fold of the ear, a thumb across the eyebrow, a fist opened downward. The spoken name is short, low, and soft-mouthed: Onno, Wenna, Mull, Roum, Habbe, Sey. Nothing sibilant. Nothing plosive. A name with an *s* in it grows a spiky nap and is thought to bring the sayer bad luck, and the four families who have such names in their genealogies have quietly worn them down over six generations into something rounder. Spoken names are used almost exclusively in the pits. Which means that the food the town eats is made largely out of its own dead relatives' names, said over and over in the grey, and everyone knows this, and nobody thinks it is strange, and a child who first works it out is considered to have had a proper thought.

## The crying house

Birth is the loudest ordinary thing that happens on the Nap, and it is done out on the bare rock at the west drop, on a scraped apron kept clean for it, so that what grows from the labour grows in the open where it can be harvested. It comes up fast, pale, ribbed, warm to the hand. The mother eats first. Everything else goes to the household. It is understood to be the sweetest crop there is, and the taste of it is the reason women here can be talked into doing it a second time.

Then the child screams for two years, and the child cannot be kept in the town.

So there is the crying house, out past the last shelf, downwind, a squat wide thing with double walls, and in it live the far-nurses. Four at present, all women, all past childbearing, all half-deaf, and every infant on the ridge passes eighteen months to three years in their hands. They are the only people alive here who hear noise every day. It has made them strange. They laugh out loud. They speak to each other in full voice out of habit, and when they come into town for the gold hour they are conspicuous the way drunks are conspicuous elsewhere: too much face, too much arm, always a half-beat too loud, always being touched on the forearm by embarrassed relatives. Around the crying house the felt has to be cut back with glass hooks twice a week and the harvest is enormous and rank and slightly bitter, and it is fed to the birds.

The far-nurses are honoured. Nobody wants to sit next to them.

## Voice-work

A pit is dug where the old woman who keeps the pits says a pit may be dug, and she decides by kneeling and putting her cheek on the rock, and nobody has ever successfully argued with her about it.

Voices are not equal. A rich low voice with grain in it — a burr, a catch, a habit of dropping into the chest — grows thick sweet crop that cures well and keeps. A thin flat voice grows a pale watery felt that has to be eaten within days. Children's voices are auditioned around nine, in the shallow practice pit, and the result of that audition sets the shape of a life more than anything else that will ever happen to them. A good voice is wealth. A good voice is also a sentence: eight hours a day sitting in a stone hole reciting the names of the dead in the cold wet, six days out of seven, from twelve years old. Good voices are ruined by forty. Every third or fourth generation the town produces someone so extraordinary that they are farmed nearly to death by love and gratitude, and there is always a slab of their last year's growth kept somewhere, coarse and hoarse and full of gaps, which people bring out and touch and feel awful about.

Marriage negotiations are about voices, principally. Two thin voices are discouraged. Nobody says why out loud. Nobody says anything out loud.

A new couple is given a pit of their own for the first year, out at the edge where the ground is fresh, and whatever grows in it in that year belongs to them, and is not shared, and is not discussed, and everybody knows perfectly well what is being grown out there and what it will taste like, and this is the single richest vein of humour on the mountain. The near tongue has eleven separate jokes about it. Three of them can be made with one hand.

## Wall-reading

Because sound leaves a body behind, nothing here is deniable.

Where a thing was said, the felt swelled, and the swelling took the grain of the mouth that made it, and the swelling can be cut out and cured and kept. So the Nap has writing without letters and archives without ink. In every house there is a box of hard dark slabs: the marriage oaths, the boundary agreements, the debt-sayings, each one a hand-span of cured nap with a voice printed in it as legibly to their fingers as a signature is to us.

Oaths are the one occasion on which people speak aloud in front of each other on purpose. Six witnesses, a scraped stone table, a fresh mat of live felt laid down, and the swearer talks into it for as long as it takes, and it rises under them while they speak. Then it is cut, pressed, and dried. Nobody has ever managed to forge one convincingly except a man called Habbe four generations back, a natural mimic who could take another man's grain into his throat, and who is the nearest thing this town has to a devil. Children are told he is still down the stair, growing other people's voices in the dark.

And there are the readers. Old women mostly, and the two deaf men, who have the finest fingers on the ridge. They are called into a house when something needs settling, and they sit against the interior wall in the dark with both palms flat and work slowly along it, and what they are reading is everything anyone ever said in that room, layered, overlapping, going back. It is not clean. It is like reading a page that has been written on eight hundred times. But grain persists, and a good reader can find a particular voice in a wall the way a dog finds one scent in a field, and say: *here, this is your father, this is the year he came back up*, and put the widow's hand on the exact spot.

That is the funerary industry here. That is the whole of history. The dead are upholstery, as the saying goes, and the readers are the ones who know which part of the sofa is grandmother.

## The trades

**Path-scrapers.** A third of all labour and no status whatsoever, which everybody agrees is unjust and nobody has ever fixed. They work bent, with glass-toothed hooks, and their backs go at thirty-five. The good ones can feel through the shaft where the felt is hungry — where growth is running faster than it should — and it is a scraper, always, who first notices that a house has been quarrelling.
