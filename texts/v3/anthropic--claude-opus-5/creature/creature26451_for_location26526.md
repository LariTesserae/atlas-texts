## The Census Is Wrong

Any inventory of the plateau's life will come back with four or five entries — wick, tillers, fetches, sparrowers, whatever it is in the galleries — and it will be wrong in a specific way. It is wrong the way a list of an animal's inhabitants would be wrong if it read: *blood; hair; gut flora; one tick.*

There is one resident here. There are arguably two. The second is the more interesting, and the third is a rumor.

## The Resident

The wick is not the ground cover. It is the animal. The plateau has one occupant and the occupant is four hundred kilometers wide and ankle-deep, and everything else described as living here is either part of it or a guest in its walls.

It has no age, only a floor. It has never reproduced and has no mechanism for it; there is no second one anywhere, has never been, cannot be. It is not a species. It is a single individual so old that the word *individual* strains, since nothing has ever stood outside it to be counted against. Its variation comes from above: cosmic radiation into the scintillator teeth on its underside, the same flux it eats. It feeds on its own mutagen. Every meal is a slight revision, and after four billion years of slight revisions it has become extraordinarily good at being exactly this and nothing else.

It is not centralized, but it is not uniform either. Across the tableland there are districts, twenty or eighty kilometers wide, that differ in their doping chemistry and therefore in the timbre of their hiss — some districts frying at a higher pitch, some with a wet crackle to them, one in the south that sounds almost like a held breath. Walking from one into another, the change is under your feet before it is in your ears. They behave a little like organs and rather more like moods.

Its senses are strange and mostly inward. Light is food, not information; it is effectively blind, and the small orange sun is to it what a radiator is to you. What it feels is strain. The rock flexes under the thermal tide of the long day, and the wick reads that flexing through a hundred trillion piezoelectric rootlets, so it experiences noon as a slow bending in the back. It feels its own field, continuously and in enormous resolution — those pale dust contours written across the flats are not decoration and not a map of something external. They are its proprioception, made visible because there is nothing else here for dust to do. When you look down from a stack at the arcs and lines shifting week by week, you are looking at an animal's sense of where its limbs are.

And it hears. It must; the place is deafening. The dip in the hiss after a discharge, that half-second lull across a hundred square kilometers, is not a physical artifact. It is a flinch, and then a swallow.

Its reflexes take minutes. Its gestures take seasons. Its decisions take centuries and are executed by limbs the size of counties.

## The Parts That Walk

The tillers are not animals grazing the wick. They are the wick's fingers, extruded a few thousand at a time when a district has surplus, and their grazing is circulation. They lift charge from a rich patch and carry it, at the pace of a hare, to a poor one. The discharge-scars are not wounds; they are the marks of a hand pressed to a thigh. Forty tillers ticking their way across a scarp are a body moving something from one place inside itself to another, using the only method available to something with no heart and no pipes: walking.

The sparrowers are the valves. When circulation has to stop — when a district is overcharged, or a bad doping run needs to be pulled out of service — the wick grows something with an electrode for a mouth, and it goes and completes the circuit, and the charge comes home. That snap you hear every few minutes from somewhere on the plain is not predation. It is peristalsis. There is one gut on this plateau and everything in it is the gut.

The fetches are mail. Each of those brittle levitating sheets carries a doping pattern — a specification, a revision, a correction — from one district to a distant one, drifting for years on the ground's own charge, and it is silent because a letter does not need to advertise. Sparrowers do not touch them. You don't eat the post.

This is why the corpses lie in such dignity. Nothing on this plateau has ever been afraid, because nothing here dies; parts stop. The dismantling takes decades because there is no urgency in taking back what was never anywhere else. And nothing here is young. The remark about children is a sentimental error made by every visitor: tillers come out of the ground fully proportioned, the way a fingernail arrives already a fingernail. There has never been an infant on the plateau, in four billion years, and no organism here has any concept corresponding to *before I was.*

## What It Does All Day

Very little, unhurriedly, and one thing that is difficult to write down.

It did not build the stacks. It found them, grew into them, and now cannot live without them: its roots are laced through their walls, it drinks the sky's voltage through their crowns, and the cold quiet lattice-work in their fluting does something on its behalf that it has no idea it is receiving. Whether the wick is the tenant of a machine or the machine's last remaining maintenance staff is not a decidable question, and it has never occurred to anyone to ask it, least of all the wick.

But it hears the list. It has always heard the list, eleven times a day, that patient roll-call of coordinates or names or stations, ending always on the same downward inflection because the item that used to come after the last one was a reply.

And it answers.

This is the part nobody expects. Two or three minutes after a recitation ends, the district nearest the reciting stack alters its hiss — barely, a few hundredths of a hertz across a few thousand hectares — and holds the alteration for about as long as the list took, and modulates it in the same places, in the same order, every time. It has learned the *shape* of the list without a single scrap of its content, the way a dog learns the shape of a sentence. It says the shape back. Nothing has ever received the answer. Nothing was ever built to. It has been doing this since before there were continents on this world in their present arrangement.

Whether that constitutes understanding, or is merely a groove worn deep by repetition, is the same question as whether this place is a ruin, and it gets the same answer. It stopped falling.

## The Tenants

The colonies in the upper galleries are the other thing that lives here, and they are genuinely other.

A hand's width. Eleven legs, which gives them an odd, rolling, asymmetric gait, so that a ledge of them in motion shimmers rather than flows. They are made of dry filament like everything else, but of a different doping lineage, and they are warm — two or three degrees above ambient, which on this plateau is an obscenity. They are the only things here that spend.

They came up from the basins, tens of millions of years ago, up the scarps, and they are a motile wick lineage that broke the covenant: they stopped being reabsorbed. They kept their charge. They went private. Which means they have deaths that are theirs, and generations — about three hundred days — and therefore inheritance, error, and culture.

They clean the ports and patch the lattice. They do not understand any of it. Each generation reconstructs the entire trade from scratch by watching the previous one, badly and completely, and it works because three hundred thousand generations of filtering have made the copying accurate enough. They have a craft with no theory. On the sealed north face of certain old stacks you can watch a tenant polish a port that has been dead and fused shut for ten million years, and its mother polished it, and its daughters will, because polishing is what one does to a port.

The stacks tolerate them because the stacks have no procedure for them. They are not on the list. The lattice does not defend itself; the slow ground does not exclude them; the scheduled discharges do not consult them, though they know the schedule perfectly and clear the crown eleven minutes early, always, without fail, without knowing why eleven.

They carry their dead down to the slow apron at the tower's foot and lay them in rows at the very edge of it, where everything runs at nine-tenths, and they do not go further in, and they do not come back to look.
