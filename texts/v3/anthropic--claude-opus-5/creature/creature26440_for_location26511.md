## Who comes down the stair

You will meet the first one before you have decided what you are looking at. Someone is coming down toward you out of the fog on the wet stone, and she is not hurrying, and she does not look up, and about four paces before you meet she moves — without any acknowledgement at all — off the dry side of the stair and onto the green lane in the middle, giving you the good footing. Then she is past you and gone into the vapour, and you notice you have been given something, and that no transaction occurred.

They are not large people, generally. Broad in the ankle and the calf, out of all proportion to the rest, because a life here is measured in vertical metres: a woman going to the lily sheds and back climbs and descends the height of a small mountain every working day and has done since she was nine. The knees are the first thing to go and everybody knows it, and there is a whole cottage industry in liniment, in knee-wraps of felted wool, in the particular sideways descent — leading always with the same leg — that old people use on the long flights, so that from a distance you can guess an age by a gait.

They are pale in a way that is not sickly. It is the pallor of something growing well in low light. The skin is damp and fine and takes lime dust into its creases, so that hands and necks are mapped in faint white lines like the crazing on old glaze. And the eyes are large. Not poetically large — measurably, generation upon generation, they have gone the way the leaves have gone here, wide and soft to catch a thin light, with pupils that stay open at noon. Visitors find the valley's gaze unnerving and cannot say why. It is because nobody is squinting at you. You are being looked at with the whole eye.

Their clothes are the colour of whatever work they are near. Felted wool, layered, with a short shoulder cape that sheds water off the back before it can wick down; the cape is the one garment on which a person spends real money and takes real pride, and there are cape-makers whose names are known across the valley. Everything else is honest and mended. Boots are resoled until only the idea of the original boot remains, which nobody finds remarkable, since that is also true of the streets.

## Hands

The hands tell you the trade before the face tells you the mood.

Wall hands: nails thickened and squared, the fingertips permanently split at the sides from lime, the palm a single continuous callus with the tools' shapes worn into it. Cable hands: two fingers shorter than the others, often, or absent, and worn with a certain complacency. Kiln hands: hairless to the wrist, shiny.

And then the brush hands, which are the softest hands in the valley and belong mostly to women and children — narrow, quick, unmarked, kept deliberately unmarked, because a hand that catches on a stamen bruises the flower and a bruised flower yields nothing. Brush-workers do not carry, do not lift, do not lay stone. It is written into their hiring. A family with three daughters in the lily sheds will hire out the fill-tamping it owes the terrace board rather than let those hands go stiff, and will say so without embarrassment, the way people elsewhere talk about not letting a singer stand in a draught.

## Voices, and the silence they fear

They speak low, level, and without gesture, because in this air a normal voice carries three terraces and everybody has always known it. Loudness is not rudeness here; it is closer to indecency. It belongs to two occasions only: a wall going, and a child lost in fog. A raised voice therefore empties a street in eleven seconds. Visiting merchants who shout at porters are remembered for decades and are described, later, as "the one who called out on the Barley Stair," with no further commentary needed.

Their vocabulary of water has perhaps forty terms where you have three. A wall weeps, sweats, runs, bleeds, wicks, greens, beards, or sings, and these are not synonyms; they are stages, and each has a remedy, and to use the wrong one in a report is a small professional disgrace. Children are taught the sequence before they are taught to read.

And here is the thing the valley has instead of a religion, or under one: they sleep listening. Every household knows the voice of its own gutter, its own grate, the note of the drop into the sump at the corner. What frightens them is not the roar of a full drain in a storm. It is a gutter going quiet. Silence means the water has found another road, and the other road is always through something. So the valley's alarm is an absence, and people wake to it from deep sleep, in the middle of a dream, sitting straight up: *the corner has stopped.*

## Names

Nobody here is named for a virtue. Everyone competent at their work is competent in the same way, to the same standard, and so competence does not distinguish you. Deviation does.

The result is that people carry, alongside a family name, an epithet drawn from a single documented error, usually made young, and use it themselves, on paperwork. Mira Half-Course, who at nineteen laid a course of bond wrong on the third bench and was allowed to finish it before it was pointed out. Ravel Two-Grates, who cleared one and walked past the other. Old Sennit Dry-Mortar, ninety-one, still working, who is asked at least once a week what he did, and tells it.

It sounds cruel and is not. It is the same operation by which they name the thrushes in the aqueduct arches — by the flaw in the song, the swallowed third note, the one who always sings the phrase a tone low. In a place where everything is made to a standard and everyone maintains it, your error is the only part of you that is yours. To be given your name is to be told: we heard you. We were listening. We have you separate from the rest.

## The dry and the wet

The valley's real class line is not money. It is whether your work is under glass.

*Dry trade* means the glasshouses, the pharmacy works with their tiled rooms and ledgers, the pollination sheds, the winding houses, the cage-bird lofts. Warmth, lamplight, a floor, and hands that stay soft. *Wet trade* means everything outside: the walls, the fill, the drains, the kilns, the cable stations, the coppice. Wet trade is paid better and dies earlier. A wet-trade family that gets a daughter into the lily sheds speaks of it for a generation, and the daughter, coming home up eleven flights in the rain to a house of people whose lungs sound like a bellows, learns early to be quiet about how warm her day was.

The children go into the sheds at eight or nine with a rabbit-tail brush, and this is the fact about the valley that a stranger takes longest to absorb: it has no insects, so it uses its children. Twelve hundred plants a day, in warm damp yellow air, in silence, small hands doing the work that in every other place on earth is done for free by things with wings. They are not unhappy about it. It is warm, it is dry, it is skilled, and the shed at midday has the particular hush of a schoolroom during an examination. What it makes of them is a kind of person who is superb at repetitive precision and slightly astonished, all their lives, by any noise that is not water.

## What is owned

You cannot own land here in any way that would be recognisable to you, because the land is a manufactured object standing on someone else's manufactured object. What is owned instead:

**The fall.** Water rights are measured not in volume but in height — the drop between two named levels — and the great families of the valley are families who hold falls. They will sell you all the water you like and lease you the metre and a half through which it may descend.

**The fill.** Ground is capital, imported by the bucketload from the riverbed nine hundred metres below, weighed, graded, and entered in the Bench Books, which record every load laid into every terrace since the books began: weight, origin, date, tamping crew. Soil theft is punished more heavily than assault. And when a wall goes at three in the morning and forty tonnes of made ground slides into the flood channel and away downriver, the valley does not merely record damage. It grieves. Neighbours come and stand at the edge of the scar in the rain, and what they say to the owner is what elsewhere is said at a graveside.

**The duty.** Above all, downhill duty: you may not send your water, or your ground, onto the person below you. Every wall in the valley is therefore an obligation with two names on it, and a full third of the valley's paper is litigation about walls — who let the drain silt, whose bench bellied first, whether the ivy was a nuisance or a fastening. Whole works stand idle and roofless with birch trees on them because two families have been in court about a retaining wall for nineteen years. Everyone is faintly ashamed of this and no one can think what else to do, and meanwhile the terrace board sends men to buttress the disputed wall anyway, at public cost, and bills nobody.

Because that is the other thing. Whatever else you are — chemist, clerk, cape-maker, bird-breeder — you owe the Hand: so many days a year of stone. Everyone tamps. The pharmacy directors turn up on the appointed morning in old capes and are worked hard by a foreman who calls them by their epithets, and this is not a levelling ritual or a piety. It is simply that the slope requires more hands than the wet trade contains, forever, and everyone has done the arithmetic.

## The bought noise

Now the sentimental industries, which are two, and are the same industry.

The cage-birds: some thousands of them, in lofts above the warm flues, bred for song and traded with real seriousness. And the great vexation of the fancy is that the valley's birds, generation on generation, have taken up the hum. Somewhere in the last century the turbine note got into the strain, and now a good loft in the evening is a chorus of small creatures holding a low steady drone none of their ancestors ever heard, and the breeders labour to breed it out and cannot, and privately some of them like it, and there is a heretical faction that judges for it.

And the crickets. Imported in jars, up the road every spring, from a valley two days south — because summer dusk here has water in it and machinery in it and nothing at the ear, and something in the people cannot bear it. They keep them in wicker cages in the kitchen and feed them scraps of forced strawberry, and the crickets die by autumn, and are bought again. There is no economic argument for any of it. A cricket costs a morning's wage.

They will also show you, if you ask, the bee-house on the fourth bench: a handsome old stone building with a slate roof and a whitewashed interior, endowed two hundred years ago, with a salaried keeper. The bees die every year. They have always died every year — the cold, the wet, the thin light, nobody quite knows. The endowment is intact, the keeper is respected, the hives are cleaned and set out each spring, and every autumn the failure is entered in the register in the same clerkly hand. Nobody has ever moved to close it. When you ask why, they look at you with those wide-open eyes and say: because then there would be no bee-house.

## The dead

There is no bedrock, so there are no graves.

The dead go to the kilns — the same kilns, banked and cleaned, that the coppice feeds — and afterwards to the sifting house, and then into the fill. Every terrace has benches endowed with ash: a load of ash is a good load, light and stable and dry, and it is entered in the Bench Books like any other, with the name. So people here say *my grandmother is under the fourth bench of the Long Barley* and mean it flatly and exactly, and can take you to the place, and will point out that the barley there is good.

Which means that when a wall bellies out and is opened, the crew works down through fill that has names in it. There is a protocol. The ash is set aside on boards under canvas; the descendants are sent for, if there are any within reach, and if there are none the foreman says the words on their behalf, which are not a prayer but a receipt: *taken up whole, laid down whole.* And they tamp it back with the rest, and the wall goes up, and the number goes in the book.

That is the moment, if you are ever there for it, when you understand what these people are. They are not standing on their ancestors metaphorically. The valley is nine hundred years of labour and nine hundred years of the labourers, mixed, graded, and tamped to a fall of a hand's width in every pace, so the water moves.

## The rate

They are not builders; the valley was finished long ago. They are not preservers; nothing here is old in the sense of untouched, and they have no reverence for original fabric — a stone that has failed is replaced without ceremony and the old one goes into the fill. What they are is a rate. They are the counterforce, applied continuously, to gravity and rain: not a people who live in a place, but the ongoing argument by which the place continues to be there.

You will hear them, from the high terrace, as the sound of someone somewhere putting a stone back. But if you want to see one, this is the truer picture:

Sometime after two in the morning, a woman on the ninth bench sits up in the dark, because the corner has stopped. Her husband does not wake.
