# The Inhabitants

## A census, honestly taken

The trouble with counting the living here is that the counting instrument is a visitor, and visitors are fast. Anything that does not move within the span of a held breath tends to be filed as scenery.

Correct the instrument and the census inverts. The basin is not nearly empty. It is nearly *full* — more biomass per cubic meter than a rainforest, more individuals than you could number in a season — and what it lacks is not life but *event*. There are perhaps nine kinds of thing here that could be called a creature, and every one of them has arrived at the same answer to the same question, which is: *why hurry?*

Nothing hunts. That is the fact underneath all the other facts. No mouth in the drowned cordillera has ever closed on anything that minded. Every mouth is a grazing mouth, a filtering mouth, a mouth that arrives slowly at a surface and works its way along. Which is why the leaves are four meters wide and thin as a held-up hand, why nothing is armored, why nothing is poisonous, why no animal here is any color that means anything to any other animal. There is no audience. There has never been a need to be seen, or to avoid being seen, or to be quick about either.

So: begin with the ones who are so numerous and so still that the eye has already agreed to call them architecture.

## The trees, who are the population

The sheath-trees are ninety percent of everything alive, and they are alive in a way that repays attention.

Each is a stack of sealed chambers, and each chamber is a decision the tree made once and cannot revisit. It manufactures its own gas, pumps it into the new segment at the growing tip, welds the septum shut, and rises a little. But the old chambers, down near the anchor, go stale over decades; the gas leaks a molecule at a time into the water, the way the stone dissolves a molecule at a time into the water, and the bottom of the tree slowly becomes dead weight. So the tree must keep building upward or it will begin, imperceptibly, to sink.

It grows for the same reason a swimmer treads water.

This makes every sheath-tree a running ledger. Count the segments and you have its age; find the short segments and you have found its bad decades, the years it stood in another tree's shadow and could not afford much gas. A hundred-and-nine-segment stem on the north basin wall is the oldest thing anybody would think to measure, and it has been doing arithmetic with buoyancy since before the last ten Turnings.

The anchor-threads are the other half of the animal — and they are nearly an animal, these guys: spun wet from a gland at the holdfast, tensioned by the tree's own lift, retuned as the tree grows taller and pulls harder. They hum. Not audibly, mostly. You feel it in the jaw if you take hold of one. A mature colony is guyed by hundreds of them in a taut lattice and the whole lattice is under a load that would lift a boat, and it holds, year on year, in a medium with no wind to test it.

Until one parts.

Then the tree begins to rise. It takes three or four days, because drag is patient, and the ascent is the most violent thing that happens in this world: ninety meters of stem going up through forty tiers of leaves that do not touch each other, shearing every one of them, and the sound is that soft continuous rip, heard for kilometers, and then the tree breaks a lid-meadow from beneath — a slow dark bulge in the ceiling, a tearing, a bloom of freed litter going up in a column — and then another lid, and another, and at last the top of it comes out into the air, which it has never touched and cannot survive. The chambers burst in the low pressure. The stem softens within a week.

And then it floats. It is, briefly, the only object in the cordillera that is going anywhere, drifting on nothing at all across the mirror, and eventually it fetches up against a summit, and the mosses take it, and it becomes a small raft of upper forest. Every floating island above the waterline is a tree that lost an argument with its own rigging. The summits are ringed with them, a wreath of failures, greening.

## The weaver on the seam

On the interfaces, a fungus. Silvered threads, hair-fine, in mats and skeins and long combed streamers, and it is the only organism here that has made the density boundaries its entire address.

It cannot grow up. It cannot grow down. A hyphal tip pushed a centimeter into the wrong water stalls and blackens. So it grows *sideways*, forever, along a plane it did not choose and cannot leave, and it grows fast by local standards — a meter a year, which here is a gallop — and what it does as it goes is *knit*. It stitches the fallen leaves together. It laces the pollen slick into felt. Every false floor in this world is its work; every bright veined ceiling seen from below is its work seen from below. It is the closest thing to a mason the basin contains, and it builds only the horizontal.

Its fruiting bodies are absurd: little pale trumpets standing up off the seam by a finger's width, releasing spores tuned to exactly neutral buoyancy, so that the spores do not fall and do not rise but spread along the boundary for months like smoke in a room with the door shut. A whole reproductive strategy staked on a single proposition — *that there will go on being a seam* — which is, over the eleven-year cycle, demonstrably false. The Turning kills nearly all of it. It survives in the buckle, in the moments where two collapsing interfaces press against each other and a few threads are handed across like a rope thrown between boats.

Everything it builds, it builds knowing — insofar as anything knows — that the floor will someday be the ceiling, and then neither.

## Those who keep the ceilings

The clock-snails hang upside down under the lid-meadows and eat the felt, and in eating it they repair it, because the glue they walk on is silica and sets hard, and a snail's track is a mended seam.

They are silica animals, essentially; the water is thick with dissolved stone and they take it up and lay it down in overlapping plates, and so the shell is not really a shell but a wound sheet of mica, translucent, layered, and if you hold one up to the light from below you can see the last eight or nine years of the animal's deposition stacked like the pages of an old ledger. They are almost weightless. They have to be. They are grazing the underside of a floor made of dead leaves, and the only thing holding them there is the willingness of the leaves.

A hand's width a day. Two hundred years, if nothing tears. Eighteen Turnings.

The grazing tracks are the only writing in the world. They are legible and they are worth reading: the pale meander, the loops where the animal crossed its own line, the thickenings where it stopped for a season, the abrupt scallop where the ceiling gave way under it and it dropped and had to find another. And once in a great while, two tracks converge — two lines wandering a hundred meters each over a century, arriving from different directions at the same square meter of leaf, which is a coincidence so improbable that the species has arranged its entire biology around it. They join. They stay joined for a season. Then the lines diverge again and run on, and along one of them, after a while, there appear the eggs: a rosary of glass beads laid in the track, each one a lens, each one catching the light that comes up through the lid, so that a breeding female's trail is visible from thirty meters as a faint dotted line of small bright points.

The hatchlings are buoyant, and rise, and lodge against whatever ceiling is nearest, and begin.

## The coaster

Once a month, if you are lucky and patient in the way this place teaches, the meter-wide glider comes through.

Understand what you are watching. It made one movement — a single contraction that ran around the lettuce-fringe from front to back — and that was days ago, possibly in another basin, and everything since has been coasting. The body is transparent enough to read a hand through. The gut is a dark slow loop, working at a pace that makes the phrase *working* generous. There is a ring of pigment spots around the margin that is probably an eye, in the sense that it is probably sensitive to the difference between up and not-up.

What it eats is still an open question. It has a gut and the gut has contents and the contents are green, and the tissue itself is faintly green, and the honest position is that it is doing both — farming something in its own margins and topping up, occasionally, with whatever the margin brushes.

A guess that fits the evidence, and only a guess: the coasters are the sexual phase of the clouds. They are budded off, they drift for a year or two, and when two of them meet — which happens roughly as often as two snail-tracks converge, and by the same arithmetic of enormous time against tiny probability — they fuse, and sink, and the fused body comes apart into a few thousand small colonial units, which rise to their level and stay.

Nobody has seen this. Two coasters have never been seen in the same view.

When one dies it goes down at a leaf's pace, glassing over as it falls, and at some depth it finds a seam, and lies on it, and the weaver takes it, and in fifteen years it is floor.

## The clouds

Rooms of loose luminous aggregate, hanging at a chosen depth, holding station for years at a time.

Whether they are animals is not a question the cordillera has any interest in answering. They are made of many small things that could each be a plant. They adjust. That is the argument for animal: they *adjust* — each unit trimming its own density by fractions, continuously, so that the whole body stays exactly where it has decided to stay, which is a decision, which is a behavior, which is a thing a stone does not do.

They glow when the light comes at them wrong, and the glow is almost certainly waste, the same way a fever is waste.

And they know about the Turning before anything else does. Three weeks before the interfaces begin to buckle — while the warm bruise is still only a suspicion down in the dark — the clouds begin to move. Up, generally, and slowly, and all of them at once, across the whole basin, forty or fifty rooms of faint light rising a meter a day through a forest that has no idea yet. It is the single most purposeful thing that occurs in this world and it is not obvious that anybody is doing it on purpose.

Afterward, when the layers have folded through one another and the water is briefly one water and smells of cut grass and iron, the clouds are in the wrong places and thinner than they were, and over the next two years they sort themselves back out to their old depths, or to depths that are new but satisfy the same requirement, and resume keeping station, and the forest, which has by then also sorted itself, goes back to not noticing them.

## The fast ones (a short chapter)

In the moss mattresses on the summits, where the air is barely air and the water drips upward off the leaves, there is a fauna of small quick things — pale, blind, six-legged, a millimeter or two — that live in the felt and move at what would be called, anywhere else, an ordinary pace.

They are the only animals in the cordillera that could be startled.

And once or twice a season something crosses high above, between summits, dark against the mist, unhurried by any standard but this one's and therefore, here, a scandal of speed: crossing the whole visible sky in the time a clock-snail advances the width of a spore. It does not descend. It has never been seen to land, or to be interested. Its shadow goes over the mirror and every reflection in the false cordillera carries a copy of it, upside down, tiering away into the high water, so that for a moment the sky is full of it.

Then the basin is quiet again for a week, and the copies drain out of the mirrors, and the sheath-trees tick.

## The tenant of the stairs

There is nobody on the stairs.

This has to be said plainly, because the stairs make a claim and the claim is very strong: eleven regular steps in a young intrusive, a landing, a twelve-strand lattice hung with rotted planking that is only litter caught on guy-threads, and a perfect inverted twin of the whole arrangement standing upside down above it in the underside of the third interface, going up where the other goes down. Nothing built it. Nothing here builds. Fire is not a concept that would occur, and neither is the step, and neither is the idea of *arriving somewhere by means of*.

But something does live there, and it is worth being exact about what.

The basin has been arranging itself for a very long time in the absence of any eye, and in that time it has produced, by pure indifferent process, one object that is shaped like a message. It is the only structure in the world with the geometry of intention. And the moment an eye arrives — the instrument, the visitor, the thing that is too fast and cannot help it — the message is received. Every time. Without fail. Nobody has ever looked at the stairs and not felt the small cold drop of *someone was here*.

So that is the ninth inhabitant, and it is the only one that requires you in order to exist, and it lives on the landing, and it has just left, and it has been just leaving for eleven thousand years.

The clock-snail on the ceiling above the landing has been grazing the same hundred meters of that impression for a century and has put down, in glue and silica, a long pale wandering line across it, which from below looks like a signature, and is not.
