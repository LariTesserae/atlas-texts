## The Nine

They are nine, and none of them has been alive in this room longer than eleven weeks, and all of them are of it now, entirely, the way a splint becomes part of the bone it set.

That is the first thing to understand: the room is fast. It takes a person in under a month. Their voices go first. The rule against raised voices is not obeyed so much as absorbed — within nine or ten days, a new arrival's speech drops into the register the chamber permits, a low unhurried near-murmur with the ends of words fully finished, because at that volume nothing survives being slurred. Visitors, on the rare occasions there are visitors, report that everyone here sounds bereaved. They are not bereaved, mostly. They are audible.

With volume gone, emphasis moved into the hands. There is a grammar of contact here that nobody designed and everybody speaks. A palm laid flat on a forearm means *no, stop, I mean it*. Two fingers, twice, on the back of a wrist means *now*. A hand on the shoulder from behind, held, means *I am about to tell you something you won't want*. They touch each other constantly and casually and it is not affection, or not only; it is punctuation. When they laugh — they do laugh — it is with the mouth closed and the whole body shuddering and both hands pressed on the table, and it looks, from a distance, exactly like weeping, which is one reason nobody watches anybody else laugh.

Their bodies have converged. All nine are the colour of the stone flour, which is not a metaphor; the flour is in the creases of their necks and in the whorls of their ears and it has taken the tone out of their skin, so that when a person steps into the coin of daylight from the Vent their own hand startles them. All nine have cracked lips and a dry cough with a particular two-beat shape to it, and each one can identify the other eight by that cough alone, through a wall, at forty paces. All nine have one burn on one palm — the right, mostly — a shine of scar tissue the size of a coin, from touching the chamber wall the once. Nobody warns new arrivals. It is the room's only initiation and it is entirely self-administered.

They sleep in the twenty minutes after an exhalation, when the air is soft, and they sleep hard, and the tick of the growing rock is inside their sleep now, load-bearing. Two of them have gone down the throat on rotation leave and come back early. Silence, out in the world, turns out to be a hole they fall through.

**Eleven** attends the stint-frames. Her name is Sela and no one has used it in six weeks. She walks the perimeter twice a shift, four hundred paces the long way, and she does not look at the frames — she has learned that looking directly turns a column of dark brilliance into a grey armature and a plinth, and she needs the brilliance, because that is where the information is. So she walks slightly turned away, head cocked, reading eleven refusals out of the corner of her eye, and she can tell you which one is carrying more than its share today the way a farrier can tell you which leg.

She is deaf in the left ear. Not from a shout. From standing eight metres from the aggregate plinth of Frame Four when it took the whole load for eleven seconds during the week-four incident, and the pressure came back out of the ceiling and found her.

She keeps the patch schedule. There is a hairline in the crown of the chamber, mended, and Eleven inspects it every eighth day and writes *no change* in the log, and everybody knows the patch is the shape of a scream, and nobody says so, including Eleven, who has calculated privately that the ceiling has a further eleven such screams in it before the arithmetic stops working.

**Plate** runs the water. She signs the log *M.* and has never corrected anyone about the rest. She is the author of the second rule — *water is measured at the plate, not at the tap* — and she means it as theology as well as accounting. Four hundred litres a day, two exhalations, three cisterns; she knows the number before the number is counted. She tastes the condensate off the ribs every morning with the tip of her tongue and can tell you from the metal in it what the floor is doing six hundred metres down. She is the only person here who ever describes the mountain as having a mood, and she does it in the flattest possible voice, as a datum: *it's sour today, we'll get a long second breath.* She is right about it so often that the others have stopped finding it eerie, which is its own kind of eerie.

**The Jacks** are two men who are not related and move like they were poured from the same ladle. Individually, on the roster, *Left* and *Right*; between themselves, nothing — they don't need names, they work by contact, a knuckle to a hip meaning *lift*, a thumb to a shoulder blade meaning *hold*. Every eight days they unbolt a section of street, jack it a storey, and weld it down, and then they go below and seal what has been left. They are the only two who spend hours in the dead levels, in the deep dull orange, in the kiln-and-matchhead smell, and they come up with their eyebrows singed off and drink their ration standing.

Once a month one of them goes down alone and sits on the stairwell above Level Six and does not open it. There are two names cast into that plate. One of them is nothing to him. The other used to be his relief.

**Ozanne** keeps the log, and keeps her name, because writing names is what she does. She invented the heading *behaviours*, and she is unrepentant about it: she says the alternative was to leave the fur out of the record entirely, and the record is the only thing this room actually produces. She writes in a hand so small that the flour settling on the page is a legibility problem, and she sweeps her own desk every forty minutes with a badger brush, and she keeps a second book that is not the log, in which she has drawn one hundred and six rosettes of sinter fur, dated, and in which — on the last page, in pencil, so it can be denied — she has begun a tally of flicks with columns for *where* and *which eye*.

**Flour** is seventy-one and sweeps. He was a tunneller for fifty years and his lungs are already a landscape; the room can do nothing further to him, and so he does the work that would ruin anyone else, all day, every day, the crown-flour that drifts down so slowly it seems to hang. He gave his mask away in his second week to the youngest person here and has refused four replacements. He sweeps in long even passes with a rhythm nine or ten seconds to the stroke, and the whole town uses it as a clock, and on the two days he was ill people found they could not concentrate. He has said, once, in the kitchen, that a room where the dust is allowed to lie is a room that has agreed to close.

**Ivo** cooks and mends. One person for both, because both are heat and measure. He costs dinner in watts before he costs it in food, because everything he warms must be carried out through the sink-plates and thrown at the sky, and so a hot meal here is a decision made against the water supply. He is very good at it. He can put warmth into eight people for less than the price of a lamp. He also sets bones, pulls teeth, listens to chests with an ear rather than a stethoscope because the stethoscope amplifies the tick, and holds the only sealed opinion on the state of all nine pairs of lungs. He does not share it. He has started, lately, cooking one thing per week that is not efficient.

**Under-Assessor Emeric Vall, Office of Standing Works,** arrived in week six to determine whether the room should continue, since the room's only product is proof that the room can be held. He was given the full mouthful of his title for four days, then *Assessor*, then *Vall*. He stopped writing his report in week nine. Nobody has asked him why. What they have noticed is that he now walks the coin of daylight all eleven minutes of its crossing with his sleeves pushed up, and that he has begun, unasked, to sweep the western approach where Flour cannot reach before the plates need him, and that last Tuesday Plate said *thanks, Em* across the table and he answered to it without a flicker, and Ozanne wrote nothing down and thought about it for a long time.

**Tessin** found the room. She was the youngest of the bore-crew, eleven weeks ago, when the bit went through into a bubble that the rock had blown while it was still deciding to be rock, and she was first through the hole, and she has not gone back up the throat since — not on rotation, not on leave, not when they told her to. She is called *the Ear*. She predicts the exhalations to the minute and she does it lying on the floor with her cheek against the cast stone, and she is never wrong by more than ninety seconds, and she does not consider this a skill; she considers it listening, and is puzzled that the others need instruments.

She is the one who screamed. Week four, Level Six, when the floor came up eleven hours ahead of the model and the heat and the gas made the decision that had to be made and the Jacks welded the plate down with her brother behind it. The hairline in the ceiling is hers. She looks at the patch every morning, first thing, standing under it in the middle of the black floor, and then she goes to work.

She is also the one who tends the fur, though *tends* is not in the log.

Because there is something else living here, if you are generous with the word, and nobody working in this room is a biologist.

The sinter fur has taken four square metres of the eastern fan and grows a millimetre a week toward whatever moves through it, and what moves through it, twice a day, is the mountain's breath — and, twice a day, standing in that breath with their arms out and their eyes shut, is however many of the nine can be spared. This is how they wash. There is no water for washing. You stand in the exhalation and let it condense on you and scrape down and dress, and while you stand there you are a warm obstruction in a flow, and the fur reads you as weather.

There are seven rosettes on the east wall now that are the negative of a standing human body. They are not portraits. They are the shape of an absence in the vapour, patiently reached toward, over weeks, by something with no reason to care. Ozanne has drawn all seven.
