## The people

Take one of them at rest, which is rare, since rest here is a posture rather than an activity — they lean, back to a wall, one foot up out of the water on the other's instep.

Short. Deep through the chest in a way that reads as heaviness until you see them walk, which is the walk of a light person. The ribcage is a bellows built to strain a thin dark-blue air, and the blood inside them is nearly black, and their hearts are slow: you can put a hand on a working mason's breastbone and count a pulse that seems, for the first few beats, to have stopped. Everyone's hands are pale and softened at the pads from wet work. Everyone's feet have the drowned look, permanently. And everyone's boots — no matter the town, no matter the trade — carry the same tide-mark three millimetres up from the welt, a pale line of dissolved lime, the whole civilisation wearing the height of the Sheet like a rank badge.

The eyes are the striking thing. Heavy-lidded, set deep under a shelf of brow that is half bone and half habit, because they spend their lives narrowing against a floor that shines. They walk with the head slightly down and the hand slightly up, shading, and this gives every plateau-dweller the aspect of someone reading a difficult letter. They do not look at the horizon. There is nothing there and it is not there. They look at the fourth pace ahead of them, always, and their gait is the flat-footed testing shuffle of a person crossing a room in the dark — which is what it is. The ground looks like distance. You learn to stop asking your eyes and start asking your soles.

They cannot judge how far away anything is and they know it, and they have simply stopped valuing the skill. Nobody points. To indicate a place, you give a cairn-count and a wind-quarter, and if you are unsure you say so, and saying so is not an embarrassment but a courtesy.

## The forty paces

Their voices are for one other person, at the range of a shared blanket. This has shaped them more than the cold has.

To speak to a plateau-dweller is to be near them. Their conversation is conducted forehead to forehead or cheek to cheek, and when the wind is up they will put a palm flat on your sternum to take the voice through the bone. Strangers do this. It is not intimacy; it is telephony. Their whole affect at close range is intent and unblinking and, to an outsider, faintly amorous, and their affect at ten paces is nothing at all, because at ten paces they are alone.

So they have built out. Every town is strung with cord: greased, lamp-blacked, drawn taut on stone posts from house to house, and each has a knuckle of bone at the household end. A cord is plucked, not tugged — a low pulse you feel in the teeth of the bone if you are holding it. There are perhaps three hundred cord-figures in common use and every child knows two hundred of them by five years old. *Come. Bring the pot. Water at the fleece. Someone is early. Don't look at it.* Long messages are chalked on slate and worn on the back, between the shoulders, so that the person walking behind you is reading you. In a market you will see forty people moving in slow single-file loops, each reading the back of the one ahead, an entire public discourse conducted as a shuffling queue.

They do not laugh audibly. Laughter does not survive the curtain, and to laugh into someone's ear is unpleasant, so laughter is a gesture — two fingers flicked open from the closed fist, at the level of the collarbone. Whole rooms of them will sit flicking their fingers in silence. It is the single most disconcerting thing about them and they consider it perfectly obvious.

## The labour of difference

Here is what they actually do all day, and it is not what a visitor assumes.

A visitor sees a people who build. In fact they are a people who *distinguish*, and building is only the largest instance of it.

Every stone that goes into an ordinary wall must be given a quirk: a nick, a struck rosette, a deliberate wrong angle at one hidden corner. This is not decoration. Two stones cut to the same dimension from the same seam and set the same way will start, over a season, to be one stone in two places, and a wall made of merged stones is a wall that is only half there, and it will fall, and worse — it will fall in two towns at once. So the mason's first act on any block is to spoil it. There is a chisel for the purpose, worn dull on purpose, held in the off hand. The quirk is the mason's own mark, and every family of masons guards its quirk the way a lowland house would guard a name, and a wall can be read like a census: nine courses of Tesk's people, then a break, then the small three-dot quirk of somebody's aunt who was working alone that autumn because of a quarrel.

The corollary is that old walls are dangerous. Weather takes the quirks first — they are shallow by design — and an unmaintained wall slowly loses its capacity to be itself. Ruins here do not simply crumble; they get *confused*. Stones go missing without being taken, leaving sockets of dry air that the surveyors come and measure. A very old unattended wall will subside into a low anonymous ridge that seems to be in several places, and children are forbidden it, and adults walk round it wide.

This is why the towns are rebuilt endlessly on their own rubble. Not vanity, not disaster. Maintenance. Their mounds are six metres of accumulated insistence.

## The unlikeness

They do the same for themselves.

No two living people on the plateau share a name; the naming is administered from the Common Room and it is one of the few genuinely centralised institutions. Siblings are put into different trades as a matter of course, and dressed differently from weaning, and taught not to synchronise. There is a low steady social pressure against sameness that a newcomer experiences as a kind of eccentricity-mandate: somewhere between the ages of nine and thirteen every child takes an *unlikeness* and keeps it for life. A filed tooth. A single silver-grey wire braided at the left temple. A cultivated limp on level ground. One sleeve always rolled, in any weather, at any temperature, forever. The unlikeness is chosen alone, over some weeks, and is never explained to anybody, and asking after it is an obscenity of the mild domestic kind, like asking a stranger's income.

Old couples are watched. So are lifelong work-partners, and twins, obviously, and the very devoted. When two people begin to walk the same walk and finish each other's half-said sentences and take the same illnesses in the same week, their neighbours will send them apart for a season, kindly, with gifts. Sometimes it does not work. There are perhaps a dozen people on the plateau at any time who are agreed to be one person in two bodies, and they are treated with enormous gentleness and a certain horror, and they are never both present at a measurement, or at a birth, or in the Common Room.

## The surveyors

The only priesthood, and their doctrine is difference, and their sacrament is being wrong.

They walk in pairs, and the pairs are constructed to be unalike: a tall one and a short one, a talker and a silent, one who counts in fives and one who counts in threes. A pair that begins to converge is broken and reassigned immediately, without appeal, no matter how good its work has been — in fact especially if its work has been good, because two surveyors who have become one surveyor will measure the world and find, with perfect confidence, that it agrees with them.

You cannot measure with one thing. That is the whole of their theology. It takes a cord and a bob; it takes a knot and a hand; it takes a claim and a correction. Their greeting is to state the distance to the nearest cairn, aloud, and to be told a different number, and to thank the person who told them. Novices find the thanking difficult and are made to do it a great deal. Their prayer is the current length of the north–south trunk road, recited from memory, which takes about forty minutes; it is different every year, and the difference is the prayer.

They are unpopular in the way that useful institutions are. They are also the only people who go out alone into the deep spacing between fog-trees for weeks, and they come back thinner and quieter, and they do not talk about the middle distance.

## The potters

Clay, fired. The one craft on the plateau that cannot make two of anything.

The kiln is chaos, and chaos is the scarcest resource here, and the potters are its priests as surely as the surveyors are the priests of the cord. Every household has one pot that is the household's — irregular, unrepeatable, faintly wrong in the neck — and a person's name is, in the practical sense, that person's cup. To be given a cup by a family is to be adopted. To break your own cup on purpose is the local form of suicide, and it is done in advance of the actual death, sometimes years in advance, by people who wish to go quietly into the general.

The potters hold that the flattening is not decay. They say the plateau is not wearing out but arriving; that the cushion, one green thing under the whole stone skin, is not a survivor but a *graduate*; that the distinction between things was always a temporary provision and the surveyors are lamplighters shouting at the dawn. They dispute the fetch-theory. They say the fetches are not the habit of animals but the beginning of the general — the plateau practising being one thing that moves.
