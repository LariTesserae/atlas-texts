## The tenant

Start with the heron, because the heron is the only large animal in the chamber that chose to stay, and it is the one that has paid the most for the privilege.

It is a grey heron, and it is no longer grey. The mineral has done to it what it does to the chevron and the coat on the hook: the flight feathers have a crust along the shafts, thin as eggshell, that flakes and re-forms; the crown plumes have fused into a single stiff quill that stands up off the back of the skull like a dropped pin. The legs are banded. If you know how to read the brickwork you can read the bird — a dull green line high on the tarsus where it stands through the flood of the day, then the black zone, then clean bone-coloured scale below. It is calibrated to the same instrument as the room.

It obeys the rules. That is the remarkable thing. Once every eleven minutes, on the knock, it shivers — a fast shrug that runs from the shoulder to the wingtip and back, half a second, and then it is stone again. Nobody taught it. It arrived able to stand still for hours, as all herons can, and the chamber began quietly to bury it, and somewhere in its first winter it developed the tremble, and that tremble is the only reason it is a bird and not a small white column with a beak. It shifts its weight. It is the most obedient thing down there.

It has stopped being a heron in several other respects. It does not display. It does not build. It has no season, because there is nothing here to tell it what month it is; the water rises at dawn whether that dawn is in June or January, and the light is nineteen lamps at eight watts, and so the bird's year has flattened out into one very long afternoon. The pectoral muscle has gone. Put a hand on its breast, if it would let you, and you would find the keel standing out like the ridge of a roof; it can no longer fly, only fall with dignity from one column to another, wings half-open, legs scrabbling for the capital. It gets about the chamber the way an elderly man gets about a house he knows: badly, and without hurry, and never falling.

It made a sound, once. One of the two people who come down heard it — the older one, twenty-odd years ago, before she was the older one. The bird opened its bill and produced a rasp, the *frank* that a heron throws over a river at dusk, and the moonmilk took it whole, two metres and gone, no ring, no answer. It has not tried again. She wrote the date on the slate and nothing else, and you can still find it: a date, no measurement, no initials.

It eats young eels, shrimp by the hundred, and about once a fortnight a rat, which it kills with an efficiency that makes the rats' scheduling look less like habit and more like policy. It strikes on the stroke because the stroke shakes down midges and the shrimp come up for them and the eels come up for the shrimp; the whole food chain in the chamber is on the same eleven-minute payroll. Forty-odd strikes a day. It has not missed since anyone started counting, which is not a mystery: in still, clear water, in a fixed light, with a bird that has had a decade and a half to learn the refraction from that particular column at that particular height, missing would be the mystery.

It is not the first. On the base of the second column, three millimetres under, there is a white lace of small bones in the anatomical arrangement of a standing bird that lay down. Different flood; different decade; nobody's relative. The two people found it, looked at it for a while, and left it exactly where it was, and the current heron stands on that column perhaps one day in seven, for no reason that has anything to do with sentiment. It is building its own plinth on the third column, a hummock of guano gone hard as sugar loaf, and it is now standing about four centimetres higher than it did when it arrived.

## The two hundred

The eels are not, properly speaking, residents. They are a waiting room.

Every one of them came up the outfall as a glass eel, transparent as a fingernail paring, on the flood tide of some estuary and then through miles of culvert, and every one of them found this chamber — cool, unvarying, full of shrimp, empty of otters and cormorants and men with nets — and did the only sensible thing. They grew. They stopped being yellow eels. They began to silver: the belly went to bright metal, the gut shrank, the eyes swelled to twice the diameter they need down here, because a silver eel is building a body for the deep blue Atlantic. And then the road out closed, or was never open, and the metamorphosis stalled where it stood.

So: two hundred animals with oceanic eyes in an eight-foot cistern, pale because pigment is expensive and light is nearly absent, arm-length and thickening, unhurried to the point of insult. They will not breed. Not one of them will ever put an egg anywhere. Whatever it costs an animal to be permanently mid-departure, they are paying it, and they lie in loose knots and pay it, and they are probably forty years old apiece, and some of them are older than that.

The alignment is the dynamo. The little brass and mahogany machine under its glass bell throws a stray field, feeble but absolutely steady, and it is the only magnetic statement in a chamber lined with iron; and an eel in silver dress navigates by exactly that. Every one of them is oriented to a magnetic south-west that begins and ends nine metres away on a mahogany base. They are all facing the Sargasso. It is under a bell jar. When the engine's brushes were re-bedded in 1968 and the dynamo ran a fortnight backwards, the knots turned round. It is in the ledger, in a hand that clearly thought it was recording a fault with the electrics.

## Households

Under the pier there are eleven brick niches and eleven crayfish, one each, and the arrangement has not changed in six years except at the fourth niche, whose occupant is missing its left claw and has been missing it since before the arrangement was noticed. They are the colour of a fingernail and they have the manners of clerks. There is no visible aggression; there is simply never a twelfth.

The rats are the only citizens who could leave and don't. Fat, grizzled, mild, they know the eleven minutes better than the people do — they will sit on the wet lip of the low walkway with the water an inch off their feet, doing nothing at all, and then move on the knock, all of them, in one soft rush, and then sit again. They are not afraid of the heron. They are afraid of the heron for about ninety seconds after it strikes, and then they stop being, which is not stupidity; it is the correct reading of a predator that fires once every eleven minutes.

The cave crickets keep their hand's-span courtesies along the moonmilk, and the courtesy is not politeness but hunger — the paste is thin fare, and a cricket's territory here is measured in the number of springtails that will wander into it per week. The springtails are the grey drifts, and the drifts are not deposits, they are populations, several million of them living on the fungal bloom in the corners where the walkways sweat. The snails write on the wall and the wall keeps it. Their grazing script is legible for months, which means the north wall is a palimpsest of every snail that has crossed it since spring, and the two people can and do tell you which trails are this year's.

And the shrimp, in numbers that make the shallows look as though they are simmering. Everything in the chamber is, in the end, made of shrimp.

## The wall

The largest living thing down there is the wall, and this is not a figure of speech.

The moonmilk is a mat: filamentous bacteria in a paste of calcite needles, each needle a few microns long, laid down by the thing that lives in it as the by-product of getting a living out of lime and iron and the vanishingly small amount of carbon that seeps through eight metres of London. It is centimetres thick and it covers hundreds of square metres and it is all one continuous organism, more or less, in the sense that a lichen is or a lawn is. Weigh it against every eel, rat, cricket and heron in the chamber and the animals lose by an order of magnitude.

It is also the reason for the quiet. The needle mat is a perfect acoustic dead-field: sound goes in and does not come out, and so the room that swallowed the heron's one cry, and swallows every dropped spanner, is not a room with a property. It is an animal with an appetite. The chamber does not absorb noise. Something eats it.

It holds a fingerprint for a decade. On the wall behind the stair there are, at about shoulder height, some hundreds of fingerprints in low relief, overlapping, of every person who has come down since the war, because everybody touches it, and it keeps everybody, and it has kept them longer than most of them have kept themselves. That is the only archive in the chamber that isn't chalk.

## The economy

Midges, in the vault. Chironomids, non-biting, breeding in the drip-lines of the flowstone where the seep water films over the brick; larvae in the wet, adults on the wing for a day or two in a roof nobody has ever been up to look at. They do not swarm, because swarming is a thing you do in a shaft of evening light and there is no evening. They walk to each other on the ceiling instead. Their wings are reduced — not gone, but short, cheap, unserious. They are the base of the whole economy and they have quietly given up the one thing a fly is for.

Twice a day the beam knocks and the vault jolts and it rains midges, and the shrimp come up, and the eels come out, and the heron moves once.

The elder root is not a plant so much as a very slow visitor from another country. A metre of it, white and blind, shaggy, dripping. Above it, in a yard, is an elder bush that has no idea; and the eels gather under the drip because the water off that root carries a little sugar and a little rot and a smell of the surface, and it is the closest any of them will get to the smell of a river.

## The threshold

The stair has its own fauna, and it is a different one, and it stops at step nine.

Cave spiders — the big orb-weavers, palm-sized, glossy, wholly harmless and shaped like something that will not be argued with. Their sheets and their pearl egg sacs hang in the corners from about step five to step twelve, and then nothing. Below step nine there is nothing in the air to catch. They are a border population. They live off the crane flies and moths that come in at the hatch and die on the way down, and they never go further, and they never come up, and their whole world is a three-metre band where the smell changes from street to iron.

The two people put a hand on the wall at step nine every time they pass. That is why the print archive is where it is.

## The two

You had the number right, from the chalk. What the chalk does not say is that they are one and a half.

The older one has been coming for twenty-six years. Her initials are on the slate about six hundred times. She was the last person whose job this was — not the last engineer, there was never an engineer, but the last name on a rota when there was still a rota, and when the post was abolished by a letter of two paragraphs she kept the key, had a copy cut at a shop in a market that no longer exists, and went on going down at fortnightly intervals on her own time. She is not maintaining the engine. She wants that understood. Nobody is maintaining the engine; the pattern shop that could make its parts shut in 1931. She greases four bearing caps she can reach with a long-handled cup, wipes the ledger slate with the heel of her hand, reads the tide bands off the brick, and times the stroke against a mechanical pocket watch, because the field around the dynamo is unkind to anything cleverer.

The stroke was ten minutes fifty-one seconds in 1974. It is eleven minutes four seconds now. She is the only person alive who knows the engine is slowing, and she knows to within a second and a half, and she has done the arithmetic and put the answer nowhere. It is not on the slate. She has decided, on grounds she has never articulated, that it would be a discourtesy to write it down.

She does not speak down there any more, and she has to be careful in the first hour afterwards, because she comes up too quiet and people lean in.

The other is newer. Three years, perhaps eleven visits. Came down originally with a survey that got cancelled, brought a camera, and lost a whole afternoon to the pulse — every exposure of every plate came back with a brightening across it like the room was breathing on the lens, which of course it was — and then stopped bringing the camera. He is the one who re-chalks the line. He is learning the taps: one for *here*, two for *coming across*, three for *wait*, and the long soft slide of a ring down the rail for *it's late*, which he has heard twice and which he says is the worst sound he knows, in the way that no sound at all can be a sound.
