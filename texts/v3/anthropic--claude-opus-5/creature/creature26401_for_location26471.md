## What Lives on the Shelf

Most of what is alive at Aunhal is only visiting, in the long sense. The birds are the loudest citizens and the least committed: they arrive in a rush in the spring, hold the cliff for five months, and pour off it, and the colony that returns is not quite the same colony. The swifts touch nothing. The choughs go over the buttress to feed on some upland pasture nobody on the ledge has ever seen. The falcons hold the eastern rib as a possession, but a falcon's possession is the air, and the rib is only where the plucking happens. Spores blow in. Seeds come stuck to feet. Frog eggs come the same way, so that even the frogs — fifty thousand of them, the substance of the place, the sound of it — are technically a population that can be topped up from outside.

One lineage cannot. One lineage came once, and has never gone anywhere since, and cannot be reinforced, and cannot leave, and for which the sixty paces are not a habitat but the entire extent of the world and everything possible in it.

That is the shrew-like thing. It is the only permanent resident of Aunhal in the strict sense of the word, and if the ledge can be said to be inhabited, it is inhabited by roughly forty animals weighing, all told, about two pounds.

---

**The body.** Call it a shrew and you will not be far wrong; the teeth say so, with their iron-red tips, and the pointed shifting nose, and the smell, which is sharp and oily and unmistakable in an enclosed space. But it is nearly three times the mass of any mainland relative — a heavy little animal, twenty-two or twenty-four grams, the weight of a hen's egg, with a blunter skull and a shorter tail than it ought to have, and the tail is thickened at the base with fat. This is the ordinary bloating that happens to small mammals when you put them somewhere with no competitors and no ground-hunting predators worth the name and nowhere at all to go. It has been happening here for a long time.

The fur is the finest thing about it. Slate-dark, with a warm rust flush along the flank that you only see when the animal turns in low sun, and so dense that water will not enter it: spray beads and rolls, drizzle rolls, rising drizzle rolls off the underside. The animal walks all day through wet and remains a dry room carried through a wet place. When one dies and the pelt finally soaks, it shrinks to almost nothing, and you understand that most of what you were looking at was air held in place.

Its eyes are small and probably poor. Its ears are all but buried. In a place with a permanent broadband roar, hearing at any distance is worthless, and the ordinary twittering conversations of its kind have been abandoned — it makes almost no sound, and what it makes it makes into another animal's fur, mouth pressed to the ribs, which is the only reliable channel left. Everything else is done by whisker, by touch of the nose-tip, by scent smeared on stone at knee-height of a mouse: a whole legible city written on the rock in grease, which the calcite is slowly glazing over and preserving, so that the back wall holds ten thousand years of tufa and also, in its outermost skin, last week's arguments.

**The dry floor.** There is almost no dry ground on Aunhal. There is one kind: the inside of the four walls the people built.

Under the schist plates, in the angle of the boulder, out of the drip and out of the updraught, the hearth-lens has kept its charcoal dry for however many centuries. Charcoal is a superb insulator and a superb place to dig. The shrews have riddled all four hearths — galleries and side-chambers, a black powder pushed out at the entrances, nests balled up of hair-lichen and moss-stem and, in one, a grey unravelled fuzz that is unmistakably three-strand plant-fibre cord. They are living in the houses. They line their beds with the rope and the fowling nets. The people's bone middens are their quarry for gnawing calcium, and the people's cistern, which still works perfectly, is the reliable drinking place in the frost hours when everything on the surface has gone to rime.

Their bones are in the charcoal at every level of it, from the top down to the schist, which tells you they were here through all the fires, and nothing whatever about how they came. The favoured guesses: a pregnant female inside a raft of nest material dropped by a raven; a stowaway in a basket lowered on the rope. Both are unprovable. It is worth saying plainly that a single pregnant animal, once, is enough, and that this is the likeliest sort of miracle there is.

**The second storey.** Under the moss cushions — a foot deep, twenty species, welded into a continuous mat over the ledge's middle third — there is a roofed country. The shrews have runways through it: tunnels at the base of the stems, floored with old rotting moss and shrew-dung, ceilinged with living green, invisible from above and open to the sky nowhere along their length. A cushion the size of a hearthstone may have four entrances and eleven feet of gallery. They have been maintained by use for so long that the moss grows differently over them, in a slightly paler line, and if you crouch at the right angle in the gold afternoon light you can read the whole network off the surface like veining in a leaf.

Almost everything that matters to this animal happens in there, in the dark, under six inches of wet green, within a body-length of frogs.

**The hunt, and the sound.** The frogs are the food. They are the size of a thumbnail and they live in the water held between the moss stems, which is to say they live inside the shrew's corridors, and a hunting shrew is essentially walking down a passage in a granary. It takes them in the mouth, kills with a bite through the back of the head, eats the whole animal, and does this some seventy times between dusk and midnight. It also takes snails, and here is the reason its life is short: the snails of Aunhal, drinking lime all their lives, have shells like crockery, and a shrew's teeth do not regrow. Fourteen months is a long life. The teeth end it.

But the extraordinary thing is how it locates anything at all in that roar. It does not listen for frogs. It listens for *holes*.

The click-sheet at dusk has no gaps in it. When a chough lands and begins probing, the frogs within a yard of it stop, and a small circle of silence opens in the sheet and travels with the bird. When a shrew's own nose comes down a runway, the same thing happens ahead of it. So the animal moves through a sound-field it can read negatively: a hunting shrew works the *edge* of its own silence, striking at the boundary where the clicking is still going on, and it reads other silences, elsewhere, as other predators, and freezes. The sheet is simultaneously its larder, its map and its alarm. Nothing here needed to invent a warning cry, because fifty thousand frogs are already shouting the position of everything that walks.

And at some temperature near midnight the frogs all stop within a minute, and the map goes out. Then the shrews go home. That door closing at midnight, which sounds to a person like an enormous quiet, is a curfew, and it is obeyed.

**Noon.** They should be crepuscular. They are not, quite. The best hours belong to the white room: when the cloud floor comes up the cliff and the whole ledge is inside it, visibility nine feet, everything running with condensate, the frogs are encouraged out into the open in broad daylight, and no falcon can hunt a thing it cannot see. Two or three hours, most days, in which the surface of Aunhal belongs to the shrews. They come out from under the moss and forage on top of it, in the white, in the roaring, small dark animals moving over a green floor with nothing above them and nothing below them and no distance in any direction — and this is the closest they come to being visible, and it is the reason almost nobody has ever seen one.

**The census, and where the surplus goes.** Twelve to twenty breeding females, each holding a basin or a run of cushion, intolerant to the point of murder. Two or three litters a season. Which means the ledge produces perhaps two hundred young a year and can hold forty animals, and every year the difference has to be disposed of, and there is nowhere to disperse to.

It goes two ways. A daughter may inherit — a female will slacken her boundary against one or two of her own, and the mothers' territories subdivide and re-fuse across generations like fields in a small country. Everything else, chiefly the young males, is pushed east along the narrowing ledge into the wet ribs and the falcon-whitened stone, where the cover thins to nothing and the stacks stand in their nine careful columns with their bird-skulls looking out. There is no cover there. That is where the pellets are. Break one open and it is fur and rusty-tipped teeth. The falcons of Aunhal are, in a real accountancy, funded by the ledge's surplus sons.

On the coldest nights — and there is frost most nights of the year — animals that cannot bear each other for eleven months lie down together in the charcoal in knots of five or six, and go into a shallow torpor, and the truce holds until the second hour of sun. There is nothing sentimental in it. There is simply nowhere else, and they get cold, and they know it.

**The edge.** This is the whole shape of the animal, and the thing you cannot see by looking at one.

Any shrew that ever went to the lip and over is not an ancestor of anything. For however many thousand generations, every impulse toward the bright open country beyond the last cushion has been sifted out of this lineage, once, permanently, with no appeal and no learning involved. What is left will not cross bare rock in daylight. Will not climb the outer face of the tufa. Will not follow a runway that opens into brightness — turns at the light as if at a wall. Their tunnels come to within three paces of the edge and then run parallel to it, for the whole sixty paces, a long careful hem, and the shrews of Aunhal have worn a border into the moss along the boundary of the world.

They have no fear of the void, because they have never conceived it. They simply do not possess that direction. It has been removed from them the way a coastline removes an inland habit — and so an animal weighing less than an egg carries, in its refusals, an exact negative cast of a two-mile drop.

**The last rule, restated.** Forty animals. Two hundred born a year and a hundred and sixty gone — into the pellets, into the frost, into the void that does not return things. And the total unchanged, for as long as there has been moss on the terrace and lime in the water and frogs in the wet.

Which means there is a defensible way of saying that Aunhal is inhabited not by forty animals but by one: a single small dark creature, four thousand years long, standing still.
