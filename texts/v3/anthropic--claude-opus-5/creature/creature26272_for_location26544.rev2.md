## Census

Three things you will see. Three you will not. One that arrived with you.

The seen ones are easy to miss the point of. You will watch the birds because they are the only fast thing in a landscape with no other motion, and you will crouch over a pool and find the shrimp because someone told you to, and if you are very lucky you will meet the goat. Then you will go down and say: almost nothing lives there.

That is the wrong way round. The basin is not sparsely inhabited. It is *completely* inhabited, wall to wall, floor to rim, to a uniform depth of about two millimetres — and the inhabitant made every shape you were admiring.

## What the Wall Carries

Start with the food, because nothing here makes any.

There is no green because there is no point in green. Photosynthesis is a way of eating light, and light in this bowl is a hazard rather than a resource: shadowless, ultraviolet-loaded, coming up off the calcite as hard as it comes down off the sky. Anything that stood in it with a chloroplast would be bleached to paste inside a week, and then plated.

But the water is food. Two decades under the plateau, moving sideways through joints in the dark, strips the oxygen out of it and loads it instead with the reduced things that rock gives up when it is leaned on long enough: ferrous iron, manganese, a little sulfide, a whisper of methane. That water arrives at the lip of the headwall having been, in effect, charged. Then it meets the thin bright air and begins, violently, to discharge.

That reaction is the basin's sun. It is seven hundred metres tall, it is vertical, it never sets, and its output was fixed twenty years ago underground. There is no season in it, no day in it, no drought year and no wet year. The energy budget of the entire basin is a flat line drawn a very long time ago.

Everything below follows from that.

## The Skin

Take a chip off a column — you shouldn't, but take it — and look at the broken edge in good light.

Under the glassy outer glaze there is a band, half a millimetre thick, the colour of weak tea or of apricot or, on the old grey fins, of ink. Below that band the stone is dead white all the way down.

The band is the population. It is the thing that lives here.

They are single cells, and there are no plants among them and nothing you could call an animal. They oxidise the iron; that is the ochre. They oxidise the manganese; that is the black varnish on the old high fins that you took for weathering. Some of them do something quieter with sulfur, and in the still air of a quiet pocket you can smell it, a thin struck-match smell under the wet-stone smell, which is the only odour the basin has.

They live *inside* the rock, in the first fraction of a millimetre, because the glaze above them is a sunscreen and the glaze is their own manufacture. They are laying down the very material that is burying them. So the life of a cell here is a slow vertical climb through its own product, at a millimetre a year, forever, with the dead left behind and below as chalk.

This is why the columns are the shape they are.

The rate of accretion is chemistry's — the degassing, the pressure, the fixed thin air. Nobody argues with that. But chemistry decides only *how much*. It does not decide *where*, and where is decided by mats: by which face of a fin is thick with cells and which is bare, by the microscopic ledges their slime holds open, by the way a film thickens at a lip and thins in a hollow. Fluting, terracing, the rimstone dams, the shelved crowns, the long soft arcades — that is not mineral doing an imitation of masonry. It is masonry. The masons are 0.002 millimetres long and have been at it without a break since before the ice, and the reason there is not one worked edge in the basin is that they do not work edges. They work surfaces, all the surfaces, all at once, evenly, at one millimetre a year.

The standing wave has a metabolism. The shape is not merely persisting; it is being *held*.

## The Shrimp

Two millimetres, translucent, blind, one pool in twenty.

They are blind in a place with more light than any of them could ever use, which is worth sitting with. Their ancestors' eyes were not lost to darkness; they were lost to uselessness. Vision needs contrast and contrast needs shadow, and the basin abolished shadow. What is left is touch, and a chemical sense of the pool's rim, and a feel for which way the water is going over the lip.

Which way the water is going over the lip is the whole of their geography. Everything spills downhill. A shrimp cannot climb a rimstone dam, cannot cross the dry-bright gap between two crowns, cannot do anything at all but graze the floor of the cupped hand of water it is in until the day it is carried over the edge into the next one down. So a lineage moves in one direction, at perhaps a metre a decade, down the terraces towards the floor — and the floor water is the hungry water, fresh from the wall, the water that dissolves. Their shells do not hold in it. The bottom metre of the column field has no shrimp in it at all, and never will, and every shrimp in the basin is on its way there.

They get back up in the air. The eggs are hard and small enough to loft in spray, and the mist carries them, and now and then one lands in a high pool that has been empty for four hundred years and is not empty any more. That is the whole reproductive strategy: fall for a century, blow back up in a droplet.

What they eat is the archive. The pollen, the spores, the scale off a moth's wing, the flake of your skin — everything the wind brings up from a hundred kilometres of lowland and drops in the water, the shrimp scrape off the calcite in the few days before the calcite closes over it. In a place whose entire character is that the ledger balances, they are the one debit. They remove things from the record. Not much. A few milligrams a year, basin-wide. It is the only theft that has ever occurred here.

And when one gets old and slow and stops grooming itself, the stone takes it where it stands. There are shrimp-shaped white lumps in the banding of every pool floor, stacked in their hundreds, going down.

## The Birds Behind the Water

Sooty, sickle-winged, about the length of your hand. Nobody has named them; the valley people know the gorge and do not go up it, and the word they use covers the whole basin and means, approximately, *the place with weather in it*.

They nest on the headwall in the splash zone directly behind the twelve great spouts, which is the only sheltered ground in four kilometres and is sheltered the way the inside of a drum is quiet. Their feet have all four toes forward. They cannot perch and they cannot walk; they can only hang. If one comes down on the basin floor it is finished — it will shuffle in a scallop of ankle-deep water in that terrible shadowless light, unable to get enough air under itself to launch, until something in it stops.

They do not call. The anatomy is intact; the behaviour is gone. There is nothing a bird could say in that noise that any other bird could hear, so a chick begs by touch, pressing its head into the adult's throat, and a pair greets by contact, and the colony is silent inside the loudest place on the continent. What they navigate by instead is the curtain. Each bird knows one gap: a particular sag in a particular spout, which pulses at a rate the wall has been holding steady for ten thousand years, and it goes through that gap at forty kilometres an hour on the beat, folded, like a thrown stone, and it does not miss, and if it missed once it would not be available to miss twice.

They feed above the rim, on the drift of small insects rising off the valleys, and they are the only two-way traffic the basin has. Everything else here arrives and stays: dust, spores, water, you. The swifts leave every morning and come back every evening with the lowlands in their crops, and that imported protein is the single largest food subsidy the place receives, and — this is the part that undoes people — it is the only clock in the bowl. The basin has no seasons of its own. It has the *stomach contents* of three hundred-odd pairs of birds, which thin in autumn and thicken in spring, and that is the entire calendar.

The nests are cups of spit and drift-fluff, and they are being fossilised as fast as they are built. Behind each curtain the wall bulges outward in a column of old nests, cemented, filled, plated, banded: a bird nests three millimetres out from its mother's rim, which is three millimetres out from its grandmother's. Cut one open and you would have a stack of small white domes going back into the rock, a strata of cups, every generation preserved in the position it occupied. There are as many pairs as there are enterable gaps in the falling water, and both numbers were set by the geometry, and the geometry does not change.

## What Lives in the Nests

The fourth organism. You will not see it, because to see it you would have to put your hand through a spout.

Behind the curtain, in the nests and the guano crust around them, there is a fly. Wingless — wings in a splash zone are a way of being killed — with a low flat body and a grip, crawling on the wet film, grazing the biofilm and the down and the droppings. Its larvae live in the seepage under the nest cups.

It is the only complete food chain in the basin: insects from the lowlands, into a bird, out of a bird, into a fly, into the stone. Two metres wide, running on imports, behind a waterfall, in the dark. Nothing else here eats anything that was alive.

## The Quiet Rooms

The nodes have tenants, which surprised me.

Not residents — nothing is anchored in them. But in a node the air is not being worked by sound, and what is not being worked settles. The dust rains out there. So the calcite inside the nodes is the richest in the basin, blacker-banded, crowded with trapped debris; the pools nearest them hold shrimp more often than one in twenty; the mats on the surrounding fins are thick and apricot-coloured in a way you can see from four metres off once you know.

And everything in the bowl that is capable of preferring anything prefers them. The goat stands in one. The fallen swift dies in one, if it is lucky, slightly quieter. You will eat your food in one, and sleep in one if you are mad enough to stay a night, and when you leave you will find you can locate three of them from memory years later.

Rooms made of nothing, in a place with no rooms, and they are the only address here.

## The One That Came Up By Mistake

A big goat-ish thing, winter coat in rags, almost always young and almost always male, pushed out of a lowland herd and following water-smell up a gorge in a dry month.

It stands in the light and the noise. It does not eat, because there is nothing; it does not drink much, because the water is strange. After an hour or two it turns and goes back down, and that is the end of it, except for one thing.

It urinates.

In a system whose entire nature is a flat line, this is a catastrophe. A litre of nitrogen and salt across a door-sized patch of mat, and for eleven days that patch blooms — thickens, darkens, roughens, lays down a band four times the usual thickness — and then the pulse is spent and metabolised and glazed over, and the line is flat again, and the band is in the rock forever. There are thousands of them in the column field if you know the look: a dark lens the width of a shoulder, at knee height, in an otherwise perfectly regular chalk.

Every event that has ever occurred in this basin was an excretion, and all of them are preserved.

Four of the animals never went back down. I have found four: low white mounds out among the fins, goat-shaped under about a centimetre of calcite, one of them with a horn still standing proud of the glaze because it dried faster than the rest and shed the film.

## The One That Arrived With You

You were an input.

You left the bootprint, which is already sealed, and a few dozen skin flakes, which are being grazed by something two millimetres long that cannot see them. You also breathed. Carbon dioxide, ten thousand parts per million, straight onto a wet calcite surface that has been in perfect saturation equilibrium since before the species — and for the four minutes you sheltered in that node with your face near the rock, the rock dissolved. Not much. A few micrograms.

So you did not only leave a mark. You ate the wall, slightly, by breathing on it, and the bead in the thumbprint hollow on the north side of the fin trembled while you were near it and steadied when you left.

And everything I have described here — the mats, the flies, the blind shrimp going downhill for a hundred years, the silent birds stacking their mothers' nests into a column of chalk — comes to a fixed total, because the food supply is fixed and has been for a geological while. The living weight of the Basin of the Weeping Wall is something on the order of nine hundred kilograms, and it wobbles by a gram or two, and it has not otherwise changed since the plateau finished filling.

The atoms will all go. In ninety thousand years the column beside you will be silt in the lowlands, and the shrimp in its banding with it, and the four goats, and your boot.

The shape will still be standing there, wet and loud and unbearably bright, with a new cohort of masons half a millimetre under its skin, climbing.
