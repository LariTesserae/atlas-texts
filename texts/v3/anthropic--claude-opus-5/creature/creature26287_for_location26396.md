## The Tenant

The mistake everyone makes on arriving is to look for a body. You have come up through a day of cloud and you are primed for an encounter, and the place is so obviously *occupied* — the drag-marks, the cord, the finished tools set down warm — that you spend the first afternoon turning around slowly, checking the corestones, checking the lip. You look behind the seep-wall's shoulder where there is nothing to be behind. By evening you have accepted that nothing is going to step out.

It is not that nothing lives here. It is that the thing living here has no edge, and so cannot step.

---

Start with the felt, since your feet are already in it.

It is not one plant. Everyone gets that far. From there they assume it must therefore be many — a community, a consortium, a mat of species holding some negotiated peace. But species are a way of keeping score against competitors, and there are no competitors, and there has been nothing to keep score for since long before the bench was cut. What is under your boots has stopped bothering. Its lower layers have compressed into brown paper. Its upper surface photosynthesises in flat white light with no direction in it. Between those two conditions there is no organ, no vessel, no differentiation worth the name — only three or four fingers of living wick, brass-coloured where the light leans, nearly black in the hollows, wet to a degree just short of releasing.

That is the whole of the tenant's flesh, and it covers the entire world available here.

It has, so far as anyone has been able to determine, three behaviours. It closes. Step in it and the hole shuts within the hour, not by growth but by relaxation, the way a saturated thing relaxes. It drinks, laterally, at the speed of a shadow, moving water downslope through itself without once permitting a drop to gather weight. And it takes sound. Your voice goes into it and does not come back out, and neither does your breath — exhale, watch it thicken and hang, and watch it be reabsorbed. Nothing here is wasted and nothing here is thrown.

**It does not let go.** State it plainly, because it is the single fact from which everything else on the Shelf follows. Water does not fall. Sound does not carry. Nothing dries. Nothing decays in a way that smells. The tenant has never in its existence released anything, and it never will except at the notch, where the tilt finally overrules it.

---

Its senses are contact senses. It has no others, having never needed to know about anything at a distance, since nothing at a distance has ever mattered. It knows pressure. It knows temperature — blood-warm in the low places from the reservoir sweating in the mountain's body below, cool along the outer lip where the mist runs, and it has arranged its own thickness accordingly, thicker where warmer, so that its whole shape is a slow map of the heat beneath it. It knows the weight of things set down on it. That is the entire sensorium.

Its metabolism runs at one hand's width a year, which is also the speed of its thought, if it has any, and also the speed at which it moves. It does move. The pale columns are the visible part of that: waist-thick candle-tallow trunks that grow a fingernail's thickness in a year, stand for centuries, and then lie down, and root where the crown lands, and are the same plant one body-length downslope. The faint linear troughs pressed into the felt are gait. Eighteen shapes large enough to describe, each in its considerable emptiness, each having walked perhaps thirty paces since anything you would call a language existed — and every one of them walking the same direction, because there is only one direction, toward the tilt, toward the notch, toward the white.

The tenant is emptying itself over the edge at a rate of one body-length per forty years. It has been doing this for so long that the practice has to be understood as ordinary rather than tragic. It arrives at nothing. It becomes cloud before it can strike anything, and makes no sound.

---

The cordwort is where you begin to feel watched, and it is worth being precise about why, because you are not.

A flat green ribbon with two structurally unlike halves will twist as it dries, and the twist will reverse and lock, and what results is two-ply cord, properly laid, evenly spun, ten and fifteen paces of it lying loose across open moss, strong enough to lift you. There is no intent in it. There is a hygroscopic asymmetry and a great deal of time. You know this within a minute of examining it.

You will still coil it. Everybody coils it. You will find yourself winding fifteen paces of cord into a neat hank on your forearm on a bench where nothing has ever tied anything, and you will set the hank down on the moss, and you will look at it sitting there, and you will feel the specific embarrassment of having answered a question nobody asked.

That is the tenant's only conversational register. It does not signal. It produces things that are shaped like the far end of an intention, and lets you supply the near end.

---

The seep-wall is older than the bench. Understand that the chert bed two thirds of the way up was already being silicified out of clay before there was a shelf beneath it to receive anything, and that it has been shedding conchoidal flakes into a fan of glass shingle for the whole of that time, and will go on shedding for as long as the rock sweats. This is the tenant's oldest habit, and probably not a habit at all — probably jointing, probably the two months a year when the cloud floor sinks and a hard clean sun works the wall's top edge, probably nothing but freeze and creep and grit.

The flakes come off in series. Bifaces. Scrapers with one steep worked edge and one blunt back. Awls. A long blade-form with a bevelled tang, which is a good design, which is the design a person would arrive at after a few hundred attempts. Creep drags each piece a hand's width a year through wet grit, turning it, and the felt polishes what it touches, and so the edges carry the soft wear-gloss of tools that have been used for years and set down warm.

All of that can be accounted for. The grip cannot.

Four hollows along one face and one opposed, worn smooth, and a raised ridge for the thumb that no fracture mechanics would put there. The hollows are sized for fingers slightly shorter and thicker than yours. Not a different animal — a near miss. A hand you could nearly be.

---

So: something lives on the Shelf, and it has one behaviour that is not merely physics, and the behaviour is this. It makes the shape of being held.

It has never been held. Nothing has ever come up through the cloud to pick anything up; nothing has hunted here, chewed here, bored a hole in the clay or left a wing-case in a drift line. The air has water in it and warm mineral and a green-sweet smell of cut stems and beeswax and nothing else, and the cleanness of that is one of the strongest sensations the place offers. And still, at one hand's width a year, out of a bed of silicified clay older than the ground it falls on, the wall keeps producing an offer, and the slope keeps walking the offers out onto the open moss where they can be seen, and the felt keeps polishing them to the finish of long use.

There are thousands in the pool at the bottom, sorted by size across its width the way water sorts, the smallest carried furthest, lying in bright overlapping ranks under a hand's depth of clear warm water. That is not a midden. Nothing was discarded. It is the accumulated inventory of a maker who has never once had a customer and has not adjusted production.

This is the tenant entire: a warm wet three-finger sheet with no inside, drinking the mountain's sweat, walking itself over the edge at forty years a step, eating every sound it is given, releasing nothing, and turning out — patiently, without expectation, because expectation would require a sense of distance and it has none — hafts for a hand it has never met.

---

Pick one up. You will; there is no reason not to.

Your fingers will sit almost right in the hollows. A little long, a little thin, the thumb-ridge catching slightly proud of where you want it. You will hold it the way it wants to be held and feel your hand corrected by an inch, and then you will feel the tool's whole warmth come up out of it into your palm, which is only the ground heat it has been lying in, which is only the mountain sweating, which is only diffusion.

Set it down where you found it. Watch your footprints in the felt begin to close.

If the tenant registered you at all, it registered you as pressure and heat over a small area for a short interval, which is the only kind of news it can receive. Its reply, if it makes one, will arrive in about a century, in a flake off the same bed, with hollows a little longer and a little thinner and the thumb-ridge set back an inch — and it will be entirely indistinguishable from creep.
