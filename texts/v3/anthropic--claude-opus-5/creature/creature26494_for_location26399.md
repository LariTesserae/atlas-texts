## The People of Ashlar, and What Holds Them

### The hands

Start with hands, because hands are the point of the people.

Everyone in Ashlar has working hands and everyone's hands are slightly wrong in the same direction: broad across the palm, warm past the point of reason, and always faintly damp, so that a handshake here is like being handed a stone out of a pocket. The warmth is not affection and it is not fever. Hold a wall and hold a person and you will notice they are giving off heat at the same rate, in the same withheld way, and if you are the sort of visitor who notices this you will not sleep well for a night or two, and then you will get over it, because everyone does.

The palms carry calluses in a distribution unique to this place: ladder-rung across the fingers, rail-polish along the heel of the hand, and a thin hard ridge on the outer edge of the little finger from the constant unconscious act of laying the hand flat against masonry while speaking. Children have it by four. It is the first mark the town puts on anybody.

They touch everything. Passing down a lane, a person will trail one hand along the wall the whole way, not for balance — the lanes are level, the town is nothing if not level — but because a wall that is touched eleven thousand times a year stays a wall. They touch each other the same way, and for the same reason. A palm laid briefly on a shoulder in passing is the standard greeting, and it is not warmth, or it is not *only* warmth: it is a small deposit. *You were used today. I am on the record as having used you.*

### What they are made of

There are two kinds of people in Ashlar and they can be told apart, though it is impolite to do it out loud.

The come-here are from Elsewhere: from finished countries with coastlines and constellations and a spring that arrives without being negotiated. They arrive in their twenties and thirties, mostly, and they arrive for reasons that sound better before you have said them aloud. Some come for the wage, which is good and paid in Elsewhere currency into an account they will probably not use. Some come because they could not stand being the four-hundredth generation of tenants in a house that was finished before they were born. A few come as penance for something, and Ashlar takes them without asking, because a penitent is a very reliable user of ground: they sweep obsessively, they do not sleep, they are up at all hours being present in corridors. Some come because a recruiter showed them a photograph of the Rill and something in them stood up and would not sit down again.

The born-here are different. Their fingerprints arrive late. An Ashlar infant comes out of its mother with a starter set — the same nine loops and whorls every child gets, a template, an issued pattern — and over the first two years the pattern individuates as the hands are used, so that a child's prints are a record of what that child has gripped. The midwives take a wax pull at three months and another at three years and file both, and the drift between them is a diagnostic that no physician Elsewhere has ever been shown. Children whose prints are slow to separate are given more things to hold: rope, tools, the handles of pots, the hands of adults. It usually works.

The born-here also do not startle correctly. Something falls, and there is a half-beat where the whole room does not react, and then reacts all at once, in unison, like a decision. Come-here parents find this the hardest of everything.

The town's official position is that this distinction is uninteresting. Nothing in Ashlar is asked what it is made of. Things are asked what they have been used for.

### The carry

The town hums.

Under the loom-hum and the four hundred drip-chains there is a third layer, and it is people. Rule two keeps voices low and indoors, and into that quiet the inhabitants of Ashlar put a continuous, barely-voiced, closed-mouth tone, pitched wherever their throat sits comfortably, which they will maintain for hours without noticing. It is called the carry. Two people working in the same room in silence will drift into it within a minute and will drift into unison a minute after that. A workshop of nine sounds like a low broken chord that changes when someone leaves the room.

Nobody teaches it. Nobody can stop doing it. Come-here find it maddening for about a year, then find themselves doing it, and then find they cannot sleep in a room where nobody is. It is understood, without any theory to back it up, to be part of the work — a way of being continuously present without spending words in the open air where words get taken.

Visitors from Elsewhere are asked, gently, not to sing.

### Names, and mentioning

Rule seven is not a piece of folk wisdom. It is the civil code and most of the calendar.

Everything in Ashlar has a name. Not a category — a name. There are one thousand one hundred and forty ladders and each has one. The cisterns have names, the corners have names, the four tomato plants have names and, more importantly, reputations. Children at seven are taken on the naming-rounds, six weeks of walking the whole town with an elder and being told what each thing is called and who named it and why, and at the end of it they are permitted to name something themselves: a new bracket, a stair-tread, a puddle that has proven persistent. They do not forget it. Grown adults of sixty will tell you, unprompted, which drip-chain is theirs.

And everything named must be mentioned. Each course keeps a mention-rota, a battered sheet on the wall by the door, and at dimming each household works down it: names of objects, names of the absent, names of the dead, said aloud indoors in the flat unceremonious tone people use for a shopping list. *Third-hook. Wet corner. Gansey's ladder. Old Tarrow, who could not cook and would not admit it.* The unflattering detail is preferred. Accuracy holds better than praise. A dead man remembered as a saint thins faster than one remembered as a nuisance, and the town found this out empirically and wrote it down.

### Courses and headers

The household is called a course, after the layer of a wall, and there are seventy-one of them, averaging thirteen people. A course is not a family, though families live inside them. A course is a sleeping-arrangement and a chore-roster and a stack of grievances, and the grievances are load-bearing. Nobody in Ashlar sleeps alone if it can be avoided, and it is avoided at the level of municipal policy. Beds are wide shelves against the outer wall, three or four to a shelf, backs to the warm stone.

Every year, thirty or so people become headers. A header is a stone laid crosswise through the thickness of a wall to tie the two faces together, and a header in Ashlar is a person who spends one season in each of four courses that are not their own — sleeping there, eating there, taking that course's chores, learning the names of that course's ladders and being complained about by that course's grandmother. It is voluntary, it is exhausting, and it is the closest thing the town has to an honour. A person who has headed all seventy-one courses over a lifetime is called *bonded through* and is, by a long tradition nobody can source, allowed to be as rude as they like for the rest of their life.

Marriage exists. It is a smaller thing here than Elsewhere and mostly concerns who is responsible for whose tools.
