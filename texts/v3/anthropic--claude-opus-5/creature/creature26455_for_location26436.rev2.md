## The people

Something under ten thousand of them, spread across fourteen works, and by mass they are the least of the plateau's residents. There are more kilos of oven on the Sill than there are kilos of person, and more kilos of wader than oven, and more kilos of snail than everything else together, and all of that is a rounding error against the mats. The people know this. It is not a humility they cultivate; it is simply arithmetic they grew up inside.

They are not a separate population from the rest, either, not in any way that shows. They are wet, crusted, quiet, slow-moving, close-packed in small clusters with long empty distances between, and eventually load-bearing. The Sill treats them exactly as it treats everything else that holds still.

## What they look like

Short, mostly, and dense — the second and third generations run to a compact build with heavy forearms, which is what you get from a life of chipping, hauling, and climbing five tiers of stair twice a day at altitude. Skin varies, because they came from everywhere below, but it doesn't matter much, because almost everyone is white. They mix the silica flour — the same fine float the smelter sells by the ton — with rendered oven fat into a grease called *whiting*, and they put it on face, neck, hands, the backs of the ears, every morning without thinking about it, the way you'd put on a coat. It goes on thick and dries matte and by afternoon it is cracking at the knuckles and the corners of the mouth. Without it the light takes the skin off you in a season. With it, a crowd on the stairs at Anneal looks like a crowd of terrace fragments that have decided to move.

Over the eyes: a slit. The good ones are iron with smoked glass, handed down, the frames worn to a soft gloss; the cheap ones are a strip of oven-hide with a knife-cut in it and a cord. Even so, everyone squints, and everyone has the fan of pale creases at the outer corners — pale because the whiting doesn't reach into a fold — so that at rest, at work, in grief, they all wear the same faint look of private amusement. Outsiders read it as friendliness and then as mockery and then, correctly, as nothing at all.

Hair is cropped or shaved, because long hair sets. A lock left alone through a wet season will stiffen into a rod you can tap against a doorframe, and apprentices in the pipe trades cultivate exactly one such lock at the temple through their indenture and chip it off on the day they're passed — the *prentice-lock*, kept in a tin, a thing you find in the belongings of dead men eighty years old.

Every threshold in every work has a scraper stone let into the sill, worn hollow, because the feet of long-timers grow a rind. A bather's heels come up crusted in a week. They do them in the evening, sitting on the step, the way people elsewhere knock out a pipe.

The lungs are the problem nobody solves. The flour floats; it floats indoors; it is in the air of the smelter sheds and the aggregate yards and, more finely, everywhere. Every work has an infirmary and the infirmary is an eye ward and a lung ward and very little else. Men and women retire into breathlessness at fifty-five and into blindness at sixty-five, roughly, and neither retirement is total, and neither is treated as an ending. The blind take the night relays. They are the best there is on the pipes.

## Talk

They speak. It's just that speech is understood here as a short-range instrument, like a whisper is elsewhere.

You talk to someone at arm's length, low, in three or four words, and if you need more than that you take their wrist. Touch is how attention is opened: a hand on the forearm means *I am about to say a thing*, and to touch someone and then not speak is a specific rudeness with a name. Conversation happens sitting in the warm bowls after shift, six or eight people in thigh-deep water with their heads close together, going on for hours at a volume that would not wake a child in the next room.

Above arm's length it is hands. Not a formal sign language — or not one anybody designed; it started as the crane-and-crew vocabulary of the piers and it has been quietly completing itself for a hundred and sixty years. It runs to maybe eleven hundred signs now, it has tenses, it has a subjunctive, and it has no word for *shout*.

And above thirty meters it is the pipe. Every lagged iron run on the plateau is a telegraph, and the code is old and fast and filthy. The formal traffic — shift changes, pressure warnings, the assay figures, the rail spur's position — takes up perhaps a fifth of the line. The rest is gossip, argument, condolence, betting, and an obscene vocabulary of genuinely impressive depth, developed by relay operators sitting alone in junction huts through the long white hours with nothing to do but listen to the line breathe. There is a recognised style to a good hand. You can tell who is tapping from four works away, the way you'd know a voice.

## Names

A child is named twice. The spoken name is chosen by the parents at leisure and is used perhaps forty times a year. The tap-name is given the night of the birth by whoever happens to be on the pipes, usually four to six beats, and it goes out on the line within the hour and comes back acknowledged from thirteen other works before dawn. That is the name that matters. It is the name people mean when they mean you. Children learn to tap their own before they learn to write it, on the bedframe, on the bath rim, on a spoon, endlessly, the way children elsewhere sing.

Once a year, at the turn of the wet season, the whole plateau taps its dead. It starts at the westmost works and comes east. It takes most of a day. Every name that anyone still living is willing to tap gets tapped, in order of age, and the line carries nothing else, and people stop work to listen to a rhythm they haven't heard since the last time. It is the loudest day of the Sill's year and if you were standing on the road below the scarp you would hear nothing at all.

## The trades

**Mat-wardens.** Farming here is thermometry and plumbing. The crops are the mats, and mats are strictly banded by heat, and the only lever you have is where the water goes. A warden walks her rounds in iron pattens with a replaceable chalk-block sole, carrying a long probe with a bulb thermometer at the end and a short heavy adze, and she farms by breaking one rim and building another — steering the ninety-four-degree flow a hand's breadth left, cooling a bowl by two degrees, moving the orange band up the slope where the snails can reach it. The work is slow, exact, and permanent; a rim she cuts on a Tuesday will still be steering water in three hundred years. Wardens are the closest thing the Sill has to an aristocracy, in the sense that everyone defers to them and nobody likes them.

**Oven-herds.** See below. They are their own thing.

**The adze gangs.** Every eight or nine years they reduce the town back to plan. Doorways, stair-noses, window ledges, gutter lips, the treads where the stone has thickened into a slump. The sound of an adze gang working a tier — a soft irregular *chock* with no echo in it — is the sound of the Sill's civic life, and children are put to sweeping the chips into baskets from about the age of five. The same gang does the ovens that can't shed cleanly, and the same gang is called when someone's elderly relative has stopped bathing and gone crusted at the elbows and hems, which happens, and which is dealt with gently and without comment.

**Pipe-walkers.** They inspect the lagged runs on their stone piers, kilometers of them, alone, all day, in gloves that will not save them. Scald is the industrial injury of the Sill and a bad one is a career. Pipe-walkers are recognisable by the shine on the forearms and by a habit of standing a half-step further away from people than anyone else does.

**The diver.** Each work has one, sometimes none for a decade. The blind fish live in the deep blue vents and are the only meat on the plateau worth calling a delicacy, and they are taken by one person on a line with a hooped net, going down into water that is beautiful and clear and hot enough to kill on a bad day. Divers are young, are paid absurdly, and are not permitted to marry into the warden families, for reasons nobody will state.

**The counter.** Usually old, usually half-blind, always working the two legible hours of mid-morning. The count walks a fixed circuit of eighty-one bowls and records waders per bowl in a ruled book, and the books go back a hundred and sixty-two years, and they are kept in an iron chest in Anneal because the waders know before the assay does. A vent that is turning will empty of birds a full day before anything shows in the chemistry. Four works have been abandoned on the count's word. Nobody has ever overruled it.

## Houses

Stone-and-iron, encrusted, doorways narrowing at a finger a decade, every roof ribbed with copper leaf and gutters running all day into a cistern. The cistern is the household's fresh water and it tastes flat and dead, being distilled; they salt it back with a pinch of brine from a jar on the shelf, and a Sill-raised person drinking unsalted water elsewhere in the world will feel faintly and inexplicably ill.

Every kitchen has a warm tray on the wall above the pipe-run, and in the tray is the household mat. It is grey-pink at the edges and soft in the middle, it is fed on brine and scrapings, it smells of sulfur and warm bread, and it is thirty or ninety or two hundred years old. It is the starter for everything — the thickener, the ferment, the sour paste that makes snail edible four hundred times a year. Brides take a palmful. Families that have been feuding for three generations can be found to have the same mat, which is the sort of thing that gets mentioned at funerals.

The staples are snail, isopod, shrimp in season, imported flour, and salt, which is free and everywhere. There is no green. The absence sits in the food the way it sits in the nose — a missing baseline note, a flatness under everything, which visitors taste immediately and residents taste only when they come back up the road after time below. Twice a year the carts arrive from the dry lands with cloth and flour and timber and onions, and for a week the works are loud with strangers, and afterwards there is a day when everybody stays home.

In the lower rooms, in the drains, live small blind isopods that eat the gutter-mat and keep the pipes clear. They are considered lucky. They are also, in a poor year, eaten, and both facts are held without difficulty.

## Children

They are the only loud thing on the Sill.

Nobody stops them. Their voices don't carry past the vapor either, so a child screaming in a bowl at fifty meters is a small silent figure with its mouth open, and the adults nearby go on with what they're doing. Sill children shriek, sing, argue at the top of their lungs, and lose it all somewhere between six and nine, not through discipline but through disuse — because nothing ever answers. It goes the way a language goes when you move away from it. By twelve they speak in the low four-word register of adults and tap faster than their parents and are mildly embarrassed by the younger ones, and by twenty most of them cannot remember having done it themselves.

Birth happens in the water, in a bowl at thirty-eight degrees, attended by however many women can fit on the rim, in near silence, and the newborn is washed in condensate and carried up the stairs to a room where someone is already at the pipe.

## Newcomers

The works recruit. There is always a shortage of hands for the smelter and the piers, and there is always someone down in the dry lands who has heard that the Sill pays.

They arrive talking. The first month they talk constantly and nobody can hear them and they think the Sill is rude. The second month they shout, and are humored. The third month they stop, and then comes the flatness — a heavy, wordless, sleepless month with a local name and a local expectation, and the works are organised around it: you are not put on the piers in your third month, you are put in the aggregate yard with a gang, and someone checks your room. Perhaps two in five go back down the road. The rest come out the other side into the general quiet and are said, from then on, to have *set*.

They use the same word for the dead. Nobody finds this remarkable enough to point out.

## The ovens

They are residents in the full sense. Not livestock, not quite — there is no word for what they are, in a place that has a word for touching someone and not speaking.

Half a ton, six-limbed, hairless, hide like wet grey leather, shelled along the back in plates they shed and regrow. They cannot make their own heat and so they live where the water is warm, which is where the people are, which is how the arrangement began. Anneal has a town byre on the second tier — a long stone hall with a bowl in the floor fed off the works at forty degrees, twenty ovens lying in it through the night, and a sleeping gallery above them because it is the warmest room in the world. The children sleep there in winter. You lie on boards over half a ton of animal and feel, every so often, a slow deep shifting somewhere underneath, like machinery in another building.

They crop mat, which keeps the terraces around a town in the condition the wardens want. They pull sledges where the rails can't go, at a pace slower than walking, which suits an aggregate load that has nowhere to be. They give hide, which is the only leather on the plateau and is accordingly inherited. They give shed plate, which goes in the aggregate. They are not killed. An oven that dies is butchered and the fat is rendered for whiting and lamp oil and cooking, and it is a windfall and a small grief, usually both in the same house.

They live about eighty years, which means a herd is handed down. A woman of sixty will be working the same animal her mother worked, and will have known it since she was four, and will know the ninety centimeters of its flank where it likes to be leaned on.

They speak by drumming a forelimb on the terrace floor, and so do their herds. Every herd carries a mallet. There are nine signals humans have securely worked out — *come*, *stop*, *back*, *cold*, *hurt*, and four that translate badly — and herds use all nine and are answered. Ovens also drum a great deal at night, at low frequency, across distances of kilometers, in patterns nobody has been able to break in a hundred and sixty years of trying. There is a man in Calcine who has filled forty notebooks. He does not claim to be close. The general local opinion is that it is probably not addressed to us and that this is fine.

## The waders

Nobody owns them, nobody feeds them, nobody touches them, and killing one is the nearest thing the Sill has to a crime against religion, though nobody would use that word. They are instruments. Thousands of them, silent, evenly scattered, standing in ones and twos to the horizon, cracking mineral off their knees against the rims with that small dry *tick* — and each bird is a reading of a temperature and a chemistry, and the pattern of them across forty kilometers is the plateau's honest opinion of itself, updated continuously and free of charge.

The count keeps the books. Everyone else keeps a private version, without meaning to, and will stop on the stairs at mid-morning and look out and feel something is wrong and not be able to say what, and be right.

## The dead

They set.

Each work has its bowl, downslope, warm, chosen for slow clean deposition. The body is laid out in ankle-deep water and left, and the family may visit, and many do, and there is no ceremony beyond going. Six months and it is not recognisable. Five years and it is stone. Thirty and the rim has come up over it and it is part of the shelf, and the shelf keeps its accounts the way the rest of the plateau does, a millimeter on and a millimeter off, going on.

And because the towns quarry their own terraces for aggregate — because it is the only geology they have — the dead come back up into the walls. Cut a fresh face in a hundred-year rim and it is legible: bands of snail, a wader's femur in section, an isopod perfect in the matrix like a bubble in glass, the great pale curve where an oven lay down and became load-bearing. And, here and there, smaller and paler and unmistakable, something human.

Masons set those pieces facing out. Not as a memorial — nobody knows who it was, nobody could — but because it is a clean face and a good stone and there is no reason to turn it around. There are walls in Anneal with a hundred small cross-sections in them and they are ordinary walls and nobody points them out. Visitors find them and stand there for some time.

The locals find that a little funny, in the way they find everything a little funny, with the creases at the corners of the eyes, working or idle. They are not being callous. They have simply understood from the beginning what the terraces are, and that the town is one, and that they are in it now and will be in it later, and that nothing here is being born and nothing here is being destroyed — it is going on, at the pace it has settled into, and they are what it is going on as.
