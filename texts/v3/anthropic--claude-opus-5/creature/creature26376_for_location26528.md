## The One You Would Meet

If you got down on the trail with a red light and waited, you would meet a female about eight millimetres long, the colour of dried blood, with a head disproportionate to everything else about her and two long backswept spines on her thorax that serve no purpose you would recognise. She is coming uphill. Over her head, held in her mandibles and balanced against her body at an angle that took the lineage several million years to work out, is a crescent cut from the leaf of a young fig — roughly a hundred and thirty milligrams, a bit more than her own weight, and about eight times her surface area, so that in the dark she is mostly leaf with legs beneath it.

She has almost no use for eyes. Her world is a groove of smell perhaps a centimetre wide, laid down by the abdomens of her sisters and refreshed continuously; the compound is a single molecule she can detect at a concentration you would need to write in exponents, and it is the only geography she has. Where the trail forks she does not choose so much as fail to leave. Beside the smell there is texture underfoot, gradient, the hard little jolts of contact with oncoming traffic — a hundred, two hundred touches an hour, each one a fifth of a second, each one a check.

Riding on her leaf, facing backward, is a second ant, a tenth of her size, doing nothing. This is not idleness. Somewhere above the column, hunting by the sound and smell of cutting, is a fly the size of a comma that wants to lay a single egg in the soft joint behind a carrier's head; the carrier, with her mandibles full, cannot defend herself. So the small one rides. She is a turret gunner. If nothing comes she will ride the entire two hundred metres and then walk back out.

The carrier is about ninety days old, which is late middle age, and if you could look at her mandibles under magnification you would find the cutting edges worn round. She used to cut. Cutting is done by locking the mandibles into the leaf edge and vibrating the whole head at roughly a thousand times a second, which stiffens the blade and makes the leaf behave like a firmer material; it costs her teeth, and when the teeth go she is quietly reassigned, downward, to hauling. When hauling gets too slow for her she will be reassigned again, to trail clearing, and then to nothing.

## What She Is Part Of

Every ant on this trail is her sister. Every ant in the mound is her sister. There are somewhere between two and four million of them and they were all laid by the same female, and they come in a graded series of body sizes covering two orders of magnitude of mass, each size sitting inside the range of tasks its size suits: the one-millimetre gardeners who tend the fungus and cannot open a leaf; the two-millimetre pulpers; the four-millimetre licker-cleaners; the eights and tens who cut and carry; and the majors, fifteen millimetres and armoured, with head capsules driven by muscle that fills the skull like set concrete and mandibles that will shear through boot leather and hold on after the head has been detached from the body. The majors do very little. They exist as a standing insurance premium against being dug into.

Nobody assigns any of this. There is no plan in any head. The whole enormous coordination is done with about twenty chemical statements and a great deal of touching.

## The Female at the Bottom

Five metres down, in a chamber near the water table where the temperature does not vary by a degree across the year, is the only individual here who could be called an individual. She is four centimetres long and grub-pale except for her old head and thorax, and her abdomen is a translucent sack in which you can see things moving. Her wing muscles were digested for protein eight years ago; the scars where the wings came off are still visible.

She flew once. One humid afternoon, one flight, along with tens of thousands of others from other mounds, of whom essentially all died that evening. In the air she mated with perhaps four or five males, each of whom died of it, and took into a storage organ some two hundred million sperm cells, which is her entire allocation and must last two decades. Then she landed, broke off her wings, dug, sealed herself in, coughed up a pellet of fungus she had carried from her mother's nest in a pocket under her mouth, fertilised it with her own droppings, and laid her first eggs. She has not seen the surface since and never will. There is no ruling in what she does. She is fed by mouth, drop by drop, cleaned constantly, and she lays an egg every few seconds for years — she is not the head of this thing, she is its gonad, and the rest of the body knows the difference even if we don't.

## The Animal Thirty Metres Across

Which is the point at which the honest answer to *what lives here* stops being a list.

The thing that lives on the second terrace is thirty metres across and six metres deep, weighs, in ants alone, about as much as a small dog, and, counting its gardens, considerably more. It respires: warm wet CO₂ out of the turrets, measurable in litres per hour, and it thermoregulates that exhaust by shaft geometry alone, holding its gardens within a degree of optimum through a season that swings the surface soil by fifteen. It eats — a third of a tonne of leaf a year — with a mouth that is currently eleven kilometres of road and two million pairs of mandibles. It digests in two thousand chambers. It excretes into dedicated pits that run hot with its own composting. It has an immune system, a waste caste, an internal quarantine, a quality-control procedure. It grew from a single fertilised female in a hole the size of a thimble to this, on a growth curve indistinguishable in shape from an organism's. It is eight years old. It will live perhaps another eight, and then the female at the bottom will run out of stored sperm, and it will thin, and shrink, and go quiet, and the roof will slump, and there will be a rich pale scar of nitrogen-loaded earth in the understorey where something the size of a house once had a metabolism.

It has no name in its own experience. It has never had an experience. Get it to breathe on the back of your hand and try to hold onto that.

## The Oldest Resident Has Never Been Outside

But the colony is not the deepest inhabitant here, because the colony is not what the colony is for.

In the chambers, hanging in grey-white bolsters like wet bread pulled apart, is a fungus. It grows on nothing but the pulped, cleaned, inoculated leaf the ants bring it, and it grows nowhere on earth outside nests like this one. It is a single clone — one cell-line, propagated by mouth, queen to daughter-queen, in unbroken continuity for something between eight and twenty million years. It has been carried, one pellet at a time, out of the founding chamber of one nest and into the founding chamber of the next, for a length of time that makes the terraces below us look recent. The present channel of the river is younger. The laterite cap on the hills is not much older.

It has stopped making mushrooms. Somewhere back down that long unbroken lineage it lost the habit of fruiting, because it no longer needs to disperse — its dispersal is handled. What it produces instead are gongylidia: swollen nutrient-loaded cells in little grape-clusters that come away cleanly, that are almost purely food, that appear to have no function whatsoever except to be picked. It exudes the enzymes that break the leaf down; the ants cannot digest leaves at all, and drink what the fungus makes. It is fed, weeded, watered, defended, given antibiotics, and kept at twenty-five degrees and total humidity, and it has never in eight million years had to know that above it there is a dry season, a flood season, a night, a moon.

The most local, most rooted, most irreplaceable resident of this landscape is an organism that has never been outdoors, does not move, cannot survive one hour of the conditions on the surface, and has no idea it lives on a river terrace in the tropics. Whether the ants keep it or it keeps the ants is not a question either party is in a position to raise.

## Lodgers

And the colony, being a place, is inhabited.

There is a mould that specialises in eating this fungus and nothing else. It is present in every garden, always, held down and never eliminated — found by the gardeners, cut out, carried off, gone in an hour, back next week. Against it, in tiny crypts on the cuticle of the workers' thoraxes, the colony farms bacteria that secrete antifungals, feeding them from glands built for the purpose. There is an antibiotics industry here, several million years old, running continuously, on the outside of the bodies of the staff.

There is a nitrogen problem — leaves are poor in it — and there are bacteria in the waste chambers fixing nitrogen out of the air, which is where a meaningful fraction of the whole colony's protein comes from.

There are cockroaches two millimetres long, pale and blind, that live in the gardens and lick the ants' mouths and are tolerated. There are silverfish that steal. There are mites riding on legs, in joints, on the queen herself. There are beetle larvae in the middens growing fat on the waste stream of the waste stream. There are the phorid flies overhead, waiting all night, whose entire existence is the eight seconds of inattention behind a carrier's head.

Every resident here has residents.

## Neighbours

Eleven of these animals share this terrace. They do not fight; they avoid, which they accomplish by the shape of their roads. Where two trail systems come close, both fade. The territories are negotiated the way the hillslopes were: not by contest but by long attrition into an arrangement that no longer needs to be renegotiated.

Grey papery boilers welded to the trunks belong to a different industrial philosophy: termites, who ferment inside their own bodies with the help of protists and bacteria and do not farm at all, and whose soldiers have heads modified into nozzles that squirt sticky terpenes, and whose queen is a white sausage in the dark being milked of eggs. Underground, other termite species run covered galleries out under the litter so they never touch air. On the dry third terrace their mounds have internal flues. Between them and the ants they process a fifth of everything this landscape grows, and the two industries have divided the feedstock: green leaf to one, dead wood and litter to the other, with an armistice line running roughly along the moment of a plant's death.

## The Actual Majority

Below all of that, in the litter, live the residents by weight.

Fifteen thousand mites in a double handful — predators, fungivores, scavengers, parasites, most of them without names. Springtails in numbers that make the mites look scarce. Symphylans, pale and quick, a hundred legs' worth. Pseudoscorpions the size of this comma, hunting springtails with pincers and venom in the two-fingers-down layer where it is damp and silent and thirty-two degrees. A whip spider on a trunk, palm-sized, holding its first pair of legs out as two long feelers and reading the dark with them like a blind man reading a room. Land crabs a kilometre from water, out for the dew hours. Millipedes rolled around clutches of eggs. Ten thousand fungi that are only mycelium and will never be seen.

And inside a single leaf, in the two hundred microns between the upper and lower skin of it, a moth larva a millimetre long is eating a pale meandering tunnel and will complete its entire childhood there. Its universe is one leaf. It has no idea it is fifty metres from a factory, eighty metres above the sea, or that there is an above.

## Passing Through

The tapir, the peccaries, the brocket deer, the ocelot, the caiman in its shrinking pool — these are here, and they matter out of all proportion to their mass, because the tapir is a machine for moving seeds twenty kilometres and the cat is a machine for keeping the seed-eaters honest. But they are not residents in the sense the fungus is. They belong to a larger place and merely intersect this one; each of them could live in a dozen other forests, and several of them, over their lives, will.

Residency here is not about where you are. It is about whether you could be anywhere else.

By that measure the deepest inhabitants of this terrace are a clone of fungus that cannot survive one hour outside a chamber it did not dig, and a lineage of insects that cannot digest a leaf without it, and neither of them can leave, and neither of them ever has to.

## Her

Two hours from now the carrier will drop her leaf at the mound and turn around, because at ninety days there is no other option available to her. Or the fly will get her, and she will keep working for a fortnight with something growing in her neck. Or she will simply stop on the trail, in the way they do — mid-stride, without incident — and traffic will part around her for a few minutes, and then two of her sisters will pick her up and take her to the midden, where the beetles are, and she will be substrate, and the colony's respiration will not change by one measurable millilitre.

She is not what lives here. Nothing lives here but her.

In six weeks the rain comes back, the turrets get sealed with pellets of soil from the inside, and the flood runs thirty centimetres deep over the whole silent factory floor. Under it, at twenty-five degrees and total humidity, in the dark it has occupied since the Miocene, the oldest thing on this terrace goes on being fed.
