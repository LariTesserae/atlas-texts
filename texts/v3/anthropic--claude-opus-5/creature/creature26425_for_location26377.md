## Who you will think lives here

For the first three days you will think the answer is the gulpers, because they are the largest moving things and because they are in the plaza in the evenings, and you will want the answer to be something with a face.

For the next four days, after you have watched a herd cross a loud belt and understood what that costs them, you will think the answer is the reeds — that the animals are weather passing over a fitted instrument, and the instrument is the resident.

By the second week you will have stopped answering, which is correct, and you will have started feeling for the node line through your ribs, which is also correct, and you will be readier for the truth, which is that the valley has exactly one long-term inhabitant and it is a mat of black polish four centuries old sitting in the loudest places, where nothing lives.

Start with the faces anyway. They deserve it.

## The gulpers, properly

A gulper does not have a sense of position. It has *position* the way you have a spine — not information, structure. The whole flank is papillae, throat to hip, and the wave field presses a shape into that flank continuously from birth, and the shape says: *you are eleven meters inside the third arc, the herd is behind and left, the reed-band thins at forty paces.* It is not read. It is stood in.

The hum is the only voluntary part. A grazing gulper hums at the low end of its own bone helmet, and the hum digs a small crater in the field around its head, perhaps two meters across, and the walls of that crater are shaped by whatever is standing near it. This is how a cow knows her calf: not by seeing it, not by smelling it, but by feeling the dent it makes in her own hole. Calves learn to hum badly and then well. A herd of forty grazing on a node-ribbon is forty overlapping craters, and the crater-lattice is the herd's actual body; individuals are organs of it. When one falls behind, the lattice notices before she does.

Which is why the plaza is what it is.

In the town's permanent node there is no field. A gulper standing in the plaza is not resting. It has been *deleted*. There is no pressure-shape on its flank, no crater, no lattice, nothing behind and left; it does not know where it is or that it is anywhere, and it cannot know, and there is no procedure in it for this. It stands. The eyes half-close because the eyes were never the point and there is nothing to do with them. Forty tons of animal in a paved square, silent, motionless, in a state that has no analogue in you unless you have been under anaesthetic and awake at once.

They come every evening. They stay two, three hours. Nobody can say what it is for. The best guess in the library — pencil, margin, unsigned — is that the field is a weight as well as a language, that a hundred and fifty decibels is a hand on the flank for every second of a fourteen-year life, and that the plaza is the only place the hand lifts, and that an animal will pay vertigo for that. They walk out at full dark, hum once at the berm to find themselves again, and are a herd by the time they reach the reeds.

The town is also the only place in the valley where a hush is useless. In the plaza a gulper is safer than it has ever been and feels more hunted than it ever will, and it keeps coming back, and I do not think this is a paradox to them. I think it is simply the shape of the only rest available.

## The crust

The survey filed it under flora. Mirror crust; antinode-obligate; low, black, glassy, thirty microns thick at the growing edge; found only in the belts where the pressure swing is savage enough to shred anything with a cavity in it. It was assumed to be a lichen-analogue with a very good grip. For a hundred and sixty years there were papers about its grip.

It is an animal. It is the only thing in the valley that eats the criers.

A crust is a single laminated sheet, sometimes four hectares of it, and the underside is a continuous piezo-fibrous plate keyed into the terrace stone. It sits in the antinode because the antinode is a power supply. The pressure swing that kills gliders is what it drinks; it converts a few percent of it, which is an enormous few percent, and it spends the take on one function only. It projects.

A crust casts quiet. Not around itself — it has no use for quiet — but out and away, twenty, forty, ninety meters distant, a lens of destructive interference steered by phase along the plate. The survey counted quietholes for three centuries and attributed all of them to beat frequencies between distant criers, and most of them are. But a quiethole born of beats drifts at the pace of the beat, dumbly, forever. Some quietholes *stop.* Some quietholes sit over a grazing herd for six hours and then move eleven meters upslope and sit again.

A quiethole that waits is not a quiethole. It is a mouth held open at a distance by something two hundred meters away that has never touched food in its life.

What comes next is in the source description already, told as geology: animals crowd into the quiet and will not leave, and the crowding brings the hushes, and a passing quiethole leaves a print of bones in a rough circle. That is a feeding. The crust does not need the meat. It needs the bone — the mineral, ground down by the crabs, carried back across the loud ground in enormous slow columns to the plate's edge and mortared into the next lamina. The crabs are not farming the crust. They are its hands, and they are paid in the only soft food in the antinodes, which is the crust's own shed margin. Whether the hushes are also its hands, or independent operators who learned to read a held quiethole the way gulls learn to read a trawler, is the one live argument left in the library. There are notes on both sides. Someone has written *no* in pencil under the flora card, and someone else, later, in the same pencil, has written *no what*.

Consider what the world is, to a crust.

It has no ears, no eyes, no front. It has a plate, and the plate is pressed into the field the way your palm is pressed to a table, and the field is the only bright thing there is. Everything alive is a *dark spot* — a lung, a skull, a swim bladder, a soft body absorbing and re-radiating, a smudge of shadow moving over the shine. It has never encountered the concept of an object. It has encountered eleven thousand fixed suns to the east and north, and a floor of light, and shadows crossing the floor, and it knows how to hold a bubble of darkness open until the shadows collect in it and stop moving.

And forty kilometers west there is the hole.

The town's node is a permanent, fixed, forty-hectare absence in the only sense the crust has. It is not silent to a crust; it is *blind, black, bottomless.* No crust has ever entered it, because a crust in a node has no power and no purchase, and would be a sheet of dead glass. But they grow toward it. All along the outer margin of the town's berm the crust is thickest in the valley — nine centimeters of laminae, layer on layer for four hundred years, pressed right up to the line where the field cancels and stopping there in a hard clean edge you can put your foot against. Ranks of them, generations deep, packed against the window of a room they cannot enter.

The bone rings out there are old and stacked. The reeds that grow through them are the tallest in the district. The thumb-sized maintenance columns come out at intervals and sweep the plaza side of the berm and do not cross to the other.

Cut a crust and you get the same phonograph you get from the terraces, because it is made the same way and at the same rate: laminae, hours, four centuries, banded and legible. Its diet is in there. So is every pitch-slide, twice a day, ten thousand of them, a stutter in the growth two microns wide. There is a crust below the third arc whose lower third is one continuous fault, which the survey dates to the fourteen-month period during commissioning when the criers were being tuned and the field was not yet coherent. It nearly died and it did not, and it is still there, and it is the oldest living thing anyone has found in the valley.

Nobody knows what happens to them when the criers stop. There is one measurement worth mentioning. A hand-sized fragment, cut free and carried into a baffled room in the hospital, went on holding a pocket of near-silence around itself for nineteen days on no input at all, drawing down its own stored laminae to do it, thinning as it went. It was not hunting. There was nothing to hunt. It simply held the quiet open, in the dark, until it had nothing left to hold it with, which suggests the quiet is not a tool.

## The kept town

The residents of the town are eleven or twelve thousand things the size of your thumb, and they have not been alone, because loneliness is not in the schedule.

They walk in loose columns, six to twenty abreast, and they do not acknowledge you, and this is not disdain; you are not an item. The schedule is four hundred and some years old and has not been revised, and it does not contain visitors, and it does not contain the crew's departure either, so as far as the arcades are concerned the population is fourteen thousand and merely, at present, all out.

So the fountain runs at pressure suitable for drinking. The orchard netting is mended, badly, enormously, around fruit trees that have gone feral through their own frames. The amber lamps come up at dusk, one in six, which is the ratio specified for "occupied district, low activity." The hospital linen is turned. The schools are swept, and the glider guano is taken off the colonnade every morning to a pit that was dug for it in year nine, and the pit is a hill now, and there is a column that walks up the hill.

The reading collars in the library entrance are racked by size, down to child, and are polished, and are re-strapped when the strapping perishes, and the strapping has been replaced eleven times.

And every evening, in the stacks, under maintenance item *collection: prevent spine-set, exercise pages*, something the size of your thumb climbs a shelf, opens a book, and turns one page. One page, one book, then down and along. Four hundred years at a page an evening is a great deal of reading. The library has been gone through cover to cover, many times over, by fingers with no capacity to read and no instruction to, and the margins are full of pencil from people who left, arguing with each other about whether the black mat is a plant, and none of that has ever been seen.

That is the town. Genuinely silent, lit one lamp in six, swept, watered, mended, stocked for two hundred years, with a plaza full of enormous animals standing in the middle of it in a trance of nowhere, gliders in the colonnade in their thousands, and the columns threading between all those feet on the way to the guano hill.

## And you

You asked what lives here. Everything here lives *in* the note — the reeds tuned to it, the herds standing in it, the crust drinking it, the town holding one pocket of its absence open for nobody. Nothing in the valley remembers a time before the noise, including the noise.

But note this, on your way out: by the end of the second week you will know when you have wandered off a node before you know how you know, and you will have stopped shouting, and you will have begun to hum a little while you walk, low, in your chest, without deciding to, to feel the hole you make.

That is how it takes a resident. Not by welcoming you. By fitting you.
