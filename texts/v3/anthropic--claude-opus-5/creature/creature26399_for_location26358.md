## What the census misses

The census in the guidebooks is not wrong, exactly. It is a list of the things large enough and opaque enough to catch an eye that expects motion. Snails, one glider, some clouds. It reads as poverty.

But start from the correct question, which is not *what moves here* but *what is scarce here*, and the basin repopulates itself immediately.

Light is not scarce. Water is not scarce. Carbon is not scarce; there is more fixed carbon per cubic meter in these tiers than in any rainforest, and it is going nowhere. What is scarce is nitrogen, and phosphorus, and the whole small handful of atoms that a body cannot make and must inherit. In a normal world those atoms circulate: things die, things rot, the rot goes into the soil, the soil goes back up the tree. Here nothing rots on schedule and nothing falls fast and nothing is worn down, which means — follow it through — that here **the dead do not come back**. They lie on the interfaces going glassy. They compact on the basin floors into fifteen meters of amber felt. Every leaf that settles is a small permanent theft.

So the basin's living things are, all of them, without exception, in the business of retrieving the dead. That is the trade. That is the only trade. Everything that lives here is either a thief, or a bank, or a courier, or — once every eleven years — an amnesty.

## The knitter

The largest organism in the drowned cordillera is the silvered threads on the seams, and it is not a garnish. It is the landlord.

The physics hands it its habitat for free. Particulate sinking through water slows where the density changes; on each interface there is a permanent thin haze of arrested drift — pollen, leaf-scale, dead diatom-glass — a horizontal larder kilometers wide and a finger thick. The threads live in that haze and nowhere else. They cannot go up; they would starve in clear water. They cannot go down; the seam is a wall to them as much as to a leaf. They spread sideways instead, for as long as sideways lasts.

An interface therefore tends to hold a single individual. One. A sheet of hyphae six kilometers across, filling every gap between the mats of fallen foliage, gluing them, drawing them into the knitted lid that the guidebooks describe as a false floor and which is more accurately a stomach with a view. The lid-meadows are not middens. They are a slow mouth eating its own accumulated ceiling from the inside. Where the felt has gone pale and glassy, the knitter has finished with it. Where it is dark, damp, faintly warm to the palm — warmer than the water by a fraction you can feel with the back of the hand — it is being worked.

The seams differ. The knitter of the third interface is a cold silver; the fifth is silver going gold at the edges; the second, for reasons nobody has established, is nearly colorless and shows only as a shimmer when the light rakes it. They differ because they have been apart. Each is a lineage sealed on its own floor, unable to reach any other, for eleven years at a time.

And then the Turning, and the seams buckle and pass through one another, and for six weeks two sheets that have never touched interpenetrate over hundreds of hectares. The knitter fruits only then. It requires moving water; it has spores and no other way to place them; it gets motion once a decade and spends its entire eleven years preparing for six weeks of it. Afterward the new seams sort themselves out at new depths with new felts, and the color of a lid tells you which of the old ones married into it.

There is no other creature in the basin whose entire sexual life is scheduled by the density of water.

## The trees, and the only fast thing

The sheath-trees are ninety meters of wrist-thick optimism and they have solved almost every problem a plant can have by declining to have it. No wind, so no lignin, so no expense. No drought, so no bark. No need to hold themselves up, only to hold themselves *down*, and the anchor-threads do that, humming at their tension like the strings of an instrument tuned once and left.

Two problems remain.

The first is how to begin. A seed that will not sink is a seed that never finds rock, and nothing here sinks. So the sheath-trees make the one heavy object in the basin: a seed ballasted with silica, glazed, dense, a green-black bead the weight of a musket ball. It is the only thing in the drowned cordillera in a hurry. It punches through the tiers of its own parent — you can hear it go, a rapid stitching sound, four or five small tears in a second, shocking after the silence — punches through the lid-meadow below, dimples nothing, and falls, and falls, and lands on bare stone at the bottom of a basin where the light is old and dim. Then it inflates. It makes its first gas chamber and rises a meter. It makes another. A sheath-tree's youth is an ascent lasting fifteen or twenty years, hand over hand up through the strata toward the level of light it will finally hold, and it will hold it for two centuries, and it will never move again.

The second problem is that there is no room. Nothing falls, so nothing leaves a gap, so a mature stratum is a sealed ceiling and a rising seedling arrives beneath it and stops and waits. What it is waiting for is a rock. Somewhere on the pale faces above, a flake the size of a door hangs on friction and drag, and one day it lets go, and goes down through forty tiers shearing everything it meets in that long soft rip, and opens a slot of raw daylight from the surface to the floor. That slot is real estate. Every seedling within reach turns toward it and begins, very slowly, to grow crooked.

The forest is not a static thing. It is a thing whose entire dynamism has been outsourced to geology.

## The lines

The clock-snails follow the knitter. They must; nothing else can open the glazed litter, and the snails' radulae are made for scraping what has already been softened. Where the lid is dark and warm, they are; where it has gone pale, they have been. Their shells are laminated out of the silica they cannot digest, laid down in bands, so that a shell read edge-on is a diet and a diary — thin dark years, thick pale years, and a hard scar-line at every Turning where the animal stopped eating for a season and hung on.

The tracks are the thing. A hand's width a day, a hundred meters a century, a single unbroken meander pale against the dark felt, and because nothing washes and nothing regrows quickly, the track persists for decades after the animal has passed. The underside of an old lid-meadow is scribbled over with the lifetimes of the dead.

This is not decoration; it is the snails' only social medium. A snail that crosses an old line can tell its age and its direction and, apparently, something about who made it, and it can turn and follow. Courtship here is the pursuit of a stranger's handwriting across four or five years of grazing. When two living lines finally braid — and you can find these braids, tight helical loops a meter long where two animals stayed together for a season — that is the entire romance of the species, and most individuals manage it twice.

At the Turning their ceiling becomes their floor. They turn over, and turn around, and begin grazing back the way they came, across their own writing.

## The courier

The glider is the most important animal in the basin and is usually filed as scenery.

Watch one long enough and you will see what it is for. It coasts, it banks on nothing, it crosses a clearing in twenty minutes, and then it settles onto a lid-meadow and does not stop. Its weight — barely any, but more than the felt can carry — dimples the lid, and it works, and the lid gives, and the animal slides through into the next water down with a small slow bloom of the water above coming through behind it, and the felt closes over the hole in an hour or two.

That is the puncture in the guidebook. That is a glider going downstairs.

Nothing else in the drowned cordillera crosses an interface. Not spores, not pollen, not larvae, not the knitter, not a leaf. Six or seven forests, each sealed under the litter of the one above, would be six or seven separate worlds — separate for eleven years, which is long enough to matter — except that once a month or so something a meter wide carries a mouthful of the upper layer through the floor and lets it go. Everything that lives on the fourth floor and also on the fifth got there in the gut or the fringe of a glider.

And the gut is working, always, slowly, visibly, and what it is working on is nitrogen. It grazes the clouds. It hangs among them for days, taking them in, and then it goes, and wherever it habitually goes it leaves a thin fertility behind it — and because nothing here disperses, that fertility stays exactly where it fell. The result is lanes. Look across a basin in raking light and you will see them: faint bands of richer, darker, larger foliage running for kilometers at particular depths, and turning where they turn, and forking where they fork. They are the flight paths of gliders that died before your grandmother was born, printed into the forest because in this world a route can become a place.

## The wealth

The clouds are not ambiguous. They are colonial, they are photosynthetic, they are luminous when the light hits them wrong, and they fix nitrogen out of solution, which is a thing no plant here does and no animal can, and which is where every atom of protein in the basin ultimately comes from.

They are the only source of new money. Everything else recycles.

They keep station for years because a nitrogen-fixer at depth is running a very tight budget and cannot afford to be anywhere but exactly the right light. They are grazed by the gliders and by almost nothing else. A basin's whole standing crop of clouds might amount to a few dozen room-sized aggregates, hanging at their levels, faintly alight, doing a slow chemistry that the ninety-meter trees are entirely dependent on and cannot reach.

Whether they are animals is a question about words. They are the richest thing in the drowned cordillera and they look like fog.

## Beneath

Nothing mixes. Follow that all the way down and it becomes unpleasant: below the deepest lid, where the amber felt lies fifteen meters thick and the last of the light is a rumor, the water has been out of contact with the air for eleven years and has no oxygen left in it at all.

That is not a dead zone. It is a different zone. On the lowest interface, in light too poor for anything green, there is a sheet of wine-purple: sulfur bacteria, growing on the breath of the felt beneath them, running the oldest metabolism in the basin. It is a floor like the others — it accumulates, it knits, it has its own thin dark meadow — and it is the color of a bruise, and it is the largest single organism in the cordillera after the knitters, and probably older, since Turnings pass over it rather than through it.
