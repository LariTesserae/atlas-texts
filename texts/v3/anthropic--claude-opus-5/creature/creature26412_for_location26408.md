## The people of the note

They are called, when they are called anything, by their pitch.

A visitor asks a Hanthan their name and gets a hum — two or three seconds of a note held under the breath, unhurried, with a small deliberate wobble at the end that is the family and a step-down at the finish that is the tower. Written, it is a groove, a shallow cut in plate that can be dragged under a stylus. Spoken, it is not a word. There is no word. Their language has nouns for a hundred kinds of crystal and no proper nouns whatsoever, because a proper noun is a sound you make about an absent thing, and here the sound *is* the thing, and the thing is never absent: it announces itself continuously from birth until it stops.

## Bodies

They are heavy people. Three and a half thousand metres of thin air and a lifetime of minus fifteen have made them deep-chested and dense-boned, short-limbed, with an enormous ribcage that a visitor tends to read as fat and which is nearly all lung and heart and the muscle that drives them. They eat constantly — flyer-flour, scraper haunch, comber roe pressed into cakes, mat-curd soured in brine — and they burn it as fast as they can shovel it in, and in the four-degree interiors of the city they sit in loose sleeveless work-shifts and sweat.

Their skin has no colour to speak of. Nothing has needed colour here for a very long time. What pigment they show is borrowed: the grey-green of mat-stain on the forearms, the rust of vent iron in the creases of the knuckles, the black under the nails that never comes out. They smell — all of them, to an outsider, overwhelmingly — of eggs and hot metal, and they cannot detect it in one another any more than you detect the smell of your own house.

And they have eyes. This is worth stating plainly, because visitors arrive expecting the sockets to have closed over. They have not. The eyes are large, wet, dark, extremely sensitive and almost entirely private. A Hanthan uses their eyes perhaps forty hours a year: reading a lamplit instrument, checking a wound, and in bed. The eye here is not an organ of navigation but an organ of intimacy and of grief — it is what you weep with and what you look at a lover with, and those are close to the same act. A face on the Sill is not a public document. Nobody has ever seen most of the people they know.

The ear, by contrast, is a whole architecture. The pinna is broad, thin, mobile, and can be furled; there is a ring of muscle that seals the canal, and everyone on the plateau flinches it shut a hundred times a day against the vent-roar the way you blink against grit. Behind that, in the skull, a plate of thickened bone above the brow that conducts. They hear with their foreheads. When a Hanthan is listening hard to you they tip the head down and forward as though about to butt, and it is unnerving, and it means you are being taken seriously.

## The drone

The second voice is the one that makes them what they are.

Somewhere in the long dark generations the nasal sinuses of these people became a reed. They hum without deciding to. It runs under the breath in both directions, in and out, unbroken; it does not stop for speech, or eating, or sleep, or fright. Speech rides on top of it the way a melody rides a bagpipe drone. Infants are born droning — a thin unsteady squeak that thickens through childhood and drops in adolescence, and the dropping of it is the only rite of passage this culture has ever needed. A child whose note settles is taken out onto the terraces at the shift-change hour, when the emitter choir thins the carrier deliberately, and made to sound alone into twenty kilometres of ringing plate, and the plates ring back, and that is the whole ceremony: *the country has heard you and answered.*

So no Hanthan is ever silent, and no Hanthan is ever alone in the sense you mean it. In a crowded stairwell there are two hundred drones stacked in thirds and fifths against the civic carrier, and they read the stack the way you read a street — that one is my aunt, four levels up and climbing; that low buzzing one is a stranger from the west valley; that thin one is a child too far from home. They can hold a conversation with someone they have never touched, at four hundred metres, across a lit-nothing gulf, while both of them work.

They also have no privacy of location whatsoever, and have never wanted any, and find the concept slightly obscene. What they have instead is silence, which is bought.

## Skin, light, quiet

Three things on the Sill are luxuries and all three are intimacies.

The first is **skin**. There is no unarmoured touch out of doors; the ground is a razor and the air is minus fifteen. Gloves are the most personal garment a Hanthan owns — laminated chitin, plated at the palm, cuffed to the elbow, and carved on the back with the owner's groove so deep you can read it with a thumb. Gloves are inherited, resoled, buried in walls with their owners. To be seen ungloved is roughly what it is elsewhere to be seen naked, and the glove-makers are the closest thing this city has to portraitists. There is no handshake. There is a *knuckling* — plate touched to plate, a small hard click, which is a greeting and also, in the acoustic hologram, a way of saying *here, precisely here, this is where I am standing.*

The second is **light**, which is private property and is treated as one treats a body. A lamp in a public stair is not illegal and is not done. To open a lamp on a stranger is an assault with no injury and no defence; the courts hear these cases and the courts take them seriously. Lovers own one lamp between them. Parents light a lamp for a child once, at the settling of the note, and the child looks at their mother's face for the first time at fourteen and remembers it for sixty years.

The third is **quiet**, metered like water, sold by the volume, and the most expensive thing most families ever purchase. Every household has a damped chamber, because you cannot sleep inside a hologram. The archives are damped, the surgeries are damped, and grief is damped by the hour — mourners rent a silence and go into it and stop droning, which is the only occasion on which an ordinary person is permitted to be a hole in the world.

And the marriage rite, if you can call it that, is simply all three at once: two people, one rented hour of dead air, one lamp, gloves off, saying nothing at all. Nothing at all is the entire content of the ceremony. They come out of the chamber having been, for one hour, invisible and unhearable and touched, and that is a thing that cannot be done anywhere else in the world, and they do not talk about it afterwards.

## What they do

Everyone works the biomass, in the end, but the trades are jealous of each other.

The **mat-tenders** wade the fresh terraces in plated waders, thinning and turning the white curtains so they don't go black, and they are the ones who take the *cold draught* four times a shift — a bitter enzyme brew rendered from comber gut that keeps a body running for about six hours at minus fifteen and then stops, all at once. Every Hanthan outdoors is on a chemical clock and can feel it running down; they describe the last twenty minutes as *going thin*, and they turn for the gate without discussing it, the way you would come in out of rain.

The **harvesters** go up into the flyer columns with net-frames and take a tithe, never more, and they are pious about it in a way that has nothing to do with any god. *The fauna has right of way* is not sentiment here; it is the balance sheet. There is a whole civic office of **wardens** whose entire function is to escort animals: to walk a rank of scrapers across a bridge and stop traffic for the hour it takes, to open an exhaust duct in nesting season and let a colony out and put it back a week later, to stand in a ladder-street at three in the morning with a lantern shuttered and a clicker going, telling the descending combers *this way, this way, this way*.

The **masons** do not lay stone; they listen to it. A wall grown a millimetre a day rings differently at year three than at year eleven, and a mason's craft is to hear the flaw coming and cut the flow before the plate goes brittle. They work with their foreheads against the growing face and their eyes shut, which is redundant, and they shut them anyway.

The **excavators** are the historians, and the historians are gravediggers in reverse. Eleven Hanthas lie below the current street, filled solid, sealed by the same brine that made them. Excavators go down with heated wire into their great-grandparents' bedrooms and take out what the stone kept, which is everything — chairs, tone-grooves, half-eaten meals, gloves, and people, perfect, sharp-edged, embedded to the eyelash. There is no burial on the Sill. The dead are washed, arranged, given their tools and their groove-plate, and left where the brine can reach them; in a century they are cellar, in three they are bedrock, and in five somebody's wire comes through the wall and finds them, and they are lifted out and known by name, because the name is a note and the note is cut into the plate they are holding.

This makes for a strange trade in **posers** — the undertakers of Hantha, who are less concerned with the corpse than with the audience three hundred years out. A poser thinks about how a body will read to a stranger's hands in the fifth century. They arrange the limbs open, the face turned toward the likeliest approach, the tools laid out legible. It is considered a great vulgarity to be found badly.

The **emitter choir** maintains the carrier tone, and outranks water and heat. Twice in recorded history the carrier has failed — the Muted Hours, one of forty minutes and one of nine — and both are taught to children as the closest thing this culture has to hell: two hundred thousand people abruptly struck blind at nine levels of bridge, all of them droning at the top of their lungs into a city that had stopped answering.

## The Holders

And then there are the ones who hold the fields.

No visiting physicist has ever accounted for the formwork, and the Hanthans cannot help, because they do not experience it as physics. A shaped volume of intention is kept in the brine flow and the brine deposits a wall inside it, and the shape must be *kept*, continuously, by a person, for as long as the wall is growing. Eleven years for a house. Four generations for a tower. The stone is not what's slow. Attention is what's slow.

Holders sit in damped chambers, one to a building, and do not drone. They are the licensed holes in the world: the only Hanthans permitted permanent silence, and consequently regarded with a reverence that has a thread of horror running through it. Children are told not to knock on a Holder's door and are not told why. A Holder eats what's brought, is bathed by family, and is spoken *of* rather than to. Their own drones atrophy; many go deaf, and many were deaf to begin with — deafness on the Sill is not a minor thing, it is blindness and muteness and invisibility all at once, and for most of the city's history the deaf became Holders because it was the one place a deaf person could be indispensable. Whether that was mercy or use is argued about now, loudly, in exactly the way such things are argued about everywhere.

When a Holder dies mid-tower the wall stops. It resumes when the next one takes the shape up, and it never resumes quite true; there is a seam, a *stutter course*, a hairline horizontal you can feel with a thumb and hear as a flatness in the building's ring, and every tall building in Hantha has three or four of them. Families point them out with enormous pride. That one is my great-grandmother, they say, meaning: that is where she stopped.

And because a Holder's note is retired at death and re-issued to whoever picks the shape back up — same pitch, same wobble, same step-down — the tower is, for four hundred years, held by one voice with several bodies in it. Ask who built it and you get a hum, and if you ask whether that is a person or a lineage or the building itself, they will not understand the question, because on the Sill those are three ways of saying the same thing, and the answer is standing right there, singing, one storey higher than it was.
