## The People of the Ninth Sill

### Bodies

Begin with the chest, because that is what the plateau makes first.

Measure a grown person here around the ribs, under the arms, and you will get a figure close to two-thirds of their standing height. They are not tall — a hand shorter than the traders who come up from the wet country — but they are deep, and the depth is not fat. It is lung, and the barrel of bone built to house it, and a diaphragm that works without rest at a rate that a lowlander only reaches while running. They breathe faster than you and never mention it. Children are born small and blue-lipped and spend their first year doing nothing but growing the apparatus.

Their fingers thicken at the tips, the nails curving over like small spoons — the sign of a lifetime of thin air, so universal here that it is simply what a hand looks like, and a lowlander's flat fingertips are considered slightly obscene, like an animal's.

The face is built against light. The plateau's sun at four kilometres, with a salt pan under it, is a hostile object, and so: heavy brows, a fold of lid that shades the eye almost to a slit, lashes long enough that in the cold hour they carry their own rime and a person wakes having to break the ice off their own eyes. Across both cheekbones nearly everyone past thirty carries a dark mask of broken capillaries, a permanent flush that is not health but weather. The nose is long and broad in the bridge — a warming organ, a radiator run backwards, taking in air at minus twenty and delivering it wet and tolerable to the throat.

And then, in perhaps one in forty of the old, the eyes have gone. Salt-glare does it slowly; forty years of looking at a bright pan takes the centre of the sight and leaves a ring. The blind here are not idle and not pitied. They are put to the cisterns, because a cistern is judged by ear.

### Water-people

Everything they are proceeds from one fact: the water does not fall from the sky, it condenses on things, and therefore water is a *harvest of surfaces*. A people whose water comes from rain build channels. A people whose water comes from fog build fur.

So their coats are fleecy on the outside on purpose. A good overgarment is felted hard on the inside, waxy with lanolin at the shoulders, and deliberately left rough and shaggy across the yoke and hood, because a person who walks through the fog for an hour comes home carrying water. At every doorway of the Ninth Sill, at the head of the ladder, there is a shallow stone trough with a wringing-bar across it, and the first act of coming indoors is to strip the coat and twist it out — a cupful, two on a good night, spiralling grey into the household trough. It is done without thought or comment, the way you might scrape your boots. A guest does it into the host's trough. The gift is the guest's; nobody says thank you, but the water is counted.

Their greeting, at the door, translates roughly as *you came in heavy*, and it means both that you are wet and that you are welcome, and it is the closest thing they have to an endearment.

The combs on the ridges are the same principle at civic scale, and here is the thing about the combs that the description of them from a distance leaves out: the rope is hair, and much of the hair is human.

Hair is a crop. Men and women both grow it, and it is cut at the shoulder every thirty months by a specialist who also spins it, and a household's cutting is recorded on slate like a yield. Human hair beads better than ungulate hair — it is finer, it holds the droplet longer before it falls — so it is reserved for the outer warp, the windward face of the comb where the rime builds thickest. And because the dead are shorn before they are taken up the hill, every new comb is strung, along its leading edge, with the hair of people who are no longer alive. The city drinks through its ancestors. They are perfectly matter-of-fact about this. It is not reverence; it is that the ancestors' hair is the best rope, and it would be wasteful to put good rope anywhere but the front.

### The voice, and the absence of ringing

They are quiet, and they are quiet in a way that has become a moral position.

There is no metal to strike, no wheel to turn, no wood in quantity, and there is thin air that eats sound at fifty metres. Into that silence they have put a very small, very precise repertoire. Ordinary speech is pitched low and close, almost sub-vocal by lowland standards, delivered mouth-near-ear in the dark rooms. Loudness is not aggression here, it is *clumsiness* — the social register of a man who cannot judge a distance, who has misread the room, who is in some fundamental sense poorly made. The worst thing you can say of a public figure is that he speaks at the volume of his own body.

For distance they whistle. It is not a language exactly, but it is more than signalling: a whistled register carrying names, numbers, tool-requests, weather, and about forty verbs, thrown across a basin where a shout would die. It grew up inside the whistling of the marmot colonies and has been shaped by them for so long that the two systems have made a treaty. There is a whistle-form the people call the *soft ask*, pitched slightly under the rodents' alarm interval, that a colony will not flee from. Children learn it before they learn to spin. And on the inhabited ridges the marmots, for their part, have learned to sit up but not to bolt when a human whistles — which means that a stranger's badly-pitched whistle empties a hillside, and the whole city knows within a minute that someone unskilled has arrived.

Their music, such as it is, is water. The cisterns are corbelled domes, plastered smooth, and over generations the masons discovered that a dome can be tuned: that the fall of the inlet drip into the standing surface, in a particular volume of air, has a pitch, and that the pitch shifts as the level rises. Every cistern in the Ninth Sill has a voice. Householders know their own the way you know a relative's cough. Children fall asleep to it. The blind cistern-sounders can tell you a level to within a hand's depth by tapping the dome and listening to the note come back, and they are paid for this in dried marmot and standing.

A marriage, in the slate documents, is a merger of catchment: two channels are cut together and the two domes are joined. The ceremony is that both families go down into the dark of the new joined cistern, sit on the steps, and say nothing at all for as long as it takes the inlet to fill the joined air with its new pitch, which nobody has ever heard before and which will be the sound of that household for a hundred years. That is the wedding. Afterwards there is food on the roof and a great deal of quiet talking, and the couple are teased, and the pitch is discussed critically by everyone, like the appearance of a bride's dress somewhere else.

### The shape of a day

They are a twilight people, and this surprises visitors who expect a hard-working peasantry out under the sun.

The sun is not a friend. From the second hour to the seventh the plateau is a lit anvil: dry, blue-black overhead, ultraviolet enough to blister a lowlander's lip in an afternoon, and there is nothing to do out in it that cannot be done better wet. So they work the rime hour and the drip hour, and they work again at dusk when the herds come in, and the middle of the day they spend underground in the dark rooms — spinning, plaiting, dressing stone, sleeping the second sleep, arguing, nursing, teaching. Noon is the vulgar hour, the hour of dogs and un-builders and idiots. Their word for a fool is, literally, *one who is out at height of light*.

The dawn work is rime-scraping. Before the sun tips the rim, children go along the flat roof-stones and the pot rims and the railings with wooden paddles, shaving the frost fur into clay jars — the sweetest water there is, taken before the melt can pick up dust, and reserved for the sick and the very old. Then the sun comes over, and the combs go black in that one-minute wave, and everything drips, and for ninety minutes the whole city stands in its channels with its hands in cold water, unstopping, sluicing, checking the troughs for lichen. Rope fouled with lichen drips wrong — the droplet hangs and evaporates instead of falling — and so there is an entire small profession of comb-scourers, mostly adolescent, mostly unpopular, who go along the warp with a bone blade and a bad temper.

Twice a year the quiet breaks completely. When the waterfowl come down onto the standing water, the whole city goes to the pan with poles and nets and shouts — actually shouts, permitted, obligatory, one of the only sanctioned loudnesses in a life — and for ten days there is meat and grease and down and a smell nobody who grew up here can ever describe without going strange in the face. Then the birds leave, and what remains is the real prize: the fouling. Nitrogen arrives on wings once a season and departs, and every child above the age of five spends the following fortnight scraping the pans and carrying wicker loads of it up to the terraces, where it is weighed, recorded on slate, and divided by a formula so old that its arithmetic has become a proverb. A generous man is described as *a good sky*.

### The uphill trades

Above the living city are the eight dead ones, and there are people whose whole lives are spent up there in the dust.

The un-builders quarry the ancestors. It is cheaper to carry a dressed lintel two hundred metres down than to cut a new one, so every summer the crews go up into the collapsed courses of the Seventh and Sixth and Fifth, prise out stone that was shaped for buildings nobody remembers the use of, and bring it down on their backs in wicker frames. They work dry — everything they drink is hauled up — and they come back with cracked lips and a reputation. They are the only people in the Ninth Sill who habitually speak loudly, because they spend their days shouting across an empty hillside to each other, and they never quite lose it; you can identify an un-builder in a crowded room instantly, and they are regarded with the affectionate condescension reserved for people who are good at something disreputable.

They are also, by accident, the city's historians. They can date a stone by its dressing-marks to within two Sills. They know the ground plans of eight abandoned towns better than they know the streets they live in. And they find the letters.

It became a habit, at some point in the Fifth or Sixth, to mortar a written slate into a wall — advice, complaints, boasts, jokes, curses aimed at the descendants who would one day pull the wall down. The un-builders find them and bring them down and they are read aloud on the roofs, and the good ones enter the language. A slate from the Sixth Sill, found perhaps ninety years ago, says: *whoever takes this stone, the lintel above it is cracked through on the hidden face, and my brother-in-law knew and said nothing.* That sentence is now the standard idiom for a concealed defect in anything from a cistern to a marriage.

Higher still are the platforms, and the bird.

The dead stay at the altitude where they died. They are carried up, shorn, and laid out for the great soaring thing with the two-metre wingspan, which does nothing all morning until the ridges make their thermals and then comes without being called. What is left goes into the ossuaries, which are stacked by altitude, which is to say by date, which is to say that a family's genealogy is a physical climb. *How high do you go?* means *how old is your line*. To visit your grandmother you ascend forty metres; to visit her grandmother, a hundred and thirty, into air so thin and dry that you must carry water and cannot stay past midday.

This is why grief here has the taste of thirst. Mourning is a climb: you go up loaded and come down empty, breathless, with your mouth like slate, and the physical sensation of the uphill is so bound to the emotion that their word for sorrow is also a word used of a steep dry slope.
