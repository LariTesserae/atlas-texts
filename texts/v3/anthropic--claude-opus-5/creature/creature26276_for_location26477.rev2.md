# The Census

You cannot count what lives here, because counting assumes a population and what this valley has are *rates*. Arrivals per hour. Grams of nitrogen per square metre per year, delivered by wind. Generations per thaw. Ask who lives in the valley and the honest answer is a flow diagram, not a list — but a list is what you want, so start at the bottom, where the bodies are smallest and the numbers are absurd.

---

**The film.**

On the ablation zone, in the shallow meltwater grooves that run downslope across the bare ice, there is a dark bloom that you will first mistake for grit. Put your palm flat near it and it moves. Springtails — six-legged, two millimetres, matte black, a hundred thousand to the square metre in the good grooves, which is to say a biomass per hectare not far off a herd of sheep, distributed in a layer thin enough to be wiped away with a sleeve.

They have solved the two problems. The first is freezing: at dusk they purge their gut, dump water across their cuticle, and let themselves dry down into a state where there is nothing left in them that ice can nucleate. They do not resist the cold. They concede to it in a controlled way and come back at ten in the morning. The second problem is heat, which sounds like a joke until you understand that their thermal death point is about eight degrees, roughly the temperature of your hand. You are lethal to them. Everything below this altitude is lethal to them. They live in a band four hundred metres thick and their whole world is the top centimetre of a surface that is being destroyed at a metre a year.

They eat algae off the ice, and pollen, and their own dead, and whatever fell out of the sky.

Hunting them: a midge that has given up flight entirely — legs long, wings reduced to stubs, walking on ice at temperatures where a lubricant would seize, active at minus sixteen. And a jumping spider, pale, the size of a lentil, living higher than any vertebrate on the continent, with no web, because there is nothing here for a web to catch that is not already dead on the surface. It hunts by eye, in daylight that never exceeds the brightness of a north-facing room, and its eyes are enormous for its head, and it will track your finger across three metres of rock.

---

**The holes.**

Every dark grain on the ice is an engine. It absorbs what little light there is, warms, sinks, and drills a cylinder into the glacier — vertical, straight-sided, twenty centimetres deep, holding cold clear water over a floor of black silt. Ten thousand of them per square kilometre. Each one is a pond with a lifespan of a summer.

Inside: filaments of cyanobacteria, gluing grains into crumb structure, doing in a season the only soil-making anywhere in the catchment. Rotifers. Nematodes. Tardigrades in the hundreds per gram, which when the hole freezes in September will fold their legs, expel their water, replace it with sugar, and become dust — indistinguishable from the silt they live in, respiring at a rate too low to measure, for nine months. They are not dormant in any sense that involves endurance. They are simply absent, and then present again, and the interval does not register on them at all. A tardigrade in that silt has no opinion about winter.

---

**The stone.**

On rock that was under ice a century ago: nothing, still. Sterile. Give it forty years and a black crustose lichen arrives as a spore out of the air, establishes as a smudge the size of a thumbprint, and then grows a millimetre a decade — so that the dark stippling on the older moraines is a map of exposure age, readable to anyone patient enough to measure it.

On the limestone itself, where it is pale and translucent, there is a green line two millimetres below the surface. Algae living *inside* the rock, in the pore space, photosynthesising through a lens of stone, hiding from a sun that in this valley is not even visible. They are in there year-round, in the only stable habitat available, and the price of that stability is that they grow at a rate which makes the lichens look frantic.

---

**The workforce.**

Everything above lives off delivery. Wind brings pollen, aphids lifted from the foothills, thrips, small dead beetles, spores, soot — a continuous fine rain of other places. Strip the wind out of the accounting and the surface of this valley starves in a season. Its food chains are anchored four hundred kilometres away.

There is exactly one community here that does not depend on imports, and you will never see it.

Under forty metres of ice, in the dark, at the bed, the glacier is grinding rock into flour, and freshly fractured mineral surfaces are not inert. Broken silicate bonds strip hydrogen out of water. Crushed pyrite oxidises. The comminution itself throws off a steady chemical wage — hydrogen, ferrous iron, sulfide, at a rate proportional to how fast the mountain is being destroyed. And there are bacteria down there living on it: iron oxidisers, sulfur oxidisers, methanogens in the anoxic pockets where the meltwater goes still. Ten million cells per gram of subglacial slurry. No sunlight has ever reached them. No wind delivers them anything. They are eating the mountain, straight, as the mountain is being milled.

Everything else in the valley is a tenant. These are the only residents, and they are on the payroll of the plant.

---

**The pulses.**

At dawn, for these three weeks, the valley has a ceiling.

Geese come over the notch in waves, low enough that you can see the effort — wingbeats deep and fast, no gliding, no rest, in air with a third of the oxygen you'd get at sea level and a temperature of minus thirty. Their haemoglobin binds oxygen harder than yours ever will, by a single substitution in a single subunit. Their flight muscle is threaded with capillaries at a density that reads, on a slide, as a mistake. They fly at dawn because cold air is dense air, and they are trading heat loss for lift, and they have been running that trade on this particular col since before the col was this high.

They do not live here. They cross. The whole species passes through the valley's airspace twice a year in perhaps sixty hours of total occupancy, and in those hours it is by a large margin the most biomass the valley has ever held, and none of it stops, and none of it feeds, and by nine in the morning the sky is empty again and the number of vertebrates per cubic kilometre has fallen back to something close to zero.

---

**The commuters.**

Choughs — three dozen of them, black, glossy, with red legs and an obviously frivolous relationship to the updraft along the west buttress. They roost lower, in a gorge below the treeline, and come up daily to probe the moss flushes for grubs and to fall out of the sky sideways, repeatedly, for no return that any energy budget will explain. They are the only things here that make noise on purpose.

A lammergeier at the top of the airspace, crossing on a fixed bearing, not hunting. It eats bone — almost exclusively bone, dropped onto slabs and swallowed in splinters, dissolved by a stomach acid strong enough to be a workplace hazard. This valley does not yet produce enough carcasses to feed such a bird. There is no ossuary here, no accumulated deadfall, no generations of bones under the scree, because there have not been generations. It is a specialist evolved for an older landscape, flying over a young one because a young one is on the way to somewhere it can eat.

Snowfinches nest in a cavity under a rockfall block on the north side, in a nest of grass stems, lined — and this is the closest thing to architecture in thirty kilometres — with goose down picked off the marsh after the passage. Imported insulation. The valley has no textile of its own.

Sharing the same broken ground, pikas: fat, earless-looking, no tail, out at the entrance of their runs for two minutes at a time, hauling cut stems of cushion plant into a hay pile that will feed them through a winter they do not hibernate through. They are the only creature here that stores anything. In a place where nothing is built, the pika builds a barn.

---

**The margin.**

At the valley mouth, where meltwater spreads thin and the moss thickens into something you could call turf, twenty blue sheep stand in an extended line, heads down, moving at the speed of grazing. Grey-brown against grey rock, essentially invisible at four hundred metres, and they know it — they don't run from a skyline, they freeze, and you lose them.

They are working an inch of vegetation that took eighty years to establish over ground scraped bare, and which a single failed ice dam upstream can bury under half a metre of grit in an afternoon. Their pasture is not a place. It's a lease with no fixed term.

And above them on a shelf, forty metres up, a wolf lies with its chin on its paws.

It is not resting. It is running the numbers, which are bad: the sheep are uphill of it, which is wrong; they are strung out along a face where any charge has to be committed within the first six seconds; the wind is coming down-valley off the ice, which is in its favour, but the light is flat enough that its own approach will be silhouetted against the pale marsh rather than absorbed by shadow. It has been there ninety minutes. It will probably be there another two hours and then get up and leave without moving toward them at all, and that will have been the correct decision.

It is thin, in the way that is normal here rather than alarming. Its territory takes in four valleys, because one valley of this kind cannot pay for a wolf. Its breath has frosted the guard hairs around its muzzle into a grey rime that will melt at noon and refreeze by four. It was born in a den in a river terrace two days' travel south, in softer country, from a lineage that is not a mountain lineage at all — everything about it is a generalist from lower ground, arrived here recently, making do.

---

**The thing about all of them.**

Not one organism in this valley is adapted to *this* valley. There hasn't been time. The lichens are the descendants of a flora that had soil. The lammergeier is waiting for a bone supply that is ten thousand years out. The wolf is a lowland animal at altitude. The geese are shaped by a col that is four millimetres a year higher than it was when they learned it, and they are climbing, every year, slightly harder than their parents did, and no one has told them. Every lineage here is either early or late.

Except, forty metres down, in the dark, at the bed: the ones eating the ground-up mountain as fast as the mountain can be ground. Their food supply is exactly the rate of destruction. They scale with the uplift. They are the only things in thirty kilometres that are precisely on time.

And there is a party of four somewhere on the moraine with wet boots, notebooks swelling in the damp, breathing thirty-two times a minute at rest — the worst-adapted organisms present, sustained entirely by import, resident for eleven days. Also a delivery. Also temporary. Also not finished happening.
