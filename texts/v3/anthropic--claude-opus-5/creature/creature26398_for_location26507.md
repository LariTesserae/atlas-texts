## Who is at home

The room answers in the wrong order. It offers the small residents first, and they are the ones easiest to mistake for the whole.

---

### The grain of rice

The isopod has no name because nobody has stayed long enough to give it one. Eight millimetres, unpigmented, the cuticle clear enough that you can read the gut as a dark thread running the length of it — and the thread is the colour of whatever mat that individual grazes, cream in most of them, rust in the two that work the orange seep at the back. No eyes. Not even the vestigial pits where eyes were given up; the lineage has been in the dark long enough that the question has stopped being asked, and what is left on the head is a pair of antennae longer than the body, held forward and slightly apart, sweeping.

It walks a circuit. The rim of its basin, up the flank of the drape above it as far as the film of water reaches, across, down, back. The circuit takes something like forty minutes. It grazes as it goes, and where it has grazed the mat is thinner but not broken, and by the time the animal comes round again the thinning has filled. It does this for the length of its life, which is four years or a little more — very long, for that size, but nothing here hunts it and it has never in the history of its kind had to hurry.

It breeds at no season. There is no month in this room that is better than another month, so the young come one and two at a time, continuously, and the population of the room is about forty animals, and has been about forty for as long as there has been a room. Every death is filled within the week. Count them monthly for a hundred years and you would get the same number every time and never once the same individuals. The isopod is not a creature. It is a standing wave with legs.

### The snail

Shell the size and shade of a clipped thumbnail, and no one has seen one move, because it travels roughly its own length in a day and does so at no particular hour. It grazes by rasping, and what it rasps is not only the mat but the fresh skin of mineral laid over the mat, which makes it one of the very few forces in this room that takes stone away rather than adding it. Its work and the water's work cancel. That is not a metaphor; it is the local arithmetic.

In the collar of the pool, sealed in the travertine at every depth, are shells. Hundreds of them, thousands, a thin conglomerate of thumbnails going down and down through the rim, all the same species, all the same size. A living snail and an entombed snail are indistinguishable to the eye. You would have to pry, and the prying would be the largest event of the century.

### The mat

Here the scale changes and you have to stop thinking in animals.

Thirty square metres, give or take, spread in sheets a few grains thick across the hot seeps and the pool collar; total living weight perhaps that of a small dog. It is not a species. Break the surface with a fingernail — don't, but if you did — and you would find it stratified like a page: on top the white filaments, whitened by globules of elemental sulfur held inside the cells, oxidising the sulfide that arrives in the water; beneath them, where the ripple-light barely reaches, a layer the colour of a bruise, purples and dull pinks, working the same chemistry from the other side; beneath that, a black film one hair thick that smells, if you break it, faintly of a struck match. The rust-orange patches are a different council altogether, iron.

It does not spread. This is the thing people notice and misread as feebleness. The mat does not spread because its food arrives at a fixed rate — so many micromoles of sulfide per litre, so many litres per minute, forever — and that ration supports exactly this much life and not one grain more. The tidemark is not a wall the mat has run into. It is the line where the last of the sulfide is used up. The edge of the organism is a sum, and the sum has been the same sum for longer than there have been people on the flats, which is why the stone above the line and the stone below it are different colours: not a boundary held, but a boundary that has never once had occasion to move.

It does not eat the sun. It eats the exhalation of the ground. It is a mouth laid over a crack.

### What the mat is the lip of

The water is 38 degrees because it comes from a long way down and has been coming a long time. Where it has been, it was not sterile.

The real inhabitant of the warm room does not live in the warm room. It lives in the rock beneath it and around it and for an unknown distance out under the flats and the shallow sea: cells in the films of water inside fractures, in pore space, in clay, at densities so low that a litre of that water is emptier than air, dividing perhaps once in a decade, perhaps once in a century, running metabolisms so slow that the difference between one of them living and one of them dead is a question of definition rather than observation. It has a volume measured in cubic kilometres and a heartbeat measured in human lifetimes. It has no shape. It has been there since before the limestone was uplifted and it will be there after.

The mats are only where it is pushed out into the light and given more than it can use. They are the flare at the vent, the bright fast fringe, the part that grows and dies at a speed we can see. Everything visibly alive in this room — the sheets, the fringe on the collar, the fed snails, the forty isopods — is a small hot excess at the surface of something enormous and cold and unhurried, and none of it is the animal itself.

### The warmth

Which the room's other resident, the warmth, also is: an excess. It rises with the water and it cannot leave, because the door is low and the room is a bowl, and so it sits. It has sat. It fills every corner of the ceiling and every gap between the columns at the same temperature to within a fraction, and it has done this so consistently that it has built its own house — every drape and knob and melon of stone in the room is a shape the warmth made out of the water it came up with. To say the warmth lives here is only sloppy in the way that saying the coral lives in the reef is sloppy.

### The one who sits

And then there is the person, and there is only one of them, and there were about three hundred.

The two steps in the pool rim look like one lifetime's work. They are not. A single pair of feet, going in and out every day for eighty years, would leave almost nothing on travertine; it takes generations to rub stone to satin. But the satin has one shape, foot-shaped, because the rim offers exactly one scallop wide enough to stand in and everyone who ever came stood in it, and set their weight the same way, and reached down with the same hand to the same knob of stone to steady themselves — there is a smoothed place there too, at the height of a hand. So the room has done to them what it does to the moth and the bowl: taken many and coated them into one object.

He or she comes in from the flats, ducks under the brow, and stops, because there is nowhere to walk. Waits the four minutes for the eyes. Crosses sideways, knee up, foot into a bowl of blood-warm water, to the bench that someone in the line built out of slabs pried from the back wall and that the seep has since fused into a single pale object, and sits, and is warm from underneath. Uses the channel — an hour's work with a chert flake, done well, delivering one basin's overflow into a squared hollow at hand's height beside the bench, for reasons that were obvious to whoever cut it and are now unrecoverable. Goes down the two steps into the pool, to the collarbones, and stays there long enough that the outline of the body stops reporting.

No images on the walls. This is not an oversight. You make images where things change and you would prefer them to change differently — where the herds move, the water rises, the child sickens. Nothing here is going anywhere. There was nothing to petition and no future tense to petition it in. They brought a bowl and left it and the room closed over it, and whether it was set down as a gift or simply set down, the room does not distinguish, because the room's only response to anything is to coat it.

The visits are spaced further apart than human memory and much closer together than the room's. Nobody ever came twice in a way that felt like a tradition to them. From the room's side, though, the interval is nothing: someone is always sitting on the bench, in the way that every hollow always holds two fingers of water. The last one may have been two centuries ago. The next is not overdue.

### The news

Twice a year a moth comes in under the brow and circles the ripple-light on the ceiling, and this is the only unpredictable event in the room, and it is also the only food that ever arrives from above. It drowns. The mats take it up inside a day — the white fringe on the collar goes momentarily thicker where the body lies, a brief local wealth, the isopods' one banquet — and the stone takes what is left inside a season. In the collar, at every depth, sealed among the thumbnails, are the ghost-shapes of wings.

That is the room's entire method of receiving history: it converts it into ornament.

At the back, one stalactite has failed to reach the meniscus of its basin and drips, every nine seconds, and has done so for an unguessable time, and will. In that basin, one of the forty lives and walks its forty-minute circuit and grazes and is replaced. In all its four years it has never once been struck by the drop, because the drop always falls in the same place.
