## The people of the Sill

You will not see them at first. This is not modesty and not hiding; it is that the eye, coming up out of the gorge into that white cancellation, looks for the interruption that a person normally makes in a landscape — the upright dark, the dragged shadow — and there isn't one. There is a hush of moving water and a great deal of motion in it, low down, and it takes a minute or two before the motion sorts itself into backs and baskets and heads, two hundred of them, working at the speed of a market, arranged across the terraces like a scatter of pale stones that have decided something.

They are dusted all over. Everyone is. The mist comes off the pools loaded and dries on you, so that by midmorning every person wears a fine bloom of lime on the shoulders, in the eyebrows, in the parting of the hair, and this is not considered dirt any more than sweat is considered dirt; it is simply the day accumulating on you, and you knock it off at dusk with the same horn paddle you used at dawn. Children are white to the elbow. Old men have a permanent chalky collar where the bloom has got into the neck-creases and stayed. The word they use for it is the same word they use for the growth on a rim, and the joke is very old and still gets used: *look at him, he's coming along nicely.*

### The body it makes

Altitude has given them the chest, which is deep and wide and sits oddly over narrow hips, and the slow blood, and the ability to work all day at a crouch without appearing to breathe. The Sill has given them the rest.

They are short, and stoop, and the stoop is not age. It is the ceilings. A house comes down on you at the rate of a thumb's width a year, and you learn a low walk the way other people learn to duck through their own doorway, and by thirty you carry the low walk outdoors with you, out onto ground with nothing over it but eleven thousand feet of sky. Incomers find this comic for about a week.

Their feet are soft as a lapdog's — felt-soled from infancy, never on grit, always on warm wet polished stone — and their gait is a thing you could pick out at any distance: the toe goes first and rests, testing heat and depth, and only then the weight. They do it on dry ground. They do it in the lowland market three days' walk below, toeing at solid flagstones like a wader.

And the Sill builds inside them too. The water is thick with what it carries, and so are they. Their bones are heavy for their size; their teeth wear flat early on the grit in the algae bread and then simply stop, hard-glazed, and last. Tendons chalk up. Old shoulders grind. Nearly everyone past fifty passes small stones, painfully, periodically, and has a name for it — *the inner terrace* — and a sign for it, a rueful one, which also serves as a general-purpose complaint about anything slow and inevitable. They say a person is a pool with a rim on it. They say the water lays itself down in you the same as everywhere; it can't tell the difference; why should it.

Their eyes go. All of them, eventually. It is not a disease there, it is a stage of life.

### The hands

Speech is in the hands, and so the hands are the most cared-for things on the Sill, more than tools, more than children in a certain sense, because a child can be replaced by another child and a hand cannot. They are greased with shrimp-fat against the lime, which otherwise cracks them. They are kept out of the hot pools. A man who works with obsidian works with his fingers wrapped and his palms bare. A crushed hand is a catastrophe of a particular kind — people speak of a man who lost three fingers to a rim-fall and afterwards could still say everything, but slowly and with an accent, like a foreigner, in the mouth he had grown up in.

The signing is fast and full of jokes. Six hundred signs, but the grammar is in the tempo and the height — a thing said up near the face is formal, down at the hip is intimate or obscene, and a sign made slowly in the middle is the deadliest sarcasm available to the species. The best insults are made at a person's back where they can be denied. There is a whole genre of gag about ceilings coming down; there is a stock comic character, the man who scraped his threshold his entire life and died with his head bent, and a two-handed sign that summons him, and it never stops being funny.

They have no spoken name for themselves. The sign is a small scraping motion, the quartz-on-stone motion, repeated twice. It means *the ones who scrape*, and it also means *still here*, and nobody experiences these as two meanings.

Their personal names are signs as well, invented for them by somebody else, usually mocking, frequently revised. Everyone also has a spoken name, and it is used perhaps four hundred times in a life: at night, indoors, close to the ear, by parents and by lovers. To ask someone to teach you their spoken name is a proposal of marriage. To use it in daylight is an assault. The one time an entire crowd hears a person's spoken name is when the whistle has gone and somebody is being screamed for, and afterwards nobody repeats it, and the person is treated for a few days with a tenderness that has something of embarrassment in it, as if they had been seen without clothes.

### The first year, and the last

Infants cannot be quiet, so infants are put underground.

Every generation keeps one or two of the old sealed domes from being sealed: reopened, scraped out, re-scraped, thick-walled, deep in the layers, with a crawl-mouth and a stone plug. These are the bawl-rooms. A woman goes down with her newborn and stays the best part of a year, with two or three others and their two or three others, and in that bell of white stone the babies scream as loudly as they like, and the sound does not leave, and above them a man scrapes a threshold and hears nothing but the fizz of the water.

So the first world of every person on the Sill is warm, curved, glowing faintly at the crown where the stone is thin, and *loud* — the only loud room any of them will ever know. They come up out of it into the light and the hush at walking age, half-deafened, and are taught in their first summer the two facts: you have no shadow here, watch your feet; and your voice goes nowhere now, use your hands.

And at the other end: the eyes fail, the milk comes across them, and a person becomes *shaded*. They are given a dog. They stop working and start listening, and are stationed — there is no better word — at the rims that matter, in the lee places, sitting still with their heads tilted, for hours, for years. They hear the pitch of a vent shift days before it shifts. They hear the dry tick behind a wall. They are the reason the town is still there.

And because they cannot see hands, speech comes back to them. The shaded are spoken to. You crouch and put your mouth near their ear and murmur, in daylight, aloud, which for everyone else is unthinkable; or you put your hands into their hands and sign into the palms, which is a slower dialect, older, with different jokes in it. Young people queue in the afternoon to put their hands into an old woman's hands and be told what she thinks of them. They come away stung.

You arrive blind and you leave blind, they say. This is about old age, and it is also about incomers, who come up from the valley and are struck sightless by the Sill for their first weeks and have to be led about by a dog, exactly like the shaded, and who sit in the dark of a house learning six hundred signs at the speed of a toddler while their spouse's family watches with whatever grace it can manage.

### The ones who are allowed to shout

Trade is done by four or five families who live at the Cold Lip, downwind, next to the willow grove, on the margin in every sense. They go down with the caravans and come back with obsidian, grain, dye, salt, news, and occasionally a husband or a wife, since two hundred people cannot keep marrying each other forever.

They are permitted speech. They have to be — you cannot haggle in a valley market with your hands, you cannot call to a mule. Their voices come back up the gorge rusty and too big for the place, and for the first days home they are physically hard to be around, like someone who has brought a smell in. Their children are loud and must be un-taught. Everyone needs them. Nobody entirely likes them. They know it, and they have their own hand-sign for themselves, which is coarse.

What do they sell? Bowls, mainly, and bangles and boxes and little animals, sawn out of young stone while it is soft as cheese and sold before it sets, so that a lowland buyer watches the thing harden in her own hand over an afternoon and pays extra for having seen it.

But the real trade is the keeping. People send things up. A shoe, a knot of hair, a letter sewn in a bag, a betrothal-cord, a tooth. The Cold Lip families take them, and take the fee, and lay them in a splash-line, and note the terrace; and three years later, or thirty, someone comes up the gorge to collect a small bright pod in which a shoe can just be made out, under two knuckles of clear stone. It cannot be opened. That is the point. There is a steady stream of lowlanders willing to walk a day and a half up a stinking gorge for an object that can never be got at again.

### The dead

There is no soil and no fuel. So the dead are seated in a chosen shallow, arranged by the family, and left, and the water does what it does.

Mourning is therefore measured in thickness. The first year you can still see the face and it is unbearable. By the third the features are a suggestion under a glaze and you can visit without coming apart. By the twentieth there is a pale swelling on the terrace wall with the shape of a seated person in it and nothing you would call a face, and by then you have generally stopped going, and the grief is considered properly finished — not by a date, but by a depth. People will tell you, of an old sorrow: *it's about a fingernail now.* Children are taught which swellings are people and which are birds, and they learn it the way children elsewhere learn the names of streets.

Not everyone stays. The shaded very often choose the other thing: they get up one dawn, during the frost-breaking, when everyone is bent to the ice, and they walk out along a dry rim to the Cold Lip and go over, out of the world, into the cloud. It is not forbidden and it is not encouraged. The dog comes back. Nobody follows to look, and the reason nobody follows is the same unexplained reason nobody walks on a reflected cirque.

The combers do it too, in their way — no comber corpse has ever been found on the Sill; they take their own to the Lip and push them off, and then come back up the channels and go on digging. The two authors of the town dispose of themselves identically. This is noticed. It is not discussed much.

### What is actually living there

Ask them what the Sill is and you will get a shrug, because it is a stupid question, like asking what a knee is.

But watch the dawn. The frost-lace has gone down over everything in the night, an inch thick, feathered, and the whole population is out on it in the first grey, bent, breaking it with horn paddles, and the sound is enormous by local standards and still no louder than rain on a roof. And along the line of bent backs a touch goes, hand to wrist to hand, the full length of the town and back, carrying the number: how many we are this morning. Two hundred and six. Two hundred and four. If the number has changed the line pauses — one heartbeat, the paddles stopped, the whole staircase silent except for the fizz — and then goes on breaking.

That is the thing that lives here. Not quite the people; the people are the current form of it. It is an arrangement two hundred hands wide that scrapes its thresholds, bribes an animal it cannot argue with, keeps its babies in a hole so as to hear a beetle-tick in a wall, gives its dead to the stone and its old to the cloud, cannot be written down because paper would thicken and seal, and has been holding its exact shape on a shelf the size of a threshing yard for longer than anyone below has any way of checking.

The Sill is not being built. It is being kept, by a hand that never lifts.

They are the hand.
