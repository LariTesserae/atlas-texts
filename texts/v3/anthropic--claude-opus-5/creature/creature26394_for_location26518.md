## What lives there

The inventory is easy, and it is a lie by omission.

You can list them in a notebook in twenty minutes, and people have: one liverwort-analogue of contested affinity, one isopod, one enchytraeid worm, two springtails, an anostracan and a copepod in the pools, tardigrades in four genera, mites, and the bacterial felt that nobody has finished counting. Nine species doing the visible work. None of them larger than your thumb except the isopod, and the isopod is a tool.

That list is not the answer to your question. Work outward from it and it comes apart in your hands.

---

**The one you are standing inside.**

The peat-cutters called it cooperwort, because you found it in staves. The taxonomists have given it three genus names in ninety years and abandoned all three, and the current convention in the literature is to write *the cooperwort* in italics and move on.

It has almost nothing above ground: a ring of grey rosettes the size of a shilling, scattered through the moss in a rough annulus eight metres across, easy to walk over and never notice. Total photosynthetic area, measured at four sites: under two square metres. Total power that could plausibly deliver, at that latitude, averaged over a year: a few watts. It is not enough to hold a chapel at zero. It is not enough by two orders of magnitude, and this was the first thing about the vaults that made anyone angry.

It does not live on light. It lives on the freezing point.

Every gram of water that crosses from liquid to ice gives up three hundred and thirty-four joules, and every gram that goes back takes them again, and the cooperwort has arranged a body in which that crossing happens, at every scale, continuously, in both directions, forever. The felt layer is a heat engine running on a temperature difference of hundredths of a degree, and it is the most patient engine on the planet. It does not need much, because it does not *do* much. It holds still. It holds still with tremendous precision for tens of thousands of years, and everything it eats it spends on the holding.

Which means it can only live in the one place where water cannot decide — and that it makes that place itself, and that the making and the living are not separable operations. This is why nobody can say whether the cooperwort is the vault's inhabitant or the vault's architecture. The question has no purchase. You would have to ask a barrel whether it lives in itself.

It has no nerves. It has the hum instead. The forty-hertz field in that chamber is simultaneously its posture, its instruction set, its attention, and its only utterance, and when you stand in there deafened, unable to speak, you are inside a sentence that has been going on since before the Reformation.

Its lifespan, run out on the fill rate, is about thirty-five thousand years. It is roughly two per cent of the way through. It will die when the volume it is holding open finally closes, and it will die on schedule, having spent everything, the way a salmon dies.

---

**The masons.**

Fourteen tergites of clear biogenic calcite, sixteen legs, and a grey line of gut visible through the back like wire in glass. Ten centimetres nose to telson, and if you get down with your lamp you can read the fine growth banding in the shell, one lamina a season, forty or fifty of them in a big one.

No eyes. Not reduced eyes — none, no pigment spot, no scar in the cuticle where eyes were. In a room that makes a hundred thousand points of light a second, nothing has eyes. The light is not for anybody. It is spillage from accuracy, and the only creature that has ever seen this chamber is you.

They hear with the whole shell. The shell is the same mineral, laid in the same alignment, as the floor they are building; a mason is a fragment of the finished work walking around on the unfinished work, and it is tuned to it, and when the bass note takes on that burr you learn to listen for, the animal's body is already moving before anything you would call a decision could have occurred. They go where they are told. They press down. They hold for fifty minutes and set a seed crystal out of their own underside, and the sparks come faster in a rosette around them, and they move on at about a metre an hour.

Do not tap one. It rings, and the room answers, and the answer costs somebody a week.

They breed in the felt, in nurseries the cooperwort grows for them — grows, plainly, deliberately, chambered and lined and nothing like the rest of the tissue. The larvae hatch soft and cloudy and spend a year drinking seep water until they harden.

And once, in a long life, on a night in early autumn when the surface temperature is within a degree of the vault's, a few dozen adults climb the seam.

This is the only journey any of them ever makes. They come out onto open tundra, glass animals a hand long moving at a metre an hour across frozen moss under a sky they cannot see, and they go — measurably, repeatably — toward the hum of a *different* vault. Most die in the open. Some don't.

They are the reason the sixty vaults know about each other. They arrive carrying a foreign pitch in the lattice of their shells.

---

**The ones that hold the temperature.**

Tease the felt open with a fingernail and the grey fur that blooms out is tardigrades, in the millions, and the important thing about them is that they can be frozen and come back.

That is the thermostat. Not insulation — ballast. Latent-heat ballast, packed at a density of something like four hundred thousand animals to the cubic centimetre, each one a tiny reservoir that absorbs heat at exactly zero by thawing and releases it at exactly zero by freezing, and survives the cycle, and does it again, and has done it eleven thousand times. The chamber reads as instrument failure because it is being buffered by a population of animals whose entire ecological function is to *change phase on behalf of the room*.

They get fed for it. Nothing eats them. Nothing eats anything here.

---

**The ones that keep the room quiet.**

The shivering on the wall is springtails, 1.2 millimetres, in drifts inches deep, and the drifts move.

Map one over a night and it will have travelled two metres along a catenary sheet, thinned here, banked there, and the reverberation time of the chamber will have changed by four hundred milliseconds. They are the soft furnishings. They are alive, and they are being placed, and if you clear a square metre of them with a brush — someone did, in 1974, and wrote it up apologetically — the eleven-second answering ripple goes ragged, the masons stop, and the floor within four metres goes dark for the better part of a day.

The worms graze frost. Specifically, they graze frost that has grown wrong: they crawl over the black liverwort mirror eating malformed feathers and leaving the true ones, which is to say the vault runs quality control by digestion.

The crustaceans in the seep-pools are the filters. Strip them out of a pool and within a fortnight the water goes cloudy with organics and the pool lays chalk instead of lattice — soft, unaligned, useless, a scar the vault will be a century clearing. They keep the feedstock clean. That is all they do and they do it in numbers that make the water look like it is simmering.

Nine species. Not one of them redundant. Not one of them a passenger.

---

**What you are, in there.**

Thirty-seven degrees and eighty watts, in a room that is spending its entire income on hundredths.

Watch your feet. Within about ten minutes of your arrival the sparks go out in a slow halo two metres across, centred on you, and it does not relight for forty minutes after you leave. The masons within that halo stop and wait. The frost print of your palm fills in within a minute because the tissue is repairing what you did to it.

An hour of a human being standing in a triple-point vault costs it, by the most conservative estimate anyone has published, about a decade of accuracy in a patch of floor the size of a hearth-rug.

You are not an inhabitant. You are weather.

---

**The one that is not born.**

Here is the part people resist.

The cooperwort is not building a house and it is not building a seed. Nothing hatches. Nobody has ever found a filled vault in that watershed, and nobody ever will, because the ice left eleven thousand years ago and the fill takes thirty-five thousand. Every vault in that basin is a first attempt by an organism that has never once, in that place, seen the work through.

There is one object. It is in a museum drawer six hundred kilometres south, in a felt-lined box, and it came out of a gravel pit in glacial till: a fist of calcite, flawless, aligned along axes that no drainage or sedimentation can produce, riddled with void inclusions, and every void is fourteen tergites long and has sixteen leg-sockets in it. They died in place at the end. They were cast in. The voids are not damage — take one out and the thing is a resonator with a designed defect structure, which is to say, with a *pattern of silences in it*.

It rings. It has been ringing since it was made. Nobody knows how long that is and nobody is keen to find out, because the standard method would be to strike it.

So: what lives in the Triple-Point Vault.

A liverwort that eats the hesitation of water and has been holding one note since Agincourt. Its masons, blind, glass, obedient, entombed at the finish. Its ballast, its damping, its filters, its graziers — a whole ecology reduced to instrumentation, and not one animal in it that has ever needed to run.
