## The people of the wedge

They are human, and ninety years is not long enough to change a body much, but it is long enough to choose one.

They are short, most of them, and deep through the chest, with shoulders built by rope and forearms built by the long wet stroke. Their legs are lighter than you expect — there is nowhere to walk far to — but the feet are wide, splayed, strong in the toes, because every house is reached by ladder and a child learns to grip a rung with the foot before it learns to grip a spoon. Knees go early. Everyone kneels for a living. There is a particular walk here, a slight rolling care, half of it the polished Ways underfoot and half of it forty years of kneeling on stone.

Their skin is pale as the ash rock, and they are hungry for light in a way that surface people cannot imitate or quite believe. When a pillar swings across a terrace, work stops in it. People stand in the light with their sleeves pushed up and their faces tipped, silent, for ten or fifteen minutes, and then go back to the cords. It is not a pleasure exactly; it is closer to eating. Children born in the narrow ends, where the plates lie flush and the mirror-relays run out, grow bowed in the leg and soft in the skull, and so the infants of the dark quarters are carried out on a rota by women called light-mothers, who have no relation to them, and laid in rows in the pillars like washing. A city with no soil learned early that sunlight is also a crop, and that it too must be hung, mapped, litigated and inherited.

Their eyes are wide-pupilled and very good in gloom, and useless on the plateau. A valley-born adult taken up through a storm gap will often sit down within the first minute — simply sit, on the dry grass, and put a hand over the eyes, and stay there. They have a word for what the open sky does to them. It translates badly: *unlidded*. The horizon is the horror; the wind is an insult; the fact that a thing can fall and nothing will catch it feels to them like living in a house with no floor.

## The hands, which are the biography

Look at anyone's hands here and you will read their life, because the sand has taken their fingerprints off.

This is literal. Adults who have worked cord and slurry for twenty years have palms and finger pads worn smooth and slightly translucent, the ridges gone, the skin split at the knuckles in lime cracks that never fully close and are dressed nightly with fig oil or swift fat. A young hand is soft and legible; an old hand is blank and shining. Nobody can be identified by a finger in the Lidded Valley, and so nobody tried; instead every adult has a **knot**, personal, tied one-handed, in a particular lay of cord, and the knot is the signature. Contracts are lengths of knotted cord hung in a dry tunnel called the Cordary, tens of thousands of them, dusted and turned twice a year by clerks who cannot read but can feel a forgery in the dark.

Greeting is a touch to the other's wrist, and then a half-turn of the palm outward — showing what you are made of. To show your palm to a stranger is courtesy. To refuse is to say *you have no business with my hands*, which is very nearly a declaration of enmity.

## Voices, and the listening

They speak quietly. It is not shyness. It is the same instinct that makes other people avoid stepping on graves.

Ordinary conversation is conducted at what an outsider would call a confidential murmur, spoken into the side of a hood, mouth turned toward the ear, at a distance of a hand's breadth. Across a terrace they use signs — a compact, close-to-the-chest sign language with about four hundred elements, mostly one-handed because the other hand is usually holding on to something. Loudness is obscene. A raised voice in a public Way will empty the Way; people withdraw from you as from a person bleeding, and someone will fetch your relatives.

And under the murmur, everybody is listening.

All of them, not just the guild. A person here carries in their head a sound-portrait of home so detailed that they can wake from sleep knowing that the tick they just heard came from the third joint back of their own ceiling and was the usual cold-shrinkage tick and not the other thing. Children are trained in it with games: sit in the dark, name the drips, name which plate just spoke. There is a social sin worse than shouting, which is **talking over a tick** — continuing to speak when the rock has said something. When the rock speaks, forty thousand people stop mid-word. Then, after two or three seconds, they resume, often mid-word. Visitors find this the single most unnerving habit in the valley, more than the burials, more than the crushed quarters underfoot in the dark: a whole city pausing in unison for something the visitor cannot hear.

## The hours are places

They do not have clocks and would not know what to do with one. They tell time by where the light is standing.

The pillars tilt through the day, sweeping slowly across terraces and gardens and Ways, and every place a pillar touches has an hour-name, and every hour has a place-name, and in their grammar the two words are the same word. You do not meet someone at noon; you meet them *at the Ninth Standing*, which is both a time and a specific polished shelf of rock where the light will be. To say *I loved her long ago* they say *I loved her in another part of the valley*. Time, here, is something that moves across the floor, and you can see it coming, and if you are slow you can be standing in yesterday while your neighbour is already in the afternoon.

Overcast days undo this. On a grey day the pillars go out, the hour-places go dark, and the city loses its grip on the schedule. By long custom, grey days are **unaccounted**: markets close, debts do not mature, no contract falls due, no appointment is binding. People sleep, or visit, or lie in the dark talking, or quarrel. They are melancholy and slightly licentious days, and the Listeners hate them, because on a grey day nobody is where they said they would be.

Light also gets carried. The **turners** — mostly girls and boys of nine to fourteen, a job you age out of — tend the mirror-relays: chains of polished cap-rock mirrors on gimbals of wet-bent cane, set at the mouths of tunnels and in the black wedges between blocks, passing daylight around three and four corners into places that have never seen the ceiling. The turners follow the pillars all day, adjusting, and their whistle — three short notes, the only whistle a civilian may use — means *light coming, cover your eyes*. In the deep quarters a family's entire allotment of daylight is a patch the size of a book cover, arriving at a known hour, and they stand in it in turn.

## What they own

Cord-length on a drip-line. A nesting crack, by the fissure and the fathom. A share in the grinding of a named stretch of Way, which is both a liability and a rent. A patch of pillar-floor. A place in the fig lottery. And a plate.

Everyone belongs to a plate — the slab of cap rock that hangs over their quarter — and the plates have names and characters and are spoken of the way other cultures speak of grandparents or weather or gods. **Old Lean**, which drops spall in cold snaps and is otherwise steady. **The Broad Cousin**, huge, silent, unloved because silence is not the same as safety. **Hoop**, cracked through and shored with wedge-work no one alive fully trusts, whose people are considered reckless and marry among themselves. You ask a stranger *whose roof?* the way elsewhere you ask what village. A man from Hoop and a woman from Old Lean marrying is a small scandal and a large negotiation, because the question is which ceiling the children will learn to hear.

## The wet rule

No dry work. Nowhere, ever, for any reason.

Partly this is the great prohibition on percussion — nobody strikes stone, and a hammer blow in the Lidded Valley is punished like arson, which is to say by exile up the baskets. But wet work is also the lungs. The air here is pale with rock flour and the flour is glass; and so every cord runs under a drizzle of slurry, every saw-pit has its dripping cane, and all of them work in a cloth veil kept damp, rewetted every few minutes, the gesture as unconscious as blinking. The veils are the city's costume and its vanity: dyed, embroidered at the hem, a young worker's first good one is a coming-of-age gift.

It is not enough. It is never enough. The grinders cough — everyone eventually coughs — and the Long Cough is how nearly all of them end, at fifty, at fifty-five, sitting up at night, breathing through a wet cloth, while a family member rubs their back with the flat of a hand. They are fatalistic and unembarrassed about it. *We are the weather*, they say. *Weather wears out.*

## The held breath

Every evening, at dusk, when the ceiling begins to give up its dew, the city stops working. This is not piety; it is water. All hands go to the cisterns, the gutters, the cords, the dish-slabs, catching two hours of rain out of a clear grey sky.

And the hiss stops. The hundred thousand strokes a minute, that low wet sound that is the floor of every sound in the valley and that nobody notices any more than you notice your own blood — it dies away, and for two hours the Lidded Valley is quiet enough to hear the drip-lines individually, forty thousand small strings of water falling into stone.

They sing then. Massed, close-harmony, in an airy near-whisper, no drums, no claps, nothing struck: whole terraces of people humming under the falling water while they work the gutters, one quarter answering another across a kilometre of dark because that much whisper carries perfectly in a room with a stone lid. They call it the Held Breath. Outsiders who have heard it once tend to talk about it for the rest of their lives, badly.

## The children, the linesmen, and the ones who stay up

The children are fearless on the ladders and afraid of the sky. They swarm the vertical city, they scrape guano from high cracks on cords in spring — dangerous, well paid, honourable, a thing to be proud of at ten — they farm the pale crickets in the dark ends and carry the paste up in jars, they turn the mirrors, they run messages because a running child makes less noise than a shout. They cannot imagine a horizon. Asked to draw the world, they draw a wedge.

Against them, the **linesmen**: the ones who ride baskets two hundred metres up the storm gaps to trade grain for whetstones and mirrors, and who come back wind-burnt, loud, with the *far-eye* on them — a flat dazzled look that lasts two or three days and during which they are excused from manners. They are half-heroes and half-suspects. Something about the upper world gets into them. A certain number of linesmen, every year, do not come down; they simply stay, up there in the dry grass and the unbearable open, and their families tie off the knot in the Cordary and do not speak of it, and their names become slightly unlucky to give a child. A city under a lid has only one way to be left, and it is always the same way, and it is always by the people best at ropes.

## What they believe they are for

In the middle of the valley, in the widest pillar, there is a boulder the size of a small house, and it is round.

Nothing else here is round. Everything else is blade and shell and angle, ninety years old and edged. The boulder is round because for four generations people have gone to it and rubbed it, with wet sand, with leather, with the heels of their hands — no prayer said, no priest, nothing asked for. You go, you rub for as long as you feel like rubbing, you go home. There are dished hollows in it at child-height and at kneeling-height. Its top is beginning to shine.

That is the whole of their religion, as far as anyone can make out, and they get faintly impatient when pressed on it. The land is unfinished. There is no rain worth the name, no river, no soil, no grass; there is nobody to wear this place down into a shape that could be lived in properly, and so the work has fallen to them. They are the erosion. They arrived early and have been doing by hand, block by block, cord by cord, what water and weather have not yet got around to. Hence the Ways, which are the first watercourses this landscape has ever had. Hence the export of flatness to a world too rough and too hasty to make its own. Hence the dead laid into a closing seam with a cup and a cord, to be finished by the rock the way everything else here is finished.

They think you are wasteful. You waste noise, you waste light, you waste water, and — worst — you live on ground that was smoothed for you free of charge, by ten thousand years of rain, and you have never once thought to thank it or to rub anything yourself.
