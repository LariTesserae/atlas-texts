## The One at the Bottom of the Throat

Someone is waiting for you when you come down. You won't see her for the first ten minutes, because of your eyes, but she has been standing at the base of the funnel for the better part of an hour, and the whole quarter has known you were coming since the grey hour before dawn. Your boots gave you away at four kilometres. Everything about you is audible here — the creak of a pack strap, the water shifting in your flask, the small percussive scandal of your breathing. To them you arrive the way a landslide arrives.

She lets you cry. This is expected; there is a word for it, and the word is a courtesy. When you can see again she will be there in front of you with a folded scrap of veil-cloth to sponge your face, and she will be looking at your mouth rather than your eyes, because your mouth is the part of you that is dangerous.

## Bodies

Small. Not stunted so much as *reduced*, the way a design is reduced when every unnecessary part is deleted over four hundred generations. Adults come chest-high to a surface trader. Long feet, wide and padded at the ball and heel, splayed for a soft floor. Long fingers with unusual play in the last joint, because the fingers do most of the talking and much of the seeing. Chests deeper than you'd expect for their size — the air here is thick and faintly poisoned, and they take it in slowly, hugely, quietly.

Their skin under the dust is nearly translucent, the grey-pink of a peeled thing, and it is written on. Every veil-cutter, every seep-worker, every child who ever ran the northern flats without a hat carries small round white marks where acid found them, hard and shiny as spilled wax. They call these kisses. The oldest cutters are covered from wrist to shoulder, and other people read those arms the way you'd read a résumé, or an obituary written in advance. A wrist bearing exactly one kiss, dead centre, means the wearer has come of age and stood under a seep on purpose while a relative counted breaths.

Their eyes are the strange part. The pupils are permanently small, contracted to pinholes even at the pearl hour; the irises are almost without pigment, so that the eye looks like a bead of clouded glass with a black dot in it. There is a translucent fold at the inner corner they can draw across for glare. They cannot see in the dark at all — worse than you can, much worse. Ten thousand years under a sky that never switches off has produced a people functionally blind to shadow and functionally blind in shadow, and the two blindnesses between them shape nearly everything else.

## Faces

Here is the consequence nobody expects. In light that comes from every direction at once, a face has no structure. No cheek-plane, no brow-hollow, no shape at all — just a flat oval of tone with features drawn on it. They can barely see each other.

So they draw the shadows on. The soot is not decoration; it is the missing information. A properly sooted face has a smear under each cheekbone, a stroke along the jaw, a dark crease at the inner eye, a line laid under the lower lip, all of it applied with a fingertip and a small hardwood spoon of lamp-black kept in a stoppered bone tube on the belt. Done well, it gives a face relief — makes it *emerge*, arrive, become a person rather than a smooth stone with eyes. Done badly, or not done at all, it is an obscenity of a mild kind. There is no worse thing to be called than unsooted. It means: I cannot find you. It means: you have no depth and are perhaps not there.

There are dialects of sooting. Grief goes heavy and low, so the face hangs. Courtship is fine work at the eye corners. A Listener on shift wears none at all, on purpose, because a Listener is not a person for those hours; a Listener is an ear with a body attached, and the featureless face is the uniform of that condition. You can walk past three people in an avenue and know from their cheekbones alone who is bereaved, who is available, and who is currently not to be spoken to.

Portraits are made in the hand. A mother learns her newborn's face with her thumbs over the course of about an hour, and afterwards would know it in total darkness, which is where she will keep it. The friezes in the old quarters are illegible not only because the damp smoothed them but because they were read — palm-read, generation on generation, thousands of hands wearing the story out of the wall in the act of loving it.

## Speech

They talk constantly. You will think they are silent people; they are not, they are simply operating below your floor.

The spoken language is entirely unvoiced — whisper-consonants, breath, clicks of tongue against palate, vowels reduced to shaped exhalations. It carries about three metres and no further, which is the designed range. Two of them murmuring together at arm's length are having a full conversation that a third person two paces off cannot hear and would consider it grotesque to try to.

Underneath that is the finger-speech, which is the older and more serious language, laid on the other person's forearm: positions, pressures, slides, and small figures traced with two fingertips. It is used for anything that matters. Business is whispered; love, law, and grief are put onto the skin. There are contracts that exist only as a sequence of touches performed simultaneously by four people in a ring, each transmitting to the next, and witnessed by the fact that all four received it. A name is a touch-figure first and a whispered syllable second. To learn someone's name is to be permitted to write it on them.

Laughter has been driven inward — a convulsion in the diaphragm, mouth shut, eyes streaming. Because it makes no sound, it cannot be shared at a distance. So when something is funny they reach out and put a flat hand on each other's sternum to *feel* it, and a joke told to a room is a joke that ends in six people holding one another by the chest, shaking. Their idea of loneliness is precise and physical: it is laughing with nobody's hand on you.

There is no music of the audible kind. What they have instead is hand-song, patterns drummed with the fingerpads on a partner's back and shoulders, long cycles handed down and elaborated, some of them attributed to named composers dead two thousand years. A famous piece can be performed on you by a stranger and reduce you to tears, and someone three metres away will see nothing but two people sitting close.

## Households, and Being Held Up

Nobody lives in a crowd because the geography forbids it. Each dwelling stands on its own thinning table of untrodden rock, and there is only so much table. So the unit is two, or three, or five, isolated on their pillar above a canyon, connected outward by bridges of pegged and lashed bone.

The bridge is the whole social contract, made literal. Two households that build a bridge between them are joined; the word for the joining is the word for the lashing-knot. A marriage is a bridge. An adoption is a bridge. A feud is a cut bridge, and the stumps are left in place on both sides for as long as the grudge lasts, so that everyone in the avenue below can look up and see exactly who is not speaking to whom. When a family finally reconciles, the re-lashing is done at the pearl hour with as many kin present as the tables will hold, in silence, everybody's hands on everybody's forearms.

And the bones are people. They do not bury; the ground dissolves and gives things back, which they find unbearable. They render. Long bones of the dead become bridge-pegs and ladder-rungs, and the pegs are named — you can be told, crossing, whose femur is currently under your left hand. A child learns the family's bridge the way other children learn a genealogy: this one is your grandmother, this one is her brother who was flat-eared and went up the throats and came back wrong, this pale one at the far end nobody remembers, be careful of him, he's soft.

So they are held over the drop by their ancestors, at all times, and they think about it exactly as much as you think about your own floorboards.

## The Ear

Three kinds of people are paid to do nothing, and the hierarchy among them is the closest thing this place has to a state.

The child-ears hear the note best. Young hearing reaches up where old hearing can't follow, and the pane sings at the very top of the register, so the true instrument of this civilisation is a six-year-old sitting still in a bright bare room. But a six-year-old cannot be trusted with half a square kilometre of ceiling. So they sit in pairs: a child and an old Listener, one shift in five, the child with its eyes shut and the old one with a hand resting on the child's neck to feel it stiffen. The child says *it is singing.* The old one decides whether the world is ending.

Around the age of twelve or thirteen the hearing goes, and the child becomes a spent ear. There is a small ceremony, and afterwards a lifelong ache that the language has a word for, an ache with no equivalent in your language: the grief of having once been the most important person in the city and now merely being alive. Many spent ears become Listeners in their turn, forty years later, waiting to hold the neck of some child who can still reach the note they can remember hearing and can no longer find.

And then there are the flat-eared: those born, perhaps one in thirty, who cannot hear the note at all, and never could. They are not despised. They are *deployed*. They are the ones who go up the throats with bundles of cut selenite and come back with hardwood and grain and gossip, because loudness costs them nothing and shadow does not frighten them, and because if one of them is caught in a fall it is not a catastrophe of the ear. They come back changed every time — too loud, too fast, walking with their chins up — and they are kept for eleven days in a dim room and quieted by relatives before being allowed back among people. Everyone is slightly in love with them and nobody wants a daughter to marry one.

The great and secret fear of this civilisation is not the fall. They have fallen a thousand times; they know how to fall. The fear is that some day the note will sound in a quarter where nobody happens to be sitting still.

## The Prism Purse

Wealth is portable noon. A family's estate is a wrapped bundle of cut selenite blocks and prisms, some of them worked by named cutters six thousand years dead, and its value is measured by how much light it can carry into a new dwelling. When the city drifts, this is what gets packed first, before food, before the bridge-pegs of the grandparents. Households have starved rather than trade a good block. Light-cutters are the one caste here permitted a certain arrogance, and they wear it in fine soot and clean hands, and they alone keep the calculations of the pane's thinning: so many hands' width remaining above the sixth quarter, so many above the ninth, and the numbers go down every generation, and everybody knows it.
