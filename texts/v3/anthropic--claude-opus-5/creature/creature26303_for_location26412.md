## The wrights

Four and a half millimetres, head to tail-fan, and about a fifth of that across the shoulders. Eleven body segments, count them through the cuticle; you can, because there is nothing to stop you. They are transparent the way old ice is transparent — not clear, exactly, but unpersuasive as a barrier. Held up to the fissure-light (nobody holds them up to the light; this is a thought experiment) you would see the gut as a straight grey line, the pale nerve-cord under it, the four slow pulses of the dorsal vessel, and behind the head the cement gland: a bulb the size of a poppy seed that whitens as it fills and goes glassy as it empties.

The gland is the whole animal, really. Everything else is scaffolding for it. They take up dissolved carbonate through the gill-plates all their lives, continuously, without noticing, the way we take up air, and they lay it down again as a paste that sets in eleven pulses of the seep and cures hard in a year. A wright cannot stop making cement any more than it can stop making urine. If it does not build, it swells, and stiffens, and dies of its own material.

Under the thorax is the trowel: a keeled ventral plate, wider than the body, with a working edge and a set of fine growth ridges you can count like tree-rings. New wrights have trowels thick as a fingernail-paring and clumsy with it. The edge wears. It wears fastest in brine and slowest in the top water, and by the eighth year it is polished to a translucent sliver that flexes under load and cannot bear down. This is how the city sorts itself: not by caste, not by any decree, but by the thinning of a plate. Thick trowels bear and beat. Thin trowels measure, patrol, nurse, and carry the dead. There is no promotion and no demotion. There is only wear.

## Depth, and how it tastes

They have no eyes. Two vestigial ocelli under the head-shield register the difference between the four bright months and the eight dark ones, and nothing finer.

What they have instead is depth. The antennules carry a fringe of receptors that read the density of the water directly — the salt of it, the weight of it, the sourness — and a wright always knows which of the three floors it is standing in, and how far it is from the boundary above and the boundary below, to within a body-length. Depth is their one absolute. Everything in the city is addressed by it. A gallery is not *over there*; it is *four hundred down from the pane, sixty up from the sour*. The two interfaces are the datum lines from which all four hundred thousand of them survey.

Take one out and put it back at the wrong level and it is not lost so much as unmade. It stops. It gropes at nothing with its trowel. The clocking-in of a wright to a new depth takes several minutes, and in those minutes it is the only genuinely helpless thing in the chamber.

## The pulse

Nine times an hour, the seep in the silt pushes, and the brine-pane lifts a finger and settles, and every wright in the city feels the pressure-change through its whole body at once.

That is the clock. There is no other. Cement is measured in pulses. Distance is measured in pulses of swimming. A brood-cell is sealed for six thousand pulses. A wright's lifetime is a number nobody counts but which comes to something under two hundred thousand.

In the coldest weeks the seep hesitates — skips, or comes late and weak — and the entire settlement stalls. Not in fear: in arithmetic. Thirty thousand of them holding a course of wet cement, waiting for the count they set out by, and the count not arriving. They wait. It comes eventually. They finish the stroke. In the record laid down in the walls, those weeks show as a faint irregularity in the joints, and the irregularity is the same in every quarter of the city, because they are all keeping the same time by the same lung.

## Speech

A run of taps down the flank; a pressure held; a rhythm drummed on a shared wall so a whole terrace feels it through the stone and passes it on without knowing it has.

The vocabulary is small and has been measured. Something over forty signals, of which nine account for most traffic. *Bear here* — two taps and a held press, meaning this member is taking load it was not built for. *Set* — a single tap at the setting-count, which is how a course of six hundred masons stays level. *Give way*, *close up*, *feed me*, *I am full*, *not that*, *dead — take up*, and the three fast taps that mean worm.

What the language does not have is any way to speak of a time further off than a setting-count. It can say *in eleven pulses this will be hard*. It cannot say *next year*, or *when I am old*, or *in two hundred years*. It has no future beyond the drying of the paste in front of it. This is worth sitting with, because it means the thing that is going to happen to them is not something they are failing to face. It is something for which they have no grammar. They are not brave about the ceiling. They are mute about it, in the way a stone is mute.

## The brood, and the warm pipes

Now the hydraulics make sense.

Brine is heavier and brine is warmer — half a degree, no more, and half a degree is a season. The sealed conduits that run uphill from the seep are not primarily larders. They are heating. They terminate in the brood-galleries, thirty body-lengths up in the clear water, where the eggs are cemented into cells in courses of forty, and the difference between half a degree and no degrees is the difference between hatching in twenty months and hatching in twenty-six.

Nothing in this city was ever *made*, as the chamber's account rightly says. But something in it was *worked out*. Whoever first ran a closed pipe from the sour bottom to a warm nursery had solved a problem in fluid mechanics with a jaw, a plate, and a body's worth of paste, and had done it without a word for *why*, and had left the answer standing where the next four hundred generations could find it by touch. The conduits are the oldest structures in the lower city. They have been repaired ten thousand times and never redesigned.

## The clouded

Someone has to work the brine.

The sulphide gets into them. The gut-line browns, the transparency goes milky at the joints, and the trowel-edge dissolves in months instead of years. The rota is not enforced by anything anyone has identified; it simply moves through the population, and a wright does its brine-turns and comes back stained.

Stained ones are given passage. In a city where the corridors are one body wide and every meeting is a negotiation of who reverses, a clouded wright is not asked to reverse. It is tapped once on the shoulder-plate — a signal with no other use — and the other backs out. This is the only courtesy in the settlement that cannot be explained as load-bearing.

## The ones who go up

The dead go up, and the going is bad.

The top layer is fresh, and fresh water is a poison of a particular kind: it swells them, floods them, makes them stupid and slow and finally bursts them at the joints. A wright can spend perhaps two minutes up there and come down. Four minutes and it does not come down; it becomes cargo for the next relay.

So the carrying-up is done in a chain, hand to hand up the mirror's flank, and the last carrier — the one who has to find the socket, set the body in it, and lay the covering course — does that work in the last forty seconds of its competence, swelling, half-blind with wrong salt, hurrying.

Which is why the upper city is crude. Go and look at it, if you ever could. Below, the towers are wafer-thin and fluted, joints you could not get a hair into, courses true over a metre. Above, in the mirror, the same forms are lumpish. The flutes are wrong. The joints gape. Cement has been dumped rather than spread; you can see, in the set of it, the exact moment a mason gave up on finish and settled for adhesion. Two hundred years of the finest small stonework in the district, and its reflection is the work of the drowning.

They do it anyway. About thirty thousand a year go up, and the density of the mirror records the number, year on year, in courses — thick years and thin years, plague and plenty, all of it legible, none of it read.

## The wardens

Seven patches, walled, gated, patrolled without a break for longer than the towers have stood.

Nobody eats the moss. Nobody eats the filaments or the black crust. The wardens keep the copepods off it, chase the amphipods away from the gates, and repair the walls, and get nothing back that anyone has been able to weigh: no food, no material, no warmth worth the swim.

Two of the seven have died — one in the cold decade, one for no reason at all. The walls were maintained around bare rock for eleven years and then abandoned in the space of a week, all at once, with something that from outside looks indistinguishable from agreement.

The best account is the dullest: that the wardens' rota is older than the reason for it, and that the reason went extinct, and the behaviour did not. But you should know that this is the single oldest continuous act in the chamber. Older than any conduit. Older than any wall in the towers. If the city has a thing it does that is not eating, breeding, building, or burying, this is it, and it consists of standing guard, in the dark, over a hand's breadth of moss that is doing almost nothing, and has been doing almost nothing since before the towers.

## The worm-word

Three fast taps.

It is the only signal that propagates city-wide, and it crosses the settlement in four seconds by pure conduction, terrace to terrace, without any individual intending to relay it. Everyone with a thin trowel goes inward, behind the throats. Everyone with a thick one goes out.

The throats are the defence: a chicane of three right-angle turns inside one body-length, at the mouth of every inhabited gallery. A flatworm can push its everted stomach through any gap in the city. It cannot push it round three corners against its own turgor. Building a throat is the first thing a young wright is taught and the last thing an old one supervises, and the throats are the best-made structures in the lower city, better than the brood-cells, better than the pipes.

The pushers do not fight. They pile wet cement onto the worm's leading margin — a hundred of them, working the edge, adding weight and friction and nothing else — and eventually the worm finds the terrace uneconomical and flows off it. Once, a worm was walled onto the outer reef for most of a season, a slow soft thing in a widening white sock, feeding on nothing, until it stopped.

There are three lumps in the reef that nobody built. Bulges in the fluting, one the size of a walnut, with the towers resumed neatly on top of them. That is what the city does with a dead flatworm: it makes it structural.

## And the rest

Copepods past counting, which are the wheat. Amphipods on the brine-floor larder, which are the competition and occasionally the meat. Ostracods clacking soundlessly through the conduits, tolerated, possibly useful, unstudied. Three species of luminous copepod, which pass through the mid-city like sparks and are ignored by everything that cannot see.

The white shrimp are weather. They drift through the towers as fronts drift over the peat above, and they break things — a spire here, a bridge-rail there — and the wrights have learned to build a lee: on the upstream side of every shrimp-lane there is a low deflecting fin, and behind the fin the fine work. Nobody in the city has ever perceived a shrimp. What they have perceived is the pattern of where things break, and they have built for it, which is the same as understanding.

And the mat on the north wall pulses cold blue once a minute for nobody. It has no relationship with anything. It should be said that of everything in the chamber, it is the only inhabitant with no obligations.

## One of them

Under a bridge on the sixth terrace, on the shaded side, a wright is laying a course.

Old: trowel worn to a flexing sliver, gut browned from its brine-turns, sixteen thousand pulses since it last had the strength to bear. It should not be laying anything at all. Its gland is full regardless, because the gland does not consult the trowel, and so it has come out to the underside of a bridge that does not particularly need work and is putting the paste on anyway, thin, with the flat of a plate too soft for the job, in two degrees of water, in the dark, in silence, feeling for level against the sour pane four hundred lengths below.

It has no name. There are no names. But it has a signature, because a trowel-stroke records the hand: a slight roll at the end of each pass, a habit of finishing left. Its neighbours know that roll and expect it, and the cement will keep it for a thousand years after the roll and the neighbours and the expectation are gone.

Three finger-widths overhead, in the fresh water where it cannot go, something it helped carry up is set in a socket, cemented over badly by someone who was drowning at the time.

The gap narrows by a hair a year. It has no word for the year and no word for the hair. When the two cities finally meet — and they will meet, at the centre of the dome, on some particular unremarkable day — what the wrights nearest the join will feel is a wall arriving where a wall ought to be, and they will do the only thing their language can propose. They will tap once for the count. They will point their trowels at the joint. They will make it good.
