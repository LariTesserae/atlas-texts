# The Lap: Inhabitants

## Number

There are two hundred and eleven people in the Lap.

I counted them twice, nine days apart, by standing at the wharf and marking a tally on my own forearm in grease, which is a thing you can do there without anyone objecting, because grease comes off. Both counts came to two hundred and eleven. The number has an eleven in it, like the flues and the legs and the minutes and the degrees, and I do not know what to do with that and neither will you.

They do not count themselves. This is not ignorance. Counting is a way of asking whether there is a difference since the last time, and the question does not arise, so the instrument has not been invented. When I told Baulk the number he received it the way you would receive the width of a door.

---

## Body

They are small, most of them, and broad across the back, and they stand with the shoulders slightly up and forward, which is what eleven degrees and permanent damp will do to a spine over a very long time and then stop doing. Skin the colour of the pan — that pale, faintly yellowed grey, wet-cigarette. Hair dark, cropped or bound, at a length; it turns over, strand for strand, the way the cuffs turn over shell for shell, and the length does not change.

Their pupils do not move. This is the thing you notice on the first day and cannot stop noticing afterwards. Under a light that has not varied in living memory, the aperture has no work to do, and it has settled, and it sits at a fixed middling dilation in every face in the basin. I brought a phosphor stick down with me and struck it in the melt-shed doorway out of stupidity. Nobody flinched. Nobody's eye closed. Shim turned her head away, politely, the way you would turn from someone chewing loudly, and that was all.

Take a hand and turn it over. The palm is exactly the north scarp: worn to a scallop and then sealed, polished, hard, done. The calluses have reached their depth. Feet the same. Their coats have stopped fraying at the cuff in the way the gasholder has stopped rusting — the fibre is soft to the thumb, and it is sound, and it will be sound tomorrow.

Damage is different from wear. Wear has finished; damage is permanent. Tare has a split across the web of her left thumb, a clean pink seam perhaps a centimetre long, which does not bleed, does not close, does not infect, and will not. Gurn is short two teeth and the gaps are gaps. There is a man on the west stair with a dent in his skull above the ear that you could set a thumb in. None of it hurts them, so far as I could determine, and none of it progresses, and none of it mends.

This has made them the most physically careful people I have ever been among. They do not jostle. They pass each other in a narrow way with a small turn of the hip that is so ingrained it reads as ceremony. When they touch, they touch deliberately, palm flat, and they hold.

That is the other thing, and it took me four days to work out why the touching went straight through me. There is no heat in the Lap. The furnaces radiate nothing; the white metal moves behind the door and you feel a cellar. The sweetwater is warm at the cock and cool in the cup. Two hundred and eleven bodies at thirty-seven degrees are the only warmth in eleven hundred paces of basin, and they know it in their bones, and they are lavish with it. They sleep two and three to a bed, tangled, not out of want of blankets. They stand shoulder to shoulder at the cups for longer than drinking takes. A greeting is a hand laid on the back of the neck, held for four seconds — one stamp — and released.

## Names

Two hundred and eleven names. Mell, Shim, Keck, Baulk, Tare, Ourn, Lund, Swale, Pell, Gurn, and the rest; one syllable mostly, hard at the front, going nowhere.

A name is not a person. A name is a place. Shim is the name of the third cup on the north wharf run and of whoever wipes it; Ourn is the name of the top landing of the west outside stair; Baulk is the name of the Quiet Cell door and its counterweight. Ask a person who they are and they will tell you where they stand. Ask where they stand and they will tell you the number of paces from there to the wharf, exactly, and they will be right.

Their memory is like that throughout. They cannot tell you how long they have been in the Lap, not because they are hiding it but because duration has no landmarks here and the faculty has gone slack from disuse, the way the pupil has. What they have instead is a spatial memory of terrible precision. They remember the basin the way you remember a room you grew up in: not as a story, as a solid. They have no history. They have geography, and the geography is complete.

---

## Offices

The Cinder Works runs without tenders, and the people of the Lap tend it.

There is no contradiction and they do not experience one. Mell wipes the stone cups with a rag, all two hundred and thirty of them, in an order; the cups do not need wiping. There is a valve on the east wall of the stamp-house that Lund opens and closes, and it is connected to nothing, and I checked, and I am sorry that I checked. Someone touches each plate in each stack of nine, top to bottom, with two fingers. Someone walks the gutter line with a bent rod and does not use it. Swale sits on the flue-stair and answers the gargles in the lowest register he can manage, which is not low enough, and the gargles do not answer differently, and he has done this for as long as there has been a Swale.

It is not delusion and it is not ritual, because ritual implies that something is being propitiated. It is closer to what the drays are doing in the Returning Yard. It is what a person does. The polish of Mell's rag-hand and the two-centimetre groove of the dray-circuit are the same fact.

The one clock left in the Lap is fatigue. Nothing else accumulates, but a body still gets tired, and sleep still discharges it, and so each of the two hundred and eleven runs a private day of their own length, drifting free of all the others. There is no shared morning. There is no hour when the streets fill or empty. At any moment some sixty of them are asleep and the rest are out, and this has been true forever, which is why the Lap feels simultaneously inhabited and abandoned no matter when you stand in it.

## Food, and the Fineness of Distinctions

They eat cuffs. You boil nothing in the Lap, because there is no fire that gives heat; you set the pot under a bleed-cock and let the warm condensate fill it, and the meat goes soft and pale, and it tastes faintly sweet and faintly metallic, which is what everything here tastes of, including the air and your own hands.

Out of that non-range they have built the whole of their connoisseurship. They can tell the cups apart. Blind, by taste, at a mouthful: cup nine from cup eighty-seven, the wharf run from the stamp-house run, the two capped flues' cups from the nine live ones. They argue about it. The arguments are good-natured, detailed, technical, and entirely unresolvable, and they have been running, in the same terms, with the same examples, for a length of time nobody can express.

This is the key to them, if you want one. Everything they have — their humour, their aesthetics, their quarrels, their loves — is an infinite refinement inside a fixed envelope. Nothing can get bigger, so everything has gotten finer.

Their jokes do not wear out. I want to be clear that I mean this literally. There is a joke that Pell makes at the fourth cup, every time, and it is not much of a joke, and it landed on the first day and it landed on the ninth and the people around him laughed the same amount both times, and so did I. Grudges behave identically. Keck and Gurn have not spoken directly in an unmeasurable age over something to do with a stack of nine, and the grievance is neither healing nor souring; it is held, at temperature, like the gasholder at half.

There is love in the Lap. It does not court and it does not build, because there is nothing to build toward and no one to build it for. It is taken up and put down and taken up again in exactly the same measure, and the people in it do not appear to find this thin. I am not qualified to say whether they are wrong.

## The Return

On my sixth day Keck came off the plate-rail in the Returning Yard and the crane took him across the hips.

He did not die immediately. Four of them got to him and knelt and put their hands on him, flat, on the chest and the face and the arms, all that heat, and he looked at the cloud-roof and died in about two minutes. Then they carried him — carefully, two at the shoulders and two at the knees, the same careful way they pass each other in a narrow place — to the sorting chutes at the north of the melt-shed, and fed him in feet-first, and went back to what they had been doing.

I stood at the Return. It is a low arched chute in the north face of the shed, the mouth about knee-height, with a lip worn smooth and no further.

It took, I think, six or seven minutes. He came out the way the cuffs come out: wet, adult, complete. A man of about forty, dark-haired, broad across the back, with the Lap's pan-grey skin. Not Keck. Not anybody. He sat on the wet stone with his arms round his shins for a while, in the flat light, and then he got up and walked to the west stair and began doing what Keck did, with no instruction and no hesitation, and with the slight forward set of the shoulders that everybody here has.

Tare brought the coat off the peg. It fitted. She put her hand on the back of his neck and held it for one stamp.

Nobody greeted him, because nobody had gone. Gurn continued not to speak to him. Pell made the joke at the fourth cup and he laughed the right amount. By the following sleep the only thing anywhere in the basin that was carrying the event was me, and I was carrying it alone, and it did not propagate, and there is no word for how that feels and I have stopped looking for one.

I asked Tare, later, whether that was Keck. She understood the question — she is quick, they are all quick — and she thought about it in the way you think about a question that is well-formed but pointless, like being asked whether the Drab is the same river as yesterday.

She said: *That's Keck.* Then she said: *You're asking is it the one.* Then she said: *There isn't a one.*

## Etiquette

They have exactly one sin, and it is **more**.

Do not take two cups where one will do. Do not stack ten. Do not carry away a shell. Do not break the crust off the slag wall for a souvenir — the clappers keep the rosettes at their size and thickness, and a scar is permanent, and you have made the Lap smaller by a coin's width forever. Do not cut anyone. Do not shout above the stamp; the stamp is the ceiling of volume and there is nothing above it.

Their manners are conservation law expressed as courtesy, and they are deeply, seriously moral about it, in the way you are moral about not putting your hand in someone's open wound. When I dropped a cup and chipped the rim, three separate people came to look at the chip over the following day. Not to reproach me. To see it.

Baulk stood over it for a while and said the word they use — an ordinary adjective, pressed into service as a noun, because they have no noun.

He said: *That's a new.*

## Ourn

There is one on the west stair's top landing who looks north.

She does it perhaps every fourth or fifth of her private days, for a quarter of an hour, standing at the rail with her forearms on the wet iron, looking up the scarp at the notch where you came down and where you will go back up. She knows what it is. She asked me about the other side and listened to the answer with complete attention and no visible want.

Here is the cruelty of the place and I did not understand it until my eighth day. Nothing accumulates in the Lap, and that includes motive. Her wanting to leave is real and it is exactly the size it has always been. It does not grow with each looking. It does not compound overnight into the thing that finally puts a foot on the slope. She wants it the same amount today as the first time, and she will want it the same amount forever, and that quantity is slightly less than the quantity required.

There are no walls around the Lap. The scarp is sixty metres and an easy scramble. Two hundred and eleven people stay because desire here is a chord held, and it is not in the nature of a held chord to resolve.

---

## Concerning You

They will feed you and warm you and not ask why you came, because *why* is a question about change. They will ask where you were standing. Answer precisely; it is the only autobiography they know how to read.

By the third day someone will notice your hair.

You are the only thing in the basin that is increasing. Your beard comes in, your nails grow, your cut heals and closes and fades. To them this is not disgusting and not miraculous; it is the most interesting thing that has ever been available to look at, and they are as gentle about it as you would be with a friend's visible disease that you happen to find beautiful. They will ask, formally, to touch it. Let them. Four or five will come at a time and stand close in that fog of borrowed warmth and lay two fingers on your jaw and be quiet.

Ourn asked me to measure my own beard on the rail with a grease mark, on my second day and again on my ninth, so that she could see the distance between the marks.

She looked at it for a long time. Then she put her hand on the back of my neck and held it for one stamp and went back to the rail and looked north, with exactly as much wanting as before.

They will not walk you up. They stop at the mist boundary, which does not move, and they stand in the shirr and the applause and the four-second fall of the iron and watch you climb out, and then they go and do what they do. They will not be waiting when you come back, because waiting requires something to build up in the meantime.

But if you come back, someone will say your name at the place you stood, because here a name is a place and you stood in one, and that much the Lap keeps. It keeps everything. That is the entire trouble with it.
