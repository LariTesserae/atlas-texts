# The Kettle: an account of the residents

## I. Begin with the missing

There are no flies.

Not none — a few small hoverers come in with the swifts and mate in the mist and die of it — but there is no blowfly, no carrion beetle, no burying beetle, no sexton wasp, no vulture, no crow, no rat. The entire profession of the dead is unrepresented. On any other mountain a dead frog is a town: it has a population, a schedule, four days of dense commerce followed by an abandonment. Here a dead frog is an absence that takes about twenty minutes to finish.

So the Kettle has a hole in it, and the hole is the exact shape of the lattice's third function, and everything that lives here has grown up around that hole the way a tree grows around a nail.

Practically, this means: nothing in the bowl knows what rot smells like. A herder who brings a dead lamb over the rim as an experiment — this has been done, more than once — finds that no animal in the Kettle comes to look. Not the weasel-thing, which eats meat daily. Not the ants, which will strip a moth in an hour. The lamb sinks quietly over two and a half days while ten thousand cicadas go on with the noise, and nothing attends it, because attending it is not a trade anyone here has had to learn.

It also means that the Kettle has never had a plague. Density like this, warmth like this, no season, no dispersal — by every rule the bowl should boil over with disease every few years and empty itself. It doesn't. Whatever begins is removed the instant it stops moving, and most of the long patient cycles that need a corpse to sit in for a week simply cannot complete a single revolution here. The residents live in a hospital that has no wards, only a very fast and very literal-minded orderly.

That is the first thing to understand about the inhabitants. They are all, every one of them, tenants of a kept house.

## II. The chorus

The cicada is the loudest thing on the massif and the least individual.

It is black with a red seam down the abdomen, a little under a thumb's length, and it is deaf — functionally, by the standards of its kin elsewhere. The tympana are there but the nerve is coarsened. In a bowl where the sound pressure stands in visible bands and will loosen the water off a leaf, hearing is a liability and has been put down.

They do not emerge in a brood, because there is nothing to emerge from. A cicada elsewhere counts years by the taste of sap changing as the tree wakes and sleeps. The four figs never sleep. The temperature never moves. So the nymphs come up out of the loam at random, a slow constant trickle, forty or sixty a day, every day, for as long as anyone has counted — no swarm year, no quiet year, no year. Stand at the base of a fig at any hour and within ten minutes you will see one heave itself out of the black, walk unhurriedly up the wax bark, split along the back, and inflate, while its brothers roar over it. There is no safety in numbers here because there is no number. There is only the flow.

The song is the strange part. Each one produces a tone that is not quite its own: it locks to the hum of the lattice, which is not audible to a person but is apparently audible to a tymbal, and the whole population sits on that carrier like ten thousand fingers on one wire. What they add is the modulation — a beat pattern, a roughness, a stutter — and a female, who is also deaf, finds a male by feeling the beat through the branch she is standing on. Courtship in the Kettle is conducted by handshake through wood.

They mate for about ninety seconds and the male goes on singing throughout, because he is not singing to her and never was.

## III. The tools

Under the roar, twelve or thirteen species of frog, and every one of them owns a hole in it.

This is measurable. Lay the cicada band out and you find narrow gaps in it, quiet slots a few tones wide, and each frog species sits in one — the rasp in one, the plucked wire in another, the wet cork lowest of all. Nobody had to design this. Anything that called into the band was never heard and left no children. What remains is a lock and a set of keys.

They do not need water in the sense that the word usually means. The bowl condenses, and a leaf of the central fig holds along its underside a row of beads, and in one of those beads — a thing you could drink in a swallow — a frog the size of a shirt button lays four eggs and a male stands guard over them for a fortnight, urinating into the bead when the volume drops, which it does not often do. The tadpoles have no gut for algae. They eat the egg after them, and then each other, and one comes out.

None of them have ever seen a pool. Put a basin of water in the bowl and they will drown in it, stupidly, with the same confidence that a fish shows in air. What they know is a curved wet surface the size of a fingernail, and the underside of a leaf, and the fact that the world is always thirty-one degrees and always damp and always deafening, which is to say they know nothing at all about the world, and they have never once needed to.

## IV. The commuters

The swifts are the only citizens with a border crossing.

Two thousand of them, and they are not really residents of the Kettle; they are residents of the sky over the entire massif, four hundred square kilometers of thin white nothing, and the Kettle is simply the only wall they can hang on. They go up at first light in that spiralling column and they are gone all day, out over the dead tableland, over the hexagon-cracked clay and the stub peaks, taking the plateau's insects — the little dry hard ones, the ones that hatch out of the eleven-minute squalls — and at dusk they come back in a funnel that pulls the mist down with it and sounds like a sheet being torn slowly through its length.

And here is what they are, functionally. They are the Kettle's mouth.

Everything the bowl has, it has because something carried it in. The rain that falls up here would not keep a herb garden. The loam is black and deep and fabulously rich and it did not come from the granite. It came from swifts: from their droppings, from the nestlings that fall, from the parasites that fall off them, from the two hundred kilograms of dead insect matter a year that they bring in from a hundred kilometers of empty air and deposit in a hole forty paces wide. The lattice takes what dies and hands it back as soil, and four trees and seven continents of moss stand on the digested airspace of a whole mountain.

A swift born in the Kettle is warm for six weeks. It is warm in the nest, in the moss and glued saliva, in the noise; and then one morning it goes up the column and it is never warm again for the rest of its life, which may be nine years. It will sleep on the wing over the freezing plateau. It will come back every dusk to a room it can only visit.

Of everything in the bowl, the swifts are the only ones who know there is an outside.

## V. Three dirty chimneys

Three of the figs are hollow, and inside them is the only filth in the Kettle.

The lattice runs under the soil and up the inner faces of the stones. It does not run up a tree. So the floor of each hollow trunk — three metres of chimney, packed with bats in the day, dripping — has accumulated across a few centuries a pit of guano, and that pit is the one place in the Kettle where the ordinary rules of the world have been allowed back in.

It stinks. It is the only thing here that stinks, apart from the vine. It is alive in the way a compost heap is alive, with mites and tiny pale beetles and a fungus that fruits in little grey buttons, and when a bat dies in the roost — which happens daily — it falls into that pit and is not taken, and rots there properly, over weeks, with maggots, exactly as a bat would rot anywhere in the world.

So the missing profession is not missing after all. It has been evicted upward. The entire trade of decay in the Kettle is carried on in three vertical rooms, out of reach of the housekeeper, by things no bigger than a grain of rice that have never in their history been outside a tree.

The bats themselves are unremarkable and enormous in number: a small brown leaf-nosed kind, and a larger one with a long tongue. They go out over the plateau in ones and twos and come back with nothing, mostly. They do not need to hunt hard. They feed on the vine.

## VI. The oldest resident

The vine has been here longer than anything else alive in the bowl, including the three ancient figs, and it is one plant.

It came up at the foot of the northern stone at some point that cannot be established, climbed it, bridged to the next, and has spent centuries roping the seven together until now it hangs in loops across the whole rim like slack cable. The trunk at its base is the thickness of a man's waist and has a texture like braided rope gone hard.

It flowers white, at night, stinking of overripe fruit, and the long-tongued bats come to it, and it is pollinated perfectly and abundantly, and it sets fruit, and the fruit falls, and nothing ever comes up. Not once in living memory. The bowl is arranged; there is room, but the room is not for that. The fruit goes to the ground and the ground takes it when it softens past a certain point.

Three flowers are open at any given time. Always three. Not four, not two. Count them at any hour of any night of any year and there are three, and when a fourth swells to the point of opening, one of the others is closed by morning and already going brown at the lip. Nobody has caught the mechanism. The vine has an enormous number of buds and appears to be under instruction.

That is what the Kettle is like about numbers. It likes small ones and it keeps them.

## VII. The post

The weasel-thing is not a species. It is a post.

Honey-coloured along the flanks with soot at the mask and feet, a metre long counting the tail, low and heavy-shouldered, and it lives in the eastern fig, in the stink, above the maggots. The herders call it *the aunt*, regardless of sex, and they will not say it twice in one sentence.

There is always exactly one. When it dies — usually in the open, on the loam, on its side in the middle of the afternoon, which seems less like accident than like a thing knowing where the drain is — the ground takes it over about a day and a half, the guard hairs last. Within a season another comes up out of the dry country, across kilometres of clay and rotted granite where there is nothing for such an animal to eat, and goes down over the rim, and the arrangement resumes.

They arrive thin. That is the detail worth having. They come in scabbed and ribby with their coats broken, and within six weeks they are glossy and slow and heavy, and they stop going out, and they never go out again. The Kettle makes them what they are. It takes about a month and a half.

It kills every day and is never hungry. Food here is not a problem to be solved; it is a condition, like the humidity. What the aunt does with the remaining twenty-three hours is sleep in the open on a root with its belly up and its flank rising, in full view of two thousand birds, absolutely unafraid, because the bowl contains nothing that could threaten it and it has been a long time since anything in its line needed to remember what a threat looked like.

Whether the Kettle could support two has never been tested, because there have never been two.

## VIII. The road-builders

The ants have done the only thing any resident has ever managed to do to the machine.

They are small and matte and they farm a particular moss — they clip it, carry it, and eat the fungus that grows on the clippings — and they have been carrying it along the same line across the glassy red lip of the bowl for so long that they have worn a road into it. A channel a finger wide and deep enough to show shadow, in a material that is not geology, that a steel chisel will skid off, that nobody has ever marked in any other way. Worn by feet. It took, presumably, a length of time that has no useful name.

And at the end of that road, outside the rim, on the dry side, there is a small grey drift.

It is their midden. Ants carry their dead out of the nest and pile them at a distance; this is older than the Kettle, older than the plateau, wired in somewhere below the possibility of revision. So they carry their sisters up and out, over the thermal boundary, into the cold thin killing air of the tableland, and put them down, and go back. The drift is two handspans across and it never grows much, because the plateau wind works on it.

They need not bother. If they dropped a body anywhere on the loam the lattice would have it in a quarter of an hour. But nobody told them, and there is no way to tell them, and so a species that has been living on top of the most efficient undertaker in the known world has spent centuries walking its dead uphill.

It is the only grave in the Kettle.

## IX. The doorstep

Outside, in a ring, there is a second population, and it does not come in.

The thermal boundary is a hand's width thick and utterly reliable, and a great many animals on the plateau have learned to lie against the warm side of it without crossing. There is a fox that sleeps most nights along the glassy lip with its spine in the warmth and its nose in the cold, and has apparently never once gone down. There are lizards stacked on the outer faces of the standing stones at dusk. Herders' dogs, if allowed, will lie there until dragged away. A hare has been seen doing it in winter and it is not a thing hares do.

They will not enter, and the reason is straightforward once you have heard it: the noise. From the rim it is a flat sawing roar with a ringing edge, and to an animal with proper ears it is not weather, it is catastrophe, and everything sensible declines.

So the Kettle is ringed by a congregation of creatures pressed against a door they will not open, warming one flank, listening to something intolerable, and going home before dawn.

And the reverse traffic: plateau moths blunder over the rim at night and are dead by morning, not eaten, just finished — their wings take up water they have no way to shed, and they sink, and they are taken. The bowl is a lamp for the whole tableland and it does not give anything back to the things it draws.

## X. The people, such as they are

Nobody lives in the Kettle. It has been tried twice that can be read in the ground.

The round hut against the western stone lasted perhaps two generations. It failed for the reason such places always fail: the Kettle is thirty-one degrees and soaking and deafening and it does not contain one calorie a person can eat. You cannot dry grain in it. You cannot store meat in it for an hour. You cannot keep a fleece. Four figs will feed one family for six weeks of the year and no more. It is a room, not a farm, and there is no country within three days' walk that could supply a household sitting in it. The children of the hut people grew up warm and could not work the cold pasture, which is the story their descendants tell, and it may even be the true one.

What is left is the bench: three slabs, two uprights, blood-warm because the lattice runs beneath it, its seat worn into a shallow curve by a very long line of people sitting down. That is the only durable human institution here — sitting. Herders come over the rim out of a wind that has been cutting them for nine hours and they sit on that bench and are warm all the way through in about four minutes, and a good number of them weep, and they are not ashamed of it afterwards because everyone has done it.

The bench faces the young fig, which is eighty years old and stands in the exact centre, in a spot that had been kept empty for as long as anybody's grandfather's grandfather could report. Nobody planted it. Something was keeping that space open, and then it stopped.

And then there is the one person nobody has met.

Eleven clay figures on the glassy lip, in a line, four-legged, badly made, no two alike. Not old: the newest has barely weathered. They are animals — you can tell a horse from a goat if you are generous — and they are set facing inward, down into the bowl.

The plateau's dead do not go into the ground up there. The ground is rotted granite and the wind takes the soil and there is nowhere to put anything. But down in the bowl is the one place in the known world where a body is received, cleanly, in a day, without ants and without smell, and every herder on the massif knows it.

They are not permitted to bring their animals in. Whatever the rule is, it is old and it is held to. So somebody has been making a rough clay beast for each beast they lost, and carrying it a day's walk, and setting it down on the lip where the taking-ground begins, and going home.

And the Kettle, which takes everything that dies, does not take clay.

Eleven of them sit there in a row, refused, in perfect condition, in the wind.

## XI. The householder

Which leaves the question of who keeps the room.

It is not a mystery in the sense of being obscure — the lattice's behaviour is fully documented and can be stated in three lines. It holds the air at thirty-one degrees. It pulls water out of a dry sky, continuously, and beads it on every surface. It removes the dead.

Say those three out loud and listen to them. Warmth held steady. Water brought in. Waste carried out. That is not the specification of a machine. That is a description of what any animal does for its own nest, and what every animal here is failing to do for itself because it has never had to.

It does not take you. You can dig it up, lie on it all night, drive a knife into it. It will not touch your boot or your knife or your body while you are using your body, and it is at your temperature the instant you touch it, whatever your temperature happens to be — which is either the mark of something extremely good at measuring, or of something with no temperature of its own to defend.

There is no evidence that it is alive and no evidence that it is not, and no evidence that the distinction was ever drawn by whoever left it. There is the ring of hard black-and-red stones like a jaw with most of its teeth gone. There is the drain in the south corner going *glub, glub* into a mountain that has no other end to it. There is the moss and the vine and the three flowers and the one aunt and the four trees sheared flat where the warmth stops, and ten thousand cicadas singing on a carrier tone that none of them can hear and all of them obey.

And there is the bench, worn hollow, exactly blood-warm, night and day, winter and summer, held at the temperature of a body by something that is not there — or that has been there the entire time, the whole forty paces of it, and has simply never been asked anything it could answer.

On the eastern stone, at chest height, worn nearly to nothing: *it was already working.*

The herders gloss it as a warning against tinkering. It reads more like the back half of a sentence whose front half has gone downstream with the rest of the mountain, to a delta with a name in another language.
