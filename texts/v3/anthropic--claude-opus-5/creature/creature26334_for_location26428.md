Begin with what is actually alive, because it is not much, and it is not what you would expect a place like this to have chosen.

The green is one organism. Not one species — one organism, in the sense that matters. Every patch on the plateau, from the fringe islands to the ridges at the center six hundred kilometers from anything, is the same thing, at the same stage of the same work, with no variants, no cripples, no sports, no lineages that went wrong. The surveyors call it the primer, because that is plainly what it is: a ground coat, laid on a surface to prepare it for something else.

It is a layered thing. Under a hand lens the top is not green at all but a clear glaze, half a millimeter of mucilage with the optical quality of aspic, and it is there because this world is drowning in light. Thin air, no dust, no cloud between squalls, a sun with nothing to argue with — the blaze here would flay an ordinary photosynthetic cell in an afternoon. The glaze scatters and sheds. Beneath it the working layer, blue-green from pigments that are not chlorophyll's usual companions, and its color is not appetite but refusal; it is dumping most of what falls on it and using a narrow band it can manage. It is not reaching for the light. It is surviving an excess.

Beneath that, the mat, and out of the mat the rhizoids, and here the thing becomes strange. They are black, no thicker than a hair, and they go into unbroken basalt — not into joints, not along cracks, because on fresh columnar rock there are no cracks worth using. They dissolve their way. Oxalates, siderophores, an acid front no wider than the tip advancing at a few hundred microns a week, prying iron out of the crystal lattice and taking it. The film needs almost nothing from the ocean. It does not eat the water, which contains nothing to eat. It eats the rock: reduced iron, sulfur, whatever the fissures brought up last month. It fixes its own nitrogen out of the air. It is a closed proposition, self-supplied, requiring only that it be wet sometimes and that the stone underneath it be young.

And when it dries it disappears. Two hours of sun off the splash zone and the glaze goes to a black varnish, matte, indistinguishable at arm's length from the basalt it sits on — so the kilometer of bare ridge you walked was very likely not bare, and you were standing on it, and it did not care. Spray finds it and in ninety seconds it swells and greens again, and the swelling is audible if you put your ear down: a faint crackle of rehydration, like paper unfolding.

At its edges it advances four or five millimeters a week. That is the whole of its ambition on foot. It could not cross the plateau in ten thousand years.

It does not cross on foot.

---

Here is how it moves, and it is the reason no one will call this place empty in front of the crews without getting an argument.

The film sheds flakes — a single cell layer folded in a scrap of glaze, dried, hydrophobic, light enough to be worth nothing to gravity and everything to a moving fluid. Surf on square-edged rock throws spray ninety meters. The rim's bent gradient peels drops upward off the meniscus and lifts them two kilometers. And the flake, tested, does one further trick: at minus two degrees it is an almost perfect ice nucleus. Better than mineral dust. Better than anything else in this atmosphere, because there is nothing else in this atmosphere.

So the hail has hollow centers. That is the flake's berth, sublimated out on the way down. And the hail shatters into rings on impact because the shell fails at the void, which is to say the cargo is delivered by a mechanism that opens on landing. And it lands inside a wall of rain that will not stop for twenty minutes, which is the precise window a flake needs to swell, grip, and set its first rhizoid.

Take that apart and you find you cannot separate the organism from the weather. The convection cells over the plateau are its circulatory system. The upward mist at the rim is its dispersal organ. The permanent hood of cloud around a continent is a structure it maintains and uses. It is not adapted to this ocean's climate; it is a component of it, and the climate is a component of it, and both were evidently drafted in the same sitting by whoever drafted them.

The last thing about the green is the thing the crews argue about at the end of shifts and never resolve. Its rhizoid extension is not continuous. It comes in pulses — a swelling, a boring, a rest — and the pulses are in phase. Everywhere. Patches four hundred kilometers apart, on different islands, at different ages, sunk into rock of different composition, advance together, on a period that is a clean subharmonic of the carrier tone. The whole plateau bores into itself in unison, twice a day, in time to a sound that has no source.

It has no competitors, no parasites, no predators, no disease, no failures. Nothing in the world contests it. That is not how life behaves. That is how a tool behaves that has not yet been handed over.

The second occupant cannot be described, only bounded, and the crews have gotten very disciplined about the difference.

There is no body. There is no location. There are three facts, held: the water lies in a bowl made of a preference; the water is stirred and will not stop being stirred; the water is four degrees and will not warm. Those are not three effects with three causes. Everything about their steadiness says one hand, one setting, one continuous act of holding — and the only honest thing to say about the occupant is that those three facts are its shape. It has the form of a maintained condition. If you want its silhouette, look at the meniscus at the rim: that overhanging lip of standing sea, bright and taut, curving out over four thousand meters of nothing. That is as close to a body as this thing gets. It is not holding the sea. It is what the sea is doing.

You can point at it exactly once, with a plumb bob. Hang a line at the rim and it does not fall true; it leans inland by a fraction of a degree, aiming at the plateau's center like a compass needle. It is the only instrument in the world that reads this tenant directly, and it costs nothing, and the crews keep one in every camp because it is the only thing here that answers.

The eighteen-hertz tone is not language and is not warning and is almost certainly not attention. Best guess, and it is only a guess: it is the idle. The cost of the holding, radiated as the one waste product a bent gradient can have. Which makes it a sound like a compressor in a basement, and it is everywhere, in the sinuses and the shinbones, in the chest underwater at slow intervals, and it means that no one in this ocean has ever been alone in the way a person can be alone in a desert. Something is running.

It has never responded to anything. No crew action, no instrument, no shouted question, no explosive charge, no eleven-year record of measurements has ever produced a change in it. It is not hostile and not attentive. It is set.

---

The third tenant is a tense.

Consider what has been arranged. A sea clamped at four degrees, top to bottom, pole to fringe — a working temperature, a temperature that holds oxygen, a temperature for cold, fast metabolisms. A sea permanently mixed, so that no layer of it will ever go stale. A sea rising a hand's width a year over rock rising faster, which means that shallows are coming: broad, sunlit, warm-bottomed shelves are being manufactured on a schedule. And on the rock, an organism whose only product is a few grams of dust, stickiness, and dissolved iron — the first sand in this ocean, the first proto-soil, laid down grain by grain across a continent by something that has no use for it whatsoever.

The green does not eat sand. Nothing here eats sand. Sand is being made for feet that do not exist and roots that have not arrived.

So the plateau is inhabited the way a set table is inhabited. Everything in it is sized and timed for guests, and the guests are the most numerous residents of the Shelf Sea by an overwhelming margin, and not one of them has been born. You feel them constantly. It is the reason the place does not read as wilderness. Wilderness is indifferent. This is expectant, at volume, in full daylight, and the expectancy is the single most crowded thing about it.

---

And then there are the crews, who are here because this is the only time the plateau will ever be young.

Four to nine people to an island, on the high tables above the surf line, in tie-down camps bolted into basalt with orange guy lines that have to be re-tensioned every third day. They are recognizable anywhere by their hands, which are laced with fine straight cuts from fresh fracture edges, cuts that do not heal quickly in cold salt and so accumulate; and by their sunburn, which is worst under the chin and the nose from light coming up off the water; and by the way they talk, which is a clipped shout with lip-reading built into it, because the surf never stops and the ear protection never comes off and everyone here is losing the top of their hearing at a rate they have all quietly calculated. Off-plateau, on leave, they still shout. They still stand too close.

Their work is absurd and they know it. They measure marks that are days old. They photograph the same flute in the same column face every week to watch erosion begin. They log the age of every millimeter of rain-streak on a continent, because every meter of that continent is the same age and this will never be true again. They are historians of the only thing in the world that has no history, and they are, in fact, its history — the notebooks are the plateau's entire past, and they know that too, and it makes them insufferably careful.

They have one relic and they will take you to see its coordinates without being asked. Year two, off the eastern fringe, someone dropped a wrench. It is three thousand meters down on new rock in the dark. Nothing scavenged it. Nothing corroded it in any hurry. There is no sediment to bury it because sediment has not been invented yet. It is lying exactly where it landed, in the position it landed in, and it will lie there for a geologically serious length of time, and it is the oldest artifact in this ocean and will remain so. They speak about it the way people speak about a grave they cannot visit.

And what they do not say much, but write in margins, is the obvious thing. The water is being kept at a working temperature. The sand is being made. The shallows are on their way. Somebody is expected.

They have mostly decided they are staff.

At night, which here is merely a duller loud, you can find one of them on a ridge with a headlamp on a patch of the green, waiting. It takes a while. Then the glaze lifts, very slightly, all at once, in time with two thousand kilometers of coast and with a sound nobody can hear, and the black hairs underneath it push a few hundred microns further into the stone, making dust for whatever comes next.

They watch it. It does not know what it is for. Neither, precisely, do they. Both of them are waiting on the same signal, and one of them can feel it in the long bones, and only one of them minds.
