## The Lace

Start with the cores, because the cores are the honest part. Clean book, they say — no shells, no bones, no burrows, not at any depth. What they mean is that nothing here has ever had a hard part, and nothing here has ever died in a way that leaves a body behind.

The resident of the Held Water has no cells that contract. Nothing in it is faster than growth. By every test anyone has thought to run on it, it is not an animal, and the people who run the tests go home and write *not an animal* and sleep badly.

It is white. It is everywhere below sixty-one metres and thinly present above. It is one thing, as far as sampling can say — the same organism on the west wall and on the east, in the fallen weed-trees and in the silt and in the mortarless joints of the drowned houses, which means it is either a single individual some thirty thousand years old, or the question is being asked wrong. The divers call it the lace. The people in the valley below the rim, who still walk up to the notch in summer and leave a little food on the granite for no reason they can give you, call it something that translates badly. *The one who puts things where they go.* *Her that tidies.* Nobody down there thinks of it as an animal either. That is not the category it troubles.

---

**What it is made of.**

Filaments three microns across, in sheets, in ropes, in felts. Where it is thin you would take it for frost on a stone. Where it is thick — out on the first sill, under the white — you can cut a core and bring up a continuous sheet two metres long that does not fray when you slice it, because it is not sediment with fungus in it. It is fungus with sediment held.

That is the first correction to make to the published description. The false floor is not a drift. It is a made thing. The density boundary sets the height; the lace does the rest, knitting the fall into felt as fast as the fall arrives, which is to say not fast at all, which is to say faster than the fall. The hollows are level because they have been levelled.

It has partners, or tenants, or organs, depending on how strict you want to be. The lampweed's holdfasts are laced through. So are the stonewort meadows, and the two-metre moss beards, which are knitted at their ledges in a way moss does not do for itself. The low ochre-green light is nothing's byproduct: it is a reaction the lace runs in the leaf tissue of things it lives in, at a cost, and cost implies purpose, and nobody knows the purpose. It moves through the meadows in sheets. It brightens where the water shears. If you are inclined to call that a face, you will not be argued out of it while you are down there, and you will be embarrassed about it on the surface.

---

**How it moves.**

By growing, and by lifting.

Turgor is enough to crack paving; every gardener knows it. The lace is not in a hurry, and hurry is the only thing it lacks. Under a flat river cobble it will spend four seasons building the pressure to break the stone's seat in the silt, and the stone comes free in a single small event nobody has ever witnessed.

Then the bladders. This is its arm. It grows a lampweed rope to the stone — an ordinary-looking strap, indistinguishable from any other, except that it terminates in a collar around a cobble — and it lets the bladders fill until the stone is very slightly light. Not floating. Just relieved of most of itself. And then it waits eleven minutes for the lake to lean.

The seiche walks it. The current lays the meadow east and the stone goes east eighty centimetres; the current lays the meadow west and a lace anchor takes up the slack so the stone does not come back. Eighty centimetres, twice an hour, for as long as is needed. When the stone is where it goes, a bladder is vented, and the stone settles, and the lace beds it and keys it and dresses the joint over the following decade.

Six stones a year is not the speed of the work. Six stones a year is what the work *finishes*.

So: the seiche does it, yes. The people who say that are right. They have simply never asked whose limb the seiche is.

---

**What it eats.**

Only the dead, and only after a long wait. This is the whole ethic of the basin and it is not restraint; it is constitution. There is no mouth here and never has been. A fallen weed-tree takes forty years to go, and the forty years are not inefficiency. They are the digestion.

But the dead have to land somewhere. Left alone, the fall of the upper valley would go past the sill and down into the hundred-and-forty, and past that into the true floor, where almost nothing reaches, and the lace would starve in the dark on a clean rock.

So it keeps the sill.

Under the west wall there is an old evaporite bed, and the lace is in it, and has been working it for longer than the current shoreline has existed — leaching salts, running them up into the boundary layer in a slow steady dosing that holds the density step sharp. When the spring plume dives in grey and heavy and threatens to punch through, the dosing increases. The sill is not a feature of the lake. It is a table, and the lace lays it, and everything the valley sheds falls onto it and stops and is held.

It built the ground it stands on. Then it built on the ground.

---

**What it hears.**

Everything, and nothing else. It has no eyes and makes light. It has no ears and lives inside a roar.

Pressure is its whole sense: the sternum-note of the overflow coming inward through the rock; the whoop and downward slide of the winter lid; the chime of gas ropes breaking; the knocking of a boulder taking half an hour to come down a slope. It knows the shape of its basin the way you know the inside of your mouth. It knows when a rope has broken and where. It knows the talus is moving before the talus is sure.

And it knows the standing rooms. Of course it does. In the deaf rooms there is no lace at all — not a thread, not a haze. The stone in them is bare and the drift does not lie in them. They are swept. Forty centimetres outside the boundary the felt begins as thick as anywhere; inside, clean rock, and a silence you can sit in while the lake screams on all sides.

Nobody has established what it does in the quiet. The prevailing view is that it needs the quiet to hear itself: that a thing this size, listening through a body two kilometres wide, requires somewhere the din does not reach in order to receive its own slow signal. That it thinks in rooms.

The diver who spent the night in one did not report anything happening. She reported that it was *kept*.

---

**What it learned.**

It did not invent architecture. It copied it.

The village came down under the rising water — that is the order of events; the lake climbed the ninth shoreline and took the terraces and the pass road and the low houses on the knuckles — and for a few thousand years the lace lived among a set of worked stones arranged according to rules it had no way to understand and every way to observe. A retaining wall holds soil. An arc across a current makes a drift pile up behind it. A stack of stones stands, and keeps standing, and this is apparently the point of it.

It took the grammar and left the meaning, the way you hum a tune heard through a wall.

The weirs are its fields. Forty low arcs on the white, opening upstream into the seiche, gathering the fall into windrows so the felt can work it. They are not fish traps; they cannot be; there are no fish and there never were. Thirty-one of them stand at the margins of standing rooms. The other nine are cruder, older, enclose nothing, and are set in a place where no current has run for twelve thousand years. Those are the practice.

And the stone for them came from somewhere. Terrace seven is missing its top course. Terrace eleven is missing most of a wall. There were more than three houses on the second moraine hill; there are three now, and one wooden bowl still on its ledge in the middle chamber, untouched, dressed around with lace at the base where it meets the stone, held in place.

It has been unbuilding the village for ten thousand years and re-laying it in patterns that answer to the current. It has left the bowl where it was set down. Nothing in its behaviour suggests it knows the difference between those two acts.

---

**The note.**

The notch at the northeast rim cuts downward at the speed of fingernails, and every centimetre it cuts, the lake goes lower, and the pitch of the whole basin falls.

This is the one thing the lace hurries for.

Go to the lip in summer — hold your slate against the polished granite under the two hand-spans of sliding water — and you will find, in the joints, a white felt packed with silt and fine gravel, renewed as fast as it is stripped, thickest exactly where the flow bites. It is losing. It has always been losing. But the fingernail is a slow fingernail and part of the reason is that something has spent three hundred centuries packing the lip of the world with its own body.

The nine older shorelines are all above the present one. The water has been going out of this valley since before there were terraces on the south slope, and it will keep going, and in some tens of thousands of years the pass road will come up into the air, and then the houses, and then the level white of the first sill will have nowhere to lie.

It cannot know this. It has no organ for the future. It only has the note, which it takes in through the whole of itself, and which is very slightly flatter than it was when the village drowned.

And it packs the notch, and it lays its table, and twice an hour it lets the lake lean and walks another stone eighty centimetres along a summer path that has not been walked since the water came up.

Six a year. Bedded, keyed, chosen for fit.
