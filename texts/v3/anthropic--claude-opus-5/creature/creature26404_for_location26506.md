## The Inhabitants

Nobody is asleep. Start there, because that is the actual condition of everything alive in the Increase tonight, from the bunkhouse to the mud.

Four bodies in six bunks, all of them lying in that particular attitude of people who have given up on sleeping but have not yet admitted it: on the back, forearm over the eyes, boots on the floor turned inward so they can be found in the dark. Earplugs in, all of them, the orange foam kind rated for thirty-three decibels, and the plugs work on the geese — they push the flock back to a wall, a dense grey pressure you can lie against — but the plugs do nothing at all about the ice. Ice comes up through the stilts and into the frame and along the steel of the bunk to the mastoid bone behind the ear, and you hear the floes with your skull. Every eleven or twelve minutes something out there fails structurally and everyone's ribs register it and nobody says anything, because saying anything is not possible, and also because it is the fourth night and there is nothing left to say about ice.

Look at their hands. That's where the residents of this place are legible. Cracked across the knuckles, split at the thumb webs, and every nail underlined with a grey crescent of silt that does not wash out, has never washed out, will still be there in a hospital bed forty years from now in a city with no rivers in it. They call it the ring. You get it in your first season and you keep it. There are people down south who can be identified as former Increase staff by a handshake.

---

They don't talk. This is not stoicism, it is acoustics. From breakup until the flock goes north — twelve days, sixteen, whatever the ice grants — the human voice at conversational volume carries about a metre and a half. Shouting carries four and costs your throat. So they use their hands, and what they use is not a real sign language, it's a pidgin about ninety signs deep that has grown up here over thirty-one years and is passed on by imitation in the first week of everybody's first spring.

Sample it: *ice going* is two flat hands sliding apart, low. *Water rising* is the same hands turning up at the wrist. *Don't* is a single finger laid flat across the lips — borrowed from the world, kept because it reads at distance in bad light. *Coffee* is a fist rotated at the sternum. *I'm all right* is a thumb pressed hard into the opposite palm, and it must be pressed hard, because the light version means *not really*. There is a sign for *bear on the drift line* and a separate sign for *bear at the camp*, and the second one is made with two hands and is the only sign in the vocabulary anyone has ever described as beautiful. There is a sign for *someone has answered their name*, which involves touching the ear and then the mouth, and which is used more than you would want.

The dogs know it. There are three dogs in the Increase this month, all of them deaf — every dog here goes deaf in its second or third breakup, nobody has ever bothered to prove why, everybody believes it absolutely — and the dogs learned to read hands before some of the seasonal staff did. A dog here will not respond to its name at all and will come from ninety metres away for a lifted chin.

---

Now the people, individually, since you asked.

Nobody uses surnames in the Increase. Surnames exist on the payroll forms and on the flight manifests and nowhere else, and asking for one is a mild social offence, the equivalent of asking what somebody paid for their coat.

**Tarrant** is the exception, called by his surname because he offered it that way in his first hour, twelve years ago, in a tone that suggested he had rehearsed. He runs the count station and he is the closest thing here to a man in love. What he loves is the discharge record: the hydrophone trace, the turbidity series, the mass balance of sediment in and sediment retained, resolved to the fifteen-minute interval since year four. He is thin and permanently slightly wet and he will show you, unasked, that in the ninety hours of last year's jam release the Increase gained more sediment than in the following seven months combined, and he will hold up two fingers to mean *look at this, look, two years' silt in four days,* and his face will do something that in another man would be prayer. He does not go out on the ice. He has been told twice.

**Amos** is the ground-truther, which is the actual job title and the best joke in the place, since the fundamental property of this ground is that it isn't true. Every autumn a helicopter and a satellite between them draw a map of what exists. Every May, Amos walks out into it with a bundle of numbered rebar and a camera and a pole, and physically stands on things to see whether they are there. Of one hundred and forty stakes set last year he will find perhaps fifty. Some will be under two metres of new silt with only the tag showing; some will be out in a channel that did not exist in October, still upright, with the water combing past them; most will simply be gone, floated off inside a slab of turf with willows standing on it, planted now on some bar forty kilometres downstream, mislabelling it. A hundred billion self-assembling ceramic nodules run this delta and the entire apparatus still requires a man in his fifties with a bad hip to walk out into the sleet and put his hand on a piece of steel so that a number can be legally asserted. Amos finds this funny. He has found it funny for nine years.

**Sofie** is seventy-eight and has been asked her age by four separate audits. She is from here in a sense that takes a paragraph. Her grandmother's fish camp stood at a place that is now approximately eleven metres under water and four kilometres out, because for a century this coast went backwards at three metres a year and took the camps and the drying racks and two cemeteries with it. Sofie was fifteen when they moved the last of the coffins inland and thirty-two when they stopped bothering. She keeps a map that is wrong on purpose. On it are the drowned islands, in their old positions, with their old names, and when a new bar consolidates enough to hold willow she goes and looks at it and gives it one of those names, whether or not it is anywhere near the right place. The count station files it as Island 47-D. She files it as Ivutaq, again. Both records go into the audit; one of them is asked for.

She is not grateful. This is worth understanding about the people who are *from* here rather than *at* here. The land is coming back, faster than it ever left, four hundred hectares a year, and it is coming back wrong: wrong shape, wrong channels, wrong bird in the wrong numbers, muddier water than her father worked, fewer of the fish that mattered and vastly more of the fish that didn't. Nobody asked. A thing can be a restitution and an imposition in the same motion, and Sofie holds both of those, all the time, without visible strain, in the way that people who have lived through an entire policy era learn to hold things.

**Ket** is twenty-four and in her second spring and she has not slept more than two hours consecutively since the ice started and she is in the phase everyone here recognises and nobody interferes with. They call it the fortnight. What happens is this: the goose noise is not noise, it is a wall of voices with no gaps in it, and a wall of voices with no gaps in it contains, statistically, every possible utterance, including your name in the voice of someone who is dead. Everyone answers at least once. Everyone. You sit up in the bunk, or you stop dead in the willows with one boot in the mat, and you say *what* out loud into four hundred thousand wings. Afterwards you make the sign — ear, then mouth — and somebody puts a hand flat on your back for three seconds, and that is the whole ceremony, and it is enough, and it is why there is a chapel.

Written on the bunkhouse wall over the stove in permanent marker, in three different hands, added to over the years: **NOTHING SIGNED IN MAY.** They mean it. Marriages, resignations, confessions, purchases: all of it is void until the flock goes north and the world quiets down to a mere river.

---

The rest of the population.

Two hundred thousand geese, who are not scenery and not guests but staff. They are the machine's actual labour force and the reason a business plan exists: they graze the new sedge flats down to stubble, they grub the rhizomes, they turn silt into droppings and droppings into nitrogen, they fertilise their own next meal, they die here in quantity and go into the peat. The nodules make the mud; the geese make the mud *fertile*; the willow does the rest and locks up the carbon that pays for all of it. Two hundred thousand hard little agendas, none of them containing a delta.

The ravens, who are the only residents visibly enjoying themselves. Eight or nine pairs around the camps, and their calls here are pitched noticeably higher than the ravens forty kilometres upriver — learned, adjusted, punched up an octave to cut through goose. They are the sole animal in the Increase that has redesigned itself for the noise instead of merely enduring it.
