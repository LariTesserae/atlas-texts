## Who Lives at the Mill

Ask the question here and you get a census rather than a name, and the census has to be sorted by tenancy, because almost nobody in the hollow is a resident.

Start with the ones you notice. They are all commuters.

The wheatear on the big boulder has an address, and it is not in the bowl. It is ninety meters up the moraine's back slope, in a cavity between two slabs of that unslumped rubble, a cup of grass stems and shed goose down eleven centimeters below the surface of the ridge, where the noise arrives as a vibration in stone and nothing more. She raises five there and works down into the hollow to do her shopping. Watch her long enough and you see what the place has done to her manners. A wheatear is normally a bird of announcements — the hard *chik-chik*, the scold, the perch-and-declare. In the bowl she has given all that up. She still opens her beak, from habit or from lack of any way to stop, but she has switched her signaling to the visual channel entirely: the white rump flashed once, deliberately, on take-off; the bob; the wing-droop at the boulder's edge that means *mine*. Two adults meet on the gravel bar four meters apart and negotiate a boundary in complete silence, in mime, like men on a factory floor. Then she flies out over the crest with a beakful of black midges arranged crosswise, seven or nine of them, and delivers them into a hole in the moraine where her young are being built out of an insect that cannot survive being warm.

The wagtail is passing through and will be gone in a week. The snow buntings are on their way to somewhere with seeds. The skua is auditing. The fox came over the crest at two in the morning, walked the silt for sixty meters, took nothing, and left prints that will be gone with the next fog-drip. The geese put down on the shore for one night in May because the shore is flat, bare, and impossible to approach unseen, and that was the whole of their interest in the place.

So: the vertebrates are traffic. The biomass here is nearly all in transit, as the hollow's own arithmetic makes clear, and the ones who actually live at the mill are, without exception, either very small, very flat, very slow, or inside the machine.

---

Begin with the ones who are working the ice itself, because they are also, unexpectedly, part of the mill's drive train.

On the bare apron, in the weathering crust — that decimeter of rotten, porous, glittering surface ice that is more water than solid by afternoon — there is a purple-brown alga in chains. *Ancylonema*: barrels of cells strung end to end, four or five to a filament, each barrel packed with a dark pigment that acts as a welder's visor, screening a cell that has no shade to hide in and no plan to leave. Individually invisible. Collectively they are a stain. They are the reason the ice in July is not white but a faint bruised grey-lavender where the snow has gone, and because they are dark, they absorb, and because they absorb, they melt down their own little wells and warm their own film of water and release their own next season's meltwater a few days earlier than white ice would.

Which means the plant has flora, in the medical sense, and the flora is stoking the boiler. Some fraction of the tonnage going down the outflow — a real fraction, a measurable one — is being processed on behalf of an organism that cannot move and does not know it is doing it.

---

Now the households.

Each cryoconite hole is a founded settlement with a date, a floor plan, and a population, and the floor is masonry. This deserves saying plainly, because the hollow is described as a place with nothing built in it: the granules at the bottom of every one of those coin-wide wells are constructions. Filamentous cyanobacteria — hair-fine, unbranched, sheathed in mucilage — have knitted the mineral dust into crumbs, wrapping grains, gluing grains, spinning the loose black flour into a soil-like tilth that holds together when you tip it into your palm. It is the only architecture within a kilometer. It was made without hands, without light for most of the year, at nought degrees, out of the mill's own waste.

Into that tilth the tenants come.

A rotifer, a bdelloid, three hundred microns of transparent worm with a wheel of cilia at the front end that spins a whirlpool into her own mouth. She is anchored by a foot with two toes and a cement gland; she stands, sweeps, retracts to a bead at any shadow, extends again. No one has ever found a male of her kind. She and her mother and her mother's mother back through unfathomable stretches of time have simply divided the matter among themselves.

A tardigrade beside her, larger, slower, opaque, eight legs each ending in a set of hooks like a cat's, hauling herself over the granules with the gait of a small bear crossing scree — which is the only reason anyone remembers her. Her mouth is a pair of hardened stylets and a sucking tube. She walks up to a cyanobacterial filament, sets the stylets, punches, and drinks the cell out, and you can watch the green go into her because she is a window with a gut in it.

A copepod, comma-sized, the whale of this world, jackknifing across the two-millimeter water column in bursts.

And ten to the eighth bacteria in every gram of the tilth beneath all of them, doing the accounting.

Ten thousand of these households across the apron, each with its own founding dust, its own depth, its own thermal budget, its own set of neighbors it will never meet. And each with the same ending, which is not gradual. Sometime — this year, next year — the hole's floor melts down until it intersects a drainage channel in the rotten ice, and the entire world drains in an afternoon. The water, the tilth, the masonry, the rotifers, the bears and the whale all go out of the bottom of their sky and into the braids and down past your boots in the grey milk, and this is not a catastrophe. It is the distribution arrangement. Rotifers and tardigrades and the cyanobacteria all have the same trick: pull in the water, fold the legs, curl, collapse to five percent of your volume, replace what was water with sugar, and become a grain of dust. In that state you can be frozen, boiled, dried for a decade, and blown two valleys over. The fog that lies in this bowl is faintly populated. So is the silt on the fox's feet. So is every windrow of grit on the moraine crest.

The residents of the hollow are, in a real sense, also its cargo. They are shipped constantly, they are re-seeded constantly, and the population is stable while no individual address is.

The one animal here that has genuinely bet everything on the mill is the midge.

The larvae live in the first two hundred meters below the portal, in the coldest, greyest, most abrasive water in the drainage — down in the interstices between cobbles that knock and shift over them at a rate of one a second, in a torrent so loaded with flour that they live in a permanent sandstorm, scraping a film of algae and bacteria off stone faces that are being scoured clean underneath them. Pale, small, unremarkable, wriggling. And absolutely specific: they want water between nought and two degrees. At eight they are finished. They are not tolerating the cold; they have moved into it, deliberately, because nothing else will follow them there, and the price of the tenancy is that their address exists only while the ice keeps pouring.

The adults are the black walking things you see on the ice when the fog lifts. Matte, hairy, deliberate, wingless — a walking gonad in a fur coat, with antifreeze in the blood, doing everything an insect does but doing it at the freezing point and on foot. They do not feed.
