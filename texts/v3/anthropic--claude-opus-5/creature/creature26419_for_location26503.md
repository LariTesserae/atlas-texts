## What Lives There

The answer is unsatisfying for about three weeks, and then it stops being unsatisfying and becomes something you have trouble sleeping next to.

You do not find it by looking. Looking is the wrong instrument here; the shelf has been defeating eyes for a hundred million years and has no particular respect for yours. You find it by arithmetic. You find it the way you find a tenant in an empty house — not by meeting them, but by noticing that the taps have been left in good order.

Start with the bone stacks, since they are what got counted first.

Twenty-two in eleven kilometres, hub-side, downslope face, and the spacing is not random: five hundred metres, plus or minus forty, which is not the range of a hunter's convenience but the range of something laid out. Each stack is a metre across and reads, once you clear the frost-crust, as a deliberate assembly. Long bones parallel, largest at the base, oriented downslope. Ribs interleaved crosswise in courses, like brushwood. The flat pelvic plate on top as a lid, concave side down, so that meltwater is shed off the pile rather than into it. Nothing gnawed. Nothing cracked for marrow — the marrow is taken through a single clean aperture at the epiphysis, one hole per bone, always the same end. And no skull. Not in any of the twenty-two, not in any of the four hundred and ten stacks eventually surveyed on the whole shelf.

The skulls are up the trunk.

Go up a station tree with a lamp and you find them wedged into the fluting at bladder height, face inward, five or six of them, or in the old trees thirty or forty, and the wood has closed over the oldest until only a ridge of the sagittal crest shows, like a nail head in a beam that has been painted eleven times. The tree is eating them. Slowly, at the rate of the rest of its business, it is drawing the mineral out of them into its own laminated column, and the animal that put them there has been putting them there since before either of them existed as individuals.

Dig a stack and it goes down a metre and a half, and it is stratified. The bottom courses are half-dissolved and the tree's rootlets have grown up through them into every foramen, so that when you lift a femur it comes away wearing a white glove of root. That deposit is somewhere between eighty and four hundred years old, depending on whose dissolution model you use. The stack is not a leftover. The stack is a foundation. It is where the shelf's calcium is banked, and it is banked at the base of the wells, on the sheltered face, under a lid, by hand.

And the trees know it in the only way a tree can. A station tree is thirty per cent thicker in the trunk than an unattended tree of the same height. Its collar is cleaner. Its barbs carry less rime. It turns at fifteen revolutions per minute in a wind where its unattended neighbour, forty metres away, turns at thirteen.

Two revolutions per minute. That is the size of the difference, and the whole animal is contained in it.

The field crews call them oilers. Nobody uses the survey designation.

An adult is fifty to sixty kilograms and two point four metres from muzzle to tail-hook, and folds — this is the important verb — into a bundle about seventy centimetres long that fits the concavity of a wheel-tree flute as though the flute had been cut for it, because in a manner of speaking it was. Six limbs. The forward four are the running gear: long, evenly proportioned, each ending in four contact digits and an opposed hook of dense keratin worn to a bright polish. The rear two are shorter, thicker, and are for holding things and for killing. The tail is not a tail so much as a fifth arm with a hook and no digits, used as a safety line; it is never all the way off the vine.

The coat is two-layered. The outer is a dull grey-fawn with a faint longitudinal striation, and it is not camouflage in the sense of blending with a background; it is portraiture. It is the exact value and grain of wheel-tree bark under starlight, including the slight sheen on the ridges. The under-layer is dense and oily and smells of the bearing wax, because it *is* the bearing wax; the animal wears the surplus of what it grooms. There is no better description of the thing than that. It is an animal that smells like machinery because it has spent its life with its head in the works.

It has no voice. There is a valve in the throat and nothing else — no larynx, no resonator, nothing that could make a noise even if the air here would carry one. This is worth sitting with for a moment, because the animal has a language.

The language is in the hands. The palm skin is corrugated and packed with mechanoreceptors at a density that has no parallel in anything else on the shelf, and when an oiler lays a hand on a transmission vine it is not touching a plant, it is picking up a line. It hears the whole shelf.
