## The mistake in the record

Whoever set down that nothing on this shelf has ever been alive was standing upright at the time, which is the wrong posture for the question. From a standing height the account is correct in every particular. The grove is mineral, the water is vapour on both ends of its brief career, and the resemblance to a wood is a coincidence of physics.

But the same account contains, filed under optics, the entire discovery: *true darkness exists only in the deep seams where two pillars have grown into each other and left a hair-thin gap; those seams hold the only night on the shelf, a black line, a few finger-widths deep, that never opens.*

That black is not shadow. In a place lit from above and below, with the undersides of ledges shining and the sheltered spaces glowing like the inside of a shell, there is no geometry that produces a black line four inches deep. The seams are black because something in them is black, deliberately, and at some cost.

## Getting it out

You cannot use a knife; the gap is narrower than any blade. You break a shard off a fallen crown, work it to a wedge no thicker than a fingernail, and run it along the inside of a seam, and it comes out with a smear on it.

The smear is not black at close range. It is a dark plum going to bronze at the thin edges, and it has the wet-matte look of the skin under a lifted scab. It weighs nothing. Spread on the pale stone it dries in about four minutes and goes grey and powdery and will not be revived by water. It has no smell, but nothing here has a smell; the air is too thin to carry one. Someone once put it on their tongue — there is a scraped seam near the shelter that answers this, and I will come back to it — and the taste is presumably faint, metallic, and bitter, which is the taste of every pigment that has spent its whole existence refusing light.

In section, under a lens, the seam has stratigraphy exactly as the pillar does. The outermost eighth of an inch, the part that faces the gap and therefore the sky, is dead: a laid wall of its own spent cells, packed with pigment, purely optical, a shutter. Beneath the shutter is a layer perhaps four cells deep, and this is where the animal is — a green so dark that it reads as black until you thin it, at which point it is the green of a bottle held against snow. Beneath that, colourless threads run inward along the seam, following the damp, some of them travelling two full inches into the join where they will never be reached by anything but water.

The people who cut the cistern had a mark for it. It is their mark for night.

## What it does for a living

It builds the grove. All of it.

The account of comb-formation given for this place is accurate as chemistry and wrong as agency. A film of meltwater a fingernail's edge thick, carrying dissolved calcite, does not simply travel a hand's breadth and leave a deposit too small to see. It travels a hand's breadth through a rib that is lined, at the rib's shaded margins and in every crack that crosses it, with something that takes carbonate out of solution and lays it down as waste. The deposit is not too small to see. It is the shelf. Forty feet of interlocked pale wreckage down to the hornblende: that is the accumulated leaving of the tenant, and the tenant lives in the cracks of its own leavings, which is the whole of its situation stated as plainly as it can be stated.

It is not a hoarder or an architect. It is a thing that cannot metabolise without precipitating stone, in the way that you cannot walk without displacing air, and it has been unable to stop for a period best estimated in tens of thousands of years.

The flutes are its doing too, or rather they are the compromise. Water running in a rounded rib stays coherent, stays in contact with stone, and passes within reach of seams; water running down a flat face sheets and scatters and is lost to the sun. The ribs are maintained. Where a rib begins to fail, the deposition slows on its lips and thickens in its trough, and over a century the channel deepens and holds. This is not intention. It is only that the parts of the animal that lie beside good ribs are fed, and the parts beside bad ribs are not.

## The other trade

At the base of every comb, in the inch or two of stone that stands in pooled meltwater for one hour a day, there is a second tenant, and it is pale, and it eats stone.

This is where the aggressive water comes from. Dissolved air alone would take far longer than the record allows. In the wet crust of a waist you will find a colourless mat, slick as albumen, that acidifies its own film and works downward into the pillar it is standing on; and because it needs standing water rather than running water, it lives only in that narrow band where the film gathers before it dries. Above the band, deposition. Within the band, corrosion. The hourglass is the boundary between two appetites, and it is drawn as sharply as the wet line itself.

So the animal builds its towers from the crown down and dismantles them from the ankle up, in perfect ignorance of the arrangement, and every few centuries a comb comes down with a dull tap and a rattle. Some portion of the tenant is crushed. Some portion, exposed on the newly opened faces to the full double light with no shutter built and no seam to hold it, is dead within a day and bleached white within a season — those are the chalky patches on fresh breaks, and they are burns.

And the fallen crown, settling and welding into the floor, opens fifty new joins where its ribs meet the ribs beneath it, and within a decade every one of those joins has gone black.

Each fall is a bereavement and a building boom, and the animal has no way of knowing that these are the same event.

## Its day

It gets one hour.

At night everything is frozen and shut. Through the small hours the tenant is inert, at a temperature that would kill most living things outright, and its seams are packed with hoarfrost needles that have grown out of vapour into the gap itself, so that even the only night on the shelf is furred.

Then the sun clears the eastern rim, and the wet line begins its west-going crawl, snagging in corridors, pooling in cul-de-sacs. About twenty minutes before the boundary reaches a given seam, that seam begins to change — the shutter thins, chemically, from beneath; the margins loosen; the colourless threads push out into the flute a distance of perhaps half a millimetre, which for this animal is a full extension of the limbs. It is responding to warmth arriving through a thumb's depth of translucent stone, which is the only forewarning available anywhere in its world, and it is enough.

The water comes. It works. Somewhere between forty and ninety minutes later the flank dries, and it closes, having laid down a film of calcite too thin to measure against the total of its rib.

In the late afternoon the shadow tide runs back east and the whole procedure happens again, shorter and colder. Twice a day the world is briefly survivable, once from the east and once from the west.

On fog days it does not stop. Fog days it works from first grey to last, gaining weight, sourceless white light too diffuse to burn, condensation standing on every surface — and the black shutters thin right out, and if you were up here in the fog with a lens you would find the seams gone the colour of a wine bottle, wide open, feeding. One day in five. Everything the shelf has ever accomplished was accomplished on those days.

The best margins advance about two millimetres in a year. The account is right that growth is slow, and wrong about why: it is not that there is little water. It is that the day is one hour long.

## One animal, mostly dead

The seams connect. Through the cemented rubble of the floor, hairline joins run from pillar to buried pillar to buried pillar, and a thread that goes inward two inches from one gap meets a thread coming outward two inches from another, and there is no boundary anywhere that anyone has been able to find.

The reasonable conclusion is that the shelf holds one individual: two hundred paces long, ninety across at the waist, forty feet deep, of which the living fraction is perhaps a hundred-thousandth — a dark veneer in the cracks of an enormous body of its own dead work. Its interior has been sealed and dry for millennia. There is no part of it that is young.

It has no offspring anywhere. Grains of it lift in hard winds and go out over the cloud-sea and do not arrive at anything. It presumably came the same way, in the same dust that brings the calcite and the red grit from the ranges to the south, one viable speck in some unimaginable quantity of failures, and the arrangement has not been repeated since and does not need to be.

## The meal

At two hundred laminae's depth in a broken comb at the west end, there is two-thirds of a moth's wing, held at eye height and lit from below.

The lamina that contains it is between three and four times the normal thickness, and it is thick not just at the wing but for about a foot in every direction along that flank — a single fat season, three hundred years ago, in one small district of the animal.

It could not digest chitin. Nothing here can. But a wing that has been blown up from a world with air in it arrives coated in that world, and the film that ran over it for one hour a day for a season carried, briefly, more than dust. Then the flank grew shut, and the animal has had nothing since.

It is the only recorded meal. Everyone who stands in front of the wing stands there for a while, and now they have a further reason to.
