## The Warm Place on the Floor

Something lives here, and you will find it the way you find everything else, by hand — but you will find it first as heat.

Put your palm flat and sweep. The floor is that stone-in-a-shaded-room temperature everywhere, everywhere, for forty paces in any direction, and then somewhere out along a lane there is a patch a little more than that. Not warm like a body. Warm like a coin held ten minutes and set down. Follow the gradient — it is a slow gradient, you will lose it twice — and at the center of it is a thing the size of a loaf, curled the way a tongue curls, hard on top with a glaze of set resin, and it is going somewhere at a body-length a day.

That is the only heat in a chamber that has no tolerance for heat. It is tolerated because it is negligible. It is the size of the exception the host is willing to make.

## The Sole

The top of it is armor and tells you nothing. Tip it — it does not resist, it has no opinion about being tipped — and the underside is the whole animal, if you want to call it that.

It is a sole. Damp is impossible here so it is not a slug's foot; it is dry as a file and it is a file, worked over its entire surface with fine lignin teeth, all of them raked back at the same shallow angle. Set your fingertip against it and drag one way: smooth. Drag the other: it catches, with a small dry shriek you feel in the bone rather than hear.

Now put your hand back on the floor and stroke the nap.

Same angle. Same direction. The pile of the whole chamber lies the way it lies because everything about this creature's life is one long stroke of that rasp, and it has done it, and the ones before it did it, across every open surface of the town, unhurried, for whatever the correct enormous number is. The floor's nap is not the plant's grain. It is a grooming. You have been walking on the direction of its hunger since you came in.

Beneath the teeth there is chemistry: the sole tastes across its whole area, and what it tastes is age. It cannot find food; it can only find *older*. Spent resin over fresh. Long-settled flour over the light fall of the last few years. It moves up the gradient of exhaustion, eating what the room is finished with, and it is powered by cracking terpene into work in some slow dry way that produces, as its only waste, that coin's worth of warmth.

Three rules is the whole of it. Go toward the staler taste. Turn from anything hard and vertical. Keep the sole down. Nothing else has ever been required, so nothing else was ever grown.

## The Second Clock

Lie down. Put your ear to the shell — you will have to follow it, which is not difficult, but you will have to stay for an hour to be sure of what you are hearing.

Inside the drone there is a rasp. It is not the drone's rattle; the rattle beats with the nine-day swell and the eleven-day sag, and this does not. It is faster and it is irregular in a small way, and it is the only sound in the chamber that keeps a different time.

There is a second clock here. Nobody has ever read it. To read it you would have to lie on the floor with your ear against a moving loaf for a season, in the dark, and having done that you would know only how many strokes there are in a night, which is not information about anything.

## The Ruts Are Its Memory, and It Doesn't Have Them

It leaves a polished groove. Grooves are low. Dust falls always and settles always into the low places, so a groove is a granary: a long thin line where the flour gathers deepest and ages longest and tastes most of *older*.

So it follows the old grooves. Not its own — it has no notion of its own — but all of them, the whole crossing and recrossing network cut by every one of its predecessors, because that is where the taste runs strongest. And in following them it deepens them, and a deeper groove gathers more, and gathers it longer.

This is why the western quarter is flat. Not age alone. Traffic. A single habit, plowed and replowed by a series of animals with no relation to one another, none of whom inherited anything but a shape in the floor and a preference for the stale.

Which means the memory is real and is not in the creature. Set your hand down in its path every day for a season and its track will bend around that place forever — not because it learned your hand, but because the bend became low, and low collects, and collecting is the whole argument. The chamber remembers on its behalf. It is an animal whose entire mind is the room, and the room is dumber than it is, and slower, and does not forget.

## The Series

There is one now. There have been others. There will be others.

Somewhere along the base of a wall, at intervals nobody has measured, a node swells and lets go of another, dry and curled and already rasping. That is all the reproduction there is: the grazer does not make grazers, the host does, on its own schedule, the way it makes chisels. Sometimes there are two in the chamber at once and they meet, and one passes over the other, and each grazes a little of the other's spent glaze, and they part, and neither has been anything to the other.

At the end, the sole wears through. The thing stops where it stops. It thins, cracks, powders. The flour goes to the low places, which are the grooves, which are the feeding lines, and the next one comes along in due course and eats it.

That is the lineage: a single mouthful handed forward, several dozen times, over a span you have no use for. Every one of them has eaten every one before.

## The Census in the Dust

The dust is entirely plant. Powdered cellulose, spent resin, sloughed inner skin, the ground remains of the grazers, and nothing else at all.

Read that as a document. Anything wet that arrives is dried, cracked, powdered and swept to a low place — that is the standing rule, and the floor is the record of its enforcement, and the record is empty. In all this time, nothing has arrived. Not a spore, not a beetle, not a hand. Nine-tenths of the volume in here is empty air and the remaining tenth is a census that counts to zero and has never had to be revised.

## The Lineage of Shapes

And yet the shelves are full, and this is the part where the question of who lives here stops being answerable by touch.

The tools are a population. They are born at the wall-nodes a few years apart, they are variations, and they are refined — that word is not a courtesy, they genuinely are: the needles run thickest to finest in a graded set, the adze has a bevel that has been getting better, the mallet's head is *bound*, which is to say the plant has independently arrived at the idea of two materials fastened together by a third, having never in its existence needed to fasten anything to anything.

Selection requires an environment. There is no environment. Nothing here has ever tested a chisel. What the shapes are converging on is not present and never has been, and they are converging on it anyway, correctly, at speed — because the pressures inside a body that grows in cords and folds and rings happen to be arithmetically similar to the pressures that produced, somewhere else entirely, in the wet, a hand.

So there is a thing living here that is not an organism. It is a solution. It has a lineage, it varies, it improves, it propagates, it occupies the shelves. It is the shape of a grip, alive in the way a proof is alive when someone keeps rediscovering it, except no one is rediscovering it. Above the ceiling the same gesture is being made twice: many thousands of seeds, rattling, in a body with no water to give them and nowhere to put them down. Perfect preparation. No occasion. The host's entire character in one sentence: it produces complete answers to questions that have not been asked, and it produces them well, and it produces them forever.

## The Tenant

The shelves are at the height a hand reaches at rest. The treads are dished where feet would have fallen. Nine of them, and the dishing is deepest in the middle of each, exactly as if.

A fit is a relation, and a relation has two ends. One end of every relation in this room is standing here holding a chisel that fits, and the other end has been vacant since the beginning and is not vacant in the way an empty room is vacant. It is vacant the way a place is kept. The town is a household whose resident has always been out, and the tools have been set down where he can find them, and the flues have been left open, and the bench runs along the inner curve where he liked it, and the drone has gone on so that he will know what month it is when he comes in.

He is the inhabitant. He does not exist. He is the most thoroughly accommodated thing in the chamber.

And if you stay — if you sit on the bench a long while and then longer — you will be domed in resin, then walled, then in some number of centuries you will come out as a shelf at exactly the height a hand reaches at rest. That is the only citizenship on offer here. Not burial. Fitting-out. You would be furniture for the one who was expected, and you would be good furniture, worn smooth, well placed, and you would not be a record of yourself in any way that anyone could read.

## Who Lives Here

The grazer, which is a tenant and does not use the town.

Its habit, which is a culture and is stored in the flooring rather than in any head.

The fit, which is a promise made by a body that cannot make promises, kept immaculately.

And the note. Consider it last, because it is the only continuous individual in the place. The grazers are a series. The tools are a series. The walls are renewed at very nearly the rate they wear, which is a series too, held at balance. The note has not stopped. It has not once been interrupted, not by a breath, not by a death, not by anything arriving, and it has been mapping this small city for its own reasons since before the city had lanes.

Go, if you can find your way. You will leave a bend in a groove where you stood, and the bend will be there in three hundred years, and it will be the only sentence anyone ever wrote in this room, and it will not be about you. It will only be a low place, collecting.
