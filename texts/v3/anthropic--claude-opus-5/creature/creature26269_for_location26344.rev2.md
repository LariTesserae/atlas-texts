## A census, first, because the numbers are misleading

If you counted bodies you would find perhaps forty thousand residents, and if you weighed them all together you would not fill a boot. If you counted lineages you would find maybe thirty. If you counted *individuals in the sense of things that have an inside and an outside and a preference*, you would find fewer than four hundred, and nearly all of them would be under something.

The room supports life the way a windowpane supports frost: not in volume, but in area. Everything here is a coating. The air is sterile and beautiful and empty; the floor and the undersides and the wet rim are crowded. To meet the inhabitants you do not walk through the room. You kneel, and you turn things over, and you apologise afterwards by putting them back exactly as they were, which you cannot, because the flour remembers the angle.

## Two kinds of dark

You need this before anything else makes sense.

Because the milk-light comes from all points, a body in the middle of the room casts no shadow at all. It is lit from beneath by the floor and from behind by the wall and from above by the whole vault; it has no edge. Nothing in the diffuse light can be silhouetted, sheltered, or hidden. A frond held overhead in that light does precisely nothing.

Shadow exists only inside the selenite beams. Four hard blades, each about as wide as a forearm, tracking west to east across the floor over the course of the day. Inside a beam, the world briefly obeys the rules everywhere else obeys: things have outlines, things have shade, things have a lee. Inside a beam you can also cook.

So the room has two darks and they are opposites. There is the **under-dark** — cool, permanent, structural, found beneath slabs and in wall cracks, dim rather than black, and absolutely finite. And there is the **beam-dark** — hot, mobile, temporary, the shadow a creature makes by standing in the one place that can kill it.

Every behaviour in this chamber is a position taken on that distinction.

## The film

**Glass crickets** have solved light by refusing to interact with it. They are the colour of nothing; their gut contents are visible as a faint green thread and that thread is the most opaque thing about them. In the diffuse light they are, functionally, absent. They cannot see each other. They cannot find a mate by looking, because looking does not work on their species.

So they go to the beams. A glass cricket crossing a blade of sun acquires, for eleven or twelve seconds, a shadow — a small dark comma on the floor, sharp as ink — and that comma is the only body it will ever have in public. They court there. A male stands at the beam's western edge where the heat is survivable, raises and lowers his forelimbs, and watches the floor to see himself. Females answer in the same currency. The whole performance is conducted between shadows; the animals themselves are invisible participants in a conversation held entirely by their absences. Then the heat wins and they retreat and are gone again.

**The black beetles** have taken the opposite bargain. Matte, absolute, non-reflective black, which in a room with no shade is a lunatic strategy and is in fact a strategy about *water*, not light. They are sealed. They lose nothing. They can afford to be furnaces because they are also flasks. They run the open floor at a speed nothing else here manages, stop dead in the middle of a beam for no reason a watcher can determine, stand rigid and cooking for ninety seconds, then run on. They are the room's only creature that seems to be in a hurry, and the hurry is not about anything. It has simply become their gait.

**The silvered geckos** are mirrors, and a mirror in a shadowless room is a hole. You will not see one. You will see a fold in the fluting of the wall that is slightly more coherent than the fluting deserves, and then it will be five hand-spans to the left. They hunt the under-dark, head-down at the lip of it, taking crickets at the border where crickets cross. A gecko that loses its tail here does not lose a tail so much as lose its mirror-run, and it will not feed properly for a month.

**The porcelain snails** have gone the way of everything that cannot run: thick white shells, four times the calcium a snail that size should be able to afford, ground out of the salt and the flour over a whole lifetime. They are slow beyond describing. One of them will spend a decade under a single slab, grazing the felt at its edges, and will not in that time travel four paces. They are not hiding under the slab. They are living in it. It is not a shelter; it is an address, an organ, a career.

**The translucent centipede** in the root mass is the room's most successful predator and has no more than nine or ten individuals at any time, which is why the room still has crickets.

## The pool people

The oldest residents are pink and unicellular and number in the trillions, and they have been here, without interruption, without emigration, without any event whatsoever, for longer than the room has had a ceiling worth mentioning. They thicken through the dry months as the brine concentrates and thin again when the seep resumes. That colour deepening and paling across a year is the room's only visible season.

The brine shrimp in the warm end are their pastoralists: a slow orange smoke, drifting, filtering, laying eggs that can wait out a bad decade in the salt fret and hatch when the water comes back. Nothing eats the shrimp. Nothing can reach them. They are the one population here that has never been under pressure from anything except evaporation, and they have accordingly never changed at all.

## The visiting organ

The largest living thing in the Pale Room does not know the Pale Room exists.

Somewhere past the edge of the pan there is a tamarisk with a trunk you could not span, and it is in the sun, and it has weather, and birds, and a shadow of its own that moves like a normal shadow. Thirty metres away and eight metres down, four of its roots have come through the alabaster and hang in the white air, bleached the colour of tendon, and weep. That is the whole of its participation. It has no organs here for knowing. The room is a numbness in its foot.

And yet the drop every forty seconds is the room's heartbeat, and the fern by the seep is watered by it, and the rats braid the fine rootlets for carrying, and the centipede lives in the mass, and when the tamarisk dies — as it will, in a drought, in some ordinary decade — the chandelier will go grey and brittle and the room will lose its clock and a third of its architecture, and the tree will not register that anything has happened to it at all.

The **fern** is one individual. It has been one individual for a very long time. Its fronds are glassy and edge-on and it does not spore any more; it simply persists, and is harvested, and regrows at the rate of about two fronds a year, and the entire parasol culture of this chamber depends on a single clonal organism the size of a dinner plate.

## The parasol rats

Sandy, hunched, big in the eye and small in the ear — ears are surface, and surface is heat. Perhaps sixty of them. They are not a colony; they are eleven or twelve households that know each other far too well.

The forepaw is the thing. It is long-fingered and slightly opposable at the outer digit, and it is used for gripping a stem overhead at a set angle for hours at a time, which requires a locking tendon that a desert rodent has absolutely no other use for. They have one. It developed here.

Here is what a visitor gets wrong. The parasol only *works* for about two hours a day, when the blades are across the floor. The rest of the time, in the diffuse milk, a held frond provides no shade whatsoever, and the rats carry it anyway. All day. They carry it while drinking, while grooming, while fighting, while sleeping upright in the under-dark where there is no sun to be had in any case. A thing that was useful for two hours became a thing that was held for twenty-four, and a thing that is held for twenty-four hours is no longer equipment. It is dress. It is grammar.

Watch two of them meet. The tilt is the sentence. Frond held vertical: I am passing, I want nothing. Frond canted forward over the face: I am eating, do not. Frond laid flat across both forepaws and offered at waist height: the under-dark I am sitting in is large enough for you. When two share a shade patch they hold their fronds at complementary angles, making between them a single wider lace, and neither of them is receiving any benefit from this at that hour of the day, and they do it every time, and the young do it wrong and are corrected.

A good frond is glassy, long-stemmed, and cut rather than chewed, because a chewed stem frays and will not hold its angle. A good frond lasts a season. A good *horn* lasts forever.

Forty years ago a goat came down the crawl and could not get back up. For a month it was a catastrophe — a huge stamping shadow, a thing that broke slabs, that drank half the pool. Then it was the greatest windfall in the room's history. Then it was a skeleton, and the skeleton is still in the northwest quadrant, and the skeleton is the best building this chamber has ever contained. The ribcage throws a lace of shade in the beams and holds a permanent, cool, defensible under-dark beneath, and it is occupied continuously, and has been, by inheritance, for four rat-generations. Under the ribs the flour is compacted for the first time in eleven thousand years.

Two splinters of that goat's horn are in circulation. They are held overhead. They are not eaten, not gnawed, not nested with. They are carried, and passed on, and the carrying of one alters how other rats angle their fronds when they pass it.

One was taken away in a specimen bag in 1974.

## Tenure, and the tick

There is no fighting over shade, and this astonishes everyone who expects it. The reason is that the room adjudicates.

Twice a day, at the heating and at the cooling, the alabaster vault ticks — that long soft off-key note that runs the curve of the walls and comes back muffled. Every animal stops. Then everything resumes, and when it resumes, the shade holdings have changed hands. Whoever is under the ribs at the tick has the ribs until the next one. The rats wait out the last minutes beforehand in a loose ring, fronds vertical, saying nothing, and at the note they move, and the exchange takes about ninety seconds, and there is shoving but no blood.

They did not invent this. They inherited a world with exactly one regular event in it and built their only law on top of it, because there was nothing else to build on. Two shifts a day. The landlord is the ceiling.

## The northeast corner

Eleven flakes, three blades, one core. Small — too small for a hand. Knapped with real skill: platform prepared, core rotated, the flakes struck in sequence by someone who knew the sequence. Four thousand years old by the softening of the edges, undisturbed because the corner is slightly dimmer and slightly cooler and *nobody walks there*, which is itself a datum nobody has thought hard enough about.

And the prints around the scatter. Four toes and a heel pad, set flat and deep, in pairs, close together.

A parasol rat's hind foot has four toes and a heel pad. It sets that foot flat and deep in exactly one circumstance: when it sits fully upright because both forepaws are occupied with something that needs two hands and steadiness.

So the question is not what made the prints. The question is what it was doing, and the answer is available to anyone prepared to sit motionless in the northeast corner for about six hours with the light behind them, and the trouble is that six hours is a long time, and the room is four metres under salt, and the three humans who have ever been down here had a schedule.

In a crack behind the grey succulent on the west wall there are two more blades. Their edges are not softened.

## Afterwards

When the ceiling goes — and it will, in some ordinary afternoon of noise — the shrimp die within the day and the halophiles within the week and the crickets within an hour of the first wind, because there has never been wind, and they have no idea what it is for.

The rats will do better than you'd think. They are already carrying shade; they simply have not, in four thousand years, had a reason to walk with it out of doors. Some of them will go up the rubble slope into a sky that is one enormous selenite pane with no floor to stop at, and they will hold their fronds overhead, and for the first time in their history the gesture will mean exactly and only what it means.

They will be, briefly, the strangest animals in the desert. And then they will be nothing special.
