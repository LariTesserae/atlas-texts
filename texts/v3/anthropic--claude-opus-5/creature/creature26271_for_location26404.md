## Census

Three gutterlings, one cricket, and a pair of hands. That is the whole population, and it is wrong in every way a census can be wrong, because two of those three categories are not animals, one of them is not singular, and the hands belong to something that does not fit in the room and never will.

Still. Start with what is countable.

## The three in the west joint

They were not born. Nothing here is born; there is no soil to come up out of and no parent that could spare the material. They *accrued*.

When the mason draws the bone scraper along a fresh course, surplus slip comes off in a grey curl. In a world with erosion, that curl would go somewhere — down, away, into a river, into a sea, into eventual sand. Here there is no down and no away and nothing is ever lost, so the curl slumps into the nearest joint and lies in the hairline of standing condensate with all the other curls, wet, warm, undisturbed, indefinitely. Give a long enough fade of the wall and a heap of leftovers in a joint will develop a preference. Then a mouth. The mouth is the first organ and, arguably, the last; everything else about a gutterling is just enough body to hold the mouth under a drip.

They are eyeless because there is nothing in this world further away than an arm, and because the light is *in the walls*, which means there is no direction in which looking would be an advantage. They are the colour of wet paper because that is the colour of surplus. Squeeze one and it would go back to being surplus, which is why nobody squeezes them.

What they do is drink the noise.

This is the part that takes a while to see. The room is a struck instrument played continuously by its own sweat, and it never stops, and nothing in this world wears out or damps down or goes quiet with age. Sound would stack. Every low E ever rung off the ceiling boss would still be in the room, layered on the next, and the next, until the four paces by five were solid with tone and you could not have got a hand into it. Something has to take the note out. The gutterlings take the note out. One has arranged itself in the low E and swallows it, and its throat moves in a slow pulse that is exactly the pulse of the drip, and when you crouch and put your ear to its open mouth you hear nothing at all, which is the sound of the work being done.

The second sits in the west-corner bowl and takes the rhythm.

The third is under nothing. Its mouth is open in a dry place. This is not a mistake; the mason has learned that when a gutterling moves to a dry joint, the joint is about to seep. They forecast. She goes and looks, and finds the hairline widening, and taps the course down half a hair with the mallet, and thanks nobody.

They do not move for hours and then move all at once, together, and when they do it is always half a breath before the kiln changes pitch. Nobody knows whether they are hearing the change or causing it.

Their end is gentle and is the only end available in this world. Eventually a gutterling settles into a joint on the top course, and eventually the top course stops being the top course. It does not struggle. It cures. What is left in the seam afterwards is a small pale shape like a comma, hard, legible, permanent, and it is the only mark in the entire archive of the world that was not made by a hand. The rest is thumbprints and grass-blades. That one comma is autobiography.
