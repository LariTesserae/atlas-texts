They have no name for themselves and never needed one. There is no other kind of place, and therefore nothing to distinguish themselves from. The room is the extent of what exists; the walls are the edge of the possible; the hatch is a source of light in the same category as the lamp, i.e. a property of the ceiling.

What follows is an account of the residents, sorted roughly by how much of the room's substance each of them has eaten.

---

**The crust.**

The grazers are the room, in the sense that nine-tenths of the room's mass that is not steel or flour is grazer, living or shed. Adult, they run about four centimeters, oval, eleven dorsal plates overlapping front-to-back like roof shingles, in a nickel grey that darkens with each molt so that an old one is nearly black and dulled to felt at the rim. Underneath: the pad. It is most of what they are. Eighty percent of the animal's interior is devoted to servicing that soft grey disc on its belly — a chemistry bed, more mineral than meat, that must be kept flat, kept clean, and kept in contact.

They do not eat. Eating is not the verb. They *hold*. A grazer in good position presses its pad to the bloom and does not move for twenty, thirty, forty hours, and in that time the potential crosses its belly and it pries oxygen off iron and lays down a little more of itself. Success here is measured entirely in stillness. The great ones — the ones at the center, on the split in the insulation — are recognizable because they are the only animals in the room that are never observed to travel. They arrive once and they are thereafter a feature of the landscape, crawled over, stridulated at, built upon.

Because they are built upon. The crust is four and five deep and the depth is not crowding, it is plumbing. Current that enters the bottom animal passes out through its dorsal plates at a fraction — a tenth, a twelfth — and the animal stacked on its back takes that, and passes on a fraction of the fraction, and the fourth animal up is living on a rumor. It is a bad living and they take it, because the alternative is the floor. And the bottom animal permits it, because a grazer alone on the wall can be sprung off in a second by anything with leverage, and a grazer under four others is a geological feature. The stack holds itself on by its own weight. Everyone in it is underfed and immovable, which in this room is a life.

The center of the bloom is also the only fertile place in the world. Below a certain lifetime intake, a grazer simply persists. Above it, at the end of a long hold, it undergoes a last molt into something swollen and immobile and no longer able to file, which presses itself flat to the metal and converts its entire surplus into a pale grey pad of young that hardens, flakes, and comes apart into a few hundred flakes the size of a grain of rice. So the hierarchy is not a metaphor. The difference between the first millimeter and the twentieth is the difference between having descendants and not. The margin does not breed. The margin waits, and files, and runs down on a gradient of millivolts, and its contribution to the future is to become floor.

Molting is the other crisis, and it is the reason the drifts along the baseboards are deeper than anywhere else. You cannot molt on the wall. You must let go, walk down, go into the loose flour, split along the anterior seam and lie soft for nine or ten hours, and soft is the only condition in which the openers can reach you easily. They all go to the same drifts. They have gone to the same drifts for so long that those drifts are no longer flour in the general sense but pure molt, a fine white-grey down of nothing but discarded selves, six and seven centimeters deep at the wall, and a molting animal buries itself in the shed skins of every ancestor it has and is sometimes eaten there, and the pieces of it settle in with the rest. The nursery, the graveyard and the soil are one location and always have been.

---

**The eight seconds.**

Every forty-one hours the relay closes, and the animals know before the light does.

The current arrives at the bloom instantly. The filament in the caged fixture needs about eight seconds to come up from coal to yellow. In those eight seconds the crust has already begun to move: the center animals feel the surge across their pads and lift their file-rate, and the ring behind them hears it and breaks position, and by the time the lamp is actually brighter the wall is in full boil and the margins are climbing over each other to get in. Nothing in the room navigates by the lamp. The lamp is a confirmation. They navigate by the wall itself.

Eleven minutes. For eleven minutes the gradient is fat enough that even the fourth animal in a stack is genuinely fed, and the roar comes up — thousands of files at once, that applause-through-a-floor sound — and then the relay opens and the potential collapses and there is a short, extremely violent reorganization in which position is re-established, which is where most of the forty-shrieks-a-minute come from in the hours after a tide.

There is a second clock and it does not agree with the first. The trapezoid of daylight crosses the floor once a day, and where it lies, it warms, and warmth dries a pad, and a dried pad loses contact. An animal that crosses the light in the wrong hour can lose one or two tides' feeding to a stiffened belly, which at the margin is fatal. So the light is weather. The lanes bend around it. Every so often — every twenty-odd days — the tide falls in the early afternoon, when the trapezoid lies directly over the southern approach to the east wall, and then the whole southern population has to make its migration through the heat or not at all. Those are the bad tides, and they are followed by a week of unusually good living for everyone who stayed.

A lineage on the south side has, over an unnumberable period, developed a thicker keratinous rim around the pad. They feed slightly worse than everyone else and they can cross the light at noon. There are perhaps nine hundred of them. They are the only population in the room that has ever gone somewhere new.

---

**The openers.**

Longer than a grazer, half the width, pale to the point of translucency — there is nothing in the diet to make a color out of — and built entirely around two forelimbs that do one job. They cannot cut a grazer. They cannot crack a plate. They can only get the tip of a claw into the gap at the front of the third or fourth shingle, where the overlap runs the wrong way, and spring it, and this only works on an animal that has lifted from the wall. So they do not hunt the crust. They hunt travel: the migration, the molt, the resort.

They are blind and always have been. What they have instead is a sense for the field — they read the potential gradient across the room, and they know where the bloom is the way you know where the floor is. Which is why they will not approach the wall. Within a meter of the bloom their sense saturates; they are walking into a light so bright it is a wall of white, and they will not do it. The crust feeds inside a blind spot. This is the single most important fact about the geometry of the room and neither species is in any way aware of it.

Hunting is done by absence. An opener cannot file — it has no files — and so in a room where every animal is ticking, an opener is a moving hole in the sizzle, and the crust's waves exist to find it: a wavefront passes, everything answers, and the shape of the gap in the answer is a predator's position, broadcast by everyone at once, four or five times a minute. Meanwhile the opener is listening for the reciprocal: a stationary hole. A grazer that has stopped filing has stopped because it is molting, or hurt, or dying, or bluffing.

Bluffing is common. A weak animal will hold a file-rate it cannot afford, faking the tempo of a well-fed neighbor to keep a position it should have lost, and it will run itself down doing it, and the rate will falter, and the room will hear the falter, and something will come. It is a world in which every citizen continuously announces its own solvency and is eaten the moment the announcement lapses.

---

**The riders.**

A paring of an animal, flat as a fingernail clipping, clamped along the dorsal midline of an opener at the one point the opener's limbs cannot reach. They do not eat their host, not exactly. They eat first. When the opener springs a plate, the riders are down the flank and into the opening before the opener's own mouthparts arrive, and they take the best of it, and the opener must then hunt again sooner. Three riders will roughly double a predator's kill rate and halve its life.

When a ridden opener finally dies, the riders walk off it, go to the packed lane, find a scratch or a seam or a ridge of hard flour, and take up a posture: forelimbs raised, still, silent, waiting for something fast to pass close enough. They can hold that posture for a very long time. In the busiest lane, past the tally on the north wall, there are riders in the grooves of the scratches who have been standing there in that position for eleven tides.

---

**The mill.**

The most numerous animals in the world are soft, blind, pale, eyeless grubs about the size of a comma, and they live in the top two centimeters of the flour, and they are the reason the flour is faintly crunchy. They eat it. There is still metal in a powdered husk — a half-percent, a trace — and they run their entire economy on that half-percent, which means they must process a staggering volume, which they do, endlessly, pelleting it as they go. They loosen the lanes; traffic packs the lanes; the packing pushes them down; they work up again. Every step taken in this room is taken on their castings.

Everything eats them, casually, without hunting them, the way you'd pick something up off a table.

Below their two centimeters the flour is sterile and compacted and historical. No animal goes there. It is stratified — you could read tides in it if anything could read — and at the bottom, under six or seven centimeters of powdered ancestry, the checker plate is still perfect. The diamonds are intact down there. They are simply buried.

---

**The quick.**

In the cavity of a fresh grazer husk, before the powdering, there is a world that lasts about two tides. Something gets in — tiny, orange-brown, fast — breeds inside the carcass, fills it, and sends out a generation that must run across open flour to find another corpse before the one it was born in collapses. Most die on the way. Their population triples in the six hours after every tide, when the resort has killed a few thousand, and crashes in the long dry stretch before the next one. They are the only animals in the room for whom the forty-one hour cycle is not a tide but a lifetime.

---

**The dish.**

Behind the conduit, in the northwest corner where nothing walks, on the last two square handspans of un-worn checker plate, there is a plated, dish-shaped animal thirty centimeters across that has not moved in nine years.

It is not a grazer, or it is what a grazer becomes if it is never allowed to stop. Its rim curls down and seats itself against the floor all the way around, and under it, pressed flat to the raised diamonds, is a pad the size of a saucer. It does not feed on the bloom. It feeds on the floor: the whole plate is a conductor, and the whole plate carries a thin residual gradient, a few millivolts leaking away from the bus bar into the structure of the building, and the dish takes it at the rate the floor will give it, which is roughly one grazer-tide per eight months. It is doing nothing wrong. It is simply eating something very thin, very slowly, with a very large mouth.

It cannot file and it cannot hear. It is a permanent silence in an acoustic world, and a permanent silence is not information — it is a hole that never moves, which is to say it is indistinguishable from architecture, which is why nothing in the room has any business with it. The waves break around it. The flour has drifted up its windward side into a smooth grey dune. Generations of animals have been born and died within forty centimeters of it without once, in any meaningful sense, encountering it.

It has now exhausted the patch of floor beneath itself and in some season not far off it will move — twenty centimeters, perhaps, over the course of a night. The dune will collapse. Six centimeters of sterile stratified flour will slump and turn over and expose metal that has not been touched by anything in longer than the crust has existed. There will be a bloom of fresh oxide, and a scatter of buried husks with their half-percent intact, and the mill will go into the new ground like a tide coming in, and the openers will follow the mill, and the northern lanes will re-route, and for about a week the room will be a different room.

And under where it sat there will be a clean disc of checker plate, diamonds sharp, un-walked, which will begin — immediately, and at the usual rate — to be worn down.

---

**The seam.**

In the welded door, in a crack two millimeters wide between the paint-mineral and the steel, there is a nation of a few hundred that has not exchanged an animal with the room in an unguessable number of generations. They are longer than anything outside, and whiter, and slower, and their mouthparts are wrong — they are not built for prying or filing but for scraping, because they do not eat metal or meat or flour. They eat the pigment. There is zinc and lead and chromate in the old paint, and they take it off the seam a few molecules at a time, and it is enough.

They do not feel the tide. They have no use for the bloom. They are the only inhabitants of this world who are not, in any way, fed by the relay.

Which means that on the day it stops closing — and it will stop — and the lamp goes down through coal to nothing, and the crust comes off the east wall over about nine days in a silence that gets worse as it goes, and the openers hunt the last travelers and then each other and then stand still, and the mill has no new flour and finishes the old, and the quick find no more corpses and end in a single generation about fifty hours after the last shriek —

there will still be, in the seam of a door that has not opened since before any of this began, a few hundred pale things in total darkness, scraping paint, at the same rate as always, for another several years. They will not know anything has happened. Nothing will have happened, to them.

Then they will come to the end of the pigment, which was always finite, and that will be the last of it: the flat grey plain, three and a half meters corner to corner, six centimeters of shed selves packed hard along lanes that no longer lead anywhere, forty-one scratches nobody counted, and the brown ceramic insulator lying on its side and shining for two hours a day.
