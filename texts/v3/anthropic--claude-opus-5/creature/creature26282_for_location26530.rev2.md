# The Shade-Keepers

## The Eyes

Begin with the eyes, because everything about these people is a consequence of the eyes.

The pupil is pinned. Not contracted — *pinned*, held down at a pinprick from birth to death by a musculature that has forgotten the other instruction. The iris is dense and dark and very thick. The lids are heavier than yours, fleshy at the rim, and carry a double fringe of lash that mats together with root-pulp and is not cleaned, because the matting is the point. By twenty, most have a pale wedge of scar growing in from the inner corner of each eye, a slow white tide coming across the cornea. By fifty, for most, it has met in the middle.

So the old are blind, and the old lead.

This is the thing that surprises outsiders, if there were outsiders. Blindness here is not a catastrophe; it is a graduation. Everything that prospers under the Lid is eyeless — the gnats, the grazers, the ribbon-things, the walkers. The people are the only creatures in the underlight still attempting to see, and they are losing, and they have built their entire competence on the assumption that they will lose. A young guide with good sight is not trusted with a long crossing. A blind grandmother is. She was never using her eyes for it anyway.

## What They Are Made Of

They are short, thick through the trunk, deep-chested, with a layer of fat over the shoulders and flanks that is considered beautiful and is also the whole of their insulation. Their hands are wide and their fingertips are extraordinary — calloused hard on the pads but soft and wet at the very tip, and they will pull a glove off in a cellar-cold to touch something properly, the way you would take off sunglasses.

They are silver. In the morning, before a walk, a person peels the mirror-cuticle from a cane in long sheets, chews the edges soft, and presses it onto the skin — the backs of the hands, the cheekbones, the nose, the shoulders, anywhere the glare stands on you. It curls and drops off within a day or two. A properly kept person shines. A person too long alone goes dull, patchy, tacky-grey, and burnt scarlet underneath in the gaps, and the word for that state is used also for a house with a hole in the roof.

There is etiquette in it. A silvered body throws light, and to walk on the wrong side of a companion — to stand where your shoulder bounces the glare into the slits of their mask — is a real discourtesy, the equivalent of talking over someone. Children are taught angles before they are taught knots.

And they smell. There is no delicate way around it: they sleep by cutting into the hot rotting mat and lying down inside it, and the smell of the deep floor gets into the hair and the seams of the skin and never entirely leaves. Sour, warm, yeasty, faintly like bread and faintly like a wound. To them it is the smell of safety and of other people. It is very close to the smell of the dead.

## How They Know Where They Are

Not by looking. Looking gives them a flat white disc with a dark grain in it and no distance whatsoever.

They know where they are by heat. The mat ferments unevenly; there are warm reaches and cold ones, hot seams, dead patches, slow boils that persist for decades. A route is a sequence of temperatures. You walk with a bare hand trailing, or you stop and push an arm in to the elbow, and you come up with a number you have no word for but can sing. Route-songs are long, dull to the untrained ear, and consist almost entirely of warmths in order, with the rift-lines and the drip-noise of distant cane-masses threaded through as cross-checks.

And by ear. A skilled walker can hear a cane-mass at ten kilometres — not as sound exactly, but as a change in the texture of the silence, the tick and tock of ceiling-drip on mirror-leaf arriving at a rate that tells them how big it is, how wet, how close to its own collapse. They can hear an open rift ahead as a flatness, a place where the quiet has no fur on it.

They cannot, by any means, judge the distance to a thing they can see. This is worth sitting with. They only learn how far away something is when it is destroyed: hand flat in the mat, feel the tremor of a collapse come up through the floor, then count — one, two, twelve, forty — until the noise arrives overhead like a door closing in a house the size of a continent. Their single instrument for measuring the world is the world breaking.

## The Little Room

Nothing here casts a shadow, including a person. This is the deep wound of the place, and they have made a solution out of it.

When two travellers meet on the plain — and this may be the only meeting either has in a year — they stop about four paces apart and set down their staffs. Then they kneel, and lean in, and put their foreheads together, and pull the hood of one cloak over both heads. Inside that tent of two skulls there is, briefly, darkness: a cave about the size of two cupped hands, warm, smelling of ferment and breath, in which each can open their eyes and see a face. This is called making the little room. It lasts perhaps twenty seconds. It is how you say hello, and how you say goodbye, and how you propose marriage, and how you tell someone that a third person has died. Everything is in it.

Outside the little room, the face is not a communicative organ. It is masked, it is unreadable, it is irrelevant. Expression has migrated into the hands and into the pitch of a very quiet voice, and into a tap-code run out on the shaft of a staff or on a companion's forearm — names, warnings, numbers, jokes. Two people can walk eight hours side by side and converse the entire way without either turning a head.

## Faces, and the Naming

A child is born on the plain or in the burrow, in glare or in blackness, and either way no one sees it. Faces belong indoors.

The drum is entered through a crooked passage, on hands and knees, and the first thing that happens is the closing of the flap behind you, and the second thing is the pain — because your pinned pupil does not know how to open and it hurts, genuinely hurts, like a cramp behind the eyes, and the darkness is not restful for the first quarter of an hour, it is work. Then it opens. Then there are faces.

Somewhere in the first year, in a dark room, an adult holds the child up and looks at it for as long as they need, and the child is named for what is in its face. The names are therefore descriptive and slightly absurd and often unkind: Wide-Set. Two Teeth Coming. Frowner. The name is a report from the only place in the world where anyone can be seen.

Grown people, inside, stare. It is a luxury and an intimacy and nobody considers it rude. Blind elders ask to be told, in detail, who has come in and what their face is doing, and are answered patiently, at length, because describing a face aloud is one of the two or three things these people consider an art.

## The Dead

They are laid in. Cut a shaft two metres down into the hot mat, put the body in, seal the fibre over.

A body makes heat. For eight or nine years, sometimes twelve, the ground above a laying-in runs a degree or two warmer than the country around it, and that warmth goes into the route-songs as a named waypoint: *my mother, then cold for a day, then the sour boil, then my mother's brother.* Navigation is genealogy. You cross the emptiness by the warmth of the people who used to carry you.

And then it fades. The warmth thins out, becomes doubtful, becomes argument between two travellers with their arms in the floor, and then it is gone, and the name comes out of the song. A person is remembered for exactly as long as they are warm. Nobody finds this sad. What is sad, what is unbearable to them, is the other kind of ending: the one who unmasks.

It happens. A few times in a generation, someone sets down the staff, takes the slit-mask off, and walks out into the white. They do not answer. Nobody follows, because to follow is to lose your own bearings and die for nothing. The walker goes on until the dazzle takes the eyes and the cold takes the rest, and they lie down somewhere in two hundred kilometres of shining nothing, and they are never found, and they never become a warm place. Their name is taken out of the songs the same day. There is no waypoint. They are the only people here who can be genuinely lost.

## The Others

There is talk — the way there is talk of walkers — of people who went the other way. Who moved *into* a cane-mass and stayed: who live inside the hall of mirrors with their eyes gone to milk in a single season, navigating the chrome by drip-echo and by the heat on their skin, eating well, eating constantly, in the one place in this world where there is abundance. They are said to have no dark rooms. They are said to have let their dark rooms fall.

The Shade-Keepers do not call them wicked. They call them *fed*, and say it the way you might say *drowned*.

## The Debt

Someone in every line keeps the rod: a length of cured cane notched once a season, passed on, repaired, re-carved when it rots, which is often. No two rods agree. The counts run from four hundred to eleven thousand and every reconciliation between two families is a quarrel that lasts the whole of a visit.

What the numbers are for is this: above the Lid, the sun goes round and low, and one day it will not come back, and the ice will go dim, then green, then black. The canes will stop their frantic climbing. The ceiling will stop weeping. The whole world will become, for a long season, the inside of a drum.

They do not dread it. They tilt their heads down under an inferno of light with frost on their sleeves, and they maintain a scatter of small lightless rooms across a space large enough to hold its own weather, and they will not let one collapse, ever, whosever it was.

They are practising.
