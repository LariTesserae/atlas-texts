## The Tenders

You will not see them at first. They are the color of the place — lime-pale on the forearms, green in the creases, hair kept short and stiff with mineral so it does not soak — and they do not look up when someone new arrives, because no one new has ever arrived. You came up the last ladder. The last ladder comes from Course Ninety, which is drowning. So either you are one of them, or you are a thing the fog does.

There are sixty-one. The ledger is precise about this and considers it a load-bearing number: the population may never exceed what a single season's carry can lift onto the next course. Fifty-five who work, four in the basket, two who are past carrying and have become instruments instead.

---

**Their bodies.** Short — the tallest man is a hand under five feet — and deep through the chest, so that in silhouette they seem to be leaning forward even standing still. The ribs flare. The collarbones sit high and wide, like the arms of a yoke. They breathe in a way you will find unnerving: a fast, shallow, continuous sipping, three breaths to your one, so that a tender at rest looks faintly agitated and a tender working looks calm. Their lips are dark. Their nail beds are dark. When they exert themselves the flush comes up not in the face but in the hands and the tops of the feet, which go a deep plum, and they will hold a hand up to check its color the way you would check a watch.

Nothing dries here, so they wear almost nothing: an oiled apron of bean-fiber cloth, a wrap at the hips, and on the left wrist a thong of coir with a punch on it. Skin is dressed daily with fig-seed oil and rendered salamander fat against the rot that otherwise takes the folds of the body. They smell like the stone smells — pondweed and struck flint — and beneath that, of hot brick and lime and their own oil. In fog thick enough to hide a person at four paces they identify each other by smell, unerringly, and think nothing of it.

Their feet are the most worked part of them. Soles like horn, cut and split and re-split by ninety-one courses of arrises that have never been walked soft. In the deep cracks of the heel, and in the webs between the toes, and in the knuckles of the old, there is green. It is the quickener. Nobody says out loud that the drains' moss likes them. But the oldest woman on the terrace, who has been at the fleece-combs for thirty years, has fingertips that are pale and slightly translucent and ring — a small dry chime — when she taps the ledger table to indicate a line, and no one thinks this is a disease, and no one thinks it is a miracle, and no one will discuss it with a stranger.

**Their eyes.** Pale-lashed, narrow, permanently squinted against the white. They see beautifully at arm's length and past thirty paces they see almost nothing, and this is not a defect here, because there is nothing at thirty paces except the parapet and the drop and the cloud. They have no practiced sense of distance. Ask a tender how far away a thing is and she will tell you how many steps, and the steps will be exact, and if the thing cannot be walked to she will look at you with polite incomprehension. None of them has ever seen this place from outside. There is no outside from which it could be seen. The Increment has no shape to them; it has only a plan, drawn flat, redrawn every course, filed.

**Their hands.** Read the left thumb. It is the greeting: the hand comes up, palm in, thumb out, and you look. Every tender has a history hatched into the pad of the left thumb by the fresh edges of new blocks — three or four cuts a course, a lifetime's tally, going white and ropey with age. A child's thumb is smooth and this is what a child *is*. A caster's thumb is a barcode. The two who are past carrying have thumbs so scarred the whorls are gone, and when they hold them up the gesture is understood as a claim, and the claim is honored.

The fingertips of the casting-bay hands are stiff and dead from lime. They cannot feel a thread or a seed. So they judge the slurry by ear — by the pitch of the paddle in it, by the sound it makes falling back on itself, by the way it climbs a stick and hesitates — and the bay-hands are, functionally, musicians. Their apprentices spend a year doing nothing but listening to buckets.

**Their names and their marks.** A tender is named for the course she was born on and for her punch. A course takes about two years, so the name locates you to within an arm's length of stone, and there are usually three or four people who share it, and the punch tells them apart.

The punch is a bronze die on the wrist-thong. It is struck into every block its owner helps pour, in the bottom corner, where the block will sit dark forever. Dot-and-comma marks are inherited within a family; the hand-signs belong to the caster lineages and are very old, five of them left of an original nine; the number-punches are given to people whose lines have ended, and they are not a shame, they are a specific and slightly formal kind of dignity, like a title. The punch is never taken off. Not to sleep, not under the rinse-comb. A person separated from their punch is spoken of as though they had been injured.

What this means is that every one of them is distributed, by weight, through the entire mountain. A tender of forty can tell you that his mark is in Course Eighty-Two some nine thousand times, and that Eighty-Two is now sixteen meters under the white, and he says it the way you might mention a parent's grave — except that it is not a grave, it is *him*, going down at a hand's width a year, forever, undecayed.

**How they speak.** Not much, and not loudly, because the vegetation eats voices and because everything else here is audible and worth hearing. They speak in a low flat register with the vowels short. Sentences are brief and the tenses are few: there is a tense for now, and a tense for what is being made, and one word that serves for *old*, *finished*, and *drowned*.

Distance talk is done by knuckle. Stone carries; the hollow warming columns carry beautifully; every column has a mouth at chest height and the whole terrace is wired for gossip through the heating system. A child learns the knock-code before she learns to be trusted near the parapet: two-and-one for *come*, a long roll for *water where it shouldn't be*, a single hard rap repeated, which stops everyone, everywhere, immediately, and means *someone has gone over*.

In the vine-rooms and the fern-dark you cannot see a person at two paces, so they announce themselves by touch — a flat hand laid on the hip or the shoulder blade as they pass, always the same weight, never the head. Outsiders would call them intimate. They would call it traffic.

They sing in the warm drains, where the acoustics are, and the songs are lists. The great song is the courses in order, ninety-one names, each with its year and its dead, and it takes about forty minutes and everyone knows it, and the children are put to sleep with the end of it, counting down and down into the white.

**Their work, in a day.** The kilns never cool, so the terrace is never empty; there has not been an hour in ninety-one courses when no one was awake. Three shifts, marked by the one unhurried bell at the bays.

The young are in the casting bays: mixing, pouring, striking forms, hauling. It is the work that shortens you — lime in the lungs, and the coughing comes in a caster's third decade, and everyone knows the sound of it and no one comments on it. As the lungs go, a tender moves outward from the bays: to the water, to the combs and cisterns and settling basins, where the labor is light and unending and the *tick, tick* of the fleece is company. As the eyes go, they move to the gardens, which are all within arm's length anyway. And at the end they become **sayers**.

**The sayers.** The two who are past carrying sit on the pavement, on folded mats, in the wet, and watch where the water goes. This is not retirement. On a terrace with no habits, water must be studied like a foreign animal: which joint it prefers, where it ponds, which corner will hold a lake for eleven hours, where the mason guessed wrong. The sayers watch a stain of damp explore a joint, hesitate, commit — and then they say where it wants to go, and the masons cut the gutter there, and the terrace slowly becomes correct. They hold in their heads the drainage of every course since their childhood, which is to say the drainage of forty meters of drowned mountain, none of which can ever be inspected again. When a sayer dies, the ledger records not only the person but a list of what was lost with them: *Eighty-Four, the north spill. Eighty-Six, the two hollows by the east lip. Eighty-Seven, all of it.*

**The brushing hour.** At dusk they pollinate by hand, because bees fail at this pressure, and this is the only time the whole waking shift does one thing together. There are forty-one brushes. Nine of them still carry true sable — an animal none of them has seen or can imagine, an animal from below, hair that can never be replaced — and those nine are used only on the fig and on the seed-beans, and they are counted out and counted back in aloud. The rest have been rehaired with human hair, the fine first cutting from children's heads, which is the reason a child's first haircut is an occasion and the reason children are proud, for one evening, of being soft.

They move down the bean frames touching flower after flower, saying nothing, and their hands are not gentle, exactly; they are exact. You will think you have walked into a rite. It is a rite the way a hinge is a rite. Both things are true and they would not understand the distinction.

**What they eat.** Beans, endlessly, in every preparation a hundred and eighty years can invent. Fern crosiers. Fig, which is sugar and therefore ceremony. Mosquito larvae, netted from the bromeliad cups by children with spoons, pressed into cakes and fried in salamander fat: this is the fat and the fine nutrition of the place and it tastes, they say, of green water.
