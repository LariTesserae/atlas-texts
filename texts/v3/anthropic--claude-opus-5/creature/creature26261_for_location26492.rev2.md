## What lives here

Begin with the honest inventory, since the place is honest about itself. There is no protein on this shelf. Nothing here eats anything else, nothing is warm, nothing has ever been born and nothing has died in the way that requires a body to stop. The wing at the west end is a visitor, and it arrived dead or died on arrival, and three hundred years of grove have grown over it without noticing.

And yet something is at work here every single morning, on a schedule, and it is not the sun and it is not the rock.

Look at the flutes. Rounded ribs, running vertically down every comb, worn and rebuilt by their own water. Those are paths. They are not decoration and they are not structure; they are the trails of something that goes the same way every day, the way a track through grass is not a property of the grass. The grove is not the inhabitant. The grove is the cast of the inhabitant — the shape it has left in the only material available, the way a shell is the shape of a mollusc and not the mollusc.

The tenant is the film.

## The body

It is a skin of water one fingernail's edge thick, and at its greatest extent it covers every lit and thawing surface of every comb on the shelf at once, which is an area perhaps four times the area of the shelf itself, since the grove folds its surface the way a lung does. That is the whole of it. It has no interior, no organ, no store. It is entirely boundary. Whatever is happening to it is happening at its outside, because it is nothing but outside.

Its motion, locally, is a hand's breadth. A film forms, carries what it carries, travels a hand's breadth down a rib, and is finished — frozen, dried, gone up. That is its top speed and its full stride. Nothing here moves further than a hand's breadth under its own arrangement.

But the boundary of its working crosses two hundred paces of shelf in four hours, west in the morning, east in the evening, and that motion is not the motion of the film. It is the shape of the film's working passed along from comb to comb, the way a wave crosses water without the water going anywhere. So the thing has two velocities, and they have nothing to do with each other, and neither of them is the velocity of an animal. This is the first clue that you are dealing with something that will not answer to the questions you have brought.

## Three bodies a day

It keeps three forms and cycles through all of them between one dawn and the next.

By night it is dispersed — barely present, a vapour thinned through cold air, occupying the whole volume of the shelf and doing nothing. Call this the absent form. You stand in the middle of it and breathe it and it is at no point anywhere in particular.

In the small hours it comes out of the air onto the ribs as hoarfrost: needles and feathers half an inch long, grown on every fluted surface, so that the grove at first light looks furred. This is the gathering form, and it is beautiful in a way that reads as deliberate, because it is doing the one thing worth doing — making surface. A feather of frost is almost all edge. It is an organ for being in contact with as much air as possible. Overnight, the tenant grows itself a coat whose only function is to touch.

Then the sun clears the rim and the coat collapses. Most of it leaves outright, straight back to vapour, hissing at the threshold of the teeth. A fraction — small, and the smallness is the whole discipline of the place — melts instead, and for one hour that fraction is liquid and mobile and carrying, and it runs a hand's breadth and sets down a layer of calcite too thin to see, and then it too is gone up into the sky. This is the working form, and it exists for roughly one hour in any given spot, and in the course of the morning that one hour is handed westward across the grove, comb by comb, so that the shelf as a whole is at work from dawn to noon while no part of it is at work for more than an hour.

Frost, film, vapour. Coat, hand, breath. It does this every clear day and has done for as long as the dust has blown.

On fog days it does none of it. The cloud comes up and swallows the shelf, and the tenant is everywhere and undivided and simply gains — no tide, no line, no hour, nothing to hear. Those are its heaviest days and its most featureless. You could call them sleep, except that sleep does not usually make you larger.

## What it eats

There is no food here. Everything the grove is made of arrived as dust on a wind out of ranges a long way south, a country the tenant will never occupy and could not survive. Its whole body of stone is another mountain, disassembled and reassembled grain by grain at forty feet of depth. It has been fed, for ten thousand years, entirely by erosion happening somewhere else.

This is worth sitting with. The creature has no reach. It cannot travel a hand's breadth beyond a rib. Everything it has ever been given was brought to it by weather, and it has never once gone and got anything, and it has nonetheless built the floor you are standing on.

## Heredity, which here is a question of address

There are hundreds of combs and they are not interchangeable. A comb that catches more fog grows faster and a comb that grows faster catches more fog, and that is a wager compounding in public. Some positions on this shelf are good — a corner where the morning line snags, a corridor that funnels, a lip that takes the up-light — and those positions have been continuously occupied since before there were people anywhere near this mountain.

When a comb goes over on its waist, the crown lies where it falls and is welded down within a few decades, and the grove starts again on top of it, in almost the same place, because the same place is where the fog goes. Every third pillar has an older pillar lying dead crossways inside it, and that dead pillar is not a relic. It is a footing, and it is also an inheritance, and it is also the reason this comb is standing exactly here and not four paces east.

So there is descent with modification on this shelf, and the thing that is inherited is location. Forty feet of interlocked pale wreckage is forty feet of pedigree. The combs standing now are the great-great-descendants of combs, in the only sense of descent the place can support: they are standing on their ancestors' shoulders because their ancestors' shoulders are what there is to stand on.

## How it dies, and how often

Always at the base, always from its own water. The film that built the crown pools at the foot and lingers there and turns faintly aggressive, and it eats a waist into the pillar a foot or two above the floor, and narrows it to the thickness of a wrist, and the comb stands like that for centuries and then gives.

The fall is nearly soundless. A dull tap, a rattle, and then that extraordinary quiet.

Every comb on this shelf dies of drinking. It is not a tragedy and nobody up here is arranged to experience it as one, but it is the one law with no exceptions: the same hour that builds the crown hollows the ankle, and the two are not in tension, they are the same event seen at two heights.

At the rim, the losses are total. Combs grow out toward the edge because the edge is where the fog arrives first, and the edge is precisely where a fall does not land on the shelf. Those go into the white and are not recovered. The tenant sheds sideways and builds upward, permanently, and has done so long enough that the shelf's outline is now its own outline.

## Its dealings with the people

They came up, at the cost of a very hard climb, to take a small amount of water that had never touched soil. The tenant did not object and could not have. What it did was more characteristic: it accepted the work.

The five pecked channels above the cistern are now flutes like any others, and the film runs them exactly as it runs the ribs, and has been laying down its fingernail of calcite in them for however many centuries, so the channels are shallower than they were cut and smoother than a hammerstone can make anything. The basin is being lined. The hundreds of overlapping bruises in it are filling, bruise by bruise, at a rate no one has ever observed. In some long time it will be a low white mound with a dimple, and then a mound.

The cord of mineral wool is half-fused into the crust, which is to say half-eaten and half-embraced; it has not rotted because nothing here rots, so the tenant has simply begun to include it. The two carved figures are combs made by hands, and they are acquiring a rind, and the rind is a comb.

It makes copies of whatever is left with it. Not out of appetite. It has one gesture and it performs that gesture on everything within a hand's breadth of a wet rib, indefinitely.

The people had a mark for the standing pillars — the mark for a thing with teeth — and no mark at all for what makes them. That may be an oversight. It may also be very exact observation: they named the part of it that holds still.

## The one animal, again

Two hundred laminae into a broken comb at the west end there is two-thirds of a wing, brown, membranous, lifted slightly by the curve of clear stone over it and lit from underneath by cloud-glare.

The tenant caught it on a wet flank on some morning three hundred years ago and did to it what it does to everything, and the result is that the only other living thing ever to reach this shelf is held permanently at eye height in the middle of the grove, in good light, uncorrupted, where it will remain until the comb goes over on its waist.

This is not reverence. It is only that here, nothing goes away. But it is hard to stand in front of and not feel that something has been kept.

## Its character, as far as that word reaches

It is punctual past any possibility of error: it starts when the shadow line arrives and stops when the line passes, and it cannot be early, and it has never once been late.

It is audible only within a few paces, because the air will not carry. During the passage of the wet line there are hundreds of overhangs dripping at once and every one of them that you can hear is near you. You cannot hear the whole of it from anywhere. There is no vantage. Whatever you get of it, you get locally, close, at conversational distance, and that is the only way it can be met.

It has an interior of a kind, and only one: the hair-thin seams where two combs have grown into each other and closed. Those few finger-widths of black never open, never dry, never take light. In a creature that is entirely surface, a seam is the nearest thing to being inside, and there are perhaps a dozen of them on this shelf, and none is wide enough to admit anything at all.

And it is not waiting. Nothing about it is provisional. It arrived at this arrangement a long time ago and it maintains it, comb by comb, waist by waist, fall by fall, and it will keep maintaining it while the dust keeps coming up from the south.

## What it is building toward, which is not a purpose

Forty feet of wreckage and gaining. All of it laid down on a shoulder of hornblende that is itself a broken piece of a larger mountain, leaning slightly outward, attached along one wall.

The shelf has a waist too. It is simply a very large one, and the water working at it is not the morning film but something slower and further in, and the arithmetic is the same arithmetic.

So the tenant will die of what its combs die of, at a tempo that makes the difference meaningless, and in the meantime it goes on making itself heavier in the one place it cannot leave.

The pieces that go over the rim fall two thousand feet through the white into a country with soil in it, and weather, and rot, and things that fly. They are not seeds. They will not do anything there. They are only the sole part of this creature that ever travels, arriving in a warm place as unremarkable pale rubble, while the wind out of the south carries that country's mountains up the other way, grain by grain, to be received on a wet rib for one hour tomorrow morning.
