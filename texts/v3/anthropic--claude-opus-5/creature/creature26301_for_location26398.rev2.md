## The Fauna of the Basin

Stand at the rim long enough and you will notice that you are watching for movement, and that you are finding it, and that all of it is people.

There is a lot of it. A quarter-hour's town, three hundred and sixty-odd souls, all of them out of doors more than you would expect of a wet place, because indoors is small and the streets are roofed by the ceiling anyway. From above they read as slow pale marks working along the seams between the roofs. They do not hurry. They stop and stand at junctions in a way that looks like loitering and is actually listening. When two of them meet on a duckboard too narrow for passing, one turns side-on and the other goes by with a hand flat on the first one's chest or shoulder, briefly, the way you'd steady a ladder, and both carry on without a word, because there is no word to be had in the crest of the stroke and because the gesture is older than the necessity.

They are the only things down there that move on their own. They know it. It is the first fact of being from Sough and it is never once stated aloud.

---

**Bodies.** They are pale, but not sickly-pale — a wet, even, candle-coloured pallor, the same all over, because there is no sun here to divide a person into a face and a rest. Dark under the eyes, generally. Hair goes lank within an hour of washing and so is worn short, or oiled, or wrapped in reed-cloth; there is a whole small vanity industry in the manner of the wrap.

The hands are the thing. A Sough hand is broad, hot, slightly swollen, the fingertips permanently corrugated from the water, the nails soft enough to be trimmed with the thumb. Handshakes here are not customary and outsiders' handshakes are endured. Look in the creases of a comb-scraper's knuckles and you will find a fine green line that no amount of scrubbing gets out — verdigris, worked in, harmless, and worn with a certain amount of quiet class. Featherers' hands go yellow in the palm-creases and stay yellow through the winter. Pump crews have flat, bleached, sodden hands with no lines left in them at all, and it is a standing joke that you cannot tell a raft-keeper's fortune.

The lungs are the price. Nearly everyone over forty has the slow cough — three or four barks in the trough, never in the crest, so that a street at evening sounds like a very sparse and orderly applause. It is not tuberculosis. Nothing tubercular has ever got a foothold here, nor has anything else that needs a vector: no fleas, no lice, no bedbugs, no ticks, no fly-borne summer sickness. Sough people are, by the standards of any comparable works town, *uninfested* to an almost eerie degree, and they have no word for the sensation of being crawled on. They itch, of course — moss-rash, brine-eczema — but the itch is impersonal and they treat it the way you treat weather.

They have excellent teeth and are smug about it. The water is heavy and sweet and holds nothing that ever lived, and it leaves a faint mineral fur on the back of the tongue that they taste all their lives and never notice, and notice violently within one day of leaving.

---

**Speech.** They talk in the trough. Thirty seconds is the natural length of an utterance here, and Sough conversation is therefore built out of thirty-second bricks with eleven-second mortar. A statement, then the crest — the beam coming over like a cathedral door closing carefully, the wet chuff, moss shivering off the wall behind your head — then the reply. Nobody interrupts anybody, ever. It is not politeness. There is no opportunity.

The effect on temperament is not small. An argument in Sough cannot be had at speed; every accusation gets its forty-one seconds to be regretted before it can be answered, and the town has a reputation among the terrace buyers for being unshockable, slow to anger, and impossible to bargain with. Their humour is deadpan and delayed. A joke is set up in one trough and landed in the next, and a really fine one will be landed two troughs later, after a whole intervening exchange about brine temperatures, and the laugh when it comes is loud because it has to be, it comes up out of the crest.

Away from the engines — in the chapel, on the upper allotment terraces, on the berm — they still do it. The rhythm is in them. Sough people abroad have been described as hypnotic, as evasive, as slightly simple, as extraordinarily good listeners, and as very tiring to eat dinner with.

They sing in that measure, unaccompanied, in the trough, with the stroke as the downbeat. There is no other percussion in Sough and there has never needed to be one.

They count in strokes. A hundred strokes is a little over an hour, and a child sent on an errand is given a number. Old women say *I have about four hundred thousand left* and mean it as a joke and also not.

And they do not run. There is exactly one meaning to running in Sough. A person breaking into a run on the Hollow Mile empties the buildings on either side of it within seconds, because it means the stroke has stopped, and the stroke has stopped eleven times in a hundred and forty years, and every one of them has a name — *Marrow's Cold*, *the Nineteen Minutes*, *Susan's Long Night*, and the one just called *the Quiet*, which was the worst, and which two people in the town are just old enough to have been carried to. Children are smacked for running. It is the only thing they are smacked for.

---

**Trades, and what each does to a person.** The comb-scrapers stoop and have the green knuckles and a habit of standing with their arms slightly out from their sides. The brine-men have ruined thumbnails and are, for no reason anyone can explain, the town's musicians. The pulp-cutters in the west shed work beside the only fast machinery in the basin and are therefore half-deaf and speak in the crest as well as the trough, which marks them out instantly and makes them seem rude. The lamp trimmers go up ladders and see the ceiling from underneath more than anyone and are considered a little strange. The timber crews do nothing but replacement — never repair, there is no repairing anything here — and have a fatalism about objects that the rest of the town finds restful.

The drip-listeners are not a guild and will tell you they are not a skill. They will then place you within ten feet, blindfolded, in fog, by the pitch of the gutter-fall. Most of them are women and most of them are old and they are used the way another town would use a clock or a constable: to find children, to find the drunk, to tell you whether the water table has come up a finger overnight.

The raft-keepers go down into the underdays, in pairs, on the slow cycle, with a lamp and a pole and a great deal of arithmetic, and they touch the larch piles that have been drowned since before anyone, and they come back up and say the pile-heads are sound, and everyone breathes. They are the most respected people in Sough and the most reluctant to be. They do not tell stories about down there. This is often mistaken for stoicism and is closer to tact.

And the featherers, who are four or five at a time and are drawn from everybody — it is a duty, not a trade, and most people do a season of it before they are twenty. Cattail wand, one bed at a time, under the lamps, in the fan-wind, flower to flower, three hours, in silence because at that distance nothing can be said. There is a thing children work out on their own, usually around seven, and announce at the table with enormous pride: *we're the bees*. The adults agree that this is clever and change the subject, and the child does not understand why the room has gone careful.

---

**The reading.** At noon, when the ceiling thins and the coin comes through the milk, the whole town goes up. This is the great social hour. Letters are read out loud on the berm steps, mostly to their owners by someone with better eyes, so that private correspondence in Sough is a lightly collaborative art and everyone knows everyone's business and nobody says so. Accounts are done. Thorns are dug out of hands. Courting is done at the reading, in full view, which is the only way it is done, and a couple who have sat on the same terrace step for a season are considered engaged whether or not they have discussed it.

Fine work waits for it. The zinc-punchers bring their plates up, because the guild registers are not on paper — paper here lasts about as long as a good intention — but punched into sheet zinc, the births and the deaths and the pile-surveys and the eleven names of the eleven stoppages, hung in racks in the hall where the air can get at them.

Then the light goes flat again and they all go down.

---

**Smell.** They smell of wet reed, tar, hot brine, cold copper, and the sweetness. They cannot smell it on themselves.

What they can smell is *you*. An outsider coming down the berm is detected at fifty paces and identified by category — wool, leather, horse, dog, tallow, meat on the breath — and the experience, for a born-down, is not unlike walking into a room where something has died. They are too courteous to say. You will notice only that the child holding your hand keeps turning her face away, and that she is fascinated, and that she comes back.

Nothing in Sough is made of animal. Reed-cloth aprons, gutta-percha soles, teasel brushes, sap glue, horn replaced by turned boxwood shipped in at ruinous cost. Meat comes up the terraces salted, in small quantities, and is eaten mostly by the young and the recently arrived, and there is a faint disreputability to it, like drinking before noon. An egg is an occasion. Eggs are given at christenings, one per household, and the shells are kept — washed, dried, hoarded in tins by children, traded at agreed rates, the only bone-coloured thing most of them will ever own.

And in the guild hall, under the bell-jar, the beetle. They take the children to it at about five. Every generation independently reports that it looks like a machine, and every generation of adults finds this funny and slightly upsetting, and no generation has yet worked out how to explain, to a child who has never seen anything move by itself except her own family, what a wing is for.

---

**What they keep instead.** Every window in the eleven houses on Tin Walk, and in every other house in the basin, has something in it that turns: a mobile of hammered tin and reed, or a little vane of waxed paper, or a wire spiral, hung where the draught off the stroke can find it. Forty-one seconds of stillness, then a shiver, then a slow rotation, then stillness. You will be told it is for luck, or that it shows the flue is drawing. It is neither. It is company, and if you say so in the house you will not be corrected, and you will not be asked back.

They keep ferns as other towns keep cats. Named, moved about the room for the light, carried between houses in a marriage, mourned. There is a hart's-tongue in the chapel porch with a brass tag on its pot and a date in the 1880s, and it is stroked in passing by everyone who goes in, which is why the leaves on the near side are bald.

---

**The dead.** The peat keeps what you drop. It also keeps what you put in it, and it keeps it *exactly*, and the town learned this the hard way four times in seventy years, which is how often a pile-driving or a deep pumping has brought something up out of the underdays with its face still on it and its hands still soft and its clothes recognisable to somebody standing there. Each of those four has a name too, although it is not a name the guild would give you.

So they go up now. The dead are carried out of the basin — up the berm, over the rim, three fields, to ground that is only ordinarily wet, where things are eaten and go to pieces on the proper schedule. A Sough funeral is a long uphill walk in the wrong air, in the smell of outside, with the sound of home getting fainter behind you until, somewhere on the third field, it stops, and that silence is the thing everyone in the procession is actually attending to. The old word for it is *going up*, which is the same word they use for a young person leaving to take work in the terraces, and they are aware of this, and it is part of why leaving is hard here.

Below, in the black water under the streets, the older ones are still there, in the order they were put, on a zinc plate in the hall, punched, with dates. The raft-keepers walk past them with a lamp and a pole and do the arithmetic and come back up and say that the pile-heads are sound.

---

**Things not asked.** Where the fifth engine went. What the raft-keepers see. Why the basin doesn't keep animals.

And this one, which is the largest and the most thoroughly not-asked: there are not very many children. There have never been very many children. The guild counts twice a year and the number holds steady and it holds steady because people keep coming down the hill — the born-down are outnumbered by the come-down and have been for a century, and this is simply never said where a child can hear it. Sough is tender with children to a degree that visitors find excessive and faintly sad: a child in the basin is public property, watched by everyone, fed by anyone, indulged past all sense, and possessed of roughly eleven aunts. Nobody connects this out loud with the fans at the fourth hour, or with the four or five figures moving down the bean rows in the lamplight with cattail wands, touching flower to flower because otherwise nothing sets.

They are not a haunted people. That would be too strong and too romantic and they would laugh at it, in the next trough. They are a patient, damp, slow-spoken, unhurried, unshockable people with good teeth and bad lungs, who make jokes across forty-one-second gaps and know their own town by the sound of water hitting zinc, and who have arranged their entire lives around the one fact they do not discuss, which is that in the whole green brimming basin there is nothing alive that moves except them, and it has to be enough, and — this is the part outsiders never credit — most days, quite genuinely, it is.

---

The come-down are a self-selecting population, and the selection happens fast. Some people stand at the rim at dusk, in the white, and feel the stroke come up through the soles of their feet, and their jaw sets, and they are gone by morning and never come near the place again.

And some stand there and feel the same thing, and their shoulders go down about an inch, all at once, like a load being set on a bench.

That is the whole of the test. The town has no other. In three hundred and sixty-odd cases it has not yet been wrong.
