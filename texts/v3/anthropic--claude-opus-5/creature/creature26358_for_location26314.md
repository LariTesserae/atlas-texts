## The Handled

They call themselves *the handled* — those who have been touched enough by others to be known. It is not a boast. It is a statement about how a person is assembled. A newborn is not yet anybody; it is made into somebody over the first four or five years by being passed hand to hand through every gallery until enough people carry its shape in their fingers that it can be said to exist. Somebody who has not been handled is not a person yet. Somebody who was handled and is now unhandled — who has gone off to another mound, say — is spoken of in a grammatical form otherwise used for tools left behind on the crown.

A grown one is about the height of a cranberry leaf and weighs less than the water in its own body suggests, because much of that water is outside the skin rather than in it. They are pale, the colour of the stem two fingers down from the green, and they never dry. Drying is the death they fear the way other peoples fear falling. The skin is thin and permeable and does a good deal of the work of breathing when it is wet, which is always; they drink very little by mouth and mostly by standing still. Their fingers and the soles of their broad splayed feet are permanently pleated and soft, and they consider a smooth fingertip a sign of grave illness.

The hair is the important thing. It grows long, on head and forearms and down the spine, and it is not hair as we would recognise it but a dense pile of fine curved fibres that will not wet through. Combed, greased with grub-fat, and laid properly, it holds a skin of air against the body — a silver coat, they say, though almost none of them have ever seen silver or their own coat. A person in good order can go down into black water with their own atmosphere clinging to them like frost on a stem, and stay under long enough to do work. This is why the combs. The flint comb is the first tool a child is given and the last object taken from a corpse. Combs herd the springtail flocks, combs card cottongrass, combs dress the dead, and combs are what a diver's life hangs on, and there is no separate word for any of these uses. To say *she keeps her comb well* is to say she is a serious person.

Their eyes are large, dark, and nearly useless. They register the milk-coloured shine of the fungus and the difference between a doorway and a wall, and that is all that is asked of them. Most go blind in their forties without particular comment; a blind elder is not impaired, merely finished with a redundancy. Faces are read with two hands, thumbs at the jaw, and this reading is done slowly and without embarrassment, in doorways, in corridors, in the middle of arguments. Their word for *lie* is built from the word for *withheld cheek*.

They speak quietly. Loud speech is not rude so much as obscene, because it drowns *the chewing*, and a person who cannot hear the chewing cannot tell whether the wall they are leaning on is alive. In the long dark galleries much of the talk is not vocal at all but done by pressure on the forearm — a whole shorthand of squeezes and drags for *stop*, *water ahead*, *I am the one you think I am*, *someone has died*.

## Names, and what a name is made of

A name here is a knot.

At a child's handling-end, when enough people know it by touch, a knot-form is invented for it — a particular sequence of turns in a short length of cottongrass cord. That knot is tied into the child's hair, tied at the lintel of wherever it sleeps, and tied once more into the long cord kept by the person whose whole occupation is keeping it. That cord is fifty times a person's height, coiled in a dry-as-possible niche, and it is the genealogy: every knot ever given, in order, with the dead marked by a second turn.

They will tell you firmly that this is not writing. Writing is a thing they have heard of from a crossing-story and consider implausible. This is simply a rope with everybody on it.

## Days

There are no days. There is the long winter dark and the brief wet green season, and inside those, a rhythm of work and torpor. They sleep in stretches of five or six hours, twice or three times between meals, in shallow niches lined with combed moss, often three or four together for the warmth, which is not much warmth. In the deepest cold they slow down to something a stranger would take for dying: pulse thin, skin cold as the wall, waking every day or two to drink and to check the chewing.

Awake, the work is: braiding, bailing, sieving, herding, and going down.

The sieving is constant. Everything eaten must be sorted out of a substrate that is nine parts inedible tenant and one part food, and so the sieve-work goes on in every occupied gallery, a soft endless shussh of mesh, the sound of a city having its supper strained. Springtails are combed into runs and penned in a side-chamber, fed peat-crumb, and killed by hand. Grubs are cut for fat, for gut-string, and for the pale sweet meat they consider the proper food of divers and of the newly bereaved. Cranberry skins are dragged in from the crown in season and stitched into sacks and lamp-shields and, for children, a kind of ball.

On the crown itself grow the sundews. At this scale a sundew is not a curiosity but a standing hazard — a low red machine of glue and patience, and there are stories, all true, of somebody's uncle. They are also the only adhesive in the world. Harvesting sundew glue is a paired job: one to hold the ankles, one to go in with a bone spatula, and both to run.

## Braiders

The braiders work the top. They are usually young, because they must tolerate the crown, and tolerating the crown is a taste that fades.

Up there, above the last braided roof, the world stops having walls. There is fog with salt in it, there is a wind that comes from nowhere and does not stop, there is the tick of frost taking, and on a clear night there is a green smear across a distance so large that no one can hold it. Nearly everyone who goes up has, once, lain flat on the moss and gripped it, unable to make their hands let go and come inside. It is regarded as ordinary and is not mocked. The braiders learn to work with their eyes shut, which costs them nothing, and to keep one hand on a fixed cord that runs back to the hatch.

What they do is open next year's rooms in the fresh green: take the living stems left and right of a chosen line, twist them into cords over the course of years, cord the cords, and let the growing wall pull itself tight. A gallery is not finished; a gallery is *far enough along*. On a warm wet day a braider can feel the corridor lengthening under her palms and will stay there with her hands flat on it for an hour, doing nothing, which is not laziness but a recognised part of the trade.

## The chip-carriers and the count

The light and the fire are the same object, and the object is a drowned pine log lying at the bottom of a shaft it took four generations to braid, in cold black water, fed on by a fungus that gives off a shine like skimmed milk held against a lamp.

Chips are mined from it in wet pouches and set into wall-niches, where they last a season and then go back down to be reseeded. The rule of reseeding is the nearest thing they have to a religious law: you never take a chip without carrying a spent one down, and you never take from a face of the log that has not been given time. Children do the carrying in the upper shaft, which is dry enough to climb; below the waterline, chip-mining is diving work.

Someone keeps the count. Once a year they go down and measure what is left of the log against a knotted line, and the measurement is announced in the fire chamber, and everyone hears it, and nobody says anything for a while.

But the count is not really the problem, and the older ones know it. The log stays where it is. The city rises a finger a year. Every generation the shaft to the light is longer, and more of it is underwater, and the point at which a diver's silver coat gives out is fixed by the size of a body and cannot be argued with. They are not going to burn through the pine. They are going to rise away from it. Somewhere ahead — four generations, six, nobody agrees — there is a year in which the light is still there, still shining, and no one alive can reach it.

Their oldest recorded joke is about this. It is not funny.

## Divers

The divers are the strongest and are treated as clerks.

They go down the knotted line with a chip in the mouth and a comb in the hair, into water cold enough to stop the chest, and they do inventory. They come back with a great-great-grandmother's comb, still sharp; with a knot they can lay against the long cord and match; with the report that the ninth floor's braid-work has slumped on the north side and should be written off. It is not adventure. A diver who describes the descent in exciting terms is thought vulgar and probably unreliable.

What they see, when the chip in the mouth lights it, is the city as it was, entire, holding its shape: sieves still on their pegs, footholds still knotted, the floors laid with the dead who were put there on purpose, hair combed, expressions intact. Thirty years under, sixty. The acid keeps everything. A diver's first descent is made at about fourteen, in company, and the whole purpose of it is to put both hands on the face of somebody who died before they were born, and to be told that face's knot, and to come up and be able to say it.

After that they are considered adult, and they are much quieter for a season, and then they are not.

## Which way to build

The one real politics of the mound runs vertically.

The braiders say: build up faster. Open more rooms in the green, get ahead of the water, stop bailing galleries that are already lost. The mound rises whether you like it or not; go with it.

The divers say: you cannot outrun your dead, and the shaft to the light does not rise with you. Slow the building. Keep the low rooms bailed as long as they can be bailed. Hold the city down.

Nobody wins. What happens instead is that every year the lowest gallery takes water — no drama, a floor gone soft, then a floor gone — and every year the topmost braiders open new rooms in the fresh green, and the argument is had again in exactly the same words. They have a phrase for the argument that means, approximately, *the yearly noise*, and it is said with a certain fondness, because a city having this argument is a city still doing both things.

## When the low rooms ask a question

On a warm wet spring the meltwater comes up faster than the crown grows, and the drowned city presses. An old floor gives. Out of a room sealed forty years comes cold water smelling of iron and sweetness, and in the water, something with a face.

There is a procedure and everyone knows their part in it, including children, who are not sent away. Bail first — always bail first, because a corridor with standing water in it will lose its braid. Then the risen one is taken up, carefully, by the shoulders and the hips, because the skin holds but the joints do not, and laid on a mat of combed moss. Then somebody fetches the long cord and the knot is matched, and the name is said aloud, which is the only occasion on which they permit themselves to be loud. Then the hair is combed. Then a floor is opened higher up, in newer braid, and they are laid in it, and it is closed, and they will keep.

The phrase for the whole event means *the low rooms have asked a question*, and the answer is understood to be: *yes, still, we know who you are.*

## The long noise

Two or three times in a life the chewing changes: a hollow rushing gnaw from a direction that has no door in it. A shrew, coming through, longer than the widest gallery.

There is no fighting it. There is the drill, which is downward and sideways, into the storage volumes and the water, because a shrew will not follow into water it cannot smell food beyond. Afterwards there are dead, and a raw torn seam through four levels, and months of braiding, and for a generation afterward that seam is spoken of as an address: *he was born in the mending*.

## Crossings

A hundred paces of open till, once or twice in a lifetime, at night, in fog, greased to the eyes and running.

Marriages are made this way, between mounds, because ninety-odd people cannot be their own future. A crossing party is four or five, roped, carrying nothing, and the danger is not distance but drying and birds and the sheer unroofed size of it. Some come back. Some arrive.

The arrived are *unhandled*, and the first month is spent fixing that: passed gallery to gallery, hands on the face, thumbs at the jaw, a hundred people learning a stranger by touch until the stranger has been touched into existence. Then a knot is invented, and tied in the hair, and tied into the long cord, and that is that; there is no further category. Their word for *foreigner* has no adult form.

## The other tenants

It should be said plainly that the people are a minority in their own city, and know it.

In one handful of the wall there are more animals than there are citizens. Mites in the tens of thousands. Springtails in drifts. Nematodes in every water-film, rotifers, tardigrades folded up in their thousands in the leaf-hollows, waiting out whatever this is. Beetle grubs the size of a person's leg, turning in the peat. Harvestmen going over the crown like slow derricks, one leg at a time.

The mites live on the people too, in the hair, and are combed out and thought nothing of.

And the fungus, down in the water, eating the only wood in the world and giving back the only light. They do not worship it, exactly. They observe an etiquette with it — reseed, do not strip a face, do not take on the coldest nights — of the kind you keep with a neighbour whose goodwill is the difference between a lit room and a black one.

All of it grinds away without a sound anyone can name, and that grinding is the sound of the city's own substance being eaten, and it is the sound they cannot sleep without. A silent wall is a dead wall. A dead wall slumps.

So: a hundred and some pale, wet, half-blind, well-combed people, living inside a standing body of water with a plant for a skeleton, sorting their food out of the wall with sieves, arguing every spring about which direction to build, carrying fingernail-sized pieces of light up a shaft that gets longer every year, and keeping — in cold black airless water directly beneath the floor they are standing on — every single person they have ever been.

Outside, fog. A rind of new ice. The curlew, once, from a long way off.

And the mound goes up a finger a year, and the city stands perfectly still, climbing, and everybody in it is descending at exactly the same speed.
