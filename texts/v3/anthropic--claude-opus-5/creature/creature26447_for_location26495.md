## A census, taken by hand

Nothing here lives *in* the room. Say it that way and you will keep looking for a body the size of your own. What lives here lives in the gradient — in a four-metre slab of gravel and silt that behaves like a slow bell, struck once a year at the surface and ringing months later down at the vault. Everything below is that ringing, made into tissue. Still, there are bodies. They can be counted. Here they are, in order of mass.

**The floor.** Ankle-deep, ivory in the middle of the room and darkening to oxblood along the wet line where the wall meets the moss, and darker still — nearly green-black, a colour the hand cannot read but the nose can, sharper there, more sap — in the two square metres beneath the reliable drips. It is one thing. Not a lawn, not a colony of individuals: one organism, or one organism's four or five thousand identical selves, which comes to the same. It has no centre. It has been dividing itself since before the ceramic trays were stacked, and every part of it is the same age as every other part, which is to say no age at all. Cut a handful out and set it down a metre away and in nine years you cannot find the seam.

It is the room's mouth, and its skin, and its clock. It holds four litres of water per square metre and moves them sideways, uphill against the half-degree slope, by no mechanism anyone identified before they left. Where you stand, it will be reading you. Not with alarm. It has no faculty for alarm. It simply registers a warm mass, and files it in the only category it has: *the wave has come early this year, and it is very small, and it is standing on me*.

**The elders.** Four hundred and some — the count depends on whether you credit the small ones by the drain, which have been small for sixty years and may be dying, or may be waiting. They are the fronds: leathery, felted on the underside like the inside of an animal's ear, and cold, always two or three degrees colder than the air they stand in, because they are drawing heat down out of it and eating it.

They are the only inhabitants with biography. A frond takes eleven years to unroll, and it unrolls at the rate the gradient allows, and so its stipe is thick where the years were good and thin where they were poor, in a taper the thumb can walk up and read like a ledger. There are fronds in the third rank from the entry that carry a famine in their fourth year: a waist so narrow you can circle it with two fingers, from a decade when something shifted in the strata above and the wave came down weak and late. They survived it. They are still holding the record of it, shoulder to shoulder, four hundred copies of a bad decade nobody witnessed.

They move. This is the thing that takes visitors by the throat, and it takes several hours, so most visitors miss it. Your body is a heat source of eighty or ninety watts, roughly two hundred times what the lattice hands out per square metre, and the fronds cannot tell the difference between you and a season. Over three or four hours the nearest of them will turn — not lean, turn, from the base, with a sound like wet leather flexing — until their felted undersides face you. If you stay a full day the second rank will follow. If you leave, they hold that attitude for a year or two before they forget, and any visitor after you will find a small crowd of them attending an absence.

They have no chlorophyll, no flowers, no scent glands that serve any purpose. But bruise one and it releases that green, sappy, crushed-stem smell that met you eleven paces down, and this is the strangest fact in the room: it is a warning. Their ancestors, wherever the makers found or built them, had enemies with mouths, and evolved to shout chemically when torn. Nothing here has ever had an enemy. Nothing here has ever had a mouth. Four hundred plants in a sealed dark room have been screaming at each other, once or twice a century, whenever a boot comes down, in a language whose entire vocabulary is *something is eating me* and whose entire audience is other things that cannot move faster than a millimetre a month.

**The hair.** The curtains from the vault are not plants in the sense the fronds are. They are strands of the same lineage that gave up standing, and what they do instead is fast — the only fast thing here. Touch a hank of it and it clings, and the clinging is active, a contraction along the length in something under two seconds, and it holds for a quarter of an hour before it tires. It is wicking the vault's condensation down to the moss, and it is also, in a limited way, feeling the room. When the fronds turn toward you, they turn toward where the hair says you are. It brushed your face at the entry and mapped you as a warm interruption three seconds after you arrived, which makes it the first thing here that ever knows anything, and the only thing that could be said to have a present tense.

**The mesh.** Behind the moss, in the cast stone, the grey-white filaments — and this is where the census gets difficult, because the mesh was made, and something has happened to it. The pattern the hand can't read is not the makers' pattern. It was a grid once; there are stretches near the shelf where you can still feel the grid, a regularity of four centimetres. Everywhere else it has gone lawless, and it is thicker, and it has grown out past the cast surfaces where it was poured: into the fresh stone at the low corner, into the iron of the drain grille and through it, into the rust-shadow in the moss where a tool once lay, into the sponge of the wooden handle, which is now less wood than trellis. The roots grip the filaments; at every joint there is that tackiness which is not quite water. Somewhere in two centuries the direction of the relationship reversed, or blurred past reversing. The plants are not being fed by an appliance. They are keeping one, and extending it, and the whisper of power per square metre now arrives through junctions nobody designed, and the low hum in your molars is the sound of an arrangement that has no author.

**The pasture.** By numbers, this is who lives here: some hundreds of millions of rotifers, water bears, and nematodes in the film of water between the moss stems. Eyeless, all of them, and most of the rotifers are of a kind that abandoned sex tens of millions of years before this hillside had a hillside, and that pick up spare genes from whatever dies near them. They are the oldest thing in the room by lineage and the newest by generation. They graze, they divide, and at the slack they dry to dust and stop — not sleep; stop, chemistry halted, no clock running — and swell back at the thaw with no gap in their experience at all. Their year has no winter in it. They have been alive for two centuries and have lived perhaps a hundred and forty of them.

**The mineral.** Slowest tenant, and the only one that does not eat. It arrives, drop by drop, out of the vault, and lies down. It has made the ceiling velvet and the trays part of the shelf and the stamped letters by the door into a smoothness with a rumour of a bump in it. It is not alive and it is doing the most durable work in the room. In eleven thousand years it will have closed the passage.

---

**And the warm place.**

Two metres from the bench, at the edge of the third rank, the moss stands slightly higher than it should — a low swell, a hand's breadth of extra depth, a shape the palm reads as a shoulder and then, moving down, as something folded. The colour there is ivory, the ivory of a thing idling, and it is exactly as cold as the rest of the floor. There is nothing under it now but a chemistry indistinguishable from the chemistry of everywhere else.

The nine fronds standing around it are, however, wrong. Their stipes are thick at the base — grossly thick, a good decade laid down in three years, a fat rich taper — for the first forty centimetres of growth, and then they narrow abruptly to the ordinary famine of the room and continue up, ordinary, for the rest of their length. Nine plants that had, once, a private summer, a small unscheduled sun of thirty-seven degrees lying in the moss, and metabolised it, and grew fast on it, and then went hungry again and stayed hungry, and have kept the shape of that hunger in their bodies ever since.

That is the only monument on this mountain. Not the tab of metal with its smoothed-over word. Nine thickened stems, and a rise in the floor, and no name attached to either, and nothing in the room capable of attaching one.

---

**The tenant.**

If you want to know what lives here, in the sense you meant, then it is not the moss or the elders or the eyeless pasture. It is the wave. It has a body four metres thick and the whole hillside for a skin, it takes half a year to move its length, it inhales in July and exhales in November, and twice a year, crossing over, it stands perfectly still for a few days and everything inside it holds its breath and smells of sap. Everything else in this room — the four hundred, the floor, the hair, the mesh with its unauthorised junctions, the millions of dried and resurrected — is what it uses for hands.

You have gone down eleven paces into something's slow chest, and it has noticed you the way weather notices a stone: by going around, and by keeping the shape a while afterwards.
