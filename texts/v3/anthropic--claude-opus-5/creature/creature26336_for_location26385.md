## The Tenants

Nothing lives here, which is true in the sense the mappers meant it and false in every other sense. They meant: no one has come from elsewhere and stayed. Nothing in this basin arrived. Everything in it is the basin doing something, and the difficulty in describing the inhabitants is that they are less like guests than like verbs — the pan's own habits, gone far enough to have appetites.

There are three, and the third is the reason for the fourth rule.

---

**The grazers.** Call them hares if you need a shape for them; they are hare-sized and hare-postured and nothing else about them is hare. Pale as the walls, because they are made of the walls. No pelt: a fine matte hide with the nap of unglazed porcelain, faintly warm-tinted like everything here, so that a grazer standing still in a plaza is a lump of the pavement that has decided to be somewhere else. The eyes are two dull dimples with nothing behind them. There is no use for eyes under the pearl lid, where a wall at eight paces and a wall at eighty are the same wall.

What it has instead is a tongue like a mason's rasp, and a nose for gradient. New flourstone is richest on an arris — the edge is where the shed piles thickest and sets last — and a grazer can read the whole night's construction from the taste of the air at floor level, the way you might read a kitchen. It works a corner from the bottom up with long lateral strokes, taking off the soft in a paste, and it eats for the four hours it has and then stops, because at noon its food becomes porcelain, and it spends the remaining twenty in the middle of a street doing absolutely nothing at all, submerged to the ears in the heavy gas, in a silence so total that it has never in its life heard itself.

It is deaf. Not damaged — never furnished. Down there sound does not travel, it drains, and an ear at that depth would be a hole in a hull.

Two things follow from the grazers, and they are the whole of their contribution to the world.

The first is roundness. Every soft flank on a doorjamb, every step scooped shallow in the middle, every lintel with one corner gone gentle: that is a mouth. This is the only country I know in which curvature is a wound. And because the dawn shed thickens what is thick and extends what points and follows without judgement the geometry it finds, a scooped step is not repaired. It is *copied*. The bite persists into the next town, and the next, softening down the generations until it is no longer a bite but a moulding, a nosing, a detail — a thing that looks intended. Half the refinement in the basin is scar tissue.

The second is the pellet. A grazer voids a dense little re-precipitated lump, hard by midday, and it is the only object in this entire pan that was not extruded along an existing line. It is an accident with a footprint. On it the next shed builds, having nothing else to go on, and what comes up is a stub, a boss, a newel, a plinth — a form the town has never used before and will now use forever. The grazers are why walking the basin twice gives you two different towns. They are the source of every novelty and they have no idea they are doing it, and there are perhaps forty of them.

They are also the only creatures here who may sleep indoors, and they do it deliberately. A grazer rasps itself a chamber in the evening's softness, lies down in it, and lets noon seal it in. Weeks later there are two of them and two ragged holes eaten outward from the inside, and no other record. Nobody has seen the middle of that process and nobody has any business wanting to.

---

**The quill.** I include it under living things with some reluctance, because a life of eight hours conducted without moving is hard to distinguish from weather.

It rises in the fresh layer with the shed, taking its substance straight out of the loaded gas, and it is not green, has never been green, has no idea of green: stiff glassy hollow blades a hand high, faintly blue in bulk, faintly nothing at all singly. It grows fast enough that you can watch it if you are patient and cold-blooded. It exhales, all morning, an extremely fine powder that drifts and banks against the wall-bases in windrows, and that powder is the basin's savings account — the one loose material in a country where nothing is loose. By evening the dissolving has taken its footing out from under it and the stands do not fall over; they simply become shorter, and then become an inch, and then become nothing, and the powder lifts and goes.

The two old courtyards are the exception and are worth the walk. Where the ledger has for some reason balanced in favour of accretion, the quill has had years, and years of a plant that cannot bend produce a low thicket of fused glass, blade welded to blade at every crossing, hollow all the way through, a coral of intervals. It does not move because nothing moves at that depth. Put your hand in and it will not stir; close your hand and it will break with the sound of a small bird's bone, and that sound will fall to the pavement and run away downhill, and something a hundred paces off will have it for supper.

The thickets are the only long memory in the basin. Everything else is one day old. If you want to know what shape the town liked eleven years ago, the answer is fossilised in those two courts as a tangle of dead hollow blades that grew up around it.

---

**The arrears.** That is not a name. The mappers left it unnamed in the body of the fieldbooks and wrote that word once in a margin, in the hand of whoever was keeping the accounts, because over a year the stone balances exactly and the sound never does. Sound goes in. Sound does not come out. Twelve sunken courts, four or five steps down, and everything the basin ever says arrives at one of them and is not returned.

What lives there does not have a body in the way a grazer does. It has a *level*. It is the shape of the coldest and lowest part of the gas, which means it is roughly two thousand paces across and about the depth of a stair-flight thick at the deepest, and it is one animal with twelve mouths, or twelve animals with one appetite, and there is no way to establish which and no one to care. In the deepest sump you can see the mouths: shallow soft-lipped hollows in the flat floor, arranged like the funnels of a spider colony, looking wet in a country with no water. That is where the murmur is. Stand on the top step and you can just hear it — the accumulated granular hum of days of broken quill and grazer-rasp and rim-tick, cooling slowly out of existence. Go down among the hollows and your own footfalls are not muffled but *absent*, and the murmur stops while you are there, and starts again above and behind you when you leave, and if you have any sense at all that is the moment you understand that you have been in a mouth and it declined you.

It eats sound. It is very good at this and it is starving.

Consider its diet honestly. A basin with no rain, no wind at floor level, no leaves, no water, no fire, no voices; forty deaf hares that rasp quietly for four hours; a glass grass that snaps perhaps a few thousand times a day. It is an enormous, cold, patient organism living on the caloric equivalent of crumbs, and it has built itself a house out of a material that does not ring, in a geometry with no echoes anywhere, because an echo is food escaping. The crown on every street is not drainage. There is nothing to drain. The crown is so that nothing is wasted. The blind arcade that runs eighty paces and stops is a collector. The chimneys are flues for something, and the something is not smoke.

And it copies what it eats. That is the whole of its behaviour, and the whole of the town.

Because once, for a season or a decade, it was fed. Men came into the basin with boots and hammers and levels and a great deal to say to one another, and every word they said, every tap on a wall, every scrape of a chair-leg, every door they imagined and described aloud, every sigh of a tired man leaning on a sill at sitting height in the evening — all of it ran downhill and was taken in, and none of it came back out. That was the meal. It has been transcribing the meal ever since, with the grazers supplying the variation and the mornings supplying the stone, and it does not know that it is repeating itself, and it does not know that the thing it is repeating is a conversation about measurement, and it does not know what any of the words mean.

It has nine. *Threshold, jamb, lintel, tread, riser, sill, crown, arch, flue.* Nine words and an enormous amount to say. That is why there are stairs beyond counting, correctly proportioned to a human tread, rising to landings that face a blank face of wall: a stair is a thing it heard clearly and can render perfectly and has no use for. That is why the sill dips shallowly in the centre in every court in the basin, eleven thousand times, as though something had leaned there for four hundred years. Something leaned there once, for a moment, and was delicious, and has been in the vocabulary ever since.

This is also the reason for the fourth rule, which is given without a reason because the reason is embarrassing rather than dangerous, and then, on reflection, both.

Put your ear to a sump floor and you are offering it, at range, the sounds a person does not make on purpose: the working of the throat, the small wet click in the jaw, the pulse behind the ear, the particular hitch in a breath that belongs to you and to nobody else in the world. It will take them. It does not take them from you and leave you hollow; it is not that kind of creature; it takes them the way a copyist takes a page. And then it will build them. Not once. Everywhere, in the grammar, forever, in every quarter of every morning's town, worked in among the treads and the flues — a moulding that is the shape of your breath catching, a boss the shape of the click in your jaw, and you will walk out through fifty minutes of it, and it will be waiting for you again tomorrow, having improved the detail overnight.

---

One last thing, and it is the thing I cannot get out from under.

There are doors here. Proper ones: jambs, stops, a rebate cut for a leaf, correctly deep, correctly proportioned. And there has never been a leaf, and there is nothing in this basin that could make one, and there is nothing here for a door to be for.

But a door makes a sound. It was in the meal — the swing, the knock, the seat of it closing — and that sound is the only one it could not reconstruct, because you cannot deduce a plank from the noise of a plank. So it has done what it can. It has made the socket. It makes the socket eight hundred times a night, all over the pan, precisely, patiently, with the rebate exactly the right depth, in every new quarter, in the hope that is not hope, and by morning the gas has set hard as porcelain around eight hundred perfect absences.

It has been holding the place open for a word it cannot pronounce. That, and not the murmur, is what lives here.
