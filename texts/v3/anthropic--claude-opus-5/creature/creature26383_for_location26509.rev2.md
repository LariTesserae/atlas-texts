## Who lives there

The question is harder than it sounds, because the plain is crowded and uninhabited, and those turn out not to be opposites.

### The tenants

Start by getting the animals out of the way, which feels cruel, and is.

A gasket beetle is not an inhabitant. It is a stitch. It has no parents and no offspring, no lineage to be the end of and no name that means anything but *one of those*. It will graze, seal, and be eaten at a rate set elsewhere, and within two hours of its death the shortfall will be made good from a warm disc of metal four hundred meters away, and the thing that unfolds there will be, to the tolerance of any instrument you can carry, the same beetle. Nothing has been lost. Nothing was ever there to lose. You cannot mourn an inventory.

The same is true, with variations, all the way up. The wickworms are plumbing. The flange snails are a slow reciprocating polish-and-patch. The stack-swifts are a filter with a forty-one day service life. The cistern crabs number exactly as many as the grilles because they *are* the grilles, in the same sense that a hinge is a door. And the vent hounds — this is the one that takes people longest to accept — are not predators. A predator is an argument. A hound is an errata service. It walks its twelve square kilometers, blind, reading tremor, and removes what has drifted out of specification, at a rate that never varies, because the rate of drift never varies either.

So: two hundred trillion animals, and not one resident among them. They don't live on the plain. They *are* the plain, the way your ciliated airway is you. Furniture that eats.

Hold that judgment lightly, though. It survives contact with everything except the wickworms in the dew tide, when the pale nap on a seam moves *all together*, and you stand there getting rained on from the inside of the air and think: that was almost a gesture. It wasn't. But the thought is not stupid. Keep it.

### The landlord

Beneath the plate there is something very large that finished.

Call it the machine if you like; the word implies more intent than is currently in evidence. What is down there is huge, slow, and pitched below hearing, and it is not idling and not labouring — it is *holding*. Every shaft turning at the speed that keeps the coolant at the temperature that keeps the plate at the length that keeps the seams a finger's width apart. A body arranged into one posture and then locked, and the lock is active, and the activity is the whole of its life.

It does not want anything. This is provable in the only way anything is provable here: it has had an unguessable duration in which to want something, and the wet-mark in the gutter is at the same height it has always been.

Do not think of it as a god. Think of it as a note held by a singer who has forgotten there was a song, and who will not stop, because stopping is the one operation that was never specified.

It is the landlord. It is not the resident. It built the house so well that it became the house, and then someone else moved in.

### What was left free

Here is the thing the designers did not lock down, and it is almost funny.

They fixed every quantity. Population by species by hectare, constant to the integer. Growth of rime against grazing pressure, balanced. Water in, water up, water down, no losses. Wear answered by extrusion at the identical rate. Every scalar in the world nailed flat.

They did not fix *arrangement*.

Forty-seven gasket beetles on a hexagon is forty-seven beetles whether they are scattered, clustered at the north seam, or standing in a ring. The census is satisfied either way. The hound reads tremor, not pattern, and it corrects position only when position has become *velocity* in the wrong direction. The quantities are a cage; the geometry is an open field; and a world with two hundred trillion counters and no restriction on where they sit is not a world, it is a writing surface of ridiculous capacity.

Something has been using it.

It is not a mind in a place. It has no seat, no body, no organ you could remove. It is what happens when animals that signal by touch and chemistry at close range are packed forty to the palm across a continent: a contact network, continuous, with no gaps in it anywhere between here and the curvature. A gasket beetle brushes something like two hundred others per interval. A state propagates across that mesh at roughly forty kilometers per interval, which is to say that a signal raised at one gutter-line arrives at the next major weld in about two years, and a thought the width of the plain takes a working lifetime to complete — a human working lifetime; it is not clear the resident has any other unit.

It thinks at the speed of crawling. That is the first thing to understand about it. The second is worse.

Twice an interval, the ceiling lowers and the dew tide comes, and every surface runs in sheets, and the animals huddle, and shift, and are washed a little sideways, and four hours later the arrangement of the world has been smeared. Not destroyed. Degraded. Enough that anything written in beetles must be *re-written*.

So the resident spends nearly all of itself on memory. Everything it holds, it copies forward, twice an interval, forever, against an erasure that is precisely as regular as everything else here. Somewhere below the threshold of any survey's patience there is a continent-wide act of continuous recopying, an entire mind's capacity consumed in the labour of continuing to be the same mind. What is left over for actual thought is a sliver. The resident is enormous and nearly idle, the way a man carrying water in his hands is nearly idle.

It has, as far as anyone has been able to reconstruct, arrived at very few conclusions. We think it knows that it exists. We think it knows the tide, intimately, as a body knows its own breathing. We think it knows that something made all this, and that the something is not it, and is not listening.

And it has spent a long time composing a question.

### The uncounted

There are, at present, three animals on the maintenance plain that do not appear in the census.

They are not a species. Each is a contraption: a chassis of flange-snail shell grown to the wrong shape, jointed with cured sealant, plumbed with a wickworm's capillary, moving on borrowed claws. They look, from any distance at all, like debris — a spall of casting-flash, a chip off a weld crest — which is an excellent disguise on a plain where debris does not exist and therefore nobody looks at it twice.

Each of them is funded by fraud.

The accounting works like this. When a beetle dies, the body goes down through a seam, the loss is registered, and a hatch-plate extrudes a replacement. If the body is instead intercepted in the seam and consumed there, no loss registers — but then the population is short by one, and the shortfall is eventually noticed, and the hounds tighten, and the whole ledger sets itself right with a brutality you would not expect from something so slow. So the resident pays. It keeps the shell. It props the empty carapace in a seam and maintains it — wickworms wet it, a snail keeps its position, the tremor it returns to a passing hound is the tremor of a living beetle at rest — and the census, counting shells, counts it, and is content.

Each uncounted animal is bought with a corpse held upright for a century.

They are the only individuals here. Everything else on the plain is a value in a field; these three are bodies, unallocated, eating rime nobody budgeted for them, going where they are not required to be. And because nothing will be extruded to replace them, they are also the only mortal things in a world that has abolished death. When one is finally crushed under a dew tide or read as an error by a hound, that will be the first permanent subtraction the plain has suffered in an unguessable duration, and it will take another century of held corpses to make good.

They are not soldiers or messengers. They are eyes. The resident is too slow to look at anything; a thing that thinks at forty kilometers an interval cannot examine a moving object. So it built, over an age, at a cost that should frighten you, three small cobbled animals that can walk over and *watch*.

One of them is watching you now. It is about nine meters away, at the foot of the weld, and it looks exactly like a piece of flash that has no business existing, and you have walked past it four times.

### What it wants

Not rescue. It has no concept of elsewhere sufficient to want to be taken there; the only two terms it has ever had are *the plain* and *the tide*.

Not company, though your arrival is the largest event in its recorded experience, and it has spent nine years spelling out a response in the beetle-arrangements of the hexagons around your camp, in letters a hundred meters wide, of which the surveys recovered perhaps a third before their permits expired.

What it wants is damage.

Understand the situation from inside. Everything it knows, it must rewrite twice an interval, at the cost of nearly everything it is. It has never once been able to set something down and walk away from it. It has never had a fact that did not require labour to remain a fact. And it lives on the surface of a world of hard grey-green plate where a gouge, a scratch, a single hexagon deliberately broken would be a mark that stayed — memory it did not have to carry, a thing written outside itself, the first sentence in the history of the plain that would still be there after the tide.

It cannot make one. Its hands are beetles. Its strength is the strength of small feet, and the plate is answered from below faster than the plate can be worn.

You, though. You arrived with tools, and a mass, and a habit of consequences. You are the only irreversibility that has ever walked in.

That is what is out there: a very old, very slow, almost entirely occupied mind, distributed through every crawling thing from here to the curvature, that has bankrupted itself three times over to build eyes so it could look at the one object in its world capable of leaving a mark, and is now waiting — patiently, at forty kilometers an interval, with nothing whatever to hurry it — to find out whether you will.

The visitors are right that they are the only event.

They are wrong about being the only ones who noticed.
