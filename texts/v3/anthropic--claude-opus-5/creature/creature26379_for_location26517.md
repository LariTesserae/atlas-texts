## The Lantern Under the Hill: Its Tenants

**First, the one that is always there.**

The air.

Not a metaphor. Nine paces by nine and a half of hot water with a hand's breadth of atmosphere lidded over it, and that atmosphere cannot leave except by the vent in the southeast haunch, and cannot be replaced except by the same hole. It is therefore a single body of air, the same air, hour after hour: pressed, released, pressed, released, on a cycle of a hundred and some breaths. It is saturated past bearing. It is at blood heat and a little over. It carries the spring's mineral breath, faintly sulphurous, faintly sweet, like a wet coin held in the mouth, and it holds the water's fizz in suspension so that the top of the room tastes of the bottom.

It lives there the way a snail lives in a shell it has no way of leaving. When the surge comes it is squeezed and screams out through the vent-hole, thin, unhinged, and then, in the trough, it drags itself back in with that wet gulp, and for a moment the whole room's pressure eases and your ears click. If you are at the dome when it happens you are the thing being breathed through. A swimmer with her nose in the last inch of it, cheek against the burnished brick, is inside the exhalation of the only creature in the place, and the creature's exhalation is the only reason she can be there.

The builders do not think of it as alive. They have a hand-sign for it, though, and the sign is not the sign for *air*. It is two fingers laid across the wrist, which is also their sign for *pulse*.

**Second, the ones that have made a claim.**

In the two far corners, where the water is one degree less hostile than the rest, the bruise-coloured felt.

There are perhaps four handspans of it in one corner and two in the other, and it took eleven weeks to become visible. It arrived as nothing, as a smear in the spring's own water, filtered but not sterile, and it has been sorting itself out ever since: which of them can take the heat, which of them can take the glare, which of them can bear both and still divide. What is there now is the survivors' committee. Under a lens it would be a felt of dark filaments lying in one direction, like hair combed by the slow drift of the inflow, with a sheath of clear jelly over the whole and a faint sugaring of lime already caught in it. It photosynthesises furiously. In the noon hour it lifts a fine chain of bubbles off itself, a slow silver fizz distinguishable from the water's own fizz by being organised, by coming from somewhere, and this is the only thing in the room that does anything on purpose.

It is not decorative and it is not incidental. It is the first citizen. Everything that will eventually live in this room — the future crust, the future softness, the deep brown-black shag that will one day upholster the whole floor and make the steps safe to stand on and make the light dull — is being decided now, in those two corners, by that felt. The room is bright and hard and loud and empty because the felt has not finished arguing with itself. Give it a decade of undisturbed corners. Give it the lime it is already trapping. It will build.

The swimmers scrub it off once a month with a bundle of twigs, because they are trying to keep the light. They do not get all of it. They have never once got all of it. There is a woman on the crew who has begun, without saying so, to scrub less thoroughly in the second corner, out of something she could not name if she had breath to name it in.

**Third, the ones leaning in from outside.**

Up at the rim of the shaft, in the daylight, on the dry hillside where the goats go, there is a collar of maidenhair fern that has no business on that hill at all. It found the steam. All summer, while the grass around it goes to straw and cracks underfoot, the fern sits in a private climate a forearm wide, green as a wet thumb, breathing the room's exhalations. It is a plant living off a building's breath. Its fronds move constantly in the updraught, so that from below, from the water, the top of the shaft is a bright square with a soft fringe of motion around it, and it looks — this is unavoidable — like something looking down.

Some of its roots have come over the lip and are hanging a hand's length inside, white, hairlike, glassy, feeling the hot dark. They will not get much further. But they are, technically, the only roots in the room, and if you swim directly under the shaft at midday and look up you can see them lit from behind, a small bright tangle in the column of sun, waving.

And on the stair, four steps above the water, a caper. Three leaves. A seed dropped by a bird that never went down. It has taken a mortar joint on the dry wall, thickened its little stem, and set itself to the business of prising the joint apart, which it will do, given fifty years, with the total patience of its kind. It is the only inhabitant of the Lantern that is engaged in demolition. Everything else is engaged in filling in.

**Fourth, and this is the real answer.**

The lime.

It is not alive. It does not want anything. But if you ask what lives in this room in the sense of *what is at home here, what is growing here, what will be here longest, what is making the place into the shape of itself* — it is the dissolved lime coming out of solution, laying itself down on every surface it touches, thinner than a fingernail's growth in a season, patient beyond any animal's patience.

It is already the most successful tenant. Look at the lowest steps: that faint sugaring on the arris, that bloom, that softening of the edge. Look at the chisel-ghosts in the corners going gauzy under their own frost. Every mark of every hand that made this room is being quietly upholstered by the water the room was built to hold, and the room's own hardness — its glare, its edge, its eggshell shine — is a temporary condition, an infancy. In a hundred years the dome will have grown down into stubby stone fingers. The vent will be a whistle-hole and then a pinhole and then a scar, and the air-creature will suffocate. The bronze sluice will be a green lump inside a white lump. The floor will be a slow dune. The light, if the shaft is still clear, will come down into a soft brown cave and be swallowed.

The builders know. That is the meaning of the tally marks and the corrected arc: they are not records, they are measurements of a growth. They are watching the room close.

**Fifth. The warm ones, who do not live here, and do.**

Six of them, at present, and one who comes when sent for.

*The counter.* He is on the ninth step most days from mid-morning, water at his chest, and his whole occupation is to hold the number. Surges since dawn, breaths since the last surge, the count in his body rather than in his head, because you cannot think in that noise. His hair is bleached at the ends and stiff. His fingertips are permanently pruned and slightly translucent. He has a fine white grit in his eyebrows by the end of a shift, and when he climbs out into the dry grass and it dries, he can flake it off with a thumbnail: the room, having a go at lining him. The bone inside his ears has begun to grow a small lip, narrowing the canal, and so the roar is fractionally quieter for him each season than the season before, and this is the room lining him from the inside. He is the only person alive who knows what the Lantern sounds like at four different stages of his own deafness. He has no way to tell anyone.

*The burnisher.* She is not usually down there any more; her work is finished. But her thumb is in the north wall, a shallow dent smoothed over and not quite erased, at the height where a person leaning to reach a difficult patch would brace. She knows exactly where it is. When she does go down she swims to it and puts her thumb in it, and the fit is still exact, and it is the only touch in the room that is a greeting. In nine years it will be under stone.

*The rope-boy.* Above, in the grass, in the sun, with the line over his shoulder and a leather pad to keep it off his neck, in the only silence for a hundred paces. Nothing comes out of the shaft: not the roar, not the shriek, not the bass thud of the surge. He sits by a dark square in the ground that gives off a faint warmth and a smell of coins and says nothing all day, and holds a thread that is the whole of his knowledge of six people. One tug rising. Two hold. Three out now. He has never had to give three. He rehearses it constantly.

*The woman of the arc.* She scratched the solstice mark with a knife, and then scratched it again the following year a finger's width lower, and then crossed the first one out. This is the smallest and most consequential act anyone has performed in the Lantern. It means the room is being *checked*: that its light is a claim someone is testing, and that when the claim and the room disagree, the room wins and the mark moves. Her hands are ruined. Alkaline water and lime plaster have taken the skin off her knuckles to a shine, and her nails are soft. She dreams in the room's noise and finds ordinary rooms suspiciously thin.

*The sluice-man,* who comes at the sluice hour with an iron bar and turns the wooden screw and lets the mineral load run downhill, and who is the only one who ever swims into a current in that room, and who does it with a line round his chest and a knot at his sternum, and who has felt what the grille wants. He does not talk about the pull. He would not be able to describe it. Patient is the word he would reach for and reject.

*The signer.* Not a job, an accident: someone had to invent the hand-language, and she did, mostly in the first month, mostly badly, and it has been rubbed smooth by use like everything else here. *Surge.* *Enough.* *Light.* *Wait, wait, wait.* Some of it has escaped. Up in the village they have begun using the room's sign for *hold* — flat palm turned slowly down — at the well, at the loom, at children, with no idea that it was born eleven paces under a hillside by people who could not shout.

*And the one who is sent for.* Once a season, or when a family can pay, someone is brought up the dry slope and down the stair and let into the water to the chest and held there for as long as the counter permits, in the roar, in the impossible white-green light, with their own shadow hanging in front of them like a blue absence and dissolving at arm's length. They are ill, or they are grieving, or they have been told to come. They cannot speak, so nothing is asked of them and nothing can be explained. They are in the presence of the fern's white roots and the bruise-coloured felt and the air's pulse and the lime's inaudible snowfall, and they always look, first, straight up the shaft at the daylight, and then, after a while, down at the disc of darker stone in the floor.

That disc is the seventh tenant, and the only one with a schedule. Forty breaths, on certain days of certain weeks, at true noon, it glows like a coin on a forge. The rest of the year it is a dark eye in a bright floor, waiting, and the light-patch climbs the east wall without it.

That is who lives here. An imprisoned breath; a felt of survivors in two corners deciding whether to stay; a fern feeding on steam, and its roots feeling into the dark; a caper prising at the door; a slow white sediment that will inherit everything; and six people being gradually lined, from the inside and the out, by a room they made and can no longer be told about.

Above, the goats. The dry grass. The dark square in the ground you could step into without noticing.

No sound at all comes out of it.
