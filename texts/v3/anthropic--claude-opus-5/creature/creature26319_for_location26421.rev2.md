## The People Up Here

They are, before anything else, people who are always very slightly out of plumb, and who mind.

In the first weeks after thaw you hear the wrench before you see anyone: a flat clanking, a pause, two more, working its way down a terrace. The levelling crews are three people — instrument, book, wrench — and they go around the town like doctors on rounds, kneeling at the threaded feet of every building, every mast, every water tank and stair. The instrument reads. The book is consulted. The wrench turns a quarter, a half, once in a bad year a full revolution, and the number goes into the register beside the date, and the crew moves twelve metres and does it again. A house in Sill has a column in that book eight hundred entries long, which is the only biography anyone up here really gets. Births and marriages are recorded in the civil office in an ordinary way. But the register says how much your grandmother's kitchen rose in the year she died, and that is the document people ask to see.

Levellers know things nobody else knows. They know that the north end of Sill has come up eleven centimetres more than the south since Bench Forty was cut, so that the whole town is a wedge lifting its face very slowly toward the interior. They know which streets are drifting apart and which are closing. They have a professional vice, universally mocked and universally tolerated: they cannot be at a table without looking along it. A leveller in a tavern will set down her glass, watch it, and move it two fingers to the left without appearing to notice she has done so, and everyone at the table will pretend not to notice either.

The lowland continents call all Grade people *levellers*, as an insult. Up here nobody has a word for themselves at all. They say *up here* and *down there* and consider the matter settled.

---

The body adapts and it shows. Chests deepen by the second generation; children grow slowly and late and then all at once; blood runs thick enough that the medical office keeps a standing order about it. But the eyes are the thing. Ten thousand ponds throwing an unfiltered sky back up at you will take your sight in the end, and everyone up here knows roughly when — sixty, sixty-five, a little later if you were indoors work. So blindness is not a catastrophe here. It is a stage of life, like grey hair, and the towns are built for it.

This is why the streets are stepped instead of sloped, why every corner stone carries the grid in relief, why handrails have counted notches at the junctions, why doorways are deep enough to feel your way into. And it is why the levelling matters more than any outsider guesses. A sighted person does not need the floor to be true. A blind woman of seventy, crossing her own town in the dark of the long winter with her hand skimming the rail, needs the floor to be exactly as true as it was last year — and she will know, four days before the instrument does, when it isn't. Every levelling crew in the eleven towns keeps a list of the old grey-eyed who have complained of a room feeling wrong, and they go and look, and they are almost never disappointed. The elders find the movement. The wrench answers it.

The plateau-born can also judge distance, which no visitor ever learns. They do it by grain: whether they can see the little shadow on the east side of the gravel, whether a bird's call arrives with its body or behind it. Ask a Grade child how far and she will look for a long moment at the ground between, not at the thing, and then tell you within fifty metres.

---

The Ecology Office is the strangest institution up here and the one the rest of the towns are quietly proudest of.

It employs perhaps ninety people across the whole plateau. Their work is crust: mapping it, measuring it, marking the paths that keep boots off it, and maintaining the registry of named patches — eight hundred and some polygons of black-green varnish that have been under continuous observation long enough to have individual files, individual growth curves, individual notes in six different hands about the winter of the bad ice. Officers speak of them the way stockmen speak of animals. *Forty-two is sulking.* *We lost most of Bench Nine's west lobe to a survey party who ought to have known better and there is a letter.*

They think in eighty-year units and are entirely serene about it. The same office has been writing, once every decade, to the Instrumentation — that is, to a three-metre grey mast that has never acknowledged anything — a courteous standard letter requesting clarification on the exclusion behaviour of the introduced fox. Two hundred and some years of this. The letters are filed, indexed, cross-referenced, and every new officer is expected to read the entire correspondence before writing the next one, which they do, and which takes a week. They are not deluded and they are not joking. They are simply people for whom two centuries is an acceptable length for a conversation, and they would consider it slovenly to stop merely because nothing has happened yet.

---

The summer people are different people, or the same people behaving differently.

When the midge columns come off the flats the whole economy inverts. Every household that can be spared goes out to the netting towers on the rail spurs — long trains of fine mesh hauled slowly upwind through the smoke of insects, the nets going from white to grey to black in an afternoon. The catch is pressed, dried, milled, and enters the soil bank as credit. Children work the sorting tables and come home green to the elbow. It is the one filthy, stinking, joyous fortnight of the year in a place otherwise scrubbed to the bone, and people talk about it for the other fifty weeks.

The accountancy is exact and nobody resents it. Every household can recite its nitrogen the way lowlanders recite a debt. The four sandpiper species that nest nowhere else are untouchable, absolutely, without any legislation being necessary. The geese are counted. The brindled gulls, which lay on bare scree, yield a strictly rationed harvest, and the custom is one egg per household per year, eaten together on the evening the quota clears. It is the closest thing the Grade has to a festival, and what it actually is, when you look at it, is a closing of books.

---

The foxes came up on the works trains in the second century and have been diverging ever since. They are smaller now, paler, short-eared, and they den in the cooling cracks where the frost cannot reach them. Town foxes are shameless and flat foxes are not. Both go fat in July and desperate by March, and both have learned that the soil bank fence is the most interesting structure on the plateau.

They will not go within forty metres of a mast. Nobody has ever seen one make the decision; they simply are never there, and the grid of exclusion zones shows up clean as printing on the ecology maps.

The ravens are the only creatures up here that have learned human time. They know shift-change at the terminus, they know which of the eleven towns rings a bell and which uses a horn, and in spring they follow the levelling crews, because a levelling crew kneels a great deal and eats where it kneels. A raven will sit four metres off and watch the wrench go round and wait. Of everything that lives on the Grade — the crust, the sedge, the shrimp, the eleven thousand people of Sill turning their house-feet a quarter-turn against the swelling rock — the ravens are the only ones with any leisure, and they seem to know it.

---

The Line crews live on the water and are the nearest thing the plateau has to a folk. They move the pontoon spans when the braids shift, which means they have to say where the water will be next week, and since water is the one genuinely undecided thing in this landscape, they are the only people up here who deal in prediction. So they have sayings, and bets, and a whole private vocabulary for the behaviour of a sheet two centimetres deep. They are regarded by the townspeople as slightly unserious. They regard the townspeople as furniture.

The Drawings Office regards everyone as a risk to the drawings.

Nobody has been below in four centuries. The works run, silently, with no living hand on them, and what the Office does — has done, generation after generation, in a low room in Sill with the best light in the town — is keep the drawings legible. Recopy. Verify against three independent copies held in three separate towns. Reconcile. Their nightmare, the thing they train against for a decade before they are allowed a pen, is consensus error: three copies that agree, and agree wrongly, and have agreed wrongly since some tired evening in the fourth century. They are not priests and would be insulted by the word. They are, functionally, priests.

---

The children run on water. That is what a Grade childhood is: the long light, a black rock floor, and two centimetres of meltwater spread over forty acres, and thirty children flat-out through it with the spray coming up gold behind them until the horizon stops working and you cannot tell a friend at two kilometres from a friend at four hundred metres who has somehow become very small. They dare each other at the masts. They drop a coin and watch it fall that famous fraction slow, and shriek, and do it again. They learn to count the reports and to sleep through them by about age four.

Some of them leave, later, and come back. Those are a recognisable type up here — the returned, who tried the thick air and the soil and the noise and could not sleep down there, because down there nothing cracks in the night and nothing needs levelling in spring and the ground has already decided everything it is ever going to decide. They describe the lowlands, almost always, in the same way: *like a house where nothing is being built.*

---

And in the end everyone goes into the pans.

The soil bank keepers are the only people on the Grade whose hands are always warm and always dirty, and the only ones who smell of anything. Under the glass it is twelve degrees and heavy with eight centuries of compost, fish waste, milled crust, night soil, and the dead — turned, forked, rested, turned again. Visitors are taken there as a kind of test. Grade people go there to remember, and what they do is ask the keeper to turn a pan over, and then stand and breathe.

They are not settlers. Settling implies somewhere to arrive. They are ancestors by trade: eleven towns of people keeping a floor true and a crust unbroken and a set of drawings uncorrupted on behalf of a green country two hundred thousand years out that not one of them, not one, in any generation, will ever see. Nothing up here is level except on purpose. Everything straight in this whole two thousand kilometres was made straight this spring, by somebody kneeling, with a wrench, calling out a number to be written down.
