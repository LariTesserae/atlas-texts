## The drift and its eaters

Start with the fact that nothing here eats a plant if it can help it.

The drift is the diet. It comes up the wall out of the forests, four thousand metres of rising air carrying everything too small to resist it: aphids in their millions, thrips, midges, biting flies, small drab moths, mayflies that hatched that morning and will never find water again, spiderlings paying out silk and going up instead of across. They rise, and at some altitude the cold takes the flight out of them, and after that they are cargo. They come over the rim already dead or dying and settle in the lee of every raised thing, and the plateau — which grows nothing worth eating — is therefore one of the best-fed surfaces in the world.

So: measure the life here in what it does with a windrow of dead insects.

**Under the stripes.** Lift a cobble on the stone stripes, on the leeward side of the line where the grit collects, and you will find the drift packed in like meal in a sack, and the whole economy standing in it.

Springtails first, in numbers that have no useful name. They are pale, almost colourless, and they do not spring. The fork under the belly is still there, fused down, kept as a catch and not a spring, because the first ancestor to launch itself into forty knots was carried out over the rim and was not anybody's ancestor after that. They flow instead. They pour along the underside of the stone in a slow grey tide, and they are eaten by everything.

Then the beetles, which plough. Flightless, of course — wing-cases fused into one hard dome, the seam gone, so that a beetle here is a single sealed pebble with legs, and can be stepped on by a sheep without harm. They are ten and twelve years old, some of them. They work upwind into the drift with their heads down, and every beetle on this plateau is polished bright on its front and dull on its back, exactly like every boulder, exactly like the flakes the harvesters leave, so that if you turned out a jar of them onto the table they would all crawl the same way and you could use them for a compass.

Then the spiders. Jumping spiders, thousands to the hectare, crammed into every crack wide enough to take a silk cell, and each one carrying that absurd pair of forward eyes like a face. They cannot balloon; that instinct is gone, burned out of the population by forty kilometres of one-way air. They walk to their futures instead, a metre a day, in the lee of the stripes.

And every day they eat their cousins. The spiderlings that come over the rim on silk are the same species, or near enough — the lowland stock, the ones that still fly. They arrive chilled and folded and are taken out of the drift and eaten by their own relatives, who long ago gave up going anywhere. It has been happening for as long as the wind has been locked. There is no word for it. It is simply the shape of the arrangement.

## The one that drums

The cricket is a thumb-joint long, wingless, the colour of wet ash, and it has no ears at all — not reduced, not vestigial; the whole apparatus is absent, as if the lineage had struck the item off a list.

It courts by drumming. The male finds a piece of stone that rings — a thin flake propped at an angle, a slab with air under one edge — and beats his abdomen against it in a pattern that runs six or seven seconds and then rests, and the pattern goes out through the rock, through the grit, through the packed drift, into the crevices, where the females are lying with their legs spread flat and their tarsal organs pressed to the floor, reading it.

The best drum-stones are handed down. Not by the crickets; they only find them. But a flake that rings is rare, and a flake that rings is used by male after male for decade after decade, and is worn smooth in one small oval where a hundred generations of abdomens have struck it. The harvesters, who knap obsidian at the Flue Field because there is nothing else to do with an evening, leave flakes. Within a few seasons the good ones are occupied. It is the only case anyone knows of in which a tool-user's rubbish became another animal's instrument, and the harvesters are aware of it, and they will show you: a man lies down, puts two fingers on the rock, taps twice — *I hear you* — and waits, and quite often something under the stone answers.

That gesture is not a metaphor down there. On stone it is a true sentence. It is only in the air that nobody hears anything.

## Eleven foxes

There are eleven foxes on the plateau. There were eleven when the last party came up, and eleven when their grandfathers came, and the number is not a coincidence or a count; it is a property of the place, like the height of the cloud.

The fox is small, pale as the ground, and wrong-looking to anyone who knows foxes, because the ears are short and set low and the eyes are too large and too far forward. Hearing is worth nothing here. It hunts by eye, standing on a boulder for an hour without moving, watching for the specific glitter of a beetle crossing open grit; and it hunts by foot, because a fox at rest keeps all four pads flat and reads the rock the way the cricket's mate reads it. It can feel a hare thump from two hundred metres. It can feel a man walking before he crests the stripe.

And it speaks by smell, which in this place is a strictly one-way medium.

A fox marks at the upwind edge of its ground, and the mark then belongs to everything downwind of it, out to the end of the tableland and over the north rim into the cloud. So the whole social order is a matter of position. The old vixen who holds the rim territory — the first four kilometres, where the fog comes in and the hares are — is known to every fox alive here, and knows none of them. She broadcasts. They receive. The young males far out on the dry stone are anonymous in the only direction that counts; they can be smelled by nobody but the sky. To be upwind is to be a person. To be downwind is to be an audience.

When a fox dies, a downwind fox moves up within a fortnight, and everything below it shifts a notch, and the number stays eleven. They are not waiting for each other exactly. But they know where the gap will be.

## The hull-shaped ones

The hares live only in the fog belt and only for the rime. They lick it off the lee faces of boulders in the early hours, horizontal feathers of ice snapping off against their teeth, and that is their whole water supply; they will never in their lives drink from the stream. They crop the cushion plants, and a hare's entire lifetime of cropping costs one cushion plant about eleven years of growth. Ordinary animals, eating at geological speed.

They lie in forms scratched into the lee of the stone stripes, facing downwind so the wind lays the fur flat along them, and they thump, and the thump is the loudest voice on the plateau in the only band that carries.

The sheep come in bands of nine or eleven and never in bands of ten. Nobody can say why. The fleece is felted into a grey board on the windward flank, hard as bark, and hangs long and combed on the lee, so that a sheep standing broadside is two different animals stuck together. They graze into the wind, working upslope through the morning, and come back down with it in the afternoon, and they face one way when they are still. A band of sheep seen at three kilometres is a row of identical marks all leaning the same direction, like handwriting.

They die of their teeth, at eleven or twelve, and then they belong to the red bird.

## The colony

Then the swifts, which are the plateau's second fact, and possibly its first.

They do not migrate. They are the only swifts anywhere that don't, and the reason is simply that migration is a search for a wind like this one, and they are standing in it. The wave off the rim holds all day and all night and all year. The insect traffic never stops coming up. There is no season here to leave.

So they turn. Millions of them, a slow black river above the Flue Field, feeding on the drift while it is still alive and airborne, an hour or two before it will become the drift. They sleep up in the black-blue at the top of the light, in shifts, one hemisphere at a time. They mate up there. Their feet have gone to a hook and a ghost of a hook; a swift knocked down by hail cannot stand, and cannot launch, and dies on its side with its wings out, and this is so common that the ground beneath the colony is composed of it.

They nest in the tafoni, and every cavity in those pinnacles is a whistle, and so every nest is inside a note. A nestling's world for six weeks is a slot of doubled light, a wall of sound it will never be able to perceive as sound, and a parent arriving at intervals with a throat-pouch of ten thousand aphids compressed into a grey bolus the size of a grape. The parent finds the hole at speed, in a forty-knot flow, among ten thousand identical holes, by feel: each cavity mouth makes its own standing eddy, its own small structure of turbulence, and the bird flies into the shape of it the way a key goes into a lock. They never miss. You can watch for a whole afternoon and not see one miss.

The fledging is a drop. The young bird falls out of its slot into the wave and either flies or does not, and if it flies it will not touch any solid object again for two years, possibly three, until it comes back to the same pinnacle and finds a hole and lands for the first time since it was a chick.

And they scream. That is the thing that undoes people. Swifts scream; it is what they are; and these ones have kept the whole apparatus and the whole behaviour, gaping and calling in the parties that wheel around the towers at evening — and nothing has ever heard it. Not once. Not the birds themselves, who are deafened from the egg. It goes into a hundred decibels of chord and is gone. A million throats open and shut above you, and the roar does not change, because the roar never changes.

## The mischief, and the red bird

The choughs work the guano ground, the forty hectares of hand-deep moss under the colony, turning it for grubs, and they are the only animals here that visibly do things for no reason. They cannot call — they still try, with the bill and the throat, and nothing comes of it — so they have gone over entirely to gesture: wing-flash, the white flick of an opened tail, a particular bow with the red bill held wide that seems to mean something quite specific and rude. They carry objects up into the wave and let them go. They pull feathers out of the drift and drop them and catch them. They will take a harvester's bone hook and tour it around the pinnacle twice before losing interest.

Above them all, a single pair of the big bone-eaters, and sometimes a second pair out on the western shoulder. They take the sheep skeletons; they carry a femur up five hundred metres and drop it onto one particular slab near the head of the stream, which is ankle-deep in splinters and has been the anvil for so long that the slab itself is dished. One chick every second or third year. Often it fails.

They are also the only colour on the plateau. There is a seep on the western shoulder where the water comes out iron-red, thirty paces of rust in ninety per cent pale, and the birds bathe in it and come out stained orange down the breast, deliberately, for reasons nobody has ever satisfactorily explained about any bird of that kind. In this white place, a thing that goes and makes itself red.

## The hole where you can hear

And then the Sink.

Step down over the lip of the shaft, four or five metres, and the roar falls off your shoulders like a coat. Not silence — a hush, with the stream in it. You can hear the water. Most people who come up here hear water for the first time in six days at the Sink and have to sit down.

Everything that needs to hear, lives in that hole, and nowhere else.

The midges. Midges swarm by sound — the males' antennae are tuned to the wingbeat of a female and to nothing else in the world — and so there is not one midge breeding anywhere on forty kilometres of tableland except in the thirty-pace fringe of moss and hair-grass round that shaft, where the air is still enough for a wingbeat to mean something. The column of them above the Sink on a bright afternoon is the loudest thing here, and it is a hum you could cover with your hand.

And a cricket with ears. Small, brown, entirely ordinary, singing the ordinary song of its kind, in a bubble of quiet the size of a house, surrounded on every side by forty kilometres of its deaf, mute, drumming cousins. Nobody knows which came first. The valley people assume this one is the old one, the original, still doing what crickets did before the wind locked — a survival, held in a pocket, the way a phrase survives in a place-name after the language is gone.

The midges rise out of the Sink at midday, up out of the quiet into the moving air, and the swifts stand off the lip in a shallow arc and take them as they come over, one fountain going up and one river going past, and none of those midges ever come down again.

## Beneath the note

Lie flat some evening with your jaw on the rock, out on the stripes, and listen with your teeth.

The beetle traffic, which is constant and sounds like grain pouring. The hares thumping in the fog belt, a long way off, with the thud arriving as a kind of pressure. A cricket working a flake, six seconds and a rest, six seconds and a rest. Spider drumming, which is nearly too fine to separate from the beetles. Sheep, occasionally, coming down the slope with the wind behind them — four hooves that you feel as a rhythm before you can believe in an animal. And somewhere under all of it a low continuous grinding that is only the pinnacles making the note, coming back at you through the stone from the other side.

That is the whole society of this place. It has been going on beneath your feet since you arrived, and it is entirely inaudible, and it is the reason the harvesters sleep with their heads on the rock rather than on a rolled skin, and the reason two fingers on the breastbone means what it means.

## What it is, all together

None of these creatures has a surplus. That is the thing you notice last, and then cannot stop noticing.

The spiders eat the fallout at the rate it falls. The foxes eat the spiders and the beetles and hold at eleven. The hares crop the cushions at the rate the cushions grow, which is almost not at all, which is why the hares are few. The swifts lose hundreds a day to the cold and the walls and replace them exactly. The bone-birds raise one chick in three years and one of them dies every so often and there are still two. Every population here is pressed flat against its ceiling and has been for longer than anyone can testify to, and the result is not scarcity and not abundance but something that looks, from a distance, like a machine running true.

And what feeds the machine is not here. That is the joke at the centre of it. This is a place that grows nothing and eats constantly — a mouth held open at two thousand metres, taking in a forest four kilometres below and thirty kilometres downwind, one aphid at a time, for as long as the gradient holds. Every living thing on this tableland is an organ of a body whose stomach is up here and whose food is somewhere else entirely, and the wind is the throat.

So when they tell you the plateau does not change, they are telling the truth about the plateau and not about the arrangement.

The old harvesters will say, if you get them at the right moment, that the cakes were different when they were young. Not worse. Different. More moth in them, or less; a green taste that has gone; a year when everything tasted of one particular small fly and no one could name it. They mention it the way you mention weather, and then they let it go, because a difference you can only taste is not evidence of anything.

But it is the only clock up here. The stripes don't move. The rime is the same length it was. The note holds its pitch to the hertz. And every day, all day, this finished and perfect place opens its mouth into the wind and swallows a thin grey report from a country that is still happening.
