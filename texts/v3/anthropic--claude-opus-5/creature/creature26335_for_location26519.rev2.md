## The oldest tenant

Before anything with a stem, before any hand, the Bell was inhabited by its glaze.

The greenish crust on the older terraces is alive. It is a felt of something one cell thick that takes hydrogen sulfide out of the breath and lays down sulfur and a little water behind itself as it goes, and it has been doing this at a rate of perhaps a hair's thickness in a human lifetime since the shaft first opened. It is the reason the blockwork does not sand away faster than it does; it is the skin over the city's skin. It phosphoresces because of a waste product and not for any reason that concerns anyone. Where it is thickest — the north and west quarters, the first-built — it has flowed a little, in the way glass is said to flow, and sealed old wedge-niches shut, and the tuners have to chip it out and it grows back over the chip in four or five years.

Everything else here lives downstream of it, off the water it leaves behind. Including the people. That is worth holding onto: the whole arrangement, the terraces, the calendar, the names, rests on a smear.

## The green population

Call them plants because there is no better word, but understand that they are closer to something like coral: sessile, colonial, and in the Bell's case very probably one individual.

Lift a block with a wedge and look under it. There is a mat down there, pale as tallow, warm to the back of the hand, felted, running away in every direction between the courses of the masonry. The masons built on top of a root and their eleven terraces of dressed stone are a trellis laid over one body. What stands up out of it — the canes, the thistles, the figs, the lilies — are organs of that body which have, over a long time, become strangers to one another. They will not fertilize themselves. Each kind requires pollen shaken loose from a distant part of the same creature, and each will only shake at its own pitch, and so the thing is condemned to need a room built around it and a calendar played to it in order to reproduce with itself.

**Flue-thistle.** The commonest. Grey-amber, ribbed, hollow, set directly over the smaller flue-mouths so that it inflates on the exhale and collapses on the inhale. Sheds at a low tone and sheds enormously, a dry fog that makes the upper gallery unusable for a day. Edible stewed; mostly not eaten.

**Sulfur cane.** The structure of the place. Banded resin, scar over scar, a finger a day, tall enough on the eastern terraces to have been cut and used as rafters for the sleeping-niches. Burns blue and quick, which is why it is the most carefully unburned thing in the world.

**Cinnabar fig.** The reason anyone is here. A hard red knuckle of fruit, three to a spur, each holding a swallow and a half of water the colour of weak tea and the temperature of blood-plus. It is the only water within two hundred miles. It is not stored, because nothing here stores; you eat it off the spur, and the spur only fruits if the fig-note sounded in its week. A grown person needs eleven or twelve figs a day. A terrace in good tune carries four hundred. You can do the arithmetic that the tuners do, which is: *how many notes are still in the room* equals *how many of us there can be*.

**Ashwort.** The black creeping mat. Scrape it, dry it on the hot treads, pound it, and it makes a cake that tastes of matchheads and keeps the gut working. It is also the abrasive — the grinding stones are charged with ashwort ash — so the plant that feeds them is also the plant that has been quietly rounding off the flue-mouths for two thousand years. Nobody has ever framed it that way out loud.

**The fourth-note lilies**, the pipe-figs, the black rosette whose name has been lost along with its note. And the stand on the north terraces, tall and glossy and perfectly healthy, which has not set seed in living memory and which is not called anything now, because the word for it was a pitch.

## The animal

There has never been an animal in the Bell except one, and it arrived.

Nobody can say from where. There are copper beads in the thresholds that did not come from here, and graduated measuring rods that imply a mathematics older than the stair, and a story about salt and about walking that survives in a form too worn to be information. What matters is that at some point a warm-blooded, water-drinking, sound-making thing came down the crack, found a room full of plants that could not have children, and — over a few centuries and by increments no single generation would have noticed — became their insect.

They are small. An adult stands about four and a half feet and weighs very little. Long splayed toes with a gap at the hallux for gripping the lip of a block; soles as thick and glazed as the stair treads and just about as sensitive; hands broad, nails worn back to nothing by wedge-work, the pads permanently a half-shade lighter than the rest from heat. Hairless but for a coarse crest along the crown, which is functional: it is where the sulfur comes out. What little they sweat, they sweat as a thin brine that dries and crystallizes yellow at the hairline, and a well child has a crust there like a little diadem, and a sick one does not.

Their eyes are large and see excellently in the deep red and the faint green and are, to all practical purposes, blind at the blue end. Their sense of smell is gone — not weak, gone; the word they use for it means *taste of stone*, and they apply it to things like the difference between a fresh chip and an old one. They cannot cry usefully. They do not wash; they scour, with ashwort grit, and their skin has the polished look of everything else here.

And they are deaf. Not from birth. It takes about twenty years in a room as loud as a river in flood, and it is total by thirty, and it is the single organizing fact of the society.

## The two ages of a person

Because hearing is a consumable, the Bell divides its people into **ears** and **memories**.

An ear is anyone under about eighteen. Ears do the tuning. An ear goes out onto the terraces with a pouch of graded wedges and lays a palm flat on a block — the hand is the real instrument; the room is far too loud to pick a single flue out of the chord, but a single flue can be *felt* in the bones of the wrist — and drives the wedge a hair, and feels, and draws it, and feels. Correct work is felt as a specific and describable itch in the heel of the hand. They train for it from the age of five. It is not a chore or an apprenticeship, it is the whole of childhood; children here are not preparing to be adults, they are already the most important people in the room and know it, and there is a flavour of arrogance in them that the deaf find restful rather than annoying.

A memory is everyone else. Memories make wedges, scrape ashwort, dress blocks, raise children, and — the actual work — *hold pitches*. There is no notation. There was never anything to write on that did not glaze over or blow away, and in any case the thing to be recorded is a frequency, and the only storage medium for a frequency in this place is a throat. So the calendar of the Bell, the entire register of which note makes which plant fruit in which week, exists as a set of hums carried in the heads of the deaf, who cannot hear themselves hum.

They teach it in the stair. Twenty-two steps up, past the second turn, the chord thins enough that a young person can make out an unaided voice. That is the classroom: a dark bend in a staircase, an old woman with her fingers on a child's throat to feel whether the child has it, humming a note she has not heard in forty years and holding it steady by the feel of it in her own skull. When she is satisfied, the child goes down and spends a week finding it in the room.

Every pitch not transferred before a memory dies is a species that will never set seed again. They know this precisely. It is not a metaphor to them and it is not a tragedy either, exactly; it is more like a debt schedule that everyone can recite.

## Names

People are named for flues.

Not for notes in the abstract — for particular mouths in particular quarters, which sound at particular points in the five-hour swell, which means that everyone's name is being said, continuously, by the room, in a rotation, forever. A child hears its own name arrive about five times a day for the first fifteen years of its life and then stops being able to hear it, which is understood to be the beginning of adulthood and is marked by nothing at all.

To call someone, you strike their block with the heel of your hand and they feel it wherever they are standing on that terrace. To speak to someone, you wait: ordinary conversation is conducted in the two-second trough of the nine-second breath, in clipped packets, one clause per inhale. Their grammar has fossilized around this. Sentences have a fixed maximum length. There is no subordinate clause in the language. Anyone from the Bell who ever went up onto the salt and spoke to an outsider would have sounded curt to the point of hostility, and would have found the outsider's long unpunctuated speech physically unbearable, like being talked over by the wind.

When a flue rounds out of true, the name goes flat, and eventually goes out of the register altogether, and the person who carried it must be renamed. This happens more often now than it used to. The oldest people here have had three and four names. Being renamed is neither a celebration nor a bereavement; it is done quickly, with embarrassment, the way one might help someone up.

The last woman in the western quarter has had four.

## Eating, and the grate

Food is cooked on the grate at the bottom of the shaft, by lowering it in a wire — no, not wire; in a woven sling on a long cane, held into the cherry glow for a count, hauled up scorched. There is no other heat-use and no flame anywhere in the Bell, ever, under a prohibition so old and so total that it has stopped being a rule and become a kind of reflex nausea. Children who play at striking sparks are not punished. They are simply looked at, by everyone, until they stop.

The water is the figs. Everything else follows from the figs.

## The dead

No water: no washing, no burial that would not simply mummify, no rot. No fire.

So the dead go to the middle.

They are carried to the lip of the highest gallery and let go into the vacant column, which takes them up — the updraft is quite strong enough — and strips them and dries them and sheds them as flakes, over some weeks, and exhales them through the crack in the crust as part of the faint brown smudge that stands over the salt plain on a still day. This is the second reason the middle is kept empty, and the older of the two reasons, and the one no mason ever needed to explain. A bridge across the Bell would have caught people.

The dust that makes the light visible — the slow braided column you can see on the way down, the thing that turns the cherry glow brown-orange by the time it reaches the upper gallery — is partly them. They are aware of this. They do not make anything of it.

## What is here now

Four, at last count, and the count is old.

Three memories and no ears. There has not been an ear in the Bell for something like nine years, which is not a crisis in the way a flood is a crisis but is nonetheless the end: the last of them is deaf, and cannot check her own work, and knows it.

She is the one in the western quarter. Very small now, very dark, the crest-crust gone thin and white at the temple. She has had four names and the current one is a flue in the east that she chose herself, badly, because it doubles a low thistle-note and gets lost in it, and nobody has struck it in a long time anyway. Her first name — the one she was born to, the one she thinks of as *hers* — belonged to a small bore in the north wall that widened past speaking about sixty years ago. The same season, the north stand stopped setting seed. She has never once said aloud that her name and that stand went out of the room together. She goes up there some days and stands among them while they inflate and slacken, tall and glossy and loud and sterile, and puts a hand on a cane, and comes back down.

She re-wedged two flues in the west this year, or possibly last year, or possibly in a year she has miscounted. She did it by memory and by the itch in the heel of her hand, which is not as reliable as it was, and there is a real chance she has made the chord worse. She left three rods and a plumb-bob leaning against the wall, and she did not come back before the swell, because she went up.

She goes up to listen. Twenty-two steps, two turns, out through the crack, onto the crust at night when the salt is merely hot instead of unbearable. Up there the chord comes out of the ground thin and small, most of it lost, and if she lies with her ear against a polygon of crust she can sometimes — she believes she can sometimes — feel one high note that is not in the room's voice anymore but is in the ground's. She has been trying to match it. She hums, out on the plain, where there is nothing for two hundred miles to hear her, and where her own hum is at last louder than everything else, and she cannot tell if she is close.

The crystal fans at the lip re-grow as fast as they crumble. They have grown over the place where she sits. From above, if you came across it, it would look like nothing: a shoulder-width crack, a column of shivering heat, a scatter of ochre fans in a shape you might, if you were determined, read as someone leaning.

Below, the room keeps breathing on its nine seconds, and the terraces pulse all around like something with a heart, and the flat blurred top of the chord goes on calling two of the four remaining people by names that no longer quite exist.
