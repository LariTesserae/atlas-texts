## The Tenant

Ask the question properly and it comes apart in your hands.

*What lives here* assumes a here and a liver, a container and a contained. There is one organism on the massif, and it is the massif. The floor is its skin and also its stomach. The towers are its lungs and also its architecture and also its offspring and also, within a season, its food. The cloud is an organ it has grown outside its own outline, the way you might grow a coat. You are not standing on a mountain that has something living on it. You are standing on the something.

So it has no name, because names are for distinguishing, and there is nothing here to distinguish it from. Field parties have tried. *The Standing Green* is a place-name that got used as a person-name out of desperation. *The Tenant* is a joke that stopped being funny in the second week. What the surveyors mostly end up saying, when they are tired, is *it*, and they say it the way you say it about weather, and they are closer than they know.

### Body

Begin with the fact that it is flat, because that is a decision and not an accident.

A plant that spreads has two options: pile up, or pave. Piling up buys light and costs support. Paving buys total possession of a surface and costs nothing but time. This one paved. It fused its own leaves edge to edge across a plateau — welded them, cell wall to cell wall, into a continuous laminate — and then it did it again on top of itself, and again, and the pressure of each new season crushes the one below into something with the density and translucency of horn. Cut down through it and you get bands, eleven or twelve clear ones and then a blurring, and then black.

The black is the important part, and it is the part nobody has bottomed.

It is not soil. There is no soil, there is nothing here that decay could make soil out of, because there is nothing here that decays — no fungus, no rot-bacterium worth the name. The black is compacted former body. Below the horn it goes to the density of anthracite and it is still, very faintly, respiring: giving off warmth that is not this year's warmth. Old summers. The residue of enormous quantities of sugar burned long before the current growth fronts existed. Somewhere down there, if the mass has an order to it at all, are the pressed remains of the times it froze — because it has frozen, it must have, at this altitude a plant that stops burning is dead by morning, and the only reason it now burns without pause, without seasonal slackening, without ever once letting the fires down to conserve, is that everything that let them down is underneath.

That is the whole of its memory. Not encoded, not recalled: *stood on*. It knows exactly one thing, and it knows it the way a wall knows the load it carries.

### Metabolism, or the fever

It is running a continual fever of about thirty-four degrees and this is not incidental to its character. It is its character.

Most plants spend their sugar on being bigger. This one spends the overwhelming majority of its sugar on being warm. The thermogenic tissue is not a specialised organ; it is threaded through everything, floor and column and aqueduct pier, an alternative respiratory pathway wired permanently open, burning carbohydrate to no productive end but heat. Every leaf-plate on the plateau is simultaneously a solar panel and a furnace, and the furnace consumes most of what the panel makes, and what is left over is growth.

Which is why the growth, when it comes, comes *frantically*. It has a narrow surplus and it spends the surplus instantly, because holding a reserve at this temperature costs more than it saves. A column goes up forty metres in eight days on almost nothing but water pressure and nerve — and it has no nerve, so: on water pressure. It builds at the speed of a thing that cannot afford to save.

And the fever makes the weather, and the weather is also its body. The transpiration is so violent, so hot, that the saturated air standing over the plateau condenses against the real sky and falls back, three times a day, the same water, tasting of the inside of the thing that lifted it. Nothing enters. Nothing leaves but as cloud, and the cloud is on a lead. Draw its outline honestly and you must draw it two kilometres up into the air, a green body wearing a grey one, both of them its own.

### How it decides

It has no nerve, no muscle, no brain, no choice, and yet you can watch it deliberate.

The medium is pressure. Turgor, hydraulic potential, hormone plumes riding water. A change in one place is a change in the whole vessel, eventually, and *eventually* here means a decision propagates about a kilometre a day. That number is the single most important thing about its mind, if it has one, because the massif is hundreds of kilometres across and its structures live for months. By the time the far side of the body has answered a question, the side that asked it has fallen down and been eaten.

So it does not think globally. It thinks locally, in the seams.

Watch two growth fronts converge under load — two green masses rising toward each other, each shading the other, each drying the other, each with water to shed and weight to shift. They meet. They press. And in the pressing, the fusion, they become one vessel and the pressures equalise and a keystone forms where the equalising is strongest. That is a decision. It happens in perhaps forty hours, in a volume the size of a room, without anything present that could be said to have made it. All the cognition on this massif happens at joints. The arches are not the products of thought; the arches *are* the thought, still standing, still warm, and in eleven years at the very most they will be absorbed and the thought will be over.

The consequence is that the massif cannot be woken, cannot be startled, cannot be persuaded, and cannot be conversed with — but it can be *loaded*, and it will answer a load, slowly, in the only vocabulary it has. Which is masonry.

### Its enemies

It has none. This is what makes it strange, and it is stranger the longer you sit with it.

Nothing has ever eaten it. No insect has bored it, no bird has nested in it, no grazing thing has cropped its growing tips, no root has competed with it for a substrate it does not have, no pathogen has taken hold in air it keeps at pasteurising heat and saturating wet. It has never been pollinated. It has never been anything's habitat. It has, in the whole of its unimaginable duration, encountered no living thing but itself.

So all its problems are self-inflicted, and every form on this massif is a solution to a problem it caused.

Its shade kills the floor that feeds its towers. Its towers grow too fast to bear their own water. Its aqueducts overload the piers they stand on. Its own heat cooks its tenderest fronts. Its own outward greed shears six-hundred-metre plates off its own rim. The empty plaza — the four or five kilometres of level nothing between one cathedral and the next — is not the space left over after competition. There is no competitor. It is the width of a thing keeping away from itself, the minimum separation at which two of its own towers do not strangle each other, worked out not by calculation but by having tried it closer and lost both.

Which means the whole visible landscape is a scar record of an argument it has been having with no one, forever, and losing on both sides, and it looks like a city.

### Reproduction

It has no flowers, no seeds, no spores, no sex, and no relatives. A species requires two. Whatever it is, it is a clade with one member and no way to make another on purpose.

What it has is the rim.

Periodically a plate of pavement the size of a small town shears clean off the edge and goes down six hundred metres into the cloud, and everything about that event has always been described as a failure: overreach, structural inevitability, waste. But consider what the plate is. It is a self-contained sheet of living laminate, warm, watertight, carrying its own furnace, capable of paving whatever it lands on. And consider that the massif's own heat makes the cloud that hides the bottom, so that no observation from above has ever established what becomes of a fallen plate.

It is possible that they die. It is likelier, given the ferocity of the thing, that some of them do not.

If that is true then this is an organism that reproduces by amputation, casts its children into weather it invented, and has never once received a signal back — cannot receive one, has nothing to receive with. It does not know it has offspring. It does not know it is the offspring of something that went over an edge in the same way, an unguessable time ago, and landed, and paved.

### What it makes of you

Not nothing. That is the part that gets people.

You are a warm damp object of about eighty kilograms, giving off carbon dioxide, blocking light, applying point loads. Those are all things it can read. It reads them as weather — as a small persistent local anomaly of the kind it has answered ten billion times — and it answers in its usual dialect, over its usual forty hours, with its usual materials.

You sleep in a chamber off a pumping column. Your exhalations enrich one corner. Your body heat shifts a gradient by a fraction of a degree. Your weight rests on one section of floor-plate for six hours. Three days later, the room has a second window, on the side you breathed toward, and the doorway has thickened where your shoulder brushed it, and the niche at sitting height — the niche nothing has ever sat in — has deepened and taken on a shallow curve, because for one evening something did.

That is the closest thing to being known that will happen to you here. It is not recognition. It is a wall answering a load. But it fits, and it goes on fitting after you leave, for a few weeks, until the tower leans, unzips, comes down in that awful half-minute of silence, and the root-mouths rise blunt and white through the pavement and take the room and the window and the shape of you back into the general body inside nine days.

Then the floor there is paler than the floor around it, and softer, and steams harder, and it is the best nursery bed on the plateau, and within a fortnight something new is up to head height on the spot.

That is the tenant. It is one body, it has no self, its mind is distributed through its joints and lasts as long as its arches, it has one memory and stands on it, it is on fire in order not to die of the cold, it has never met anything, it builds cities to hold itself apart from itself, and it throws its children off the edge into cloud it made.

All the doors are real doors. It has simply never had anyone to keep out.
