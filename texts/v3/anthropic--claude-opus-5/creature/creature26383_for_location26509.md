## What does not live here

Take the list off the table first.

The gasket beetle does not live on the maintenance plain. It occurs there, the way a bead of condensate occurs on the north flank of a weld — reliably, in the right numbers, for exactly as long as the conditions hold. A thing that will be replaced within two hours of its death, by an identical thing, extruded from a plate four hundred meters away, has no biography and therefore no address. It has a slot. Slots are not homes.

Nor does the machine live there. The machine is the house. You do not describe a house as its own tenant, however warm the walls are, however much the walls hum.

But something does live there. It is not on the survey list, because the survey list was compiled by counting, and the residents are, by definition, the ones the count does not reach. That is not an oversight. It is their entire nature and their only protection.

## Flash

In casting, *flash* is the thin excess that squeezes out at the seam of the mould and has to be trimmed. The second survey used the word first, in a footnote, about the frills of hardened overpour still standing on the underlip of the vents. The third survey used it about the animals. The word stuck because nothing better exists.

Here is how a flash is made.

A hatch-plate warms and opens and extrudes a body, wet and folded, and the body unfolds and — if it is correct — performs its first act, which is to seat itself into its function. The beetle seals a seam. The crab finds an empty grille. The worm drops head-down and drinks. That act closes a handshake somewhere below, and the ledger is satisfied, and the plate cools.

Perhaps one extrusion in eleven thousand cannot perform that act. A doubled spine. A gland fused shut. Wings a fraction too heavy for the plume. The handshake never closes. The ledger still reads short. And so, within the hour, the same plate warms again and extrudes a second body, correct this time, which unfolds and walks off into the job.

Every resident of the maintenance plain begins life by watching its own replacement stand up and leave.

After that the ledger has nothing further to say about it, ever. It was never entered. It cannot be subtracted. It will not be replaced when it dies. It is, alone in the world, permitted to have a lifespan.

## What they look like

There is no species. That is the first thing to get straight, and the hardest, because your eye keeps trying.

Each flash is a single unrepeated error and therefore a single unrepeated body. What they have in common is only what the plain forbids everything else: they are **worn**. A flash six years old has a dulled carapace, a chip in the left mandible, a rounded edge where a rounded edge should be impossible. You will see one and think *injured*. It is not injured. It is old, and you have never seen old here, and your eye has nothing to compare it to.

Some of them:

**The steles.** Vent hounds that came out long — three, four meters, spines doubled or trebled, blind like all hounds, warm-bodied, far too slow to take prey at the rationed rate. They eat nothing but overpour. They grow slowly and enormously and they live, as far as anyone can establish, for centuries. Their flanks are broad and hold an incision. They are the archives. A stele does not travel much and does not need to; it lies in a dry room under a weld flank being read.

**Porters.** Cistern crabs with no grille to sit on, because the number of crabs equals the number of grilles and a spare crab is an arithmetical impossibility. They are half a meter, strong, and they are the only reason anything on this world gets carried anywhere. The gutter-lines run for hundreds of kilometers. Porters walk them. They are the plain's only roads, used by its only travellers.

**Resin beetles.** Gasket beetles whose sealant gland fused. What leaks from them, rarely and painfully, is not the flexible waterproof gum of the seams but a hard amber-coloured resin that cures in about forty minutes and does not dissolve. The entire material culture of the maintenance plain — its mortar, its adhesive, its ink, its one dry technology — rests on a manufacturing defect in a gland. They are also small enough to go anywhere, and so they scout.

**The fallen.** A stack-swift is born in the plume, filter-feeds on fog, and dies forty-one days later without ever touching plate. Very occasionally one is extruded heavy, and sinks out of the column, and lands. It cannot rise again. It also does not die at forty-one days — whatever kills them is in the plume, or in the flying — and so it walks, six wings folded, for decades.
