## The Tenant

The census in the pocket is careful and it is wrong, in the way that a careful census is wrong: it counted everything that was there.

Four hundred glasswrights, nine thousand moths, five shrews, a herd of sifters, an eel, a colony of swallows with nowhere to hawk. All correct. But a room is not populated by the things in it. A room is populated by whoever it is shaped around, and this room is shaped, quite unmistakeably, around somebody's habits. There is a saddle worn into the threshold stone. Something has crossed that slab tens of thousands of times, always with the same slight favouring of the left foot, and the pocket has kept the mark the way soft limestone keeps a thumbprint: faithfully, and early.

She has not arrived. She lives here anyway.

---

**Depth.**

Look into her the way you look into the puddle, and expect the same trouble with the floor.

Nearest the surface — most nearly present, most nearly a fact — there is one person, alone, roughly the size of a person. Below her, less distinct, seven: the cairn is a portrait, stacked with care, sized in order, tallest at the base to smallest at the top, which is how a household stands when someone wants to remember all of them at once. Below the seven, blurring, the many thousands of feet that the threshold has already suffered — a congregation, a queue, a long habit of coming here for something. And below *that* the light goes down and keeps going, past any reasonable floor, into a green with no bottom, where whatever it is that the pocket will eventually be *for* is waiting, patient with the geometry.

She is the shallow one. She is the part of it that has nearly happened.

---

**Her body, such as it is.**

She is not translucent and she is not luminous and she is not a ghost; ghosts come afterwards, and nothing here comes afterwards. She is unfinished in exactly the manner of the walls. Count her fingers twice and you will get different answers, and this is not a trick — her hands have not settled, and are trying options. Over the course of a morning her face will migrate a hand's breadth through several arrangements, sliding through them like the oxblood seam sliding up through the ochre and the bone, and then settle, and then reconsider. On some countings she is a boy of about nine. On others a woman near sixty, thick-wristed, with a cough. Most often she is the average of everyone the threshold is expecting, which is nobody in particular, and which weighs about what a person weighs.

Her clothes are not present. Their wear is. There is a mend at the left elbow, neat, doubled, the work of someone who has repaired this same elbow before and has stopped resenting it — and there is no coat around the mend, and will not be for a long while. The palm of her right hand is callused in the precise shape of a small tool held at a particular angle for years. The flint by the fern, when it exists, will fit that callus. Three retouch scars: she is not going to be a beginner.

Her shadow is the only part of her that behaves like other things here, and it behaves worse. All shadows in the pocket lag. Hers lags for hours. At fog-time you can watch a woman's shadow cross the west wall at an unhurried pace, stop at the crystal bank, bend, straighten, and go on, and there is nothing anywhere in the room to be casting it, and there was nothing there this morning either. That was yesterday's crossing, arriving. Shadows are the most honest clocks in the pocket and hers is the slowest, because she is the furthest from having occurred.

---

**Her day.**

The pocket has days, though nothing here has ever seen a sun, and it has them because she keeps them.

Twice a day the fog rises to ankle height and smells of wet slate and, faintly, of cut grass. That is her, coming in. There is a door at the eastern end — not a doorway, no gap, no wall to hold one, just the worn slab and the single blade of meadow grass eleven centimetres tall, bending steadily in a draught that does not exist. The draught is the door standing open. The cut grass is the outside of it. She comes in off an afternoon that has not been buried yet, stamps the light off her boots, and the fog sits for two hours while she works, and lifts when she goes out again. The swallows go with her. That is the whole of the mystery of the swallows: they are not trapped, they are *hers*, they follow her out through the gap that leads only to more stone, and they hawk insects in a summer that is nine metres and several ages away, and they come back to their mud cups full and unbothered. Nobody has followed them because nobody has been asked.

Her work, while the fog sits, is maintenance, and it is all of a kind: she keeps the pocket from finishing.

She skims the lichen off the light. It grows there, edge-on, a faint mottling in the air, feeding on brightness; when it thickens the room dims and the shadows catch up, and she does not like the shadows catching up, so she draws her hand through the air in long steady sweeps like a woman clearing frost from a window, and the mottling breaks, and the day is eleven o'clock again. She banks the crystal drifts back against the wall where the shrews can find them. She takes the print of her thumb out of the floor when she has been careless, and sometimes leaves it, and in four hundred years it will be flint-hard and there will be an argument. She checks the oak.

The oak she is protecting. Two true leaves, in a handful of grit, in the exact centre of the room. There is no space for it to become anything, and it is not trying, and she has decided — this is her one clear opinion — that not trying is a discipline and not a failure, and that when the ceiling goes she will want it ready. She waters it out of the puddle with a cupped hand and does not put her hand in far.

---

**Rent.**

She pays the glasswrights in speech.

This is not charity and it is not worship; it is the arrangement. Four hundred grey translucent walnuts have strung the whole interior with filament and they live on noise, and a pocket this quiet is a poor harvest — a drip, a wingbeat, the small cutlery sound of sifters chewing rock. So she talks. Deliberately, at length, to the room, in a low unhurried voice, in a language she is inventing by ear, and every syllable snags within a foot of her mouth and hangs there as a faint pearl of tension and is drunk.

She has never once heard herself speak.

She has heard herself *later*. That is how she learned the language: by standing in the west end at fog-time and receiving, out of the felted silence, a clear orphaned sentence in her own voice, arriving from no one, forty minutes or a year out of true — and by taking it seriously, and answering it. Everything she knows how to say came back to her before she said it. The grammar was assembled from its own echoes. When you visit and the pocket coughs at you from an empty corner in a warm considering tone, that is her half of a conversation you are not in yet.

---

**The tally.**

Forty-one strokes in chalk, halfway up the north wall, grouped in fives, the last group incomplete. They are getting clearer, because she has not finished pressing.

They are not days. She has no use for days. They are the sentences she has caught and matched — the ones she has heard arriving and recognised as hers, and understood, and been able to place. Forty-one is not many for the amount she talks. Most of it never comes back, or comes back so displaced and out of tune that she cannot claim it, and some of it comes back in a voice she is fairly sure is not hers at all: twice now, two syllables, spoken kindly, in a tone that means *someone is calling something a name.*

She keeps those two syllables. They are the seventh stone, the small one, on the top of the cairn. She has not decided whether the name is hers or whether it is what she will call somebody. The shrews treat the cairn as a boundary marker and are furious about it. The eel, when it surfaces, faces it. She has noticed that and has not drawn a conclusion.

---

**What the animals think.**

The sifters do not think anything; she is weather. The shrews hate her with the total unrelenting hatred of the blind and the busy, and rush her ankles, and she lets them. The moths land on her at fog-time in a matte black hood, nine thousand small absorbencies, and she stands still for it because she has a theory about the moths.

Her theory is that they are the dark, being stored. The pocket is bright — indecently, groundlessly bright — and the district is limestone, and limestone knows what it is doing, and in the deep future there will be no light here at all and nothing that remembers why that ever seemed wrong. So something has to be keeping the difference. Nine thousand roosting holdings of genuine black, up in the vault, drawing no wage, eating nothing, doing nothing but *being kept* until they are needed. She is not sure she is right. She is sure that they are the only honest things in the room, and she is kind to them.

The eel she does not have a theory about. It comes up, in a puddle it cannot fit in, and takes a breath it does not need, and looks at her with the patience of something that has read further down the page. She nods to it. It sinks away. This has been going on for a while.

---

**What she doesn't know.**

That the light is her.

Not the rock's memory of the shallows — that is the geologists' answer, and it is not a good one. The room is lit because its tenant has never lived underground and has not yet been informed that underground is dark, and so arrives every fog-time expecting, with total innocence, to be able to see. The brightness is an assumption. It holds because nothing has contradicted it. It is the last thing here that is still entirely unset.

She also does not know she is early. She has no memory; she has the other thing, the forward-facing kind, and it is crowded, and she takes it for the past. She believes she has lived here for years and that the threshold is worn because she wore it and that the seven are people she has lost. This is not a mistake so much as a tense the pocket does not offer.

---

**The lamp.**

On the crystal bank there is a clay lamp, unglazed, unfired, dry-wicked, holding a thimbleful of oil that has never evaporated.

It is hers. She has not made it. She does not use it and could not — there is nothing to light — and she keeps it anyway, moved a little further along the bank each week, out of the drips, the way you keep a coat by a door in a country with no weather.

It is not waiting for the dark to be pushed back. It is waiting for the dark to arrive, so that there will be something for it to be *for*. The day she strikes it will be the day she stops assuming, and the ceiling of daylight will go out all at once across nine metres of pale limestone, and one small flame will stand up in the middle of a room finally agreeing to be a cave, and then, at last, in the ordinary direction, the feet will start on the threshold stone.

Until then: nine degrees. A drop every eleven seconds, silent. A woman's shadow crossing the west wall on yesterday's errand. Somewhere in the felt, a kind voice saying two syllables to nobody.

She skims the lichen off the light and checks the oak.
