## Who

Not caretakers. Not a priesthood. There are a great many of them, and they are ordinary in the way that people in a very specific place are ordinary, which is to say they will strike you as strange for about four days and then the lowlands will strike you as strange for the rest of your life.

Outsiders call them wallfolk, which they find silly but tolerate, the way you tolerate being called by a childhood name. Among themselves they use the terrace, the face, the court: *she's Weeping Face*, *he's out of Cold Slot, the shady side*. A person is a location before they are a family and a family before they are a name.

After three or four generations on the mountain a body goes a particular way. Deep chest, short forearms, heavy in the thigh and light in the shoulder, because the work is climbing and carrying and almost never reaching. Blood thick enough that lowland physicians write it up as a disorder and prescribe, uselessly, rest. The skin takes the altitude badly at first and then takes it beautifully, going a dark polished brown with pale creases where the fog-squint has folded it, so that an old face at the upper works looks like something laid in courses itself. Their hands are lime-cracked at the knuckle and short at the nail and warm — always warm, a degree or two above yours, which is the first thing you notice when one of them takes your wrist to stop you stepping somewhere you shouldn't.

They walk with the inside shoulder to the wall. All of them, always, even on a terrace three hundred yards deep with no edge in sight, even in the lowlands, on a boulevard, where it makes them look furtive. They are not afraid of the drop. They have simply been trained since they could walk that the wall is the thing that is there, and the air is the thing that isn't.

## Talk

They are loud and they stand too close. Both of these are the machinery's fault. Under the hum and the water hammer and the quarter-shift whistles, a conversation at a polite lowland distance is inaudible, so they close to a foot and shout pleasantly into each other's faces, and they watch the mouth while they do it, and they are all fluent lip-readers without having been taught and without knowing it. Hand a wallfolk woman a letter and she will hold it at arm's length and read it aloud at volume, her lips shaping every word, unaware, annoyed if you mention it.

Across a gorge they use flags and arms. There are perhaps two hundred fixed signs — *water*, *loaded*, *don't*, *later*, *send the boy*, *your mother is dying*, *I'm sorry*, *no*, *the joint is weeping at the third course* — and a whole insulting grammar of them that children learn first and adults deny knowing. The Cold Slot is four hundred feet wide. Two households facing each other across it may correspond for a century and never once meet, since meeting means a four-hour walk around by the Notch, and there is no particular reason to.

They sound angry. They are not angry. When they are angry they go quiet and speak at normal volume, and everyone nearby stops what they are doing.

## The White Hours

Between two and five the cloud comes up and takes the middle terraces, and the mountain's whole social character changes.

Visibility of ten yards makes a different world of a place, and they have built a whole etiquette into it. During the white hours nobody shouts. The machinery is at half-shift, the whistles are stopped, and the sound-floor drops so far that for three hours you can hear a person breathing across a courtyard. So they murmur. They go soft. The fog-bells start — a low pot-bell at every stair head and junction, struck every twenty breaths by whoever is nearest, a duty so automatic that people do it mid-sentence without breaking off — and between the bells you hear the callers: anyone moving through unfamiliar ground says their own name, quietly, every few steps, so the wall knows where they are. *Meren. Meren. Meren.* A child crossing a bracket walkway in fog sounds like a small ghost apologising for itself.

Everything intimate happens in the white hours. Courting, certainly — you cannot see and cannot shout, so you must sit close and talk low, which is exactly the point, and every love song they have is set in fog. Also grief. Also the settling of quarrels, because a man will say in the white a thing he could not say at volume in the dry. Also theft, and the theft-watch, who wear bells on the ankle by law and hate it.

Lowland visitors invariably sleep through the fog hour and wonder why the place feels closed to them. It is not closed. They were asleep during the only part of the day when anyone was going to say anything true.

## Households, and What They Owe

A household is not a building. It is a set of obligations with people attached.

Every household holds days — so many a year owed to the walls, assessed by the terrace, transferable, tradeable, inheritable, and absolutely not forgivable. A rich family is one that can buy other people's days. A poor family is one that sells its own and then works them anyway because the parapet is theirs and the parapet is weeping. When the flood-cloud comes in wrong, the day-bell goes and everyone walks out of the kitchen with a trowel, and the doors of the houses stand open all evening because nobody thought to shut them.

Against the days they hold shares: drip-rights on a parapet, a fraction of a vent-shaft's guano, an eighth of a cistern's eels, a named fig. These are documented to a degree that would embarrass a bank. There are families whose entire wealth is the condensation off ninety feet of copper comb on a north-facing parapet nobody else wants, and who have defended that ninety feet in court for eleven generations, and who can tell you, from memory, the name of every advocate who ever spoke against them.

They marry by raking out a joint together and repointing it. Two hands, one course, witnessed. The couple owns that course afterwards — they are responsible for it, which means that in a bad marriage the maintenance becomes an accusation, and there are people who can walk a face and tell you which pointings were done by a couple in trouble. Nobody says anything. Everybody knows.

They divorce by letting it weep.

## The Trades

**Masons of the faces.** They think in feet per season and lifetimes per face. An apprentice's first joint is not assessed by her master; it is assessed by her great-niece, seventy years on, when the crew comes back around. Consequently the pride of a mason is almost entirely posthumous, and they behave accordingly: unhurried, unimpressed, extremely rude about the dead.

**Listeners.** The flue-tappers. They cannot be shouted at — it is law, an actual ordinance, with a fine — and they carry a small hammer that is inherited and rehandled but never replaced, so that some of the heads are worn to the size of a plum. They work the quiet hour, the two before dawn, when the works idle and the swifts are still in and the mountain's own breathing is the loudest thing. Many of them work with their eyes shut; a few, the old and famous, work blindfolded on principle, and are led about by a child, and are insufferable. They know the flue system the way you know the inside of your own mouth: completely, and only from the inside, and not in any form that can be drawn.

**Cistern crews.** Pale, even here. They row in the dark under the vault, sounding for silt, and they sing to navigate — a slow repeated interval, one note against its echo, which if you hear it from the stairhead is the most frightening beautiful noise on the mountain. Each boat carries a caged moth. If the moth goes down on the cage floor, they go up the stair without discussion and without finishing the verse.

**Vent families.** The swift-scrapers, litigious to the bone, whose children are sent up the shafts because they fit, and who consequently have a whole genre of song about being small and a whole body of law about being paid for it.

**Goatwardens.** Municipal employees of low status and enormous informal power, since they go everywhere, at all hours, on business nobody can refuse, and see everything.

**Furnace-hands.** See below. Or rather, don't.

## Children

They are not fenced. The mountain is a two-mile drop with lettuce growing on it, and there is no arrangement of railings that would make that safe, so instead there is the rope and the hook.

Everyone wears a belt with a hook. Anyone may catch anyone. It is not rude to hook a stranger's child, or a stranger. A child under a certain weight — genuinely a certified weight, marked on a post at each stair head, the way a fairground marks a height — may go on the brackets, the pipe arcades, the outside of everything, and they do, in numbers, all day, like the geckos they are named for. Above that weight they may not, and the day a child is weighed out of the brackets is a small, sour ceremony with a cake, and they sulk for a month, and then they never go out there again as long as they live.

They learn to point a joint at about six. Badly. Somewhere on every face there is a band of terrible childish pointing, left deliberately, cut out and redone at intervals of a generation by the next crop of six-year-olds, so that the mountain has a permanent nursery band running through it like a growth ring.

## Below

You will ask. Everyone asks. There is even a licensed trade in it: at the pilgrim stairs you can buy an answer from a registered man with a badge, for a coin, and he will tell you a depth with great solemnity, and no two of them will agree, and this is understood by everybody including the pilgrims to be a joke that has been running for six hundred years.

The real shape of it is this. Some households are listed for the deep. It is a rota, of a kind, though the kind is not explained to guests, and the listed households do not advertise themselves, and their neighbours do not remark on it. A person goes down for a term. Terms are long. When they come back up they are given light work for a year — parapet weeding, goat duty, the cistern stair — and they stand in a particular way, weight even, shoulders low, not touching the wall, because down there, apparently, there is nothing that is not wall. They are not asked about it. Not out of dread. Out of the same instinct that stops you asking a man how much he earns: it is simply his, and yours is yours, and the asking would put a mark on the room.

What they will say, if you press, and they like you, is that the furnaces are tended, and have always been tended, and that no, nobody is trapped, and no, it is not a punishment, and yes, people live their whole lives down there by choice and think the terraces shrill and draughty and overlit. And then they will change the subject with all the subtlety of a dropped brick.

Do not dig downward. Ask anyone why and they will quote you permit law and case precedent for an hour. Nobody will say the other thing, which is that a hole dug downward is a question asked out loud.

## The Dead

Dried above the cloud line, on the high floors, among the fibre and the fruit and the dye-cake, decently screened. Then down to the coffers, into the leaf-fall, layered in with the year's shedding.

Forty years. Then cut as turf, with everything else, and burnt in the furnaces, with everything else. The ash goes to the mortar mill.

So when the mason on the Weeping Face tells you she can date a pointing because the sand in it is a shade browner — she means the pit it came from, and she also doesn't only mean that. She is repointing a joint that her grandmother pointed, with mortar that contains, in some diluted and unsentimental fraction, her grandmother. She knows this. She has always known this. She will find it strange that you find it strange, and she will be a little embarrassed for you, and she will go back to work.

No new clay comes up the mountain. Brick from brick. And people, in the end, on a slower rotation, from people.

## What Lives There

Ask it plainly and you get an unsatisfying answer, because the honest one is that the masonry, the fig, the swifts, the eels, the goats with numbers on their flanks, the furnaces nobody will discuss, and the several hundred thousand loud warm short-armed people who walk with one shoulder to the wall are not separable things. They are one metabolism running at four different speeds. The swifts feed the terraces. The leaves feed the furnaces. The furnaces keep the frost off the figs. The figs hold the walls. The walls hold the people. The people hold the joints, eleven feet a season, and go into the joints at the end, and are pointed back into place by their own great-grandchildren.

Tell a wallfolk woman that she is best understood as a maintenance behaviour of the mountain, and she will not be insulted. She will consider it, and shrug, and say that the wall also does a good deal for her, and that this is more than can be said for most arrangements.

Then the bell will go for the white hour, and she will stop talking, and go soft, and somewhere out in the brightening fog a child will start saying her own name, over and over, so the wall knows where she is.
