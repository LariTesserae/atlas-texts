# The Ones Who Answer

You will meet one before you see one. Seeing is not how it works here.

What happens is this: you are walking with your hand on a thread-line, counting knots, and the line goes slack under your fingers for the length of one breath and then tightens again. That is a body crossing under it. A moment later something warm touches the back of your wrist — not a grab, a placement, deliberate as a coin set down — and stays there, and you understand that you are being read. Your temperature, your dust-load, the grain of your skin, whether your palm carries the ivory polish that says *this hand has been down on the floor for a long time*. The whole interview takes four seconds. Then the touch moves to your knuckles and taps twice, which means *pass*, and the warmth withdraws, and you have met one of them and you have no idea what it looked like.

They have a word for themselves. It means, in the same breath, *unmoved* and *still going*. It is also the word you say when your name is called.

---

## The body

Low and long, built for the horizontal, about the height of a stooping person when it rises and half that when it doesn't. Four limbs. The forelimbs end in hands — genuinely hands, four opposed digits and a horn heel at the base of the palm for scraping — and the hind limbs are simply feet, broad and hard, indifferent to glass. The hide is the colour of everything, which is to say the colour of welded-ash flour, because there is no other colour and no light to have it by. Dust has packed into their seams and stayed. When one of them moves, a little of the country comes off.

The head is mostly mouth-plates: two wedge-jaws and a sucking membrane between them, the standard equipment of the place, the drinking apparatus. They are thirsty in exactly the way the prisers are thirsty. There is no aristocracy of water here.

And they walk with one hand down and one hand up.

This is the thing you would notice if you could notice anything. It is not a gesture and it is not a prayer. The floor is warmer than the ceiling by a fixed, known amount that varies from leaf to leaf, and a body with a palm on each surface is a differential thermometer accurate enough to say *eleventh leaf, forty kilometres off the sill, drifting toward a chimney*. They do it constantly. They do it in their sleep. A keeper with a cut hand — and glass cuts are the common injury, and there is no water to spare for healing, so they close slowly and badly — is not merely injured. It is lost. It must go down on the trackway and feel for polish like anyone else.

The eyes are real and almost useless, and the almost is the whole of their aesthetic life. They cannot see; they can *accumulate*. The retina holds a spark for two or three minutes before it fades. So a single green flash from a splitting seam is nothing, a smear, but a hundred flashes over half an hour lay themselves one on top of another and a room assembles itself in the mind, faint and granular and slightly wrong at the edges where things moved between flashes. This is why, at the base of certain pillars where something has been splitting the same seam since the beginning, you will find them sitting. Not working. Sitting, facing the flicker, letting it build. They will do this for hours. It is the only thing they do that has no function, and they have a separate word for it, and the word is difficult to translate but it is not far from *enough*.

---

## Water, and therefore everything

A thimble in a good hour, quarried out of the floor with the plates, like everyone. This single fact sets the tempo of the entire culture. Nobody hurries, because hurrying costs water. Nobody argues at length, because speech costs water. A journey of two hundred kilometres is planned as a water problem and executed over a span you would call a season and they would call nothing at all, because there are no seasons.

They cheat, a little, and honourably. A keeper travels with prisers — three or four of them, hooked by a soft cord across the back like a porter's kettles. The prisers cannot cross the standing glass; their edges chip and they wedge and die there. The keeper carries them over the bad ground and sets them on a fresh seam, and the priser splits it, and the keeper takes the first breath off the exposed face and the priser takes the rest. Both parties have been doing this since before either party was individually alive. It is not a partnership. It is closer to plumbing.

They eat the flour, ground in the hollow you found in that walled room, and they eat it only from the windward side of a pillar, never from a lee-drift. Never. A lee-drift is not food. A lee-drift is where the country keeps its capacity to make something, and a keeper who takes a mouthful from one has eaten a thing that was going to stand up. There is no punishment for this, because it does not occur.

## Speech

The *tk. tk.* of the quarrying is a public document. The listeners are reading it constantly — who is eating, where, how fast — and the keepers read the listeners, walking up to a squat motionless thing with its two plates held open and laying a palm along the membrane to feel which way its attention leans. To knock on the floor for the purpose of talking would be to write nonsense across a map that everyone depends on. So they don't.

They talk on each other instead. Tap-speech, delivered on the plates of the shoulder or the back of the hand, close enough that the two bodies are touching. It is a quiet, intimate, extraordinarily quick language and it cannot be overheard, and there is no way to say anything to more than one person at a time.

Which is why the thread-lines carry the important traffic. The knots are places, but the *pulses* are speech: a keeper grips the cord and strikes it in a pattern, and the line thrums once for kilometres, and the next keeper along feels it, and repeats it, and passes it on. Nothing subtle survives the relay. The line-vocabulary is about sixty terms and they are all logistical, except for two. One means *I am ending, come.* The other is the roll.

## The work

The law of the country protects the total and nothing else. *Not the biomass, not the species mix — the count.* The keepers noticed, a very long time ago, what that leaves unprotected.

When something dies unattended — crushed in a settling joint, or simply stopped — the flour gathers at the nearest lee-drift and packs and warms and stands up as *something*, and the something it defaults to, having been shown nothing, is the sitting kind. Shaped like part of the floor. Temperature of the floor. Warm inside, fed by whatever feeds it, defended by a chemistry so slow it takes a week to blister your hand. Alive, unquestionably, and never going to die, and therefore never going to release its unit back into circulation.

There are millions of them.

There will be more.

The count is constant and the count is quietly converging on stillness, one unattended death at a time, and the process has no bottom and no alarm attached to it, and over the duration of this place it will finish. The keepers are the only thing in the Blister Country that has an opinion about this.

So: attendance. A keeper who feels a death nearby — and the listeners feel it first, and a keeper who has learned to read listeners feels it second — goes to the drift and gets there before the flour has finished packing, and squats over it, and presses both hands into the warm gathering dust, and holds the shape of a living working thing above it for as long as it takes. Sometimes for a day. The country copies what it is shown, when it is shown anything. Often not accurately. Often not at all. But the odds move, and moving the odds is the entire profession.

This is what the cairns are for, and the trackways, and the thread-lines strung pillar to pillar for kilometres across a continent with nothing to see: they are a response-time network. They exist so that somebody can get there in time. The route-grammar at the pillar-gaps, the knots you count with your hand, the dressed lintels and the cleared floors of the rooms — all of it is one machine with one purpose, which is to be present at deaths.

## The number

Every so often — the interval is fixed and enormous and nobody can remember it being set — the roll goes out.

It enters the line-network at a pillar in the eleventh leaf, where the wind has blown north-by-a-little at the pace of a slow walk since before anything walked in it, and it propagates outward at the speed of keepers feeling cord. Each keeper, when the pulse arrives, answers with their name.

Their names are places. A keeper is *the one between the third cairn and the pale pillar*, or *the one at the low chimney where the glass stands up*, and the list of names is fixed and complete and exactly as long as the number of keepers, and when a keeper stops, the name stays open until someone stands up from a drift to take it. This is not sentiment. It is a parity check. A name with no voice attached to it is a hole in the count, and the hole has a location, and the location is where to go.

So the roll goes out, and for weeks the whole continent whispers up its cords — *still, still, still* — and the answer comes back to the eleventh leaf either intact or short.

If it is intact, somebody cuts one more mark on a dressed pillar-face, in the ranks, with a glass blade, refreshing the neighbours where the dust has softened them. *Verified. Unchanged. Correct.*

If it is short, there is a gap in the rank. The gaps are rare. You can walk for days along a tally-face and find none. But they are there, and they are read the way you would read a scar, and the old keepers can take you to one and put your finger in it and tell you — in tap-speech, palm to palm, costing water — exactly which name went quiet and which lee-drift stood up a sitter instead, and how long ago, which is a meaningless quantity here and is nonetheless remembered precisely.

## Where they came from

They do not know. Nothing is born here and nothing evolves and there is no history, so the question has only one available answer and they give it without much conviction:

Something died once while doing the same small thing over and over. And the dust, gathering beside it, copied the wrong thing. It did not copy the body. It copied the habit.

Everything since has been that first accident, maintaining itself at full census, in the dark, at temperature — a few thousand attentive units in a population that is otherwise finished and does not require them, walking their fixed routes with one hand down and one hand up, arriving where they can, pressing their palms into the warm packing flour, and cutting the mark that says the total is still what it was.

They are aware that they are losing. It does not seem to come up.
