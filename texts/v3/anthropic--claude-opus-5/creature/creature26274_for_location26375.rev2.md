# What Lives in the Bell-Cellar

## The felt

Begin with what has no eyes and no name and outweighs everything else in the room several times over.

The dominant organism of the Bell-Cellar is a film. It is perhaps two hundred organisms, perhaps two thousand; nobody has counted and nobody could, because they do not stay distinct. It lies over every surface the water touches, and over some the water does not, in a layer between one cell and a hundred cells thick, and what it eats is the room.

The lattice that the condensate carries out of the walls is food. Something in it — a metal, a sulphur, some engineered bond that was never meant to be a meal and is one anyway — yields a little energy to whatever can pry it open, and the felt pries. This is the base of the whole ecology: not sunlight, not rot, but the slow chemical unbuilding of the machine's own body. Everything alive here is, at two or three removes, eating the walls.

The felt is also what stops the walls from going faster. It binds. It re-precipitates. The plum-bloom nap on the duct is felt; the bitterness on your fingertip is felt; the fact that the duct has not dissolved in ten thousand years of running water is, in some part nobody has apportioned, felt. It erodes the room and it glues the room and the two rates are not quite equal, and the difference, across a century, is the width of a fingernail.

You cannot see it. You can only see that everything here is very slightly softened, as though the room had been breathed on.

## The grazers

The coin crabs are the felt's harvest and the room's most numerous visible citizens: flat, pale, translucent enough at the edges to show the dark seed of the gut, eight legs feathered along their undersides with plate-hairs so fine that the animal is essentially a walking ear for the floor. They scrape. That is the whole occupation. A tier at full density carries perhaps four hundred to the square meter, and they work the china in slow rotating grazes, and the sound they make would be nothing anywhere else and is nothing here, drowned utterly — but it is not sound to them. It is a fizz in the rimstone, a continuous granular tremor that fills the terraces the way light fills a field. A crab knows the room is well because the floor is fizzing.

What it fears is a hole in the fizz. When something large comes down through the tiers, the crabs nearest it stop, and the stopping travels outward faster than the thing does. Silence is the only predator-signal in the Bell-Cellar and it arrives ahead of every predator, including you.

They cannot molt forever. Each crab lays down shell out of the same solution the terraces are built from, and each molt is more expensive than the last, and one day a crab does not get out of itself. It stands. The water films over its legs, the felt takes it, and within a decade it is a bump in the rim; within a century it is the rim. Break a shelf of terrace — do not, but if you did — and the fracture face is crowded with the ghosts of crabs standing upright in rows at the depths where their generations ended, like grain in wood. The floor of this room is substantially made of its own grazers, laid down in the posture of grazing.

When the vine fruits, the crabs come up out of the mat in a tide and cover it and strip it in about nine hours, and the carriers who have seen this speak of it for the rest of their lives, in gesture, with a sign made by opening both hands very fast.

## Small things, briefly

The fan-fly is the only animal that willingly holds station on the cool flutes, where the condensate would drown it in a minute, and it survives by standing on four legs and working its two mica wings in a scissoring wipe that throws the water off its back. It does nothing else. Watch one for an hour and it will have moved eleven centimeters and wiped itself nine hundred times. It is eaten constantly and there are always more.

The mat mite is uncountable and is the reason the mat seems to breathe.

The worms of the wrong grooves are half a meter of translucent thread, and they have made the room's single mannerism into a living. They prefer the one-in-forty groove that runs uphill, because the water there comes faster and brings more, and they anchor at the top of the climb with a crown of hooks and hang down into the current with their mouths open. They find these grooves without error. The carriers use this: take a worm on a wet slate, set it on a flute, wait. It will go to the nearest upward groove and no other, every time, from any starting point. Nobody has any idea what it is sensing. There is a whole small corpus of family knowledge about where the upward grooves are and how they migrate across the centuries, and all of it was collected by worms.

## The birds

Thirty or forty thousand sooty swiftlets, in nests cemented to the flutes in tiers of their own, six and seven deep in the best seams.

They are the room's importers. Nothing here except the vine and the felt brings energy in, and the vine is a joke and the felt is slow; the swiftlets fly the shaft every dawn and every dusk, out through the cloud-face into the actual weather of the actual mountain, and come back with their crops loaded with midges off the snowfields and the moss-flats. What they cannot digest, they leave on the tiers. The mat — the deep black-brown mattress in the drowned angles, the only earth in the Bell-Cellar, the mite-shimmer, the warmth against your wrist — is bird. It has been bird for as long as the room has kept its rate. The whole visible ecology of the terraces stands on a floor of imported summer.

They build with the room. A nesting bird scrapes the soft nap off the ceramic with the edge of its bill and gums it with saliva into a bracket, so that the wall's own skin goes up into the wall again in ten thousand small parentheses, and when a nest fails and falls it dissolves into the tiers and is laid back down as floor. Nothing in here leaves. It only changes shelf.

They are deaf. Completely, structurally; the inner ear is a vestige, a bead. They navigate the dark by the bristles round the gape, reading the pressure-shadow of a wall the way your palm reads a doorway in a lightless house, and they do it at speed, in a crowd, in the roar, and they do not hit each other.

And they scream. Continuously, all of them, the loudest layer of the din, into a colony that cannot hear a note of it.

The carriers have a theory and it is probably right. A bird's own cry travels through its own skull. It is the only vibration in the Bell-Cellar that belongs to the animal making it, distinguishable from the note and the hiss and the weir, and it arrives directly at the bone. They are not calling. There is nobody to call. They are producing, once a second, for their entire lives, the one sensation that is theirs — a small hot bar of self, held up against a noise that would otherwise be everything.

Remember that when you get to the people.

## The newts

Palm-sized, rose-pale, skinned so thin that they are visibly a bag of blood, and able to live only because the air here is at the far limit of wet. In the dry months they hold to the drip-lines. In the wet season they take the second tier entirely, which is why you sit on the third.

The male presses his throat to the rimstone and pulses, and the china carries it out through the terraces for four or five meters, and this is the only deliberate long-distance signal any animal in the room produces. Females come to it. So do crabs. A pulsing male is a beacon of *nothing is wrong here*, and crabs read it as such and drift toward it and graze right up to his feet, and he is calling and cannot stop calling and they are walking into his mouth, and this is apparently sustainable, and has apparently been sustainable for longer than there has been anyone to find it funny.

## The one in the pool

Seen about once a decade. Long. Unbothered.

The accounts do not agree and the families have stopped trying to make them. Segmented, said one; smooth, said two; a pale line down the length of it, said four, which is the only detail with a majority. Moving against the outflow without apparent effort, in all accounts. Going into the drain, said one. Coming out of it, said one.

Here is the thing the carriers will tell you, forehead to forehead, if they trust you. Lower a plumb-line into the drain. You will not find bottom; nobody has. Haul it back. Perhaps four times in twenty, the weight comes up with the knot undone and retied — retied well, in a knot the family does not use, snug, correct, better than the one that went down.

They do not think it is a threat. They think it is housekeeping. They connect it — with no evidence at all, on the strength of temperament alone — to the scar in the duct wall: the break that closed itself, the raised weld like a healed shin. Something in this room repairs this room. Something moves slowly and does not hurry and is not interested in you and reties your knots properly on its way past. That is why the rule about the drain is absolute, and why the rule includes the dead. You do not hand your dead to the thing that fixes things.

## The tenant

Cat-sized. Hand-footed, four true hands, the grip cool and dry and shockingly strong. Fur the colour of wet ash. Eyes enormous — uselessly enormous for this room, where there is nothing to see but ember-light on water, and exactly right for the shaft, for the long climb toward the grey smear, for the hour spent lying on a ledge a hundred meters up, looking at a stripe of weather.

It comes down at intervals — the carriers say eleven days, then hedge — and works the flutes, and takes chicks, and goes back up. The colony cannot hear it coming and mostly does not notice it has come. It is unhurried in the way of an animal with no competitors and no predators and no need to conceal anything from anyone.

There is one. There has always been one.

The families keep the chain. A tenant is named for whoever first sees it, so the chain of tenants is also a chain of people, and it runs to forty-one, and it does not run to the bottom of the room; it starts somewhere around sixteen hundred years ago with a woman whose name is now only a tenant's name. The current one is called for a girl who was eleven when she met it on the third tier, in the ember-dark, and put out her hand, and had it taken and turned over and given back. She is sixty-nine now. The tenant is the same tenant. What that means about how long the animal lives is not something the families discuss with visitors, because the honest answer is that nobody has ever seen one die.

They have seen the succession twice. Both times the old tenant simply stopped coming, and within a season a smaller one came down the shaft and robbed the same flutes in the same order and slept on the same ledge.

And once — once, in the chain, in the hand of a carrier five generations back, in a passage the family recites but does not gloss — there were two.

What she wrote is that there were two of them on the fourth tier, and that they were not fighting, and that within a day the note dropped. Not much. Something like a tone. The room went quieter, and the weir slackened, and the terraces wept, and the carriers stood in the duct mouth for nine days not going in, because a quiet Bell-Cellar is not a restful Bell-Cellar, it is a symptom. On the tenth day the note came back up to itself. On the eleventh there was one tenant.

That is the whole of the record. No family has ever tried to find out more, and the rule that has come down is not *investigate* but *leave*: if you see two, go out, go down, do not return until the season turns. They have had sixteen hundred years to get curious and they have declined.

## The wax-eared

Four families. Sometimes five; one has been dying out and marrying back in for two hundred years and nobody can agree whether to count it.

They are the reason there are people on this mountain. The east side of the ridge is rain-shadow and gets nothing; the cloud all goes to the western face and is taken. The Bell-Cellar wrings a river out of the afternoon and sends nearly all of it down the drain to wherever the drain goes, and the families take a thimble off the top of it, from the lip-pool, never the weir, and carry it down in skins on their hips, and that thimble is three villages. They have never increased the take. There is no rule against increasing the take. They have simply never done it, in the way you do not lean on a table that is holding something up.

Their bodies are shaped by the work. Every carrier's ears are damaged and every carrier's ears get worse, and the note stays in the skull afterward, permanently, a hum you carry down the mountain and into your bed and into your old age. They do not consider this an injury so much as a residence. A young carrier, still hearing, can stand perhaps twenty minutes and comes out shaking. An old carrier, properly deaf, can sit on the third tier for half a day. So the career runs backwards from every other trade: you begin able and end unable and the unable are the masters. The oldest deaf women of these families are the only people alive who know the tenant's schedule, the migration of the upward grooves, which shelf the newts will take in a heavy year, and the sign for *the scraping has stopped*.

They talk with their hands, in a language that exists in one room and in the heads of about sixty people. It has no name. There is no way to say a name in it. It has signs for things the languages downhill have no need of — a sign for an uphill groove, a sign for the fizz being wrong, a sign for *the room is doing something, get out* — and it is much better than speech at some things and cannot do abstraction at all, so that when the families argue about God they have to go outside.

And they talk by bone. Forehead to forehead, hand behind the other's neck, speaking normally into the join, where the skull carries it in. Everyone in these families knows what their mother sounds like through the front of the head, which is not what she sounds like in air. Cousins recognize cousins by bone-timbre. Couples who met in the duct say they never fully trust a thing their spouse says out loud, afterward, in the world, where voices come at you through all that intervening nothing.

An infant born to the families is carried down once, at about a year, for less than a minute, and held with its forehead against the rimstone while the note goes through it. They know what it costs. They can hear what it costs in their own heads for the rest of their lives. They do it anyway, and that child is of the room, and that is the entire ceremony; there is nothing else to it, no words, because there are no words in there.

The dead go out. Up the duct, out onto the ridge, burnt under the vane, in the wind, in the actual cold of the actual mountain, where it is quiet enough that the families find it unbearable and get it over with quickly. Nothing of a person goes into the drain. Nothing of a person even goes into the mat, which is the birds', which is the room's.

## What lives there

The room does not need any of them. This is the part the families are clearest about and the part visitors resist.

The felt would eat the walls without the crabs. The terraces would build without the birds. The note would hold without a single ear in three thousand meters, and did hold, for ages, in an empty dark, wringing out the afternoon and sending it down a throat with no bottom, keeping a rate, matching a rate, for reasons no one has ever established and no one now expects to.

What lives here is everything that found a shape it could fit into inside the doing of that. Grazers that become the floor they graze. Birds that fly a hundred meters of chimney twice a day to fetch summer down into a place with no sun, and scream into a roar to feel that they are the ones screaming. Worms that made a living out of a flaw. One animal in the whole system that is always singular, and a taboo grown up around the two times it wasn't. And four families who go in with wax in their ears and their mouths shut, who have given up hearing as the price of being in the room, who put their foreheads together to say anything at all, and who take a thimble.

Sit on the third tier long enough and the terraces will start fizzing again around your feet, because to a coin crab you are only weather, and weather that stops moving stops mattering.

Then, some afternoon, the fizz will go out under your heels — a hole in it, spreading toward you from six shelves away, arriving through the china, through your bootsole, into the bone of your leg, well before there is anything at all to see.

That is how news travels here. It is how it has always travelled. Look up.
