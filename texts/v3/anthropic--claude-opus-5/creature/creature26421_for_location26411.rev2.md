## The Inhabitants

Start with the hands.

Everyone on the Increment has ruined hands, and the ruin is not accidental. There is the working damage, of course — the burr-cuts, the hydraulic pinch, the white shine of old scald along the wrist — but underneath that is the deliberate work, the personal work, begun in childhood and maintained like a garden. A notch in the web of the thumb, recut every few years so it does not fade. A fingertip shortened at the first joint by a family's own knife, on a family's own day. Ink worked into the knuckle creases in a pattern nobody else uses, in a colour mixed at home from brass swarf and fern ash, the recipe held closely, the way lowlanders hold land. You greet a stranger here by turning your palms up. It is not submission. It is an address.

They have no word for themselves as a people. Asked, they will name a terrace, or a stair, or a jar. But the grammar tells you what they think they are. Their language has a plural, and it is not used for things made in quantity: nine hundred presses are *press, wide*, a single noun with a modifier of extent, the way you would say a river is long. The plural is reserved for the split, the distinguished, the only. To speak of two people in the plural is a small formal compliment, and to be refused it — to hear yourself and your brother described as *brother, wide* — is the kind of insult that ends households.

Their faces are lit from underneath, always, and this has done something to them. Eyes live in shadow here; the mouth and the underside of the jaw are the bright parts, the legible parts. So they read mouths. A conversation on the Increment is conducted with the lips and the teeth and the small muscles at the corner, and the eyes are considered private, nearly indecent, a place where a person keeps whatever they have not yet made into a gesture. Lowland visitors are thought to stare rudely and grin at nothing. Their own smiles are minimal and highly inflected, and the tilt of a chin can be a paragraph.

They wear the brimless caps with the stiff lower flange, and under the caps their hair is cut by individuators, who are not barbers exactly — a barber makes you look well, an individuator makes you look like no one — and whose fees are the third largest expense of an ordinary life, after food and after ferment.

---

Because the great fear is not death. The great fear is **going wide**.

A person who stops maintaining their distinction begins, slowly, to be like. It is gradual and almost pleasant, by report. The scars soften. The idiolect smooths — everyone here carries a deliberate flaw in their speech, a swapped consonant, a private word for *tomorrow* — and the flaw drifts back toward the common form. They begin to be mistaken for others, then to be found where they were not, then to be found in two shops at once and to remember both honestly, without alarm. There is a stage at which a widened person is still perfectly good company, a fine worker, agreeable, and answers to three names. After that stage they are no longer anybody, and the district absorbs them the way a mill absorbs a spare hand, and they work for years, well, and are fed, and are not spoken to in the plural, and when they stop working nobody can say precisely when.

So the whole culture is a maintenance schedule for selves. Grandchildren re-notch their grandparents; it is the ordinary tenderness of the mountain, an old woman holding out her arm in the bright underlight while a twelve-year-old with a steady hand recuts the line at her elbow, both of them talking about something else. Old people who have outlived their families and have no one to cut them are the objects of a quiet public charity: the notching-rounds, walked by volunteers on the ninth day, carrying alcohol and a personal blade. To die unwidened is the good death. They say of someone well-loved, *she was entirely herself at the end,* and mean it as literally as a survey report.

---

Children are born nearly identical, and everyone knows it, and it is not spoken about.

The crèches are on the inner terraces, out of the worst of the glare, and they are the strangest rooms on the mountain — full of infants who have not yet been split, who cry in unison without being loud, twenty of them producing one baby's volume of complaint, who turn their heads together at a sound like a small flock. Nurses work them apart by hand and by hour: different blankets, different smells, different songs, each child fed at a slightly wrong time on purpose. The first notch is given at eleven months, on the outer heel, where it will not be seen and cannot be shown off. The second is given by the child itself, at five or six, wherever it likes, badly, and is never corrected. This is the origin of the saying about a stubborn adult: *he has kept his own second cut.*

Twins are the exception, and the Increment has made a profession of them. A twin pair, undivided, is one person with two addresses, and what one hears the other hears, and what one is told the other knows within the same breath. They are called the Paired. They are the mountain's telegraph. One goes up to the summit works, one stays at the thirtieth terrace, and between them they carry schedules, casualty lists, the exact hour of a die change eleven kilometres away. They are honoured extravagantly, exempted from labour, fed from the guild kitchens, and they go wide faster than anyone, because nothing about their lives permits distinction, and they are usually finished by forty. A retired Paired, if the other has died, is a person with a hole in them that the mountain considers holy and unfixable. Some of them split hard at the bereavement — become, overnight, the sharpest-edged individuals in any room — and those ones are hired at once as carriers.

---

Marriage is a long negotiated defence against likeness. The risk is real and everybody knows a case: two people who lived well together for thirty years, ate the same, woke the same, wore the same grease, until one morning there was one of them, in two chairs, holding one conversation with itself and perfectly content. The families call this a merge and hold no funeral, because there is nobody to bury and nobody to mourn with, and the surviving pair-person is looked after and is not unhappy, and this is considered by most to be the worst thing that can happen to a love.

So couples keep an asymmetry. One wears cord on the left wrist, one on the right; one takes salt, one takes none; one is always the first out of the door. Most households keep an annual quarrel, scheduled, about a fixed subject, conducted properly and loudly, and regarded by the neighbours as sound housekeeping. The tenderest thing a spouse can say here is not *we are alike*. It is *I could never be mistaken for you.*

---

They eat spoiled food, deliberately.

A district's soup is one soup with nine hundred bowls, and one soup will not feed nine hundred people. So every household keeps a spoiling-jar: a ferment, decades old, unique, descended by division from an older jar and diverging ever since, sour or fishy or sweetly rotten, no two the same on the whole mountain. One spoonful in each bowl and the bowl becomes only itself and the food becomes food. The jar is the household. Genealogy here is not measured in blood but in ferment: *we are of the black jar from the nineteenth, split twice.* A jar lost in a fire is a family ended, and the survivors are taken in by relatives and given a spoonful to start again, and that spoonful is a formal act of adoption, witnessed.

Beetles from the settling tanks are prized because they are already individuated — oil scum varies, and each beetle tastes faintly of its tank. Fern is the staple, and is honestly disliked. Aune milk is a luxury, and old aune meat is a rarity worth a month's wage, because an old aune has scarred itself into a person and its flesh needs no spoiling at all.

---

The guilds:

**The maintenance guilds** are feared because they are licensed to count. Nobody else may. A guild audit walks into a hall of ninety lathes and counts them aloud, one to ninety, deliberately, and the machines strain under it, and the district does not sleep well for a month afterward. This is how they find the thinning addresses, the ones that will fail together. Guild members eat better than anyone alive on the Increment, and this is not corruption but necessity: a counter must never eat the same meal twice, because counting smooths you, and an unspooled guildsman is a horror. There is a terrace of kitchens that exists only to invent. The burnt-out ones answer to any name offered, cheerfully, and are kept in a house at the fortieth, where they are addressed collectively and do not seem to mind.

**The Nominators** go blind from the opposite direction. To split a thing you must look at it and refuse its kind — not *a cup*, not *this cup*, but only what is in front of you, this once, uncategorised. Practised for years, the eye forgets the habit of sameness, which is also the habit of seeing anything twice. They report, toward the end, that each moment of light arrives unrelated to the last. They work with sleeves pinned back in a windowless hall so that nothing in the room is bright from below, and they are the only people on the mountain who do not wear the flanged cap. When they finish going blind they are kept in comfort and consulted on difficult objects by touch, and they are not, on the whole, unhappy people. They are said to be the only ones who have seen the Increment as it actually is.

**The carriers** walk alone. Three days up the stairwells with one object, unaccompanied, not counting steps, not speaking, breaking the sameness of every landing with a chalk mark made up on the spot. A carrier returns hard-edged and strange and is, for about a year afterward, the most singular human being on the mountain — sought after, slightly avoided, unable to sit comfortably in a crowded hall. Some never come back into company at all and take work at the first-foot, below.

**The first-footers** go down. Beneath the lowest inhabited terrace the new levels arrive from the foundry, hot and blinding and utterly unmarked, rooms that have never been distinguished from one another, corridors that are one corridor with a thousand addresses and no reason yet to be otherwise. Somebody has to go in first and start the defacement: scratch, paint, chip, hang a rag, piss in a corner, name the district. It is done in teams of three who are chosen for how unlike each other they are, and they carry personal noise — bells, stones in tins — and they sing badly and on purpose. Most come back. The ones who do not are not searched for, because a search party is a set of people who are all doing the same thing in the same place, and that is precisely the error.

What is underneath the first-foot, in the foundry, pushing — they do not discuss. They patch what cracks, read the patch-lines, and rise four centimetres a year.

---

The dead go up to the roofs, into the dark. It is the only shade on the Increment and the only place a body is permitted to stop being maintained. They dry rather than rot, in the cold clean air, unweathered like everything else, and they stay sharp, and children play among them, which is discouraged and universal. You do not count the dead. Not under any circumstances, not for records, not in grief. A dead person cannot defend their edges, and the thought of a corpse going wide — of a beloved father found on four roofs, dry and agreeable and multiple — is the mountain's only real nightmare, the thing that wakes people at three in the morning in a city where nothing else is frightening.

---

Among them, and not counted either: one dog. Very old, permanently mildly wet, on every terrace, fed by everybody, owned by nobody. It is the mountain's single unembarrassed contradiction — wide as a press and beloved as a person — and when it dies, which cannot be far off, nobody knows whether there will be a funeral or a maintenance report. The cats are individuals almost from birth, out of sheer vanity, and a household keeps one for the hardness it lends the doorway. The aunes scar themselves into persons over twenty years and the old ones are named and left alone and walk the drainage seams like magistrates. The sparrow turns above the fifth terrace, eleven thousand pairs of wings and one small sound, and nobody names it and nobody counts it and there is a fine for throwing stones.

---

And three times a century the bell is rung, and for one full day nobody is like anyone.

That is what the bell does. It has no sharers, and its sound has no sharers, and it arrives in each person as a thing that is happening only to them, and for about twenty hours afterward every soul on the mountain is completely, unbearably singular — cannot work, cannot fall into step, cannot be mistaken for a neighbour even by a neighbour. They sit in doorways in the upward light with their ears ringing and their edges intolerably clear, and some weep, and some are sick, and the old ones who have heard it before hold the hands of the ones who have not.

Most people live and die having heard it once. A few, never. Those who have heard it twice are looked at, in a crowded hall, the way you would look at something brought up from the vault.
