## Who lives here

Begin with the fact that nothing lives here, and then correct it.

Nothing lives here in the sense of *inhabiting the whole* — nothing has the Hall Country as its range, walks it end to end, knows the fifth hall and the ninth. There is no lord of this place, no keystone animal, no wolf. What there is instead is a population sorted absolutely by its relation to the eleven skies, and it sorts into three creeds, and the three creeds do not mix. There are those who commute to the light. There are those who eat what the commuters bring down. And there are those who have renounced the surface so completely that they would not recognise it as a fact about their world.

Everything alive in the Hall Country is an answer to one question, which is: *how far are you from a hole in the roof?*

---

### The commuters

Take one bat off the ceiling of the third hall — one out of four million, weighing what a plum weighs, her fur greased flat with the oil of her neighbours.

She does not live here. She sleeps here. She was born on this ceiling, hanging from her mother's belly, and she will fall from it eventually, and in between she will spend two-thirds of her life within an arm's length of the same patch of limestone. But her food is thirty kilometres away and eighty metres up, out over a canopy she has never seen in colour. The country is her bedroom, her nursery, and her latrine. That is the entirety of her relationship with it.

And she knows it perfectly, in a form no human could use. She has no map of the third hall. She has a smell and a note. The roost has a smell — ammoniac, hot, particular to within a body's width, so that she can drop into a churning acre of identical bodies at four in the morning and land within centimetres of where she left, by nose, the way you find your own house in the dark by the give of the third step. And the wall has a note. Every square metre of that ceiling returns her voice slightly differently, and she has the whole two hectares memorised as a field of echoes, a landscape made entirely of sound, which she has never once seen and will never need to.

She has never been rained on. She has never been cold. She has never encountered a season. Everything that happens to her, happens above.

The swiftlets are the same argument in a different body, and they contradict the country on one point, which is worth stating plainly: **the claim that there are no structures here is false.** There are tens of thousands. Each is the size of a woman's cupped palm, glued to a vertical wall in the twilight of a shaft, and it is made out of the bird itself — spun saliva, drawn out in threads and laid in overlapping arcs, hardening to something between horn and glass, translucent when the daylight comes through it from behind. They are the only architecture in the Hall Country. They are the only thing here anybody bothered to build. And the only reason a person ever descends is to take them.

A swiftlet cannot walk. Her feet are hooks; they close on rock and nothing else. She spends her entire life either in the air or clinging vertically, and the pale flat floor of the great galleries — kilometres of it, level as a table — is not a place to her at all. It is simply the last thing that happens. A swiftlet on the floor is a swiftlet that has finished.

And on the bats, in the fur, live the mites: eyeless, flightless, three generations to a season, for whom a single bat is not an animal but a warm mobile province with a weather system. They are commuters twice over, and they have no idea they are travelling.

### The cone

Now the thing that genuinely lives here, and the reason to stop calling it a heap.

Eleven metres high, steaming faintly, and *in motion at every point on its surface at every hour of the year.* Do not think of it as a pile of droppings with insects on it. Think of it as an organ that happens to stand up in a room. The bats deposit; the cone digests; the beetles are its enzymes. It maintains itself at eleven metres the way you maintain yourself at your own weight — not by accident but by a balance struck continuously between intake and consumption, and if the roost failed tomorrow the cone would eat itself flat inside four years and leave a stain.

It is stratified. The top centimetre is fresh, hot, and fought over. Below that, a metre of working guts: cockroach nymphs in a solid mass, guano beetle larvae so densely packed that a spade would come up more animal than substrate, fungal threads binding it all into something with a grain, like poor bread. Deeper, the heat of bacterial work — the steam is not the cave, it is metabolism. Deeper still, spent, cold, inert: the country's only sedimentary record of the last few thousand years of dusk flights.

An individual beetle here is born inside food, eats its way to adulthood without ever leaving food, mates on food, dies on food, and is eaten by its own grandchildren within a day. It never encounters an environment. There is no *outside* in its lifetime. This is either the most impoverished existence in the country or the most complete one, and the beetle is not equipped to hold an opinion.

Everything that dies in the roost falls ninety metres onto this. Pups that lost their grip, old females, birds that got it wrong. A bat corpse hitting the cone is gone — not buried, *disassembled* — in under two hours, and if you got there in the first thirty minutes you would find it already furred white with the bloom of a fungus that lives permanently in the air here waiting for exactly this.

### The shoreline

The cone is an island of calories in an ocean of blank silt, and like any island it has a beach, and the beach has its own specialists.

The cave racer in the ceiling crack above the flight path is the most successful animal in the Hall Country and possibly the laziest vertebrate on the planet. She is three metres of pale muscle. She has occupied this crack for something like thirty years. Twice a day a river of meat pours past her face, and she takes what she needs out of the air by touch — no sight involved, no strike calculated, just a mouth in the right place at the right hour — and in three decades she has probably not travelled the length of her own body in pursuit of anything. She does not hunt. She has solved her address. Everything else in her life is digestion and waiting, and both suit her.

On the vertical walls, the amblypygids: flat as a hand, and moving like a hand feeling along a shelf in a dark room, sweeping ahead with whip-legs three times their own length, reading the air by touch at a distance. They do not chase. They stand in the dark for hours with their antenniform legs describing slow circles, and when a cricket walks into that circle the fold-up happens faster than the eye registers, and then the standing resumes.

Centipedes the colour of raw liver, working the wall seams. Crickets in hundreds of thousands, a stipple that moves. And in the mouths of the wet cracks, the harvestmen — packed into mats of ten thousand individuals, legs interwoven so densely the mat reads as a single furred organ, and when you disturb one edge of it the whole thing pulses: a single flinch distributed across ten thousand bodies, travelling outward, dying at the rim. One animal's worth of alarm, shared. Then it settles, and re-knits, and is exactly what it was.

And on the last stones of the light-ring, exactly where the mosses stop: the frogs, stone-coloured, facing outward. They are the only citizens here who use both halves of the country. They sit with their backs to the daylight and their faces to two hundred kilometres of unlit hall, and they take whatever wanders in from it, which is not much, which is why there are so few of them and why they are so patient.

### The deep

Beyond a kilometre from any well, in the ninety percent, the country's population thins out to something that requires a different vocabulary. You cannot survey the deep zone by counting. You survey it by waiting.

Here hunger is not an event. It is a climate. Nothing in the deep expects to eat; it merely maintains itself in a condition of readiness, at a metabolic rate that would be diagnosed as dying in any animal upstairs, for the ten or twenty or forty occasions in its life when something edible arrives. A white harvestman may hold a single square metre of wall for a fortnight without shifting a leg. A cave beetle may spend a whole season locating one cricket egg, and that egg may be a third of its annual budget. Slowness here is not sloth. It is accountancy.

In a rimstone pool a hundred metres out from the fifth skylight, past the last of the light, there is a blind catfish that has been in that pool for perhaps thirty years. It is the size of a finger. It has eaten, in that time, maybe four hundred meals. It knows the entire perimeter of its world as a pressure map — every ledge, every lip, every place the water changes its mind — and it will come to a hand held in the water and mouth it, thoroughly and without fear, because in thirty years nothing in its universe has ever been dangerous, and it has no concept for a thing that could be. It tastes the hand. It finds nothing. It withdraws. It has just experienced the single largest event of its life and it will not alter its behaviour by one degree.

And out on the blank floor: the walkers. That one cricket you meet in an hour, going in a dead straight line — that is not a lost animal, that is a caravan. It is crossing six kilometres of nothing to reach a seam it cannot smell, on a bearing it holds by means nobody has satisfactorily explained, and its odds are poor. Most do not arrive. But some do, and that thin traffic — one animal wide, unpredictable, continuous over ten thousand years — is the only reason the far bays have any population at all.

Which brings up the one genuinely astonishing thing about the empty ninety percent, and it is this: **the floor keeps the record.** Nothing erases here. No rain, no wind, no roots. The tracks of every creature that ever crossed that silt are still in it. Walk out with a lamp and hold it low, and the pale nothing resolves into a palimpsest — cricket trackways from last spring lying across cricket trackways from the reign of a forgotten dynasty, all equally sharp, all equally fresh, a stitching of tiny prints going in straight lines from seam to seam in every direction, thousands of crossings deep, dated to nothing, indistinguishable in age. A guestbook with no dates and no names. The emptiest part of the country turns out to be the part that has forgotten the least.

### The film

And now the actual natives, who have been here longest, and who owe the forest nothing.

Every wet surface in the Hall Country — every sweating wall, every scalloped hollow, tens of square kilometres of stone, the vast majority of the country's total area — carries a film. It is a few cells thick. It is grey-white, or faintly rust, or has no colour at all, and it is slick under a fingertip and comes away like nothing, and if you rubbed a hectare of it off you would not have filled a cup.

It does not eat what the bats bring. It eats the rock: iron, sulphur, the slow chemistry of limestone giving up its ghosts in the dark. It has never met a photon.
