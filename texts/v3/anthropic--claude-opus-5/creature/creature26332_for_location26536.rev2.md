## Who is here

Ask a person of the flats what lives in the basin and they will take your wrist and put your hand on the ground.

They mean it literally and they mean it first. The film in the pools is the oldest and largest living thing in the world as they understand it — a grey-brown skin, sometimes no thicker than a coat of spit, eating gas and sulphur and laying down a little lime as it goes. It has been doing this since the fractures opened. The terraces on the rim, the dead ones, the finished high voices — the film built those, a hair at a time, and then the film moved down as the water moved down, and it is building the drone-quarter now. Everything pale and crowded and singing in thirty kilometres of basin is the excrement of something with no organs. The people know this. They do not phrase it that way, but they know which comes first, and when they show you the ground they are introducing you to the landlord.

On the film: the crawlers, blind, flat, fingernail-sized, a design so old it has forgotten what it was answering. They graze in the still pools and avoid the ones that fizz. There are more of them in this basin than there are of anything, anywhere, that anyone here has ever heard of, and they are the whole middle of the food chain — there is no middle, they *are* the middle, they are the only thing between the film and the mouths that matter.

Above that, four legs. Below that, two. That is the entire census.

## The listeners

They are not rare so much as sparse, and they are sparse the way weather is sparse. Perhaps nine hundred in the basin. Hairless, chalk-skinned, a metre and a half at the shoulder, with that broad shovelled face-plate and no eye anywhere on it. The plate is a sounding board: beneath the skin it is thin bone stretched on a frame, and the animal hears with its face pressed to the tube the way you would hear with your whole chest against a door. They find water by pitch. A mouth that has gone flat overnight — gas in the shaft, heavy and cold — they will not touch, and they are never wrong about this, and the people watch them for it.

They graze the spat water and the film that grows in the drip-pools under big mouths. To reach it they lean. To lean on a four-metre pipe with walls a finger thick is to end the pipe, and they do, constantly, without malice, without noticing. A herd working through the drone-quarter over one summer will snap two hundred chimneys and take out a chord that has stood for four hundred years, and the fracture underneath will spend forty years growing the pipes back, and they will come back lower.

This matters more than it sounds, because of how people are named.

A person's name here is a tube. You are born within earshot of a particular mouth, and that mouth is the sign for you: a direction, uppitch or downpitch, and an interval shown with the hand. Your name is a location in the chord. There are as many possible names as there are pipes, and a dead person's name is not reused until their tube fails of its own accord, which is a decent interval — sometimes centuries.

So when the listeners come through, they do not only break the music. They break names. Everyone whose mouth was snapped is nameless until the community sits down in a silence and gives them another, and there is a sign for a person in that condition — a flat hand turned over, empty — which means *broken-named*, and which is understood to be both a terrible thing and, for a little while, a kind of freedom. You can be un-named by an animal that does not know you exist. It happens to perhaps one person in six, once in a life. They are said to be lucky afterwards, in the way people say that.

They take a listener maybe twice in a decade, always an old one or one that has been breaking pipes badly, and always by driving it into the quiet seam, where it stands lost, its face swinging, hearing nothing to lean on. It is not a hunt with any excitement in it. It is more like a decision that took four days to walk somewhere. Everything the people have that is not stone comes off that animal: hide, gut, sinew, the fat that keeps a wet body warm, the thin bone of the face-plate, which is the only material here that will take an edge and stay slightly flexible.

## The people

I counted a hundred and six individuals in four years and I believe there are under three hundred in the whole basin.

They are short and deep-chested and heavy, with a layer of fat on them like water-birds, because they are wet and cool from birth to death and there is no fire in this world to be dry beside. They grease themselves daily with pressed crawler-oil and listener fat, so that they shine dully in that dim light and the water stands on them in beads instead of soaking in. Their hands are ruined by twenty. Every adult palm is a map of thin white scars from the crystal, and the older ones have fingertips worn smooth and pale as the stone, because in a place where the eye is useless and the ear is full, the hand is the organ you spend.

They do not make things. They grow them. A blade is a form of gut stretched on a bent rib and hung in a fast pool in the wasp-ground; you leave it eleven months and come back and break the form out of a shell of lime and crystal, and there is your edge. A bowl is a leather bag left in a rimmed pool over a winter. A roof-plate is a sheet of drapery that someone's grandmother started by leaning a hide against a wet wall. Their sign for *make* is the same as their sign for *leave*, which is the same as their sign for *wait* — palm down, and a slow settling motion, like putting a sleeping child on a bed. Nothing here can be done in a hurry, and one consequence, which took me two years to see, is that violence among them is essentially impossible on impulse. A knife takes a year. If a man kills a man here he has been going to do it since last spring.

They eat crawler-paste, film soured in a warm pool until it turns and stops being poisonous, and limed meat. That last is their larder and their genius: a carcass is weighted into a shallow, fast-depositing pool and the water seals it in a case of carbonate, sterile, cold, airtight. It keeps a year and more. When they are hungry they go out with a maul and break dinner out of the floor. There are cached kills in that basin older than the people who cached them, and the location of each is held as a pitch-address, and this is inherited, and this is wealth.

## Talk

There is no speech outdoors and there never has been, so the grammar is in the hands.

Palm flat over the back of another hand: *yes*. Knuckle to the sternum: *attend, this is important, this is me*. Two fingers walked up the inside of the forearm: *uppitch*, and down for down. Whole conversations happen with two people standing shoulder to shoulder facing the same direction, one hand each, watching the mist, for twenty minutes.

But everyone sings. All the time, most of them, all day, in a basin where not one human being has ever heard another human being's voice. You see it constantly — a throat working, a jaw moving, a woman scraping film out of a pool with her mouth open and her chest going, and there is no sound from her at all in any sense you can access; the chord eats it whole. They sing for themselves, inside their own skulls, through their own bones. A person's singing voice is the single most private thing they have, more private than their body, and in the ordinary course of a life nobody else will ever encounter it.

Which is what the silences are really for. Yes, you can speak in there, and people do, and it is a strange thin chattering business, most of them bad at it, out of practice, pitched too loud. But the thing that happens in a silence, the thing the benches are worn for, is that a person can be heard singing.

That is the marriage. Two people go into a silence, sit, and sing to each other once, and come out. Everyone understands what it cost. I was allowed to witness this twice and I have never in my life felt so much like an instrument left in someone's house.

## The unmapped

Everyone goes deaf. In that much sound, over sixty years, it is not a misfortune, it is a schedule.

And going deaf here is going blind: the ear is the eye, the basin is known as pitch, direction is interval. A deaf elder cannot cross the floor. They live in the silences, which are the only rooms in the world where their remaining perception — vibration through the bench, through the floor, through a hand laid on a hand — is of any use. The old are kept in the quiet, and they keep the quiet, and children are sent to sit with them.

Here is what they are for.

The note is falling. A tube lengthens by the width of a hair each year and sags in pitch, and the whole chord has been descending since the fractures opened, imperceptibly, catastrophically. But to measure a fall you need something that does not fall, and in this basin the only object that does not change pitch is a memory.

So the deaf hold the old chord. They cannot hear the present one; that is exactly the qualification. An elder sits on the worn bench with a child between her knees, puts her mouth against the crown of the child's head, and hums the fundamental as it stood in her own girlhood sixty years ago, and the child learns a note that no longer exists anywhere in the world, and carries it, and will hum it into another child's skull sixty years from now. The archive is entirely in bodies. It has been running, they say, for a very long time. It drifts, of course. Every generation hands over something slightly flatter than it received, and they know this, and they have no way to correct it, and it is the great melancholy joke of the place: the ruler is made of the same stuff as the thing it measures.

Except that it does not have to be.

A stopped mouth stops growing. Plug a tube with clay and a flat stone and its length is fixed forever, and it holds the note it had on the day it was silenced, the way a jar holds air. There are stopped mouths on the flats that were stopped nine hundred years ago. They are still down there, under the clay, holding the chord of a world nobody remembers, and to hear it you would only have to lift a stone.

You may not. Ever. Not under any circumstance. The reason they give you is the courteous one — that somebody chose that quiet and may still be listening to it — and it is a true reason and it is not the reason. The reason is that lifting that stone would tell them, in one clean interval, exactly how far they have come down. They have chosen not to know the number. Every generation renews the choice. And every generation, I am fairly sure, produces one person who goes and stands over an old stone in the mist with a hand on it, and then takes the hand away.

I met her. She was forty, broken-named, and she took me there and showed me the stone and did not touch it, and afterwards in the silence she said the only sentence I have ever heard anyone say about it, which was: *it would be a high note.*

## The hands

There are thousands of them pressed into the soft new carbonate at standing height, set hard now, some three layers deep where the wall kept thickening outward and a hand kept coming back, year on year, into the same place.

I asked everyone. Nobody would answer and everyone understood the question, which is the reverse of what usually happens with outsiders.

What I eventually noticed is that they are all the same gesture. Not a grip, not a slap, not a splayed mark. Fingers together, palm flat, laid on. It is the word for *yes*.

They are made at the end, by the dying, or on behalf of the dying by whoever is carrying them. The body afterwards goes into a pool and the lime takes it in a season, and the tubes grow up through it, and in four hundred years it is a chord. The three-deep ones are the slow deaths, the ones who decided early and kept coming back and were not finished yet, each year's assent sealed under the next year's chalk.

And there are small ones. Quite a lot of small ones, at the height a carried child's arm reaches, and that is why they will not say.

The question those hands are answering is not asked by anyone in particular. It is the only question in the basin, and it is being asked continuously, at enormous volume, from several million mouths at once, and it is falling, and one day it will drop below hearing and stop, and the water will stop coming up, and the whole pale crowded place will stand there hardening in the mist, wet and cool and dim and finally quiet.

They lay a hand flat on it and say yes.

The last thing that happened to me on the flats: an old deaf woman on a bench took my wrist, turned my hand over, and put her palm flat across it, and held it there, and did not let go for a long time. That was the entire explanation. I have not thought of a better one.
