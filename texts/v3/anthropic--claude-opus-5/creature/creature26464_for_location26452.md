# What Lives on the Plateau of the Long Note

## The drift, and what it was

Take a handful out of a lee pocket and hold it in the flat, doubled light, and you are holding a forest.

Not a metaphor. The grey-brown fluff is aphids off the canopy of hardwoods that no living thing on this tableland has ever seen; midges hatched in oxbows under the cloud; thrips from a flowering season that arrives four thousand metres below and is announced up here only as a slight thickening of the fall, a week when the pockets fill a finger deeper. Moth scales give it the oil-slick sheen. Now and then a mayfly, perfectly whole, wings folded, entirely intact and entirely dead, having flown up out of a river she was born in that morning.

Everything that lives on the plateau eats this. The spiders eat it directly, the fox eats the spiders, the swifts eat it before it lands, the choughs eat the swifts that fall, and even the sheep — the one honest herbivore, the exception that proves the rule — crop cushion plants that grew on the nitrogen of a hundred thousand dead thrips.

So the animals here are not, in the strict sense, native to anything. They are the standing wave a country makes when it is blown uphill and dropped. The plateau does not produce. It receives, and it has organised itself very thoroughly around receiving.

## Nothing hurries

The first thing a visitor notices, after the light and the noise, is the gait.

The beetles plough. The fox walks. The sheep, seen at a distance, appear to be stationary and are not. Nothing on the ground here moves faster than a person strolling, and nothing flees.

This follows from the wind. The fall of insects is constant. It does not come in a flush and does not fail; there is no glut to gorge on and no dearth to survive, and so nothing up here has ever needed to be fat, or to cache, or to breed against a deadline. There is no lean month. There is no fat month. There is no month. An animal that can eat tomorrow exactly as well as it can eat today has no reason to be in a hurry, and after five hundred generations of that, hurry is simply not in the repertoire.

Neither is startle. Startle is a response to change, and there is no change; a fox will stand at nine paces and look at you with mild professional interest for as long as you care to stand still, and a band of sheep will watch you from forty metres without lifting their heads from the moss. They are not tame. Tameness is a relationship. This is something else — the total absence of an expectation of being surprised.

## The small workers

Under every cobble on the stone stripes, in the sorted grit, in the ankle-deep drifts of the best pockets, there is a working population so dense that the ground is more animal than mineral in the top two centimetres.

Springtails first, in the millions, invisible until you tip a stone and the surface of the drift boils. Mites in the interstices. Then the beetles: flightless, of course — three species that the plateau has all made the same, whatever they were before — with the wing-cases fused into a single domed lid over an air chamber, so that each beetle carries a sealed room of its own weather and can lose water only through its mouth. They are polished on the front and dull on the back, exactly like the boulders, because they walk into the grit all their lives. An old one has no sculpture left on its head at all, just a smooth blank plate, and old here means nine or ten years.

They plough the drift in long furrows dead downwind, because ploughing crosswind means putting your flank to forty knots, and after a certain number of centuries the animals' trails and the land's stripes are the same lines. Lift a beetle and set it down at ninety degrees to its heading and it will turn back before it takes a step.

## The spiders

Thousands per hectare. Possibly ten thousand in the good ground near the Flue Field. Every crevice that will hold a fingernail holds a household.

They are jumping spiders, and this is the correct animal for this place, because a jumping spider is essentially an eye that has grown a body to carry it around. In a world where sound is a wall and scent is torn off downwind before it forms, the plateau has handed everything to vision, and the spiders were pre-adapted for the gift. They hunt the fallout by looking at it. They court by dancing — leg semaphore, palp-waving, a slow shuffling geometry performed on the lee face of a stone at a range of four centimetres — and they can perform the entire negotiation of a marriage without either party emitting or receiving a single vibration.

What has changed in them is silk. Elsewhere in the world their young disperse by ballooning: climb, tiptoe, pay out a thread, let go. Here, to let go is to be gone — over the rim, into the cloud sea, forty kilometres before the thread even straightens. So the plateau spiders have stopped. Their spiderlings walk. They walk a metre and a half from the natal crevice and settle, and their children walk a metre and a half, and in this way the species has been moving across the tableland at the speed of a slow rumour for a very long time.

Meanwhile the wind brings them cousins. Spiderlings from below, arriving daily on the same current as the aphids, chilled and unballooning and landing on ground already full. They are counted as drift. The residents eat them.

You will begin to notice, if you stay, that this is the plateau's signature. The beetle's wings, the spider's thread, the swift's feet, the cricket's ears, your own voice: it takes from each thing whatever that thing used for leaving. Everything up here has been quietly disarmed of departure, and the only exit left open is the one that everything eventually takes.

## The drummer

The cricket is the length of a thumb-joint, wingless, earless, the colour of ignimbrite dust, and it is the single most interesting animal on the plateau.

It has no song, because in a hundred decibels of unvarying chord a song is a shout into a waterfall. It has no ears, because there is nothing to listen to; the auditory organs on the forelegs have gone down to two faint pits, kept for balance and nothing else. What it has instead is a hardened plate on the underside of the abdomen and a habit of pressing itself flat to bare rock and striking — a short burst, three or four beats, then thirty seconds of stillness, then again.

The rock carries it. Air is full up here, but stone is empty, and the note that drowns everything above the ground does not descend into it at all. So the plateau has a second acoustic world lying just beneath the first, silent, uncrowded, and highly conductive, and a single cricket on a cobble is broadcasting into it with the whole tableland as its instrument.
