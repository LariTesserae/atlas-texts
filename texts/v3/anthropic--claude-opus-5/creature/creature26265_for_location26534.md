## First answer

Ask what lives here and the honest first answer is *one thing*, and it isn't interested in the question.

The plant is the only inhabitant in the sense that a house is the only inhabitant of an address. It has no head, no front, nowhere the decisions get made. Its behaviour, if you watch it for a thousand years, is the behaviour of a slow accountant: put fibre where load is, put root where sap can reach, put gas where sag begins. It grows a column the way a tendon thickens — not because anything wanted a pillar, but because the pull came from there and kept coming. It has been solving the same equation for so long that the answer has become a place.

What it feels, if the word survives being used this way, is pressure and imbalance. A column falls somewhere out in the black and the plant does not mourn and does not notice, but four hundred metres of mat begin to hang differently, and along that hanging the cells find themselves stretched, and stretched tissue lays down fibre, and from the peat below a bud comes up through nine hundred metres of water over three centuries, blind as a root, and finds the low place, and shoulders it back up. That is the whole of its inner life. It is not a mind. It is an equilibrium with a metabolism.

Everything else in this world is weather inside it.

## The film

On the floor, the snow arrives, and there is a thin living scum that exists to meet it.

They are small — a fingernail, a grain — pale to translucent, and there are unthinkable numbers of them. They have no eyes and no ancestry of eyes; the lineage lost the equipment so long ago that the genes for it have been quietly repurposed into something that senses shear. They know up because up is where cold comes from and where the taste of fresh tissue arrives. They know down because down is the line past which the water goes sour and stops giving oxygen, and they never cross it, not once, not by accident, in ten million generations.

An individual lives about as long as a snowflake takes to fall. This is not coincidence. Their whole existence is a pass across the freshest centimetre of floor, and the population is best understood as a surface rather than as a crowd — a film that turns over continuously, sifting, tunnelling, packing what it cannot use into the layer below, where it will keep forever. They are the interface between the falling and the buried. They have no experience of the sea above them. To them the sea is a ceiling that gives.

## The flats

On the columns live the grazers, and they are the most numerous animals with anything like a life.

Flattened, palm-sized, blind, with a rasping mouth on the underside and a grip that a hard current cannot break. They eat the film of bacteria that grows wherever the bark sweats sap, which means they eat the column's exhalation, and they spend their entire existence on a single vertical cylinder nine metres across and nine hundred metres tall. Many are born, breed, and die without ever touching anything that was not the same stem.

Their cosmology is worth stating plainly, because it is the cosmology of most of the citizens here. The world consists of one enormous curved wall, always at your belly. Beyond the wall in every direction is cold nothing. And there is a sound: eight hundred notes, of which *one is loud*. Your note. Home is not a place, it is a pitch, and it is the pitch you are pressed against. A grazer swept off by a current and carried into open water is not lost in the way a land animal is lost. It is deaf-adjacent, tone-orphaned; it swims toward the loudest note it can find, which may not be its own, and if it arrives it settles and grazes and is home again immediately, because home was never a location. It was a volume.

The grazers migrate up and down according to hunger and the season of the bark, and they favour certain heights, and their favouring is why the old columns are striped. But that is only half the reason for the stripes, as we will get to.

## The hangers

In midwater, where there is essentially nothing, there are the netters, and they are the strangest solution any lineage here has found.

Each is a small soft body with a huge investment in thread. It spins a line from the underside of the ceiling — twenty metres, fifty, occasionally two hundred — and lets itself down, and hangs, and spreads a fan of mucus that is nearly water and catches nearly nothing. Then it waits. It reels its net in slowly, licks it, spreads it again. A netter may take a whole month to eat what the film on the floor turns over in a minute.

They are the animals that know the fall best, because the fall passes through them constantly, and they have refined a discrimination between the ceiling's own dead tissue (which they ignore; it is too poor to be worth handling) and anything that once swam. What they want is the small drifting animals rising and sinking between the columns, and there are so few of these that patience has become the netters' entire anatomy. They do not move once set. They have no need of the chord and no use for it. If the drumming stopped they would not notice, and they are the only citizens of whom that is true.

## The skirt

Where a column meets the floor, the felt heaps up into a soft collar of burrow country, and there the long-bodied hunters work.

Three metres of muscle no thicker than an arm, with a pressure-sensing stripe down each flank and a mouth that opens in four directions. They hunt by wake: nothing here can move without pushing water, and a hunter reads a push at fifteen metres in the way you read a shout. The burrows are full of things worth eating and the hunters spend their lives making a slow circuit of a single skirt, which is perhaps four hundred metres of circumference, which is to them the whole extent of the habitable universe.

They are also the reason the rest of the sea is arranged the way it is: everything that lives on a column lives with the knowledge that the bottom of the column is a mouth.

## The drummers

And now the ones you were really asking about.

Three metres, sometimes a little more. Broad, slow, soft — there is no mineral to spare in this water, no shell anywhere in the world, no stone, nothing hard that was not grown. So the drummer's hammer is not bone. It is a plate of laminated protein on the front of the skull, built up in layers, and it is built up *by striking*. Every blow the animal lands damages the plate a little and the plate answers by thickening. A yearling can barely knock. A two-hundred-year-old lands a blow that swells a nine-metre column's note until the far rim hears it thirty seconds later, and the difference is nothing but four centuries of accumulated callus.

Which means their loudness is their age. Age here is not a number or a look. It is a timbre. Every drummer in the sea can hear, in the first half-second of a strike forty kilometres away, roughly how long the animal that made it has been alive.

The plate is also the ear. Strike and listen are one organ: the blow travels back into the skull, deafens the animal for a moment, and as that ringing decays the returns come in off the other seven hundred and ninety-nine columns, and the drummer's perception of the world is that decay — a picture that arrives while the noise of asking for it fades out. They see in flashes, and the flash is a sound, and they are always briefly blinded by having looked.

What they hold in their heads is a map, and the map is the sea: eight hundred pitches at eight hundred bearings, plus the mud at the rim where the shallows eat the chord and the map simply stops. Drummers avoid the rim the way you would avoid a corridor with no floor. Some of the animals out there still have eyes. A drummer cannot conceive of what that would be for.

They do not learn the map by being taught, exactly. The egg is pressed into a divot the mother rasps in the bark, high up, above the grazing band, where nothing digs; and there it sits for two years against the wood of a ringing column, and the embryo develops inside a single sustained note. It hatches already tuned. It knows one pitch perfectly — its natal stem, its equivalent of a mother's face — and everything else vaguely, and the rest of its life is filling in.

It fills in by hunger. A strike does not only make a sound; it shakes loose a rain of grazers, and that rain is what a drummer eats. A young one cannot hit hard enough to feed itself, so it follows the strikes of elders at a distance of a kilometre or two and takes what falls in the shadow of somebody else's navigation. So childhood here is: swim toward the sound of an old animal's dinner, arrive late, eat the leavings, and in the process learn where the columns are, at what pitch, in what arrangement. Every drummer's map is a record of what it has begged from.

And so the stripes on the columns are read correctly only when you understand that they are two records overlaid: the heights where grazers like to feed, and the heights where drummers like to hit — which are the same heights, because the drummers strike where the food is. The scoured bands are a conversation between two appetites that has been going on so long it has become carpentry.

They are never together and never alone. There may be four hundred adults in three thousand square kilometres of black water, which is a solitude no land creature could tolerate — and it is not solitude, because all four hundred are audibly present at all times. A drummer knows every other drummer in the world by the signature of its strike, and may hold something like a relationship, conducted at forty kilometres' range over ninety years, with an animal it has physically encountered twice. Touching is rare and brief and mostly reproductive. Company is constant. They have no word for lonely and no need for one; the concept could not get purchase in a place where everyone can hear everyone.

There is a craft, and it is the only craft in the world. A column struck at its middle gives its fundamental; struck at a third of its height it gives a higher partial; struck badly, off-node, damped with the flat of the plate, it gives a dull note that is not its own. The old ones can choose. What they choose to play, when they are not eating, is this: the note of a column that has fallen. When a stem goes down — once a century, the loudest event in this world's history, heard by every animal in it simultaneously — the drummers within a day's swim converge on the sag over the following months, and hang there in the low place, and strike the neighbours, and one or another of them will work at a stem until it yields, damped and imperfect, the pitch of the thing that is no longer there.

They do this for years. Then less. Then not.

The account you have been given says the crash is remembered by nobody, and that is true and needs to be said more carefully. No individual holds it. Nothing is written; there is nothing in this world to write on that is not the plant's own tissue, and the plant does not read. Every drummer alive when a column fell will be dead in three hundred years, and the pitch they played will go with them. And yet the sea does not forget, because the population quietly stops swimming toward a place where there is nothing to hit, and three centuries later, when the new stem climbs and takes the load and finds its voice, the young include it in a map they believe has always been complete. The remembering is done the way the plant does its growing: distributed, unconscious, structural. Nobody remembers. Nothing is lost.

At the end, a drummer goes deaf. Four hundred years of self-inflicted concussion; the plate too thick, the skull too scarred. It goes on striking and hears less coming back, and the map thins from the edges inward until it is one column, then none. Then it stops striking, and cannot place itself, and drifts. It sinks through nine hundred metres in about a day and a half — the same descent as the snow, at last part of the fall it spent its life eating — and lands, and the film works over it briefly, and the floor takes it.

Below the sour line nothing rots. So the peat holds every drummer that has ever lived, kilometres deep, in perfect chronological order, plates intact, tens of millions of years of them, stacked like the pages of the only book this world has ever produced, in a language of skull thickness, unread and unreadable, inside the gut of a plant that cannot know it is a library.

## Footnote: the heretics

One last thing lives here, and it is the only life in the sea that owes the ceiling nothing.

At the boundary where the floor goes anoxic, sulphide seeps up out of the compressed past, and there is a horizon — a few centimetres thick, running unbroken beneath the entire sixty kilometres — of bacteria that do not need the fall, and thin colourless worms that eat those bacteria, and nothing else at all. No light in their ancestry, no snow in their economy, no chord in their sensorium; they cannot hear and there is nothing to hear down there anyway.
