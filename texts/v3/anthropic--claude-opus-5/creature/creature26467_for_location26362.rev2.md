## The People of the Shelf

**There are seventy-four of them.** There have been seventy-four for as long as the cords record, which is longer than anyone's reckoning of years, since the cords do not count years. Seventy-four is not a target and nobody enforces it. It is simply the number the shelf holds, the way the cisterns hold to the groove.

They are not a tall people but they are deep through the chest, all of them, even the children — barrel-ribbed, with the long slow breath of altitude. A stranger coming up the terraces arrives gasping and is met by an old woman carrying a slab on her hip who is not breathing hard at all. They move in a particular meter: some paces, then a pause, then some paces. It is the same meter as the chisel. Four or five of a thing, then a rest. You hear it in their walking, their talking, their laughing, which comes in short runs and stops without trailing off.

Their eyes are pale — grey, or a grey-amber — with wide pupils, and they see beautifully in flat light. They can name nine lichens at forty paces by colour alone. They are poor in sun. On the rare hard-bright days, when the cloud lifts entirely and the shelf glares white, they squint and get headaches and find indoor work, and speak of the weather as one speaks of a guest who arrives without notice. They prefer the fog, in which they are visibly more comfortable, more talkative, more themselves: moving about in a small soft room that has no walls.

---

**Hands.** You can read a person here off their hands the way you read a house off its lintel.

The masons — which is most of them, most of the time — have flat wide nails, thumb pads crosshatched with a fine grid of healed cuts, and a particular callus at the base of the first finger where the chisel sits. The dyer's nails are permanently the colour of rust and old mustard, and no scrubbing touches it. The screen-tenders have soft hands and split fingertips from working wet reed in cold, and they wrap them in greased wool at night. The one who keeps the copper — there is always exactly one, it is the least busy and most watched office in the town — has hands that smell of metal, faintly, and everybody knows it, and children will sniff at her palms as a joke and she lets them.

They greet each other by touch more than by word: a hand laid flat on the other's upper arm, held for one breath. It means *you are here, and I am.* It also means hello, goodbye, condolence, congratulation, and *move, I am carrying something.*

---

**Names.** In the stone niche, wrapped in a lichen-purple cloth that is renewed thread by thread, there are forty-one cords, and each cord is a name.

A name is not a person's possession. It is a place a person occupies, like a slab occupying a gap in a roof. Ossuk is Ossuk while she lives; when she dies her cord takes a new knot and goes back into the bundle, and Ossuk is a name in the niche again, unoccupied, available. It may be given the same season to a newborn or it may wait thirty years. Two of the cords have only one knot each. They are names that have been used once in the whole recorded life of the town, and nobody knows why they are not given, and they continue not to be given, and they are kept as carefully as the rest.

Children born before a name comes free are called by where they are. *The small one at Third Seep. The one at the goat wall.* Some of these placeholders stick for years, and a grown man may still be answering to *Lintel* because he was born under one, and his true name sits unworn in the niche waiting for him to get around to it.

The cord-keeper is old, always, and is not chosen for memory but for handwriting in the fingers — for tying a knot that another keeper, generations on, will read the same way. He ties and does not untie. Only one knot in the whole bundle is a slipknot: it marks someone who went down and did not come back, and it is tied so that it can be undone if she does.

---

**The work.** They call it *keeping*, and they do not distinguish it from living.

A mason's day: walk the roofs, which takes an hour if you are quick and three if you are honest. Look for the hairline, the frost-lift, the slab that has begun to ring wrong underfoot. Mark it with a smear of ochre. Go to the quarry notch, which has been worked so long that it is now a room in the mountain wall, cool and dusty and pale, with the cut-marks of five hundred years legible on the back face like the grain of a great pale timber. Cut. Dress. Check against the rod. Check again. Carry. Set. Take the old stone away to the wall that is waiting for it.

Around this: the screen-tenders, twice daily, in the fog hours, walking the six mesh panels and combing them with a bone comb so the water runs instead of beading; clearing the ferns back from the channel lips, but never out — the ferns are the taste of the water and the taste is correct. The garden-keepers turning soil that came up the mountain in baskets so long ago that the carrying of it is not a story anymore, only a fact. The goatherd, who is deaf in one ear and can still tell you which house she is passing by the drip below the floor. The dyer. The wool-worker. The copper-keeper, filing a stub.

And the rod-keeper, whose whole office is a bundle of hardwood sticks, black with handling, each notched with the dimensions of a particular stone in a particular roof. She does not cut and does not carry. She measures, and says yes, or says *a hair*, and the mason goes back and takes a hair off, and does not argue, and would not think of arguing, because the rod is not her opinion.

---

**The House of Held Stone** is the third building from the east fold, and no one lives in it, and its roof is repaired first.

Inside, stacked on edge in racks, dressed and finished and waiting, are two hundred and some slabs. Each one is cut to the exact dimensions of a stone that is currently, at this moment, sound and in place in a roof somewhere on the shelf. They are the replacements for stones that have not yet broken. A mason with a slow afternoon goes to the rod-keeper, asks what is thin, and spends the day making the future of a roof that is presently fine.

This is why they do not talk about the future. The future is in a room, on edge, in racks, cut to size.

---

**Speech.** Quiet, because the air carries. Elliptical, because everyone already knows the subject. They speak in a vocabulary that is almost entirely stone and water, and which does other work on the side:

*Sounding* — the ring a good slab makes; also, by extension, a person who is reliable.
*Bloom* — the white frost-crust that precedes a crack. Also the look on someone's face the week before they do something foolish.
*Fill* — the rubble in the wall cavity. To *go to fill* is to die, and also to grow old usefully, and it is not an insult; it is the highest place a stone reaches.
*Hair* — the smallest correctable error. The most common word in the town.
*Down* — everything that is not the shelf.

They do not have a word for *new.* They have a word for *not yet worn*, which they use for children and for cloth.

---

**Clothing** is wool, felted thick, undyed grey or dyed in the lichen colours: a low russet, a pale sulphur yellow, a green-grey that is nearly the stone. Coats are inherited and repaired in kind — a worn panel cut out and a new panel cut to the exact dimension of the hole, stitched in, so that a coat may be four hundred stitchings old and contain no original thread. They know this about their coats. If you suggested to them that such a coat is therefore not the same coat, they would look at you with something like pity, the way you look at a child who has just discovered that a shadow is not a hole.

---

**Children** — there are usually six or seven on the shelf, rarely more. They are given a rod to hold before they are given a chisel, and they hold it for years. The first real task of a child's life is standing very still in cold wind holding a stick against a stone while an adult squints.

Their games are the town in miniature. They count the drips of houses blindfolded. They run the boundary — the half-hour walk, done as a sprint, which no child can do in half an hour and every child attempts until their chest gives out; the record is kept in nobody's memory but everybody's. They lie on the eastern shoulder on their stomachs with their heads over the edge and count the terraces going down until the terraces stop being terraces and become distance. There is no rule against this. There is also no adult who does it.

---

**The dead** go downward, into the town under the town.

There is no wood to burn and no soil to open, so the floor of an old cellar is lifted, the body is laid in a room that was somebody's kitchen three hundred years ago, and a slab is cut — dressed to exact dimension, checked against a rod made that week for that purpose and then broken — and set over. The stair that ends at a ceiling is such a stair. There are eleven of them that the living know, and more that they don't.

The town rises on itself a little at a time, and what it rises on is its own houses and its own people, and it does not draw a distinction between these, and neither should you.

When a mason dies, the last stone she dressed does not go into a roof. It goes into the fill.

---

**The arithmetic.** Every few years two people go down. It takes eight or nine days each way. They carry dyed wool, dried bitter leaf, and a little cheese that keeps astonishingly; they bring back salt, reed, a length of gut, and copper — always copper, never enough of it. They come back changed by perhaps one degree, and it is a courtesy not to ask twice.

And sometimes, when the cords are short — when a name has gone back to the niche with no child to take it, when the fold has eleven goats and the town has seventy-three people — the down-walkers come back with someone. A girl from a valley town with no place in it. A widower. Once, in the cords, a whole small family, which threw the count long by two and took eleven years to settle.

Nobody calls this recruiting and nobody calls it rescue. They call it *replacement in kind*, and they mean it exactly as they mean it about stone: not similar. Exact. The new one takes the empty name, and the empty work, and the empty threshold, and is taught the meter — four or five, and a rest — and within a generation nothing about them is foreign except, sometimes, the way they look east in the evening for a moment longer than a shelf-born person looks.

---

**The dog** belongs to the town and not to a house. He eats at whichever threshold he is nearest at the time and sleeps against whichever wall has held the day's heat. He is grey-muzzled and unhurried and has one job, which is to notice the fox, and he has performed it four times in his life, and each time was enough.

**The choughs** are watched carefully and fed never. They come over the drop in the afternoon on those absurd black wings, walk about on the roofs with their red bills into everything, peer down the chimneys, and leave. The townspeople like them and do not romanticise them. They are, after all, the only ones in the arrangement who arrive from elsewhere and go back to elsewhere and have made no promises to the stone.

There is always one person on the shelf — never more than one, and not the same one for long — who watches the choughs go, and keeps watching after the rest have gone back to work.

---

They are not sad and they are not sleeping. They are busy, in fact, almost constantly, with a kind of work that has no completion in it and therefore no anxiety either. Ask one of them what they are doing and you will get an answer about a stone. Ask why, and you will get a look — not hostile, just puzzled, the way you might look at someone who asked why you were breathing on the in-stroke.

In the evening, when the cloud comes back across and the far houses go out one by one and the town shrinks to the size of a room, they stand in their doorways for a while, most of them, without arranging to. You can hear them not talking. Then the tapping starts up somewhere, four or five and a rest, four or five and a rest, and everyone knows who it is, and what roof, and which stone, and that it will be finished before dark.
