## The count

There are ninety-one of them, or a hundred and six, or seventy, depending on whether you count the rim-house, the ones who have gone white, and the four who left last year and will probably come back.

Ninety-one is the working figure, because ninety-one is how many mouths draw water off the Cold Face headers, and the headers are metered, and the meter is the only census anybody trusts. They are spread across eight kilometres in a settlement built for maybe four thousand, which means that on an ordinary day a person sees two other people, one of them at a distance of half a kilometre, waving from a catwalk to indicate that they are alive and that the west line is holding.

They are human. This needs saying because the first impression is otherwise. At a hundred metres, coming toward you along a lattice bridge with the vapour behind them, they read as something the place grew.

## Bodies

Thin. Not starved — there is enough to eat, of a sort — but with no reserve anywhere, because fat is mass and mass is heat you have to carry back up. Long-legged from the intervals. Feet ruined, uniformly, from grating: everyone here walks on eleven-millimetre bars for six hours a day, and the sole of a forty-year-old foot in this basin is a horn plate with a corduroy pattern in it.

They do not sweat well, and they know it, and they have organised their whole culture around the fact. On the floor the air is saturated at forty-two degrees, which means sweat cannot evaporate, which means a body has no way to shed anything and will cook from inside in under an hour with no warning that a person recognises as warning. So the floor is not lived on. It is visited, on a count, wet.

Teeth: gone by fifty, everyone, no exceptions, and not from the pools — nobody drinks the pools — but from breathing. Below the second terrace the air carries a fine acid mist, and it takes enamel the way it takes enamel from anything, slowly and impartially. They wear a wetted mouth-cloth on the low ground and they still lose them. The old ones' remaining teeth are the exact colour of the sulfur seams, which is a thing they are vain about. A rim-born child can be identified by smile for about ten years, and is, constantly, and finds it tiresome.

And they rind. Silica-saturated water deposits on anything cooler than itself, and a person is cooler than the water. Splash and it sets: a film in an hour, a crust in a week, in a month a shell with your fingerprint in it. So the evening, everywhere in the basin, is scraping. It is done in pairs, with a flat blade of cast sinter, and it is the main social act of the day — backs, shoulder blades, the nape, behind the ears, the webs of the fingers, the soft place under the jaw. You cannot reach your own scalp and you cannot reach the middle of your own back, and this is the reason nobody here lives alone, which nobody says out loud, and which everybody has thought.

The old are knuckled. Places get missed for years — the point of a shoulder, the outside of an elbow, the ridge of the shin — and the rind builds and cracks and re-seals thicker and crooked, the way the pipes do, until the shape of a seventy-year-old is bulbous and ropy and follows approximately the original line. They stop being scraped somewhere in there, by agreement, and their hands go last, because the hands are still needed. Their opinion is that they work no worse.

## The day

They have no hours. They have the bend, which is the middle of the afternoon, when the two-metre bimetallic strips flex over and the sluices change and the whole basin's water goes a different way for six hours. You can hear the bend from anywhere: three hundred knockers stumble out of their phase and find it again. Before the bend and after the bend is the whole of their calendar of a day.

Then dusk, which is the bright hour, when the differential peaks and the stacks surge and the dead lamps stay dead and the living ones come up orange. Dusk is when people are together. Dusk is scraping, and eating, and the counting of who is on which terrace. Everything social in this place happens in a one-hour window of failing light, in a hanging wire room, seven or eight people, quietly, once a day.

And deep night, when the differential collapses and the machines all but stop and the knocking dies away and the basin goes so quiet you can hear the sinter ticking. Every child in the basin wakes for that, every night, for years, because silence is the warning for a fall and their bodies learn the warning before they learn the difference. You are taught, eventually, to check the light and not the sound. Some people never fully learn it. There are adults here who have not slept through the deep night since they were nine and who describe this without complaint, as a fact about the world.

Nine seconds. That is the rule that shapes where a body is permitted to be. You do not stand anywhere you cannot get under plate in nine, and the geometry of the basin's habitable space is entirely defined by that radius, so their sense of place is not a map but a set of overlapping circles with a shelter at each centre. Ask one of them about a location and they will not tell you a direction. They will tell you what you can get under from there.

## Work

**Walkers.** Most of them. Each holds a circuit of four or five towers and walks it twice: gutters, screens, header pressures, the state of the rind in the low elbows. It is not skilled and it cannot be automated because the failures are unpredictable and slow, and the place has no way of noticing itself. A walker spends five hours a day alone inside the sound of their own grating, at forty minutes per interval, and the result is a person who talks beautifully to one other person and cannot manage three. Their conversation has long silences in it that are not awkward and are not meaningful either. They are simply the size of an interval.
