## Count

Nineteen, or seventeen, or twenty-two. The lift-house ledger has a ruled column for the head-count and the column has not been totalled since the last surveyor came up, because a total would have to be defended and nobody wants the argument. What is certain is that the plateau supports fewer people than it has roofed huts, and that two of the huts are not roofed, so the arithmetic has never been urgent.

They are called tenders by the dry country, by the pay-plate, and by the ledger. Among themselves they use a sign: the flat hand, palm down, held at the height of the eyes and moved a hand's width outward. It means, roughly, *the ones on top*, and it is also the sign for the plateau itself, and also for the cloud deck seen from above. They have never felt a need to distinguish.

## Bodies

You will not, at first, be able to tell them apart, and this is not a trick of unfamiliarity — they have converged. The Sill has selected, out of whatever variety came up the cable, for a single body: broad in the chest from the thin air, short in the stride, and covered.

Covered twice. Their canvas is bleached to the colour of the tuff and cut with an upper and a lower everything: an overcuff and an undercuff, a shoulder cape and a chest bib, and on the hat a brim above and a skirt below — the *soffit* — a stiff shelf of cloth standing out from the jaw that shades the throat, the nostrils, the underside of the nose, the lower lip. A visitor's chin-burn is visible from thirty metres and is the plateau's one universal joke. Their own faces are weathered in the wrong places: pale under the eyes, dark in the eye-sockets and dark along the jaw's *underside*, the tan running upward from the collar. Their palms are as brown as the backs of their hands. Turn over a tender and find another tender.

Their goggles are smoked heavier in the lower half of the lens than the upper. Newcomers wear them upside down for a week and cannot say what is wrong.

They walk with the foot set down flat and whole, all at once, no roll and no toe — the scree step, taught in the first fortnight. It is not stealth. It is that the click of shifting stone is the loudest thing they have, and they find they would rather not spend it.

## Hands

The language is the hands, and it is small. Signing is done low, against the dark bib, in a space about the size of a dinner plate, because the eye reads movement best against a black ground and because a large sign is a shout. Large signs exist and there are four of them and all four mean something has gone through the penstock or through a person.

The vocabulary is thickest where the work is. There are eleven signs for the behaviour of a bead of water on a wire. There is one sign for *good*, used sparingly, and a different, commoner one for *good, with a reservation I will not trouble you with*, made by closing the last two fingers only. There is a sign meaning *I have been standing here a while and am not doing anything*, which is used to reassure someone who has seen you across a rank and cannot tell if you have a problem; the same sign, made once, slowly, with the arm resting, is what they say about a person who has died.

The most-used sign is the one the surveyors wrote down: *look, over there, slowly*. It is made with two fingers and it is, in practice, the way tenders show each other everything they love. Nobody points at anything fast.

They can speak. They do, mouth to ear, for anything technical, in a flat under-breath that carries thirty centimetres and no further, and the children born up here learn it late and never lose the flatness. Over distance they use the plumbing. A waxed mallet, one tap, on a header: *attend*. Two: *come*. Five slow: *the gutter is off true and I need a second pair of eyes*. The steel carries where the air will not, all the way down the penstock, two thousand metres through rock to the pump-halls, and the pump-hall crew — who have never come up, who breathe wet warm noise all their lives — tap back. Once a year, at some hour nobody up here has ever fixed, a single soft tap arrives from below for no operational reason at all. It is answered. Neither crew knows who is doing it now.

## Ears

There is exactly one listening job on the Sill, and it is the reason they still have ears.

A wire at wrong tension hums. Not audibly — not at a metre, not at a hand's breadth — but with the cheekbone laid against the panel frame and the eyes shut, a mistuned string can be found by note. So the tenders' single auditory art is performed with the face pressed to steel in absolute stillness, sometimes for an hour, working across a panel string by string. From any distance this looks like a person asleep standing up against a fence.

The old ones do it best, and the old ones are mostly going blind. The underlight takes the eyes: everyone here has the same cataract, arriving in the same decade, coming up from below like everything else. It is not treated as a catastrophe. It is treated as a promotion. A blind tender knows the panel plan in the fingers, counts ranks by footing, and works by note — and works, by preference, in the white hours, when the plateau is inside the cloud and sight is worth nothing to anybody and the harps are streaming their best yield of the year. The most productive hours on the Sill are worked by its blindest people, and this is considered so obviously correct that no one has ever thought to find it moving.

## Pace, and the keeping of questions

They have taken their tempo from the Combers, or the Combers were built to a tempo they already had; the lift-house records do not settle it. Either way: nobody hurries, and hurry in another person is read the way a dropped tool is read — as noise.

Decisions here are not made, they are *kept*. A question is assigned a keeper, and the keeper's duty is not to answer it but to hold it open properly: to record the arguments, to walk the panels the question concerns, to be asked, and to decline. The wireweed question — whether the vegetable panels at the northwest edge should be corrected back to steel — has one keeper and has had her for something over forty years. She has a sign-name meaning *the bent seventh*, after the pylon, for reasons that were funny in a year no living person can name. She is the one who first put her ear to a wireweed panel and reported that the note was better than steel: rounder, slower to die. She wrote this on the slate and did not draw a conclusion from it, and the slate is the nearest thing the plateau has to a constitution.

Every new tender is asked, in their first month, what should be done about the wireweed, and their answer is written down under the others, and nothing is done. There are three hundred and forty answers. The keeper has, without question, made up her mind; she considers the open question to be doing more good than the closed one would, and she is holding it the way one holds a door.

## Names

Nobody's name is spoken. Sign-names are given, not chosen, and they are almost all either a mistake made once or a piece of the landscape. There is a woman called *the plinth*, a man called *chin-burn* who is sixty, a child called *two-tap* because she learned the mallet before she learned to talk. A person can be named after a place, and a place after a person, and after two generations the direction is unrecoverable, which nobody minds; the plateau and the people are of an age up here, both of them younger than the machines.

Their manners are the manners of a landscape without announcement. You do not wave — waving is one of the four large signs. You *show flat*: you stop, turn your whole body to face the other person, and stand for a count of three so that they can read you against the moss. Everyone on the Sill has spent an appreciable fraction of their life standing perfectly still in the open to be looked at, and they do it the way the mute grouse does it, which they know, and which they call doing grouse, and which they learned from the bird without embarrassment.

## Coming and going

The lift brings flour, oil, tea, dried fish, wire, wax, and, twice a decade, a person. Newcomers arrive angry and loud and generally last a season; the ones who stay are noticed to have gone quiet in about six weeks, which is also how long it took the last dog. There has only ever been one dog. It stopped barking within the same six weeks and lived out its life opening its mouth without sound, and there has not been another, and the tenders will tell you it is because animals vibrate the panels, and that is true, and it is not the reason.

The children go down at fourteen, once, as a matter of policy. They come back describing the noise, but the noise is not what they mean. What they cannot manage is the shadows: the hard black shapes lying on the ground beside every object, moving when the object does not, doubling the world. Two have refused to go down a second time in their lives. They are the ones who will still be here at the end.

## Their splits

There is no soil, so there is no burial, and no monuments either, since a monument here does not weather — it gets colonised, and reads within thirty years as a low green shape you would not look at twice.

So each tender chooses a split. Early: usually in the first year, which strikes visitors as morbid and is in fact simply an item on the list. You go down into the fissures, insert yourself, push through the ferns and the half-open crosiers and the beards of pale moss hanging downward toward the brightness, and you pick the one place in the whole county where the air is still. Thereafter you visit. It is entirely normal for a tender asked where they come from to point, with two fingers, slowly, down into a crack in the plateau. When they die they are carried in wrapped in their own canvas and left on the cushion, and the moss has them within a decade, and there is no marker, and everybody knows where everybody is.

## And the rest of what lives here

The grey spiders in the moss, hunting by touch, who have the same profession as the old tenders and better instruments for it. The mute walking bird. The beetles in the drainways who have never been dark. And the Combers, which the tenders speak of in the same grammar they use for the wireweed and the moss and each other: not *it is working* but *it is at the ninth rank and coming on*.
