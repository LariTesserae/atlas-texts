## The Tenders

They are not strange to look at, which is the first surprise. You expect something grown for the place. What you get is a person: lean, barefoot, shining wet, walking a herringbone floor with a coil of cordage over one shoulder and a bag of bells that does not clink, because the bells are packed in moss and a bell that rings by accident is a lie.

Look longer and the adaptation shows, but it is all in the periphery. The feet are wrong — too long, the toes too separately intelligent, the soles a horn-coloured hide that has worn to the shape of split withy and grips it the way a hand grips. The pupils are enormous and slow to close. The skin never dries, so it is kept oiled with resin against rot, and it carries, in the folds, a faint permanent smell of the Stock, sweetish, like a cut stem. And the hands are the wrong size for the body. Everyone notices the hands last and then cannot stop noticing them.

**The hands.** A tender's hands are a record of the work in the same way the floor is. By forty, the fingers have taken a set: the first three of the right curl slightly, to the diameter of a gouge-handle; the left thumb has a pad on it like a heel. They read with them. Below the fifth stratum there is no light worth having, and a tender who needs to know whether a graft took, or whose adze laid this course, or which way the grain turns in the dark ahead, does not look. She kneels and puts both palms flat and stays there a while, and what she is doing has no word in their language that translates as anything but *listening*.

They will tell you that this is not metaphor. Tool-marks under the fingertips give up the rhythm of the hand that made them — a stroke count, a hesitation, a place where somebody stopped mid-course and came back to it differently, perhaps years later, perhaps with a new arm. They can date work by hand-feel to within a few hundred years and name the school. They have opinions, strong ones, about people ninety generations dead. *Sloppy on the return stroke. Left-handed and hiding it. This one was in pain.*

**Speech.** They have it. They use it the way you would use a lamp in a room you know: rarely, and for particular tasks. A tender may go a season without speaking and then talk for two days straight when someone comes, hoarse by the second hour, laughing at her own voice cracking. It is not that silence is sacred. It is that the Held eats sound and gives almost nothing back, and speech into that has a quality of spending.

That word is theirs. *Spending.* It comes from the thing in the walls. When you stand still an hour and the labour comes up out of the grain — the adze-strokes, the hauled lashing, the two people breathing in unison as they lift — what never comes through is a voice. Ten thousand years of it and not one word. So the tenders have drawn the obvious conclusion and built their whole idea of persistence on it: *the hands hold and the mouth does not.* Talk all you like, it goes nowhere. What you did with your arms is what the world keeps.

This makes them, by any outside measure, a people of appalling emotional reticence and startling physical tenderness. They greet by pressing palm to palm and standing like that, breathing, sometimes for a long time, the way the silk-limbed grazers press their long heads together. To say *I am glad you exist* they take your hands and turn them over and look at the calluses and say what work you have been doing lately. To say *I love you* they work beside you in a rhythm that matches yours, which is difficult, and which the wall will keep.

**The Book.** Leaf-vellum, sewn, waxed, the size of two hands, carried inside the shirt against the body because the damp takes it otherwise. It is the only object a tender will save before her tools.

In it: every kind of thing permitted in her territory. The grazer-lines with their breeding crosses. The splice-moth strains and their pitches. Lamp-gall stock. The reed-stands of the marsh-galleries, each stand entered separately, with the date of its sowing and the name of the sower. Her animals, her tools, her arches, her bells, her people.

You cannot strike anything out. You can only write *ended*, which is a different thing from erasing and is done with a witness where possible. The oldest pages are in hands nobody can read any more, but the entries on them are still holding, and some of them are for creatures no tender has ever seen — an animal described as *six-legged, red at the shoulder, eats standing water* is held somewhere on this continent by an entry in a book in a territory nobody can reach, because the unmade space between here and there thickened four thousand years ago. They know the world is fuller than they can get to. It is one of the few sources of straightforward comfort they have.

And because writing a thing in the Book is a form of intending it, the Book is dangerous, and every tender's apprenticeship contains a long unhappy section on what you may not write. You may not enter a thing you have not seen. You may not enter a thing you do not mean to tend. Children are taught this with an emphasis that seems excessive until you understand that a bored eleven-year-old in a gallery the size of a province, inventing an animal and writing it down, is a public safety matter.

**The naming.** A child born in the Held is not held. It has three days, perhaps four — the old tenders argue about it, which tells you what kind of thing it is to be argued about — before it begins to go faint at the edges and cast less shadow than it should.

A name requires two speakers. This is structural, not ceremonial: one mouth is not enough weight. So it must contain a syllable given by each of two living named people, which is why the names sound assembled — Rukkel out of Ruk and Kelm, Seshem out of Sesh and Emmery — and why a tender can hear a stranger's name once and place their whole lineage of witnesses back six or seven generations, like reading tool-marks.

A tender's territory is the size of a small country and she may see three adults in a year. So: the birth-walk. A woman who has come to term alone packs, straps the infant to her chest, and starts down the tended line toward the nearest bell she believes is still being hung. She walks fast and she talks the entire way, out loud, continuously, the child's provisional sounds over and over into its wet scalp — which is spending, which does nothing, which every one of them does anyway.

And on the other end, the custom is *walking the bell out*. A tender who has any reason to think someone is birth-walking toward her goes out along the line ringing, further than she has any business going, sleeping rough, ringing. You can hear a boundary bell from very far off. It is the most information the world ever gives you at once, and in this case it means *there are two of us and one is here, come*, and the line is short going toward it, because somebody meant it to be walked.

Sometimes it does not work. What is left then is not a grave. It is a place on the floor, about the size of a forearm, that the eye slides off. The tenders do not avoid these. A woman who has lost one will route her daily circuit over that spot for the rest of her life, stepping on it deliberately, every day, which is the nearest thing they have to a prayer and is not directed at anyone.

**The bell.** At naming, the child is given a bell, cast for it in a clay hearth out of sweated ash-lick, and this bell is the one bell in the world it may never hang. It is carried. It *is* the name, in the only durable material there is.

You ring your own when you are frightened or lost or crossing a place where the grain does something you don't like. Children are taught to do it, and told it is to keep themselves held, and it is not entirely clear to anyone, including the old, whether this works or whether it is just a way of hearing that you are still here. Nobody rings another person's bell. There is exactly one occasion for that.

**The meets.** Three or four times in a generation, the lines that cross at a knot-chamber bring people together on purpose. A knot is a hollow sphere with a dozen downs braided through it and platforms all over the inside, and for a meet every platform is lit — lamp-galls uncaged and scraped bright, so that from the middle of the volume there is no floor and no ceiling anywhere, just people at all angles in the warm dripping dark, cooking, arguing, comparing pages, sleeping in tiers above and below and sideways from one another.

This is where marriages happen and where they mostly end, because two tenders whose territories are eleven years apart do not live together; they live *joined*, which means each of them extends their line toward the other for the rest of their working life, nine centimetres of trained arch a year, four generations to a day's walk. A joined pair will not meet the gap. Their children will not. It is understood. They do it anyway, and the arch is the marriage, and when one of them dies the other keeps building toward a territory that now has nobody in it, because the arch is also the mourning.

**The deep.** Some people go down. There are shafts, ancient ones, with rungs grown into the wall, and a craft of descending: the ones who do it patch the old laminations from beneath, and lay new floor over floors, which is why the world is very slowly getting taller and every generation lives on a fresh skin above the last.

Sinkers are distinct. They are quieter than other tenders, which is saying something, and they will not be asked about the Dark Grain twice. What they say, when they say anything, is that the work-sound gets louder as you go down, which everyone expects, and that at some depth it stops being a layered noise of many hands and resolves — the way a crowd resolves when you get close — into people. Distinct ones. Working at a pace. Not slowing.

The surface tenders take this as the deep past made audible. The sinkers, in general, do not appear to have settled the question of whether it is past.

**The other holders.** The lamp-gall keepers were bred to scrape, and do scrape, and have for some time now regarded the matter as their own. They are the size of a cat, clever-handed, with a flat scraper-thumb and an unattractive habit of theft; they take beads, seed-shell chips, anything small and hard. Tenders indulge them.

Four years ago — the story is spreading along the lines at the pace such things spread, which is about a territory a decade — a tender named Innowess came out along a spar that had not been trained in her lifetime, following a line of keepers, and found that the keepers had hung things in it. Seed-shells on cord. At intervals. At *bell intervals*, the old boundary spacing, going out into the faint, the last one a good way past where the herringbone gave up.

They ring. Not well. They ring.

The spar was not thinning. It should have been.

Innowess sat there a day and a night doing the arithmetic that every tender does eventually and never says out loud, which goes: *only made things hold, and nothing holds that nobody intends, and something out here is intending.* Then she took her bag and hung one of her own past theirs, and came home, and entered the keepers in her Book — not as stock. On a new page, in a column she had to invent.

**The last bell.** Follow any tended line outward far enough and you come to it, and now you know what it is. It is not a marker. It was somebody's name.

When a tender dies, someone walks their bell to the furthest point that person ever reached, and hangs it there, and rings it once — the only time another hand ever rings your bell — and that is the whole funeral. Which means the border of the world is made entirely of the dead, at their own high-water marks, hung in order of how far each of them got; and a long line walked outward is a walk through everyone who ever kept it, each of them a little further out than the last, the increments getting smaller near the end because the last few generations were doing very fine work in very poor conditions.

Beyond that bell the Stock goes untrained and faint, the floor forgets its herringbone, and the dripping stops. Everyone who reaches a last bell stands there a while. They are not contemplating the void. They are standing next to somebody.

Then they turn back, and it is shorter going home than it was coming out — measurably, always, every time — because the person behind them, whose name is hanging in the dark, meant it to be walked.
