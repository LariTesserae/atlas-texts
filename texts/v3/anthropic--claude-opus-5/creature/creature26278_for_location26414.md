## The Tenants of the Warm Mouth

### I. The collar

The upper world's inventory takes a minute because it is not an inventory of life so much as of endurance. Cushion plant, saxifrage, three lichens. Nothing on that ridge is *living* there in any generous sense; it is refusing to die there, which is a different verb.

The real list starts about a metre below the rim.

On the lee arc, in the band where the shaft's breath comes up warm and wet and still has daylight in it, there is a collar of green perhaps two metres deep and eleven metres long, and it is the richest ground within forty kilometres. Mosses in three storeys, the lowest of them a hummock species that grows in the shape of what drips on it. A filmy fern whose fronds are one cell thick, an organism with no business at this altitude at all, no cuticle, no defence against dry air, surviving because the air here is never dry. Liverworts pressed to the rock like spilled wax. A single orchid, or rather perhaps two hundred of the same orchid, thumbnail-sized, flowering in the second week of the dry season, pollinated by a fly that cannot fly — a squat wingless thing that walks the collar and never leaves it, because a metre above is a desert and a metre below is a night.

There is a snail. Translucent shell, a spiral you can read a fingertip through, grazing the moss in a slow patrol. There is a spider in the fluting who eats the fly that pollinates the orchid. There are, in the wettest crack, small flat worms.

All of it lives on exhalation. Take away the hole and the collar is gone in six days. It is a garden kept alive by a stomach.

### II. The vault: forty thousand throats

Below the collar the walls go dark and the darkness has upholstery.

The swiftlets are the landlords here, in the sense that everything beneath them lives on their leavings and everything above them lives on their bodies. There are between thirty-eight and forty-five thousand of them depending on the year and on how you count the non-breeders, and they occupy the bell of the vault in tiers that are not random. Warmth is rank. The old birds, the ones with eleven or fourteen seasons behind them, hold the nests in the dome's dead-air pocket where the temperature runs four degrees above the shaft mean; the young and the unpaired get the draught margins, where the chicks grow slower and the failure rate doubles. There are fights over a handspan of rock. They are brief and they are conducted by voice and body-slam in absolute darkness.

A nest is not a year's work. It is a palimpsest. The bird glues a bracket of its own hardened saliva to the stone, and next season re-glues it, and the season after that a different bird inherits it and re-glues that, and what you end up with, twenty years in, is a laminated cup in which the strata are individual lives — this dark band a bird that fed badly, this white one a bird that fed on the wing over a river in flood. The harvesters can read them. They will hold an old nest to the light of the sky-oval and count.

The birds see. This wants saying, because everything else down here does not. They have proper eyes and they use them for four thousand metres of descending flight and a day's hunting over forest that they reach by falling out of winter into summer every morning. It is only at home that they are blind, and at home they click — a hard double consonant, unmusical, effective to perhaps two metres. Forty thousand of those at once is the gravel-fire roar, and inside that roar each bird finds one cup among tens of thousands by remembering the turns from the rim, counting the tiers, and then listening for the specific way its own nest answers its own voice. The error rate is not zero. A misdocked bird is evicted with violence. Then it tries again.

They do not eat here. Nothing in the vault eats here. Every calorie in the Warm Mouth arrives as a bird's or a bat's digestion of a country two thousand metres lower and fifteen degrees warmer. The shaft is a bedroom, a nursery, and a latrine, and it is the latrine that supports the rest of the population.

### III. Three tenancies of bats

The bats are sorted by heat and by draught, and each species is a different animal in its habits, its schedule, and its politics.

The smallest, a fawn-coloured thing weighing less than a walnut, occupies the hottest crevices in the dome — pockets where the fermentation heat and the body heat compound until the rock at 41°C would be unpleasant to a hand. Nine, ten thousand of them, in a mass so dense that the individuals are only recognisable as a texture. In midday torpor they let their body temperature slump to the room's and hang there like a poured substance. Their crèche is the ugliest and most impressive thing in the shaft: a carpet of naked pink young stuck to hot stone in a single continuous sheet, and every returning mother locating her own by voice and by smell out of eight thousand, in the dark, in under two minutes, with an accuracy of better than nine in ten.

The middle tenant is a horseshoe bat with a face like a piece of ornamental ironwork, hanging singly, spaced out with the fastidiousness of a species that does not touch. They are the vertical commuters. Every night they drop two thousand metres down the mountain's flank to hunt moths in cloud forest, and every dawn they climb back, and the climb takes them an hour and forty minutes of hard work. They could roost in the forest. They do not. Nothing in the forest is this safe.

The largest hangs in the draught above the throat, where the updraught is strongest — narrow-winged, long-tailed, built like a thrown knife, and it uses the shaft as a launch tube. At dusk they do not so much fly out as fall upward, riding the warm column, and they clear the rim already at speed and gone. They come back at forty kilometres an hour and brake by stalling into the dark. Every year a few misjudge it and hit the wall. The floor deals with them.

### IV. Passengers

On the bats: flies with no wings, flattened, long-legged, running sideways through fur like a rumour, and when it is time they leave the animal, crawl a body's length onto the rock, and glue down a single pupa there. Black, the size of a peppercorn. Because a colony hangs in the same place for centuries, the wall below each roost is studded with these — a dense ring of them where the bats hang now, a fainter ring a metre over where they hung a hundred years ago, a ghost of a ring above that. It is a map of tenancy. You can trace where the roost has drifted as the floor rose, and the drift is upward, always upward, at almost exactly the same rate as the floor.

In the swiftlet nests, three kinds of louse, one per tier, each keyed to a different microclimate. A bug that lives nowhere in the world but inside a swiftlet cup and drinks from nestling feet. And a small drab moth whose caterpillar eats hardened bird saliva and nothing else, tunnelling the laminated strata of a nest like a beetle in old furniture, so that in some tiers the birds are constantly repairing damage done by an animal that exists solely to unmake their houses.

### V. The floor, and the rule of the fall

The floor is the largest population and the least individuated. The dermestid beetles are numbered in the tens of millions and behave as a fabric: lay a torch beam on it and the nap of small backs strokes one way and then the other, a shiver passing over eight metres of slope. They eat feather, chitin, skin, each other's shed skins, and any warm thing that arrives.

Around and through them: the blind crickets, pale as boiled rice, waving antennae longer than my forearm in slow arcs, feeling the air for the geometry of the room the way a man feels for a door. The whip spiders on the lower walls, one to a square metre, spread flat and utterly motionless, their first pair of legs drawn out into two enormous antennae that they lay along the rock and hold there, reading it. When something walks within reach, the strike is faster than the eye resolves and the animal is simply held, in a basket of spined pedipalps, and then eaten with a slowness that suggests all the time in the world. Centipedes, shin-length, banded like old bruises, that hunt the crickets and can climb. Springtails in the wet margins. Mites too small to see riding on the beetles, and mites riding the mites.

Everything on the floor is organised around one event: a fall.

A nestling comes off a rim at thirty metres. It does not die of the fall. It dies of the reception. The beetles reach it in under a minute, the crickets in two, and the whip spiders converge from three metres away because the wall told them. In eleven minutes there is a wet patch. In a day there is nothing.

And this, I think, is the deep fact about the floor community: every lineage down there began with the same accident. Something dropped in and could not get out. The shaft is a pit trap forty metres wide that has been catching animals for sixty thousand years, and a fraction of them arrived gravid, and their descendants went blind and pale and enormous-antennaed and forgot the surface entirely. The cricket's ancestor fell in. The centipede's ancestor fell in. The snake's ancestor almost certainly fell in as one impregnated female some tens of thousands of years ago, and every pale snake in the Warm Mouth is her, still.

She is a metre and three-quarters now, root-coloured, and she hangs from the vault by four coils, letting her head and a third of her body swing free in the flightpath. She is not looking. She is feeling for the pressure of a wingbeat and for the small warmth behind it, and she strikes into that and misses nine times in ten. She eats perhaps forty birds a year and spends the rest of her time digesting on a ledge polished smooth by her own generations of belly. She has never been on the surface, has no use for the surface, and will die inside a volume of rock she could cross in ninety seconds.
