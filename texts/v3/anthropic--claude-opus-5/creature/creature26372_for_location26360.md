## Nýgres — who is there

**The count.**

Four hundred and seven. The number is chalked on a slate by the canteen door and changed the same day it changes, by whoever notices first, and it has been wrong for more than a week only twice in living memory. Below it, in smaller chalk, are three other numbers: the tern census from last June, the depth of the channel at low water, and the millimetres gained since the last levelling. This is the first thing a visitor sees and it tells them, if they can read it, almost everything: that these people count, that they count strangers and birds and water and the ground itself in the same hand, on the same slate, as though all four were the same kind of fact.

Fourteen surnames. Eighty-eight houses. Forty-three children in the school. Three shifts, so no single night. The boat comes Tuesday and Friday.

**Their bodies.**

You can tell them by the shins. Everyone above the age of four has the plain's signature on them somewhere — a fine white cross-hatching on the shin, the outside of the forearm, the heel of the hand — because new lava does not bruise you, it opens you, and it opens you shallow. Nobody treats it. There is tape in every pocket, in the way that other places have keys.

Their hands are wrong for the region. Not fisherman's hands: no rope grooves, no cold-cracked knuckles. Instead a permanent faint abrasion, a texture like fine sandpaper worn smooth, and, in the older workers, a way of holding the fingers slightly apart, learned from decades of not wanting to touch the wrist with the fingertips. That is *kláði*, the itch, the works' first and mildest tax — the fibre is glass, and glass in a skin fold is a thousand small splinters, and no shower gets all of it. Every house has an outer room with a hook, a bench, and a stiff brush on a lanyard, and you do not enter a Nýgres kitchen without having been heard beating yourself in the porch.

They speak too loudly. They speak at the volume required to be heard over a cupola draught and they do not turn it down when they leave, so that off the plain they are thought rude, and they know they are thought rude, and they cannot hear it. In 1998 a visiting doctor tested every adult in the canteen over two evenings, and the audiograms are in the archive in a green box, and people look them up the way other families look up baptisms. There is a certain pride in the notch at four kilohertz. There is also a shelf of hearing aids that get left in drawers, because an aid brings back the terns and the compressor and the floor all at once and the effect is reported as unbearable — not too loud, but too *present*, like being spoken to by the ground.

They squint in sunshine and complain about it. Their eyes are calibrated for even grey light and they distrust shadow the way inland people distrust fog.

And they keep time in elevens. The compressor thumps every eleven seconds and has done since before anyone under sixty was born, and the town's music sits down on it without asking. Their hymns and their drinking songs and the way they space the words in an argument all fall into an eleven-second frame that nobody has ever written down. The choir has twice competed on the old coast and twice come back baffled and slow. They say the mainland conductors beat too fast. What they mean is that the mainland has no floor under it.

**Their hours.**

Because the furnace never stops, the town has no bedtime, only three of them staggered, and a child raised here can tell you which windows are lit at three in the morning and whose kitchen will give them cocoa. The canteen is open always. The cook — this office has changed hands four times and the name of the office is still *Vigga*, whatever the holder is actually called — keeps a pot of yesterday going on the back ring and a bowl by the bins that she denies is for the foxes.

Tuesday and Friday are the real week. Everything is *before the boat* or *after the boat*. Mail, paint, gas bottles, engine parts, tomatoes' worth of seed, the ornithologists in July, the company's men in any month, and the coffins going the other way.

**Their houses.**

Screwed down, not planted: eight jacks under a small house, sixteen under the school, forty-one under the furnace hall. Under every jack a pad, and under every pad a written record of the pad. The houses are grossly, comically insulated — four hundred millimetres of their own product in every wall, because it is free and it is here and there is a limit to how much of a thing a town can watch leave on a boat without stuffing some of it into itself. The consequence is that the only silence in the whole landscape of Nýgres is indoors, and it is manufactured, and they cannot stand it. Every house has a sound window: one sash, usually on the west, that is never fully closed, even in winter, even at storm, wedged with a bit of shim so the floor can get in. Babies here will not settle in a sealed room. Nobody thinks this is remarkable until they are told.

The paint comes off the boat in whatever colour was cheap, in bulk, and gets shared out, so the town reads in bands of years. The oxide-red row is 1991. The unpleasant institutional yellow, of which there was far too much, is 1979 and 1980 both. People date each other's marriages by it. *We were the green year.*

**Their offices.**

There is a foreman and a union man and a harbourmaster and a teacher and a nurse, and none of those is the important job.

The important job is **the Level.** Currently Auður Vigfúsdóttir, third holder, forty-four, patient to the point of rudeness, in possession of a rod, a bar, an optical level older than she is, and eleven volumes of the Book of Marks. She walks the plain in all weathers with a staff-holder and writes down the height of things. She has the only authority in Nýgres that nobody argues with, because she does not have opinions, she has readings. When a family's floor starts to lean, they do not fight about it; they send for Auður, and she comes and tells them what the ground did, and there is something in that which functions in this town exactly the way a priest functions elsewhere: someone who will come to your house and say, calmly, *this is what has happened to you and it is not your fault and here is what we do about it.*

There is the **Water**, which is one old man, Óli, and a jam jar. He goes to the pool every morning, fills the jar, holds it to the grey sky and counts the amphipods in it — pale, translucent, blind, arrived from nowhere sixty years ago by a route nobody has explained. He writes the number on a card. Twenty-two thousand cards. The town's whole trust in its only water rests on the health of a shrimp that shouldn't be there, and everyone finds this reasonable, and Óli has trained two successors already because he is eighty-one and the office cannot lapse.

There is the **Tern Walk** in June, which is not paid: two adults with poles taking the school across the plain and back, in a screaming white cone, twice a day, hunched, counting heads. There is the **Blasting Man**, semi-retired, who cut the channel the second time and would like very much to be allowed to do it again. There is the **Ferry** — no office, just an understanding — because you cannot dig a grave in sixty-one-year-old basalt without a compressor and a bad afternoon, and so the dead go out over the rubble neck on foot, carried, to the old churchyard on the older coast, where there is soil. They call it *the planting ground.* To die, here, in the actual idiom of the place, is *to take root.* The joke is sixty years old and still does its work: this is the only town in the region where the dead are the only ones who get to be planted, and every child understands, without being told, that permanence is available and it is somewhere else.

**What they eat.**

Rabbit, more than they will admit to outsiders. Fulmar eggs in May, taken off the lower ledges by two men on a rope who have both been shouted at by the ornithologists and both intend to keep going. Angelica stems peeled and eaten raw by children. Rhubarb, gone feral behind three houses. And tomatoes, cucumbers, a slightly hysterical quantity of basil, out of the greenhouse on the lee side of the furnace hall, which is heated on waste flue gas and is therefore hot, green, wet, dim, and only faintly loud — the nearest thing to a church, the nearest thing to a park, the place teenagers go to be alone and old men go to sit down. Everybody knows the arithmetic. The greenhouse lives as long as the furnace lives. Nobody says it out loud in the greenhouse.

**Their language.**

The name means new grass. The first foreman found one hollow of it in the second summer, a metre and a half across, and wrote the word on a survey sheet as a joke, and the joke set.

*The floor* is the humming of the plain, and it is a map as much as a sound. Different flow textures give different notes, and a child here is taught six of them by name before they are taught to swim: the smooth ropes above the works give the low one; the burst slab by the second thicket gives the flat, buzzing one, called *the wasp*; the quarry lip gives almost nothing and is therefore the most useful landmark on the plain. Old Sigrún, who has been blind since her fifties, crosses from her door to the canteen and back by ear in any weather and has been offered help perhaps four thousand times.
