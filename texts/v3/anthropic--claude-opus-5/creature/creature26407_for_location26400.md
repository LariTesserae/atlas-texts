## The People of the Sump

There are thirty-nine of them this year. They know the number exactly, the way you know how many fingers you have, because every evening as the lake sags back down the wall each of them knocks their name into a stone and waits to be answered, and any silence in that roll is walked out and looked for.

They call themselves, in the flat bellowing tongue of the bowl, *the ones who answer*. Their word for a stranger is the word for quiet. It is not a metaphor and it is not politeness. A newcomer stands there at the tideline, ears stunned, mouth shut, making no contribution — and to the plants, which feed on vibration, a silent body is a dead spot in the field, an acre of shade. To be quiet here is to be a small, portable famine.

## Bodies

They are short, and they are fat, and they are almost hairless, and they are the colour of the flour they powder themselves with, which is to say no colour at all.

The fatness is not indulgence. It is their water. Nothing in this bowl is wet; there is no spring, no dew, no ice, no rain, no rime on any north face, and so these people drink nothing at all and have no word for drinking except one they learned from the river valleys and now use as an obscenity. Their water is made inside them, wrung out of wax by their own slow chemistry, the way a camel's is, the way a seed's is. A hand's depth of fat over the ribs is three months of thirst answered. So a heavy woman is a rich woman, a well-loved child is a round child, and thinness is not a fashion or an ascetic virtue but a visible stage of dying, discussed in the same tone as a cough.

They do not sweat. They cannot afford to. Instead they shed heat into the dense gas by conduction, which the gas is greedy for, and they do it with their extremities: broad thin hands, long thin ears held out from the skull, feet like paddles, all of it laced close to the surface with blood. When a man overheats you can see it in his hands going dark. When he is very hot he climbs — up out of the lake, above the shimmer, into the thin cold nothing where the heat pours off him in a few minutes and where he stands hugging himself, deaf, blinking, and comes back down.

Because that is the other half of the arrangement: above the line they are cripples. Ears grown for a heavy medium hear almost nothing in mountain air — voices come through as far-off insect scratching — and eyes grown for amber dusk are scalded by the high sun. Also the sobriety. Down in the lake the gas is faintly narcotic, and they have lived in it since birth and think of that soft slowed generosity as simply what being awake feels like. Come up out of it and the mind sharpens horribly, goes fast and thin and suspicious. They distrust everything decided above the line. Bargains struck up there with copper-traders are honoured, grudgingly, and then talked about for years as a kind of illness that got into the village.

Their hands are the real organ of knowledge. Sight gives them three things and no more: the shimmer of the tideline, the stillness of a sink, and motion. Everything else is touch and hearing. They read a pipe-frond by laying both palms on it and feeling whether the wax is climbing; they read a hulder's health by the buzz of it in the ground; they read each other constantly and without permission, because in this gas a body has no privacy at all. Ten paces away, in the middle of a conversation, you can hear the other person's stomach working. You can hear a pregnancy at four months. You can hear a heart pick up when its owner begins to lie.

This last fact has made their law simple and their liars famous. Disputes are heard at the focus of the Ear, in full drone, where everyone's insides are on display, and a person who can hold their pulse steady through an accusation is called *quiet-bodied* and is respected, consulted, and never entirely trusted with anything.

## Manners

Loudness is charity. This is the whole of their ethics and it takes some getting used to.

The plants eat noise. A drone held over a stand of pipe-fronds is a season of feeding; a stretch of silence over a young tallow tree is a wound that shows in its paddles two years later. So every human sound in the bowl is understood as fodder. They shout not because they are deaf but because they are farming. They sing while they work and while they eat and while they walk from one place to another, and the singing is loud, tuneless by any standard from below, and pointed — sung *at* particular plants, at the terrace, at a sick frond, the way you'd water a row.

Children are soothed with volume. A frightened baby is held against a bellowing chest, and the bellow is comfort, and quiet is what frightens it. Babies are born, when there is time to arrange it, on the stone terrace at the focus of the Ear, so that the first cry goes into the great four-hundred-year trees; a strong first cry is remembered and mentioned to the child for the rest of its life. Their lullabies are the loudest music they have.

They knock before they speak. Every person has a name-rhythm, a short figure of taps and scrapes, and infants learn to knock theirs a year before they can manage a word. A rhythm carries across the whole bowl through the ground and through the gas; a spoken name does not. Genealogies are therefore percussion — a lineage is recited as a run of figures, and the dead are knocked, not named, and the knocking of a very old dead person is done slowly and with a stone rather than the knuckles.

Some rhythms are lost. The names of whoever built the Ear are lost, and this is the sore place in their culture, the thing they get drunk about. There was a long Cold Fall, generations back, or a sickness, or both; the accounts collapse into each other. What survives is a wall of dry stone built to a parabolic tolerance nobody now living can even measure, and a terrace of trees so overfed that they are cathedrals, and no figure to knock over them in thanks. So on the night after the seiche's highest tide they knock an empty rhythm — a shape with the taps left out, three silences in a row where a name should be — and it is the only sanctioned silence in their year, and it makes everyone miserable, and nobody will give it up.

## Work

They herd loafs. A loaf is blind, slow, buoyant, and stupid, and will drift wherever it is not being clicked at, so a herder walks behind a school with a pair of clapper-stones and steers them like smoke, corralling them at night inside a ring of hung fleece where the shrike-swimmers' hammer-clicks are muffled. A loaf gives fat, gut, and a flotation bag that dries into a translucent bladder used for everything a pot would be used for anywhere else — because there is no clay here, clay wanting water, and so there is no pottery in the bowl at all.

They mine wax: the middens under the tallow trees, contested with the hulders, who are bigger and have right of way. They cut and dry pipe-frond and re-slot it — tuning it — which is the closest thing they have to a learned profession, and the tuners are usually women in their forties with ruined fingers and an exact and unpleasant sense of pitch. They knap glassbeard filament into blades. They own, between all of them, eleven copper beads, worn on the wrist, from the river valleys downhill, which are considered unserious places on the grounds that a river makes so much noise for free that the people there have never had to be generous.

They wash in dust. Every morning is the flouring: mineral powder worked into the skin so the day's wax cannot cake and seal, and every evening the caked mixture is scraped off with a glass flake and saved, because it burns. Their lamps run on themselves. Nothing here is ever damp and nothing is ever stained and nothing is ever clean in the way you mean it.

And when they want to be alone they go into the huts, which are hung with fleece and packed with dust and are the deadest, softest, most muffled rooms in the world. To an outsider a Sump house feels like being buried. To them it is the only place a person can hear themselves think, and it is where they go to grieve, to sulk, to have sex, to die if they are given the choice.

## Those Who Went Up

Not everyone can bear it. Some are born with an intolerance to the roar and scream through their childhoods; more often it comes with age, when the ears finally go and a person begins to live in a slackening, hissing version of the bowl, answering the wrong rhythms, missing the shrike-clicks, wandering nearer and nearer the sinks without knowing it.

Those people go up. There is a cluster of low shelters just under the lip, above the highest cairn, where six or seven of them live at any time: the deaf, the noise-sick, the disgraced. They keep the tideline cairns, watch the north notch for the cold, and deal with the traders, since they alone can hold a conversation in thin air. They are pitied, in the way you pity someone with an illness that is also a kind of trespass. They have seen the sky. They know the shapes of stars, which nobody down in the amber has ever seen and nobody down there considers interesting. Children are told not to bother them and bother them constantly.

## The Dead

A body is carried to a sink at the hour when the lake is lowest, wrapped in glassbeard fleece so the silica will hold its shape for a few hundred years, and dropped.

Nobody speaks. There is nothing to be gained by speaking: pure gas swallows a voice into a groan, and the whole point of a sink is that it is the one place in the bowl where sound does not do its work. This is their entire conception of death — not stillness, not darkness, not cold, but a place where you cannot be answered. Below their feet the bones lie sorted by size and waxed over and undecayed, the whole ancestry of the settlement, silent, in a hole where dust hangs instead of falling.

Then they climb back up out of the low ground and start making noise again immediately, on purpose, at volume, and this is not disrespect; it is the opposite of disrespect. It is the argument.

## What They Believe

That the bowl is an ear.

Not a god's, exactly — they are not much interested in who it belongs to, and get bored by the question the way you'd get bored by being asked who owns the weather. What matters to them is the fact of the shape: the hood-cliff cupped against the sky, the parabola their ancestors built inside it as if to agree with it, the vents at the bottom breathing warm sweet gas in and out like something asleep and listening. They live inside an attention. Their obligation is not to be good, or clean, or humble. Their obligation is to be *worth hearing* — which is why the Ear must be fed, and the trees must be enormous, and the drone must not stop, and the west end must eventually be rebuilt properly by someone who understands it, and why, twenty times a year when the cold comes over the notch and punches the voice out of the bowl and the loafs drop into the grit and the fronds crack and there is nothing to hear at all, thirty-nine people sit in the softest rooms on the mountain with their hands over their faces and wait, in genuine terror, to find out if they are still being listened to.
