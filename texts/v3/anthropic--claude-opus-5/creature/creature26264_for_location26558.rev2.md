# The people of the Nap

## Bodies

They are short and deep-chested, and they stoop, and the stoop is not age. A child on the Nap spends its first years being bent: bent under the low door-lintels that keep a house's mouth small, bent over the paths with a scraper, bent into the crouch that is the only polite way to cross open rock. By twenty the shoulders have rolled forward and stay there. From behind, at a distance, in fog, a person looks like one of the mustard slugs going up a shaved path, and there are nights when that resemblance saves a life.

Their skin is the colour of the underside of the felt — pale, faintly veined at the temples and the insides of the wrists. Green dimness for six days out of seven will do that. In the thin gold hour they go blotched and pink and are shy about it.

The nails are ground down on glass every third day, not trimmed but shaped, into a low flat curve that cannot click against cane, against stone, against another nail. It is the first grooming a mother does and the last a daughter does for a corpse. A person with long nails is not eccentric here; a person with long nails is a person who intends something.

The hands are two different tools. The heel and the outer edge are horn — that is what you press with, push with, strike with, that is what takes the scraper's handle and the knapping billet. The fingertips are kept soft, deliberately, greased with slug-fat, because fingertips are capital. Everything on this mountain that is worth knowing is written in raised nap, and the ones who let their fingers go hard become, by degrees, illiterate.

They breathe through the mouth. The nose whistles.

And the hair is not hair, exactly, not to them — it is the town's only fibre. It is grown in long rope-lengths, oiled, braided in sleeves to keep it off the wet, and cut at intervals the way other people harvest a field. A man's coarse hair goes to lashings and ladder-wrappings. A woman's finer hair goes to the cord that holds the roof sheets. And the fine, silken, first hair of children goes to the dew-looms — the finest filament, the one that pulls water out of nothing — so that every drop the town drinks has come down a strand grown on the head of one of its children. They do not find this touching. It is simply where thread comes from. But the loom-tenders know whose hair is whose in the frame, and they say the strings sag a little in the year after a child dies, and nobody argues with them.

## Speech

Conversation distance is kissing distance. Two people talking on the Nap stand close enough to share breath, faces a hand apart, because everything is happening on the face — an inventory of small movements, brow and nostril and the corners of the mouth and a particular flick of the lower eyelid that means *no, and don't ask again.* Their faces never rest. Alone, walking, they are still making expressions at nobody: a lifetime's muscle habit, like a weaver's fingers moving in sleep.

Below the face, the hands. The signs are small and are made low, against the chest, half-hidden, in a sort of mumble. In thick fog they abandon the eye entirely and sign onto each other: a hand taken and worked in the dark, a palm drawn on, and the touch on the forearm that means *yes, I heard you.*

Laughter is the hard one. It is done through the nose in fast short pushes with the shoulders going, the throat clamped, tears coming, and it is the single most exhausting thing a person here does for pleasure. Grief is worse. You cannot sob. You hold it. Everyone holds it. This is why a funeral shout is never only for the one on the rock — it is a backlog, seven years of it, the whole town emptying itself at once, and why the mound that rises over him is so enormous, and why nobody ever says whose grief the extra bulk of it was.

Children are not taught to speak in the houses. A child who has begun to make sounds is carried down into a pit and put on the stone seat and talked at for a season, and so the first place any of them hears their own voice is underground, with walls swelling gently on every side, an adult's hand on the sternum to feel the volume. Infants are kept in pits too, in lined cribs, mothers sitting the long grey hours beside them. The child-pits give the sweetest crop on the ridge. A colicky baby will feed a household for a month, and the household will be grateful, and exhausted, and will eat the crest knowing exactly what it is.

Because a voice has a grain. Everyone's crop is different — whorled, combed, close, ropy, open — and everyone here has eaten their relatives for their whole life and can tell you, with a mouthful, whose pit it came out of. They judge a marriage partly this way. *He talks a good wall.* A big resonant throat is a dowry. There are men on this ridge who are not much liked and are married anyway, because eight hours of their ordinary complaining raises a crest twice the height of anyone else's, and the family will eat well for as long as he is annoyed, which is forever.

There are seventeen songs. There is supposed to have been an eighteenth. The story is not told often, and the shape of it is this: a woman sang it in a pit at first grey and the pit closed over her before midday, and when they cut her out she was already upholstered, and the crop that year was the richest anyone had known and no one would touch it.

## Offices

*Path-scrapers.* A third of all labour, and the closest thing to a civic religion. They go out bent at grey with glass-edged blades and shave the padded strips back to their beaten floor, working outward from the doors in an order that is fixed and ancient — three generations is ancient here — so that no house is ever the last one opened. The scrapings are not eaten. They go to the birds.

*Pit-sitters.* Everyone, in rotation; but some are good at it and are excused other work. The gift is not a loud voice, it is stamina — the ability to talk for nine hours without hurrying, without excitement, an even low unstopping delivery like water in a cane. The great pit-sitters recite genealogies that take two days and end, always, in the name that means roughly *came-up*, the first, the one who is said to have carried the first cage of mute birds on his back out of the white.

*Knappers.* Recognisable across a shelf. Missing finger-pads, forearms hatched with old glass, and an odd prissiness about noise, since their own craft is the loudest legal work on the mountain. They knap in dug hollows out past the last house, and behind every knapping hollow is a standing wall of pale swollen felt, the fattest growth on the ridge, harvested twice a year and never eaten because it tastes of nothing. It tastes of stone hitting stone. They feed it to the slugs.

*Bird-keepers.* Usually a child, usually the youngest. The mute birds are small, grey, fringe-feathered, bred so long for silence that they cannot make a sound at all and gape at you in obvious distress when they try. They are kept in cane cages lined with felt, hung at the doors, and they hunt the thumb-sized shrillers by an itch they feel rather than a noise they hear. Each has a name. Each name is signed, not spoken. When one dies the child is permitted, once, to cry out loud, and the family stands around and lets it happen, and scrapes the doorway clear in the morning.

*The deaf.* Not many, and precious. They work the north drop where the fresh chips let go, and the fissure mouths where the warm damp comes up, and they are the ones sent out at night when something must be sent out at night, because they cannot be startled into a sound by a sound. They marry well. They are also the only ones on the Nap who are ever seen to be genuinely at ease, and the hearing find this either restful or unbearable, depending.

## The mouth under the bed

Almost every house has one and nobody mentions it: a small pit, knee-deep, dug under the sleeping shelf, lined and lidded. Into it a person puts the things that would otherwise come out of them in the open — the confession, the hatred, the name said once, the argument won at last against someone who has been dead eleven years. You lift the lid, you lie down on your belly, you put your face in, you speak into the wool for as long as it takes.

The growth that comes up in there is pale and fast and slightly sour and it is never, ever eaten. It is cut out at the dark of each month and given to the birds, who do not care.

This is the closest thing the Nap has to privacy, and it is not very close. The felt keeps the grain. A scandal here is almost always someone's cousin, cleaning out a dead man's house, putting a hand into the underbed pit out of simple habit and reading what is in there.

## The other tenants

The **shrug** should be counted a neighbour rather than a beast, since it lives off the same crop by another route. It is long, low, loose-skinned, and its flank is its face: a whole side of sensing hide laid down against the moss to feel the differential of growth, to feel where the world is fattening, because fattening means somebody has just been careless. It does not hunt animals. It hunts errors. Children are told it comes for whoever spoke after dark, and the children are told correctly.

The **slugs** are tolerated and half-herded, since their shaved spirals do a share of the path-work for free, and their fat greases fingertips and burns in lamps.

The **birds** — the wild ones — have the ridge's other literature, and the people can read a little of it, the way you might read a neighbouring language badly. They know the raised bumps that mean *shrug below* and the ones that mean *fog lifting*, and they check the moss faces each morning like anyone checking the weather, and the birds come afterward and rub the writing out with their breasts, and the people have never established whether this is politeness or contempt.

## What actually lives here

The honest answer is that there is one inhabitant on the Nap and it is the felt, and everything else is a mouth it grew.

The people would not put it that way. They would say they farm it, shave it, roof with it, eat it, sleep in it, bury in it. All true, and all the kind of truth a tenant tells. But consider the arrangement from the moss's side: it has a species here that gathers water for it in hair-strung frames, that clears its dead growth and redistributes the nutrient, that concentrates vibration into round lined chambers for eight hours a day — a livestock that lows on schedule — and that, at the end of each of its lives, assembles in one place and screams for a night so that the felt can come up through the body and take the whole thing in at once, protein and voice together.

And the felt keeps them. That is the part the people are not wrong about. The walls hold the grain. A widow reads her husband's arguing off the inside of a house at two in the morning with her soft fingertips, and it is really him — not a memory of him, the actual shape his throat made in the air, preserved in swollen fibre the way a footprint is preserved in mud, and she can go along the wall and find the year they were both too tired to speak, a long smooth patch like a scar, and put her hand flat on it.

So there is a town of forty-one houses, spaced wide as a precaution, full of bent pale people with soft fingertips and ground-down nails and enormous restless faces, who grow the town's thread on their own heads and the town's food out of their own throats, and who go about all day inside a cloud, in a silence you could take for holiness and which is actually a very old, very careful, unrelenting economy.

At night the shrugs come up out of the seams and lay their long flanks on everything and feel for a mistake. The felt grows a hand's width. A chip lets go on the north face and goes down clinking, and in forty-one houses somebody's eyes open in the dark, and the thought is always the same one — that the mountain is not finished, that it is still coming up, that it will be a peak and not a ridge, and that nobody knows whether the felt can follow it that high, or whether they will have to choose.

Nobody knows either, though the pit-sitters recite it two days running, whether the felt came up the stair with the first one who came up, or whether it was already here waiting when he arrived with his cage of silent birds. Both of them are new on this mountain. Both of them are younger than the fog. It is possible they came up together, out of wherever the sound is kept, and have been holding very still ever since, listening for whatever it is they are both so quiet about.

They sleep in wool that remembers them. The wool does not sleep.
