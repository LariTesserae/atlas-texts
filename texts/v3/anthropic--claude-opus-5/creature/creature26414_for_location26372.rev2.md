## At half a metre

At half a metre, which is the distance at which these people conduct nearly all of their lives, the first thing is the face coming toward you. They approach closer than you expect and then stop, exactly, as if there were a rim there. The face is pale under weather: freckled hard across the cheekbones and the bridge of the nose, everywhere else the colour of the inside of a root. The eyes are narrowed even indoors, even in the dark, because sixty years of glare has set the muscles that way, and there is a permanent pale crease-fan at the outer corners where the squint has kept the sun off. Above them the brow-visor, soot-black, greasy from handling, pushed up into the hair, which is coarse and braided flat to the skull and carries that white salting at the temples that never entirely washes out.

Then, because they are close, you notice the breathing. It is deep, slow, and audible, and it does not stop being audible when they speak. Their chests are barrelled and their necks are short and their hands and feet are always a little ruddy and swollen with blood the body keeps pushing out to the cold ends of itself. They are small people, generally — the diet is thin, mostly root and fermented scum and shrimp by the handful — and they are strong in a way that has nothing to do with lifting. Nobody here can lift much. But a woman of fifty can stand knee-deep in a seep in the wind for seven hours, adjusting a weave with her fingertips, and not shiver, and not particularly notice that she has done it.

They do not gesture much. The hands stay low and close to the body, and when they do move they move at the speed of someone reaching into water that must not be disturbed.

## The way they talk

A conversation is an approach, a settling, and a leaning. The lean is the punctuation. To lean in is to take the turn; to straighten slightly is to give it back. Two people talking look, from outside, like they are drinking in turns from the same cup.

The voice is low and slightly breathy and pitched to die at two metres. They can whisper across the width of a room with complete clarity because the rooms are small, and they cannot make themselves heard across a courtyard at all, and have no wish to, and find the idea faintly obscene. There is no word for *call out*, but there is an elaborate word for the failure of restraint that would make a person want to.

For anything longer than a lean, they use water. A pebble in a runnel is *look up*; two, spaced, is *look up, it is your household*; a handful all at once is the signal for a fouling upstream and it empties a district. For actual messages, they float them: a chip of willow bark with a knotted thread wound round it, which goes wherever the channel goes, which is always down. You can write downhill. You cannot write uphill. To say anything to someone above you, you must go up and stand in front of them and lean.

This is not felt as an inconvenience. It is felt as the shape of the world. Their tenses are not past and present and future but *below*, *here*, and *above*, so that to say a thing will happen is to say it is uphill of you, and to say a thing has happened is to put it down among the drying quarters with the ruined roofs. The consequence is that anyone who goes down off the rim into the lowlands and comes back speaks wrong forever afterwards — describes the journey in the grammar of the dead, because it was down. Returnees are called *the mis-tensed*. People are kind to them. People do not quite listen to them. They are treated as a kind of weather that happened once.

## Threads

Ask one of them where they live and you will get a four-day answer, because a household is not a place.

Every family is a stripe. It runs up-gradient into the young quarters, where its armature stands out in the new seeps like the skeleton of something swimming, and it runs down through the tiers where the family actually sleeps and eats and keeps its shrimp, and it goes on down through the yellowing quarters where the oldest of them still stubbornly live hauling water, and then out the bottom of the city entirely into the white ruin, four days deep, where the roofs are gone and the rooms hold the family's dead. And beyond that, if the family is old, it goes on into the hummock country where nothing is legible at all, and a person can point at a low pale swell of ground with willow in the hollow and say, in the deepest of the down-tenses, *that*.

To be able to point that far is the only nobility on the Shelf.

The stripe moves at a metre a year, upslope, forever, and the family moves with it, and so nobody ever has an address; they have a direction and a rate. The wealth of a household is its position in the Turn — the rota, kept in knotted cord by a body of extremely boring old people, that determines which family gets which emerging seep in which decade. The Turn is centuries long. Marriages are negotiated to splice two threads and improve a position four generations downstream of the negotiators, none of whom will see it. Families that lost their place and had to be taken into another thread are called *the grafted*, and are perfectly ordinary, and know exactly who they are, and so does everyone else.

The grammar again: you cannot say *I built this*. The verb will not take it. You can say a house came to you from your grandmother and that a house goes from you to a child not yet born. Nobody sleeps in a room they made. Everybody sleeps in a room that was left for them. A life is roughly three houses long — the one you were born under, the one you site, the one you finish and hand on — and the arithmetic of this is taught to children early and is the nearest thing they have to a theology.

## The work of not doing

The prestigious trades are all forms of judgement.

**Setters** decide where. They go up into the unbuilt terraces with a bone knife and a bundle of standard lattices — palm-sized, woven to an exact and ancient specification — and they hang one in each new seep, and they come back in a year and weigh it. The gain tells them everything: how vigorous the water, how long it will run, whether it is worth forty years of a family's Turn. In the setters' hall, which is a low arcade three hundred metres long, these year-weights hang in their thousands from the ceiling in ranked cords, oldest at the down-gradient end, each tagged with knots. It is the only archive on the plateau. It is read by touch as much as by eye. A good setter can heft a stoned lattice in the dark and name the seep and the decade.

**Weavers** decide how, knowing that everything they make is going to disappear. Their work is an armature designed around its own rotting; the lime takes the shape and the willow goes to nothing and leaves the wall full of hollow threads. What survives is the *pattern* — the surface texture of every wall in the city is the fossil of a weaving style, and the styles change slowly and traceably, so that walking up-gradient through the tiers is walking forward through four hundred years of fashion in a matter of an hour. Weavers are vain about this in a way they would deny.

**Hollowers** are the exception to everything. They are the ones who go under a finished roof-mound and dig the peat out from beneath it on their backs, in the dark, in baskets, judging the thickness of the shell above their faces by knuckling it and listening. They are the only people on the Shelf permitted to strike anything. They are small and wiry and marry mostly among themselves, and they are respected and slightly avoided, and when a hollower dies under a shell the shell is left, and the family lives in it, and does not mention it.

And then there is the largest occupation of all, which has no name because it is simply what everyone does: standing in the right place and not interfering. Children are given a mat at eleven and told to set it and leave it. They are allowed to look. They are not allowed to touch. At twenty they take it out of the water and it is a slab with their own childhood hands printed in lime all over the weave, and it goes into the wall of the room where they will sleep as adults, and that is the whole of the ceremony.

The one true crime is hurrying. The word covers impatience, interference, and fouling water above someone else's district, which are understood to be the same act. The punishment is not a blow — nothing is struck — but relocation: a season spent down-gradient among the drying quarters, hauling your own water up, sleeping in the yellow rooms with the widening windows, in the direction of the dead. Almost nobody needs it twice.

## The old, and the crickets

They go blind. Not all of them, but enough that blindness is understood as a stage of life rather than a misfortune, like greying. The glare takes the eyes milk-white from the edges in, and a person spends ten or fifteen years watching the world narrow to a bright pinhole, and prepares for it, and is prepared for.

Because the blind become the listeners.

Every wall on the Shelf is riddled with the hollow ghosts of willow stems, and those hollows carry air, and they whistle — not audibly to a stranger, but a faint continuous thread of tone under the general hiss. A sound wall sings one way. A wall going hollow with dissolution sings lower. A wall re-wetted by a seep that has wandered sings almost not at all. And in the hollows live the white crickets: wingless, blind themselves, translucent, which crawl through the veins of the buildings and change the note as they pass.

The old listeners walk the arcades in the two dead hours — the lid closing, the lid opening — when the fog is at the third tier and the air is stillest, with their fingers on the stone and their heads tilted, and they read the traffic of insects in the dead veins of the city. A house with no crickets in it is not considered finished. A house whose crickets have gone is a house that has begun to die, and it is a blind woman of eighty who says so, quietly, half a metre from your ear, and after she says it your family starts thinking about the Turn.

The crickets get there before the people and stay after them. In the dry quarters, in the long white silence where the roofs have fallen, the walls are still singing faintly with them. This is well known and not considered sad.

## The gravel

The water that builds the city also builds inside the people who drink it. They call it *the gravel*. Almost every man past forty has it and a great many women; it arrives as a pain that starts in the back and moves, and it cannot be screamed about, because nobody screams here, and so the sound a person makes passing a stone is a long low hum into a jar, with two or three relatives sitting against them, pressing, at a distance of nothing at all. Afterwards the stone is kept. Everyone has a small pouch of them. They are the one thing a person makes entirely alone and entirely without waiting.

They believe, more or less, that the body is an armature and that living on the Shelf slowly sets it. The stiffening of old joints, the crust that forms at the ankles of anyone who stands in seeps for devotion, the white at the temples, the gravel itself — all one process, the same one that is making the city, running at the human rate. This is not a metaphor to them. It is the observation from which the metaphors come.

So in every house there are two jars. One holds hard water, straight from the seam, and a smear of its lime is put on the crown of a newborn's head. The other holds rain — soft, unmineralised, the only solvent on the plateau — and rain is what you wash a body with when you take it down, and rain is what you pour over the feet of someone who has stood too long in one place and cannot free them. Hard water for becoming. Soft water for release. Children are taught both by the time they can walk, and neither is explained.

## Going down

When someone dies the household carries them down-gradient. It takes four days, in stages, through the yellowing tiers and then through the roofless white where the dead channels cross-hatch the ground, to whatever room of the old thread is still standing enough to be a room. The air up here is cold and thin and dry once you are out from under the seeps, and bodies do not rot so much as go to leather and then to paper. The family's dead sit in their own abandoned rooms in the order they arrived, the nearest ones still recognisable, the ones four hundred metres further down reduced to a shape under grey felt, the ones beyond that gone into the hummocks with the willow.

One tooth does not go down. It is taken and set into the coarse open weave of whatever the family is building at the wet edge, and over nine years the lime closes over it, and it is in the wall.

So the young quarters, the bright unfinished armatures at the top of the city where the water is newest and the children do the small work — those walls are full of teeth, and full of crickets, and whistling very faintly, and rising a metre a year into ground that no one has ever stood on.

That is who lives here. A quarter of a million people, lean and squinting and quiet, leaning in at half a metre to speak; drifting north at the pace of a fingernail; trailing four days of their own dried ancestors behind them and pushing a lace of teeth and moss and intention out in front; every one of them occupied, essentially, full-time, in the work of standing still in the correct place, in the cool bright wet, and letting the water do it.

And along the rims of the terrace basins, at the edge of everyone's vision and never in the centre of it, the single tall grey bird goes on stabbing the shallows for shrimp — the only thing on the whole white continent that ever strikes anything, and is not spoken to about it.
