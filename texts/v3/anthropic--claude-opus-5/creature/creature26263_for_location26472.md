## The Census, Enlarged

You asked what lives there. The honest answer takes a while, because it depends on a question the room refuses to settle, and because everything that lives in the Loud Room lives in the lee of something. That is the whole principle. Every organism here is the shape of a pocket of calm — the width of a boulder's shadow, the length of a lull, the four dry meters under an invisible floor. Learn to see the calm and the census reads itself.

---

**The grazers.** They are the residents. Everything else is a visitor, a monument, or an argument.

Pale, long-limbed, blind, roughly the size of a thumb joint with legs three times that; the guides call them lull-crickets, and one of the five scripts on the dry wall has a word for them that a philologist has guessed — from a cognate in a hymn about weather — means *the ones that come out when it stops.*

They are blind in the brightest room in the world, and this is not the irony it looks like. The light here is shadowless and constant. It comes from a plane, not a point; it flickers only with the pulse, and the pulse is better read as pressure. Light in the Loud Room is not information. It tells you nothing about where you are, what is near you, whether it is coming. So the grazers put their eyes down, some unrecoverable number of generations ago, and reinvested in the sinus and the leg. They read the room the way you read it in the first ten meters of the crawl: as a weight in the head. A surge is legible to them four to six minutes before it arrives. You can watch the drift begin — not a stampede, a slow migration downward along the walls, the whole scattered population of an entire terrace deciding at once, without a signal you can detect, to be somewhere else.

They feed on the underside of the sill. Something accretes there — a film, faintly greenish, cool to the touch, that appears to be the fraction of the river the sill declines to convert but also declines to drop as flour. It renews in hours. The grazers hang inverted from nothing, in loose rows, spaced with the regularity of animals that can feel each other's kicks through a shared surface, and they browse it clean and it comes back. This is the base of the food web, and it is not really a food web; it is a hem.

Their courtship is a drumming. Stridulation is pointless here, so the males strike the flour-covered stone of the dry room with the flat of the hind femur, and the signal travels through calcite. It works only when the room is quiet enough that the stone is not already ringing. Which means the reproductive season of the species is not a season at all: it is the sum of all lulls. In an even year, with a good pulse, that is perhaps ninety hours of possibility spread across nine months. In a storm season the stone screams for three weeks, and no one drums, and a great many of them simply die of having been born at the wrong time.

They carry a mite. Blind, of course, one to a cricket, sometimes two, riding at the wing base. It eats nothing but its host and lives nowhere but on it, and it is, as far as anyone has established, the only animal in the cave that has never touched rock. Three links: hem, cricket, mite. That is the food chain. There is no fourth.

---

**The four.** Everyone says four. Since roughly the eleventh scratched name, only three have been seen.

They sit in the warm pools of the upper terraces — squat, wide, the colour of wet lime, with a skin that takes a beading of condensation and holds it, so that at certain angles they look upholstered in glass. The pulse can be counted in the throat, and it is slow enough that the first surveyor to try it assumed the animal was dead and wrote it down as dead, and came back the next season and found it in the same pool at the same angle and struck out the word.

They have names, and the names are older than any living person, and they are reissued. Nobody knows whether the animal in the second pool is the animal a Bronze-Age hand meant, or its ninetieth descendant, or a thing that does not descend. The fourth name belongs to a pool that a storm season took out in some century — the terrace fan collapsed, the dam went, the water went elsewhere — and the animal was never found again. The name is still counted. The census is four. Three are visible.

They eat, apparently, almost never. One feeding has been recorded in the whole documented history of the room: a grazer came too near the lip, in a lull, and the thing in the pool took it, and the taking lasted a minute and a half from first movement to last, and the witness — who had come for the lulls, and had a hood, and was not a naturalist — wrote only that it was like watching a door close in another house.

What they do do is listen. The tympana are enormous, absurd, plate-sized relative to the head, and they are the one organ in the animal that is unmistakably expensive. Whatever else is true of the four, they have been listening continuously to this room for a length of time nobody can bound, and they have not been observed to be doing anything else. Put your head in the stone hood and speak, and the nearest of them will sometimes open one eye. Not toward you. Just open it.

---

**The crust.** By mass, this is the dominant organism in the Loud Room, and it is the reason the footprints are still there.

The white patches at the edges of the dry room grow about a millimetre a century, feed on the flour and on what the grazers leave, and lay down a rind that is chemically indistinguishable from the floor and biologically not. It has taken the footprints into itself. It has taken the fifth cup's contact ring into itself. It is, very slowly, closing the names. The three most recent scripts stand proud and sharp; the fourth is softening; the fifth — the one nobody reads — is a shallow braille under a skin of white, and in ninety years it will be a texture, and in three hundred it will be wall.

It does not grow within a hand's span of the rod. There is a clean halo there, bare stone, the exact reach of a spread palm, and the argument about whether that is the heat or the touching has been going for as long as there has been anyone to have it.

---

**The green.** Three plants, and I mean three individuals.

The cushion of liverwort on the third terrace, hand-sized, emerald, is sitting in a pocket of dead air the size of a hand behind one boulder, and has been that size in every drawing made of it. It is not thriving. It is exactly filling the calm available to it, which is what thriving looks like when the room is the room.

The streamers in the deep pools are one thing, functionally — a single continuous population, scoured to nothing every storm season and rebuilt from cells lodged in the calcite. Call it the room's hair. It is cut back with a regularity no living thing outside has to endure and it does not appear to mind.

The fern by the crawl is ours. It is not in the earliest sketch. It is two meters tall, shredded on the upwind side, and grotesquely healthy, and the reason is that it stands in the one place in the chamber where warm wet air meets a steady trickle of imported mud, spilled grain, breath, and skin. It came in on a sleeve. It lives on visitors. It is the only domesticated thing in the cave and nobody domesticated it.

---

**The swifts.** Tenants of the shaft, not the room; but they own something here that nobody else does.

Their nests are cemented with a saliva that takes up dissolved lime from the spray and sets to something the surveyors' picks would not touch. The nests are built on nests. There is a column of them in the north wall of the shaft, twenty meters up, fused, three and a half meters tall, and it is older than the bench, older than the doorframe, older than the steps, and it is the only structure in the Loud Room that has been continuously maintained by its builders since it was begun. Two thousand years of concrete cups. The swifts have out-persisted every human institution that has ever taken an interest in this place, including the shrine.

How they navigate is not understood. What is understood, and is easy to check and strange every time, is that they never cross the sill. Not once, in every hour of watching anyone has ever logged. They bank along it. They skim it, at the height of your chest, and turn, and use it as a floor. A swift wheeling down into the chamber and out again has flown through the glare, not through the plane. To a swift, the Loud Room is a room with a bright floor and a ceiling thirty meters up a chimney, and the dry room where the crickets breed and the crust grows and the people sleep is not a place. It is under the ground.

They know the altitude of the sill better than we do. They have never needed to be told about the scorch.

---

**The cup.** There is usually one person, in any given generation, who could be said to live here part of the year, and no tradition of calling them anything. Not a priest; the shrine on the hill is a separate and much smaller matter, tended by whoever is nearest. They come for the lulls. They sleep in the flour, in the light, and dream of open water like everyone does. They use the hood. They learn the signs, and add to them, and lose them again, so that the sign vocabulary of the Loud Room has been invented perhaps forty times.

What they do that can be traced is this: the fifth cup gets put back on the bench with the other four, and then, some years later, it is on the floor again, half full of dust. Up, down, up, down, across two thousand years, in no discernible rhythm and by no continuous instruction. Somebody tidies. Somebody else needs it to be reachable. It is the longest conversation in the room and it has no content whatsoever, and both sides of it are still being argued by people who have never met and never will.

---

**And the sill.**

You can decline this. Most surveys do; they list the crickets, the four, the swifts, the crust, the three plants, the mite, and stop, and note that the room is nearly empty.

But something in that chamber takes in a river every hour, breaks it down completely, and puts out light, heat, sound, and a fine white residue it has no use for. It has done this without interruption, without variation, without wear, and without any visible organ, for a length of time that makes the doorframe modern. Everything else alive in that room lives on its heat, or its light, or the film on its underside, or the flour it declines. The grazers are hanging off it in rows. The crust is eating its leavings. The fern is standing in its exhaust. The four have spent their whole enormous lives listening to it.

It eats. It excretes. It has a metabolism you can measure in cubic meters per hour, and a population of commensals arranged over it exactly as they are arranged over a whale, or a vent, or a gut wall. Once, long ago, something was thrown at it fast, and it responded, once, and the mark is small and neat and high in the corner, and it has never had to respond again.

And a hand's breadth out of the stone at the back of the dry room, at the height of a standing adult's chest, there is a rod the colour of a fingernail, blood-warm, that does nothing, and is the only object in the cave that human beings have made without making it — dished, polished, shaped over an unknown number of millennia purely by being touched by everyone who came. Nothing grows near it. Every name is clustered around it.

So: eleven kinds of quiet, three plants, a hem of green, a column of fused spit older than the empire that cut the steps, four names and three animals, and a warm place worn smooth by hands.

And whatever is on the other side of it, which has been fed a river an hour since before the hill, and has never once, in all that time, been observed to want anything.
