## Who lives on the Increment

**Count.** There are fifty-three of them this year. The ledger permits no fewer than forty-eight and no more than sixty-four: below forty-eight a course takes too long and the margin fails; above sixty-four the bean frames and snail-beds cannot carry them and the margin also fails. So the number of people here is an engineering quantity, kept like a water level, and it is written in the same column, in the same hand, as the count of forms, flywheels, urns and working kilns. They have no word for *population* that is not also the word for *plant*, in the sense of machinery. A tender asked how many live here will say *fifty-three of us*, and then, because the question feels unfinished, *and six kilns, and two pumps, and one fig.*

**Hands.** You will know them by the thumbs. New stone has no mercy in its edges, and every course is new; a child's first lesson here is not walking but placing the hands, and the lesson does not fully take until it has been taught in blood. By thirty a tender's palms are a cartography of thin white lines, and older people are read the way you would read a hillside if hillsides were permitted here. The skin is thickened, faintly green at the cuticle and in the creases from the quickener slurry, which stains and does not wash. They are pale — the cloud floor throws up a white light that is bright but somehow unpaid-for, and nobody here has ever been properly sunned. They are barrel-chested and slow in the lungs, and they walk at one steady rate whether carrying a coping stone or nothing at all, because at this pressure haste costs more than it buys. Feet are bare or in felt; boots wear the pavement and the pavement is currency.

They are quiet. The vegetation eats voices, so speech has retreated into the hands, and the hand-language is old — older than the ledgers, which is to say older than the numbered courses, because the shapes of it are the shapes of the maker's punches, and the punches go down into the white past anything anyone can date. Every block on this mountain is signed in the language its makers spoke with their fingers. The tower is entirely written. Most of it is buried.

**Names.** A person is named for their punch. There is a woman here called Dot-and-Bar, a man called Hook, twins born in the Sixty-Eighth who are Comma Left and Comma Right and who have not spoken to each other in nine years but sign across the casting bays out of pure habit. A child has no punch and so no name; they are called by nursery-words, *little*, *small one*, *second*, until their first pour sets clean and un-cracked, at which point the master of the bay strikes their mark into the face of it and the child is, from that morning, a noun. Nobody forgets the day. It is the only ceremony they have that isn't also maintenance.

The consequence is that the dead are legible. If you go to the south parapet at evening, when the fog sometimes thins and falls away, and you look down at the coping of Course Eighty-Seven, you can find your grandmother's mark on the third block from the corner, crisp, unweathered, exactly as sharp as the day she struck it, twenty meters down and drowning at a hand's width a year.

**Work.** They are all of them casters — everyone stands a shift at the bays, because eighteen days is the metronome of the world and no one is exempt from it — but on top of that there are trades.

The *brushers*, who do the dusk pollination with sable, and who are chosen young for steady hands and a tolerance of tedium, and who are, without anyone deciding it, the closest thing the Increment has to a clergy. The *fleece-walkers*, who unbolt, carry, rehang, comb out and de-louse the black slate combs, and whose clothing is permanently soaked, and who smell of wet wire. The *drain-crew*, who go down into the warm flues on their bellies to scrape scale and to visit the salamanders — six of them, named, fed by hand, each one older than most of the people — and who come back up steaming and half-drunk on flue air. The *riggers*, who are the ones with the shoulders, and who spend two and a half years doing nothing much and then work one season like the world is ending, because it is: the move.

And the *water-readers*. Their whole discipline is this: when a new course is laid, the water does not know where to go. So a reader takes a stool and a wax slate and sits out in the rain for hours, marking where the sheet goes, where it ponds, which joint it explores, which corner becomes an eleven-hour lake. They do this for three years. They build a map of a habit the terrace never quite finishes forming. Then the course is abandoned to the cloud, and the map is carried upward and used to cut the gutters of the next one, which will be a different shape and will disappoint them differently. A reader who works forty years accumulates forty years of knowledge about rain on stone and has never once seen rain on stone that has been rained on long enough to have decided anything. They are patient people. They tend to be strange at parties.

**Sleep and shelter.** Nothing permanent is built for the living, because the living move and the stone does not. They sleep in tents: oiled fleece-cloth on cane hoops, guyed to bronze rings cast into the pavement, pitched over the warm places where the flues run close, so that the bed is the floor and the floor is faintly, endlessly alive. A household is a roll of cloth, a rope, a mark. The only heavy things anybody owns are tools, and tools are not owned. When the move comes, a family's entire dwelling goes up the ladder on one person's back, twice.

They are, therefore, nomads with a migration of about fourteen centimeters a year. It takes them three years to travel a distance you could measure with your forearm, and the journey costs them a season of unbroken labor, and they have made it ninety-one times that the ledgers can prove.

**Food.** Beans, figs, fern crosiers in spring — and snails. The snail-gardens are under the north terrace in the wet moss, six long trays of them, and they are the hinge on which the whole place turns: the snails eat spoiled greens and give back protein and, more importantly, shell. Burnt shell is lime. Lime is stone. The tower is being built, course by course, out of the patience of gastropods. A tender will eat a snail with real affection and then carry the shell to the kiln-yard without a flicker, because there is no irony available here; the loop is too short and too visible for anyone to pretend it is a metaphor.

**The dead.** Nothing may be taken from below the cloud, and nothing is sent down into it — what goes down is only what is left behind, and a person is not left behind. So the dead are calcined. It takes a day and a half in the east kiln at low breath, and what comes out is bone-white and goes into the lime store, and eighteen days later it is a block, and the block carries the dead one's own punch struck into its face by whoever loved them most. The grief here is not that the dead are gone. It is that you can see exactly where they are, and go and stand on them, and will do so daily for three years, and then climb one rung and leave them to the white.

**What they believe.** They do not know why the first course was laid. The ledgers begin at Course One and Course One is plainly not the bottom — under it, in the dark, are the unnumbered courses, signed in punch-marks whose hand-shapes no living finger can make. This is the theological problem, and nobody solves it. The working answer, the one a tender will give you while doing something else with their hands, is: *we go up because down is wet.*

There is a heresy, though, and it is quiet and it will not die. It says the cloud is not rising. It says the mountain is sinking — settling under the accumulating weight of everything they have ever made — and that every course they lay presses the whole works a little deeper, and that the four centimeters of margin is not a gain but a slower loss. Nothing in the ledgers can distinguish these two accounts, because the ledgers record only the difference between the stone and the white. The people who believe this are called Sinkers, and they work exactly as hard as everyone else, and the reason they work exactly as hard as everyone else is the most interesting thing about them.

**The fig, and the birds.** Two factions, and they are the only politics the Increment has.

The ledger says a tub-fig dropped fruit on the south walk eleven years ago and a seed took in the coping joint. The minority says: impossible. Our figs have no wasp. Our figs are hand-coaxed and seedless and have been for ninety-one courses. A *fertile seed* did not come from us. And the two pale swifts in the dead flue go somewhere, every year, for eleven weeks, and come back thinner and then fatter, and their young, when they fledge, go out over the parapet and do not come back at all — go *out*, to whatever out is — and a bird that has been somewhere carries in its gut what somewhere is made of.

If that is true, then the rule has already been broken, and it was broken by a bird, and there is an elsewhere, and the fig on the south parapet is a letter from it. Its white root is nine centimeters into Course Ninety. There is a numbered tag on the branch and a scratch on the stone where last winter's fissure stopped.

They have not cut it. They argue about it at the brushing hour, in hand-signs, without breaking the rhythm of the sable, and they do not cut it, and the reason is this:

On the Increment, nothing weathers. The arrises stay sharp. The mold-marks stay crisp. The dead stay exactly as they were, twenty meters down, undulled, drowning in perfect focus. There are only two things on this whole terrace that soften, thicken, crack, go gray and give way — that are, in the proper sense, *erosion* — and one of them is the fig.

The other fifty-three are asleep just now, under oiled cloth, on warm stone, above the white, at the top of everything, listening to the fleece-combs tick.
