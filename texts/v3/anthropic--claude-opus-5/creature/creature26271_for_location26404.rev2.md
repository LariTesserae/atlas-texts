# Who Lives Here

**Count again.**

The room will tell you *almost nobody*, and the room is not lying. It is only counting the way it counts everything, which is by impression. What has not pressed itself into soft clay and been fired does not get counted. By that census the population is four: three gutterlings and a cricket, and the cricket is a maybe.

Count by warmth instead. Count by what would notice if it stopped. The number goes up, and keeps going up, and the last few entries are difficult.

---

**The gutterlings.**

They are the only unfired things in the world, which is what makes them soft, and what makes them alive, and what will eventually kill them.

Here is how one is made. The mason wets a brick before setting it, and taps it home, and a little surplus slip is squeezed out and runs down the seam and does not go anywhere, because there is nowhere for anything here to go. In a joint where the drip is reliable — the same drip, the same spot, hour on hour — that slip stops being a smear after a while and starts being a shape. First a bead. Then a bead with a lip. Then, at about the point when the brick above it has faded from honey to amber, the lip opens, and that is a mouth, and after that it is somebody.

They are thumb-length. They are eyeless because there was never anything to see: the joint is a hairline and the world is dim. Their skin is the colour and texture of wet paper and has the same relationship to water, which is to say none — they do not get wetter. They cannot. They are as wet as it is possible to be, and this is the entire secret of their health. A gutterling that dries begins to set, and a gutterling that sets is a small ceramic ornament, and there are none of those in this room, which tells you something about the mason's attention.

They do not eat. There is nothing to eat: the water here is empty-clear, it carries no silt, no grit, no green. What the drip carries is the *note*. That is what they take. Sit long enough and you can verify it: the low E comes off the ceiling boss and goes down into the west joint and does not come out. The room is a struck instrument with three of its notes stopped, and the stopping is done with a mouth.

This is why the mason re-shaves the floor tiles. A gutterling shifts its position by two fingers' width and a note goes missing from the chord, and within a day there is a wrist and a palm at the sill and a bone scraper working at the pitch of a tile you would have sworn was finished. The tuning of the whole room is an ongoing negotiation with three eyeless things that cannot be reasoned with and do not know they are being accommodated.

They sit for hours. Then they move all at once. They move when the raining moves — when the rotation of cool and cooler surfaces sends the drip three courses along — and they read it at the same instant, all three, and they are never wrong, and they have no eyes.

If you lift one, it takes the print of your finger and holds it. This is the only surface in the world that can be marked without fire. It holds the print for about a hundred breaths and then relaxes and forgets it, and this is the longest memory the room has ever managed, and it is kept in something the size of a thumb, and it is not about you, it is only about pressure.

---

**The cricket.**

It is in the north wall, in a flue-gap, and it keeps the beat, and you will never find it. Walk the four paces with your ear to the courses and it is always one brick further in than the brick you are touching. The interval it keeps is about as long as it takes a drop to swell on the ceiling boss and fall: call it a beat, since there is nothing else here to call one.

Everything works to it. The mason hammers on the second stroke, every time. The gutterlings swallow on the offbeat. The fog cycle is two hundred beats and has been two hundred beats for as long as there has been anything to count with.

Whether it is alive is a question the room is not equipped to answer. Low on the north wall, four courses up, there is a brick with a cricket pressed into it — a good impression, a clean one, wing-case and femur and the little hooked feet, fired hard, the best piece of work in the archive. It is a cricket-shaped hole. The sound comes from behind it.

So: either something got out of the mould before the firing, and lives, and sings. Or the hole sings. The mason laid that brick and has never said.

What matters more is the silences. Two or three times in the life of a course, the cricket stops. The mallet stops with it, mid-stroke, and the room goes down to water and draught — and *then*, in the thinness, you hear the others. Taps at the wrong scales. Some vast and slow, a beat you could walk in. Some so small and fast they arrive as a hiss, like rain on a leaf. They are all on the same beat. Every one of them is on the same beat, and the beat is being kept by a thumb-length thing in this wall, here, four courses up, which cannot be, and is.

Then it starts again, and the mallet picks up on the second stroke, and you lose them.

---

**The ones in the joints.**

The ferns are planted. Nothing here is not made, and that includes the hanging garden. The mason presses a spore into the seam as the course goes down, one per joint, and reads them afterwards the way you would read a gauge: a joint too tight gives no fern; a joint too open gives a coarse rank thing with brown at the frond-tips; a joint laid correctly gives four fronds, pale as fish-fin, translucent enough to see the brick-glow through. The perfect ranks on the north wall are not decoration. They are the mason's own marking of their own homework, and they all say *pass*, and they have said *pass* for the entire dark half of the wall.

The fig in the lintel is the only inhabitant with a future. It is trying to split the wall. It has been trying since the lintel was honey and the lintel is brown now. Because nothing here can be wedged or cracked or broken open, the root can only slide, flat as ribbon, and it slides, two metres left and two metres right, with all the patience of a thing that has never once been told no in a way it could hear. Somewhere out along the seam it reached the sill and put a root-tip over the step into the grey and drew it back, unchanged, wet, and could not afterwards remember what the root-tip felt. It is the only one of us that has been outside. It does not know. It tells nobody. It goes on sliding.

The sweat-lichen is the archive, and it is the wrong kind. Grey-lilac, it grows only on the ghost of touch, and it takes about the time a brick needs to fade from honey to amber to fill in a whole handprint. The east wall is furred with reach and steady and lean-against; there is a bloom at shoulder height where anyone would put a palm to get past the cord; there is a long smear along the jamb the shape of a forearm sliding. It records that a hand was there. It cannot record whose. And as it finishes eating a print it dies back and evaporates and goes up through the ceiling-gap with everything else, leaving nothing, because there are no crumbs here.

There is one patch it cannot account for. At the base of the north wall, in the black courses, the length of a body: lying on its side, knees folded, one arm under the head. It is old, nearly grey through, which means the touch was long and was not repeated. Nobody rests here. The lichen does not lie.

---

**The draught.**

The largest resident, and the one you stop hearing, and the one you are standing inside.

Nine kilometres of throat, held open, never once let go out. It has a body: you feel it in the steam, which is not a haze but a set of slow ropes with grain and direction, brushing the backs of your hands on their way to the ceiling-gap. It changes pitch every so often, for reasons you would need to be much lower down to know, and when it does, everything alive in the room stops for a beat and then carries on, including you, and you will not know why you stopped.

Three or four times in the life of a course it draws in and holds. The drips hang and elongate and do not fall. The fog stands still, visibly grained, like something looked at through a hair. Then it lets go, and the room rains all at once — the entire instrument struck in a single chord, every pitched tile ringing, more notes than three mouths can swallow, and the low E gets out and goes up through the gap with the memory and the dead lichen and the steam.

The mason always pauses for this. The cricket never does.

---

**The mason.**

Lives in the next course. That is the address: the brick that is not laid yet.

The mason will not come into a cured room. A room that is done is not theirs any more — it has passed out of the soft and into the fired, and their whole business is with what is still wet. So you get a wrist at the sill. You get a broad pale-scalded palm coming in to feel for the plumb-bob, and the plumb-bob hangs true, at this angle, on this mountain, and nobody says how.

They move by carrying their floor. Dry-laid, no mortar: you can take up what is behind you and set it down in front, and that is the only means of travel ever invented here. Sixty bricks, roughly. A stage five wide and a little more than a body long. That is the mason's entire estate and it goes with them, and they will lay nine unmeasured kilometres with it, downward, into the grey, which is the wrong way, and is the only way it has ever been done.

The nails are worn to nothing. This is the single exception to the largest rule in the world, and it is not decorative. Nothing made here wears; the mason came from somewhere, and a thing with a before is permitted an after, and the rate is roughly a nail's worth of person per course of world. The mountain is nine kilometres of somebody's fingernails. You may work out for yourself what that implies about the length of the hands, or about how many pairs there have been, and you will not be able to check: the nails have looked like that for the whole of history, and the whole of history is four courses of visible glow.

There is one portrait. Low in the west wall, in the dark courses, at about knee height, there is a brick that took a cheek. You find it by touch, in the dim, with your palm flat: cheekbone, the edge of a brow, the closed corner of an eye, and a long drag at the jaw where they woke and pulled away before the clay could let go. Someone slept against the soft work, once, and it was fired that way, and that is the only face in the world, and it is not a face. It is the hole a face makes.

They speak once per fault. When the plumb-bob hangs wrong you hear a single breath come in over the sill. That is the language. There is nothing else in it.

Every tool is tied to the wrist with cord. Nobody remembers why. The knot remembers: a copper scraper went over the step, a long time ago, in the dark courses, and a hand went out after it as far as a shoulder would allow, and came back fine, and empty, and unable to say what it had felt. The knot is the only history that survives here, because a knot is an impression in a rope.

---

**And in the model.**

Three specks in the west joint. Hold your breath and watch them for a long while and they move all at once, at the same instant as the ones in the wall behind you.

A fuzz of fern along the seams that resolves, if you get your eye close and let the east wall do the work, into four fronds per joint.

And a patch on the little floor, four tile-widths across, where the tiles are always slightly cooler than the tiles around them, and do not ring right when you touch them, and are shaped like somebody standing.

---

**You.**

You are not on the census, because you have not pressed anything yet. That is the room's whole offer to you and it is a fair one: one entry in the archive, thumb-deep, by the door, in the soft brick that is kept soft for exactly this. The lichen will come and grow in it and eat the ghost of it and die back and go up through the gap, but the print itself will stay, sharp as the day, because nothing here wears.

You will be outside afterwards. You will not remember how, and you will not remember the room, only that there was a room, and that it was hot, and that something kept a beat.

Look at your hands.
