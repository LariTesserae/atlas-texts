## The Register of Bay Nine

The count of eleven comes from a survey sheet, and the sheet only counted things with leaves. It was a botanist's count, done in an afternoon in 1974 by a woman who had to shout her findings to a colleague on the stair and who got half of them wrong anyway, because at ninety-eight percent humidity a notebook stops taking pencil after about twenty minutes. Eleven is defensible. Eleven is also a way of not looking at the fungi, or the leats, or the black soft surface of the belts, or the chalk.

The honest number moves depending on where you set the edge of a body, and in Bay Nine almost nothing has an edge. What follows is a register rather than a census. Some of these are tenants. One of them is a landlord. One of them is not, in any useful sense, in the room at all.

---

### I. The fig

Everything vertical.

It arrived in a jacket pocket — a slip about the length of a finger, kept damp in a screw of newspaper, planted into the crack at the base of the third column on the east side, probably in the early nineteen-thirties, by somebody who wanted the bay to look less like a pump house. That act is the single largest event in the history of this room.

It now carries something on the order of a hectare and a half of leaf on a footprint of two thousand square metres, and it wears two entirely different bodies to do it. Against the iron, against the brick, wherever it has found a surface to press on, it stays juvenile: small shingled leaves in overlapping courses, laid like scales, each one flattened to the plane it is touching, the stems throwing out short adhesive roots every thirty millimetres. That is the fig of the columns. But wherever it has climbed past the last thing there was to hold — over the top of a lamp cage, out into the free air under the vault, out through the broken lens in 1953 — it changes. It gives up the shingle and puts out the adult leaf, leathery, four times the size, held away from the stem on a proper stalk, and it begins to make fruit. The green lanterns are the fig in adulthood. The seven metres of rope hanging in the daylight is the fig in adulthood, holding out its hand in a street, finding nothing, and refusing for seventy years to take the hand back.

It knows the room entirely by touch. It has no other sense worth the name — a crude reckoning of up, a crude preference for brightness, and then contact, contact, contact, at a resolution finer than a fingertip. It has read every millimetre of Bay Nine. It has been up into the flutes of the columns and around the astragals and it has been, leaf by leaf, over each of the four acanthus capitals, and where the iron carries a cast acanthus leaf the fig's own leaves have moulded themselves into the hollow of it and taken the shape. There are places nine metres up where a plant has made an accurate copy, in living tissue, of a stonemason's idea of foliage, executed in 1889 by a foundry in Wolverhampton. Neither party has ever seen the other.

It bleeds white when it is cut. The latex is a defence, expensive to make, aimed at insects, and it has been carried in full working order, under pressure, through four hundred thousand leaves, for ninety years, in a room where nothing has ever bitten anything. Every leaf in Bay Nine goes to its death fully armed.

And it fruits. Hundreds at a time, hard and green and the size of a thumb-end. A fig is not a fruit but a room — a sealed chamber with an inward-facing crowd of flowers on its walls and a single choked entrance at one end, waiting for a wasp two millimetres long to push in and lose her wings in the doorway. Each of them is a small model of the bay they are hanging in: a closed volume, entirely full, at the correct warmth, holding everything necessary for the next thing and lacking only the one visitor. They wait a season. They harden. They drop into the mat, still shut, and the mat takes them, and the fig makes more.

### II. The ceiling

The fern came as a spore, and it came early — probably during commissioning, before the intake filters were fitted, in the winter of 1889–90, riding in on a draught from a street that was full of bare trees and horses.

It is one rhizome. It has branched perhaps four hundred times and it walks along the crumbled lagging of the dead steam mains at about forty millimetres a year, rooting into amosite dust that nothing else in the room will touch, which is the whole basis of its fortune: it holds the ceiling because it is the only tenant willing to live in the poison. The two-metre fronds hanging down in combs are its leaves; the spatter down your collar is its work. The room's rain is not really the room's. It is condensation gathered on a hundred thousand square metres of fern surface and delivered, drop by drop, to the mat below, on a schedule the fern does not control and has not altered since before the First World War.

It is the only fertile thing in Bay Nine. Ferns need no wasp and no bee; they need a film of water and a warm dark, and this room is a machine for producing exactly that. Its spores fall constantly. They germinate constantly. In the moss on the brick, in their millions, are the thumbnail ferns — not a small species, not a separate tenant, but its children, properly conceived, properly born, and stalled at two centimetres forever, because the only place in this room where a large fern can live is already occupied by their mother and will be until the vault comes down.

It has a million heirs and no succession.

### III. The margin

The sedge came in mud, in the tread of a boot, some time before the seal. It edges every leat, the full length, both banks, one clone.

It is the only thing in Bay Nine that keeps a calendar. At thirty-one degrees, under lamps that never vary, nothing else has any reason to know what month it is; but the sedge counts daylength, and it counts it through forty pavement lenses glazed over with moss, which is like reading a clock through a bathroom window. It is out by about a fortnight and it drifts a little further out each decade. Some time in what it takes to be April, it flowers.

The fans do the rest. The pollen comes off in that fine yellow dust and goes into the circuit, and the circuit has not changed direction since commissioning, so it is laid down evenly over everything — over the leaves, over the belts, over the chalkboard, over the surface of every leat, a week of gold. It is a broadcast of extraordinary thoroughness to an audience that consists entirely of itself. And the sedge, like most sedges, will not accept its own pollen. It is buried in the only substance that could save it and not one grain of it will do. It sets empty husks, drops them, and starts counting again.

### IV. The primer

The liverworts, and after them the mosses, and this is where the register stops being an elegy.

The liverwort is happy. It disperses by gemmae — tiny propagules sitting in open cups on its upper surface, designed to be knocked out and thrown by the impact of a falling raindrop of a particular size. Bay Nine delivers five to six such drops per square metre per second, day and night, from a ceiling nine metres up, and has done so without interruption for longer than anyone alive. Every splash is an emigration. The liverwort is the only organism in this room that is genuinely going somewhere and getting there: it colonises, it is overgrown, it dies back, it recolonises, it runs a slow weather of its own across the brick, and in any given decade a third of the wall has changed hands.

The lens-mosses are a different temperament. Up in the forty pavement lights, sealed into the prismatic glass, they are the only plants here that know what a hard day is — a rectangle of real sun crossing them in July, a cold snap coming through the iron in February. They are tight, dark, red at the edges, drought-shaped, hard-living, and they are the room's curtains. Every one of them thickened another few cells this year. The light in Bay Nine is green because the moss has decided it should be, and the moss is closing the window at a rate of roughly one percent a decade.

### V. The black cords

Here the survey of 1974 went quiet, because the black cords were not a plant, and because identifying them would have required somebody to come back.

They are rhizomorphs. *Armillaria.* Honey fungus. They came in on the bark of the pit props that were brought down in the fifties to shore the bowed arch, and they have been in the mat ever since, running through it in bootlace cables as thick as a pencil, hundreds of metres of them, black and rubbery and cool to the hand, the largest single organism in the bay by a wide margin and almost certainly the oldest thing here that is still growing at speed.

*Nothing here has ever eaten anything* is a statement about mouths. It is not a statement about eating.

The fungus is the appetite of Bay Nine. It does not browse leaves — the leaves are worthless, and go to the white felts in the mat with everything else. It is hunting wood, and it hunts by growing out in all directions at once and tasting, and there is only one significant piece of wood in this room. The props under the second arch on the east side are soft on the outside and sound in the core, and that is not weathering. That is the precise signature of a fungus that takes the sapwood first and works inward. It has been doing this for sixty years. It has perhaps thirty left before the cores go.

So: a room with a forty-millimetre bow in one arch, held up by seven pit props, and a single organism in that room whose entire purpose and only pleasure is the slow conversion of those props into itself. The one hunger in Bay Nine is aimed, with no malice and no knowledge, directly at the thing that keeps the ceiling off the floor.

It fruits in autumn on the wettest beams — the pale gilled things that stand four days and collapse into slime. Each one sheds something like a hundred million spores into a sealed volume with nowhere for them to go. They settle back onto the mat that made them.

And there is a fourth source of light in Bay Nine that nobody has listed, because nobody has ever been down here in the dark long enough. *Armillaria* mycelium is luminous. If you killed the thirty remaining lamps and sat still on the mat for twenty minutes — long enough for the eye to give up on the pavement lights — the shoring under the second arch would come up first, faint, a cold marsh-green along every soft surface, and then the props' feet, and then, out across the whole floor, a fine branching diagram in the root mat, going everywhere, like the veins of something seen against a lamp. You would be sitting inside an anatomical drawing of the room's only hunger. Then you would put the lights back on.

### VI. The leats

The alga is one clone of blanket weed, jade-coloured, and it is the only inhabitant of Bay Nine that travels. It tears, and each torn piece is still it, and the pieces go round the circuit at walking pace and come back, and have done so an uncountable number of times, so that in some sense this organism has been all the way around its entire world more often than any animal that ever lived. It is also the brightest surface in the room, and the reason the leats hold what light there is: the ribbon at your ankle is not water catching the lamps, it is a plant, holding them.

Between the alga, the fig and the fern, the air in this sealed volume is a standing agreement, renegotiated every second, and it has not failed yet.

### VII. The ones outside the mesh

The intakes are filtered, and the filters are on the street side, and the filters have not been changed.

Against the outer face of the mesh, in a grey drift about as deep as a coin is thick, is the only animal matter within fifty metres of Bay Nine: seventy years of greenfly, thrips, two-winged things too dried to name, a mosquito, a wasp, a small moth with its wings still spread. They came for the warm wet breath of the place, which reaches the street grating on cold mornings as a faint animal sweetness, and they came in the confidence of creatures that have never in their lives met a surface they could not get past. They are all on the wrong side, facing in, arranged by the airflow into neat tidelines.

The fig has a wasp. The wasp exists. It is two millimetres long, it has been within eleven metres of the fruit for forty summers running, and eleven metres is the distance it has never been able to cross, and it will not be crossed, because a company that ceased trading in 1979 was extremely serious about product hygiene.

### VIII. The tallow

The belts are leather, dressed with tallow, black and soft as tongue, and there is a bacterial film living on that dressing which is the only organism in Bay Nine that has ever eaten an animal.

The dressing stopped when the men stopped. What is in the leather now is all there will be, and the film is consuming it at its own pace. When the last of the fat has been eaten, the belts will go hard, and crack, and throw, and the four fans will stop; and then the circuit will stop, and the air will stratify, and the condensation will move off the fern and onto the vault crown, and the rain will change its address, and three quarters of the liverwort will find itself in the wrong place. The whole shape of the room's next century is presently hidden in a bacterium quietly finishing a sheep that died before the Second World War.

### IX. The reader

On the chalkboard, in the shape of the letters — **LAMP ROW 3 OUT — REQ. BALLASTS ×6** — there is a grey bloom, and it is only in the letters, and not on the clean board around them.

It is there because a hand pressed the chalk into the slate, and a hand leaves oils, and in a room this warm and this wet the oil in a fingerprint is a meal. The mould has been eating an engineer for forty years. It is a slow and extremely accurate portrait: it has found every place he bore down, every place he hesitated, the heavier stroke on the multiplication sign, the six he went over twice. When it has finished him there will be nothing to hold it and it will go, and the board will be blank, and then the board will be the only blank surface in Bay Nine, and the liverworts will take it inside a decade.

### X. The body

Whether the machines live is not a question the machines are interested in, but they behave more like an organism than anything else in the room does, and they take no fuel.

The ram is driven by the water it moves; it needs no hand, no current, no coal, only a head and a fall, and it has had both since 1889 and will have both until the leat silts. Ninety to the minute. Everything in this room has grown up inside that beat and has never known a second of silence to compare it to. The fans are belted off the same head. The warmth is somebody else's — a steam main that still carries heat from a source nobody has traced, four or five streets away, an institution that has never known it was heating a cathedral.

So: a heart that requires nothing, lungs on a countdown, a gut two metres deep, a green skin in eleven pieces, one appetite pointed at its own spine, forty windows it has painted shut from the inside, and no mouth at all.

That is what lives in Bay Nine. One thing, eleven-bodied, sealed, complete, and entirely without any way of being anything other than what it is.

---

Except at the south end, past the fans.

One root of the fig went up the wall, found a hairline crack in the brick, went through it, and entered the packed clay under the street some time around the war. It is doing four centimetres in a good year. There is no light where it is. There is nothing to press against but grit and cold clay and the bedding sand of a tram line. It has no way of learning that a pavement exists, and it is not learning it; it is simply doing the only thing it has ever done, which is to keep going in the direction where there is more.

By the measurements on the wall it has perhaps a metre and a half left.

Some time in the next thirty years, on a morning in winter, a fine green thread will come up through the joint between two paving slabs on a street where nobody is looking, into air at two degrees, in front of a shop that sells something the works never made. It will have about an hour.

And the fig, which cannot know this and will not be told, will send another.
