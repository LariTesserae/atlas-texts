## Those Who Sound

Begin with the correction: something does live here, and it is not lonely. The plateau is crowded. It is simply that nothing here has ever had a reason to be visible, and so the question *what does it look like* has no answer and never did — not for want of light, but for want of relevance. Ask instead what it sounds like. Ask what it feels like against the hand. Ask what it is doing.

You asked about the people, so start with the people, but understand that they are a minor tenant. They know this. They will tell you so, cheerfully, in the first hour.

---

**The body.**

Small, broad, and heavy in the foot. A person here is built low and wide, with a chest like a barrel because the chest is an instrument, and shoulders that carry weight and also carry rime.

The rime is the first thing your hand learns. Water condenses on anything cooler than the air, and a person is cooler than the air for the first minute of any rest; over decades the mineral settles into the creases of the skin and stays. A young adult is faintly gritty across the collarbones. A woman of sixty has a crust like the lip of a terrace pool along her shoulders and the backs of her hands, and when she taps her own knuckles the sound has stone in it. The old are quite literally becoming landscape, and this is not a metaphor they use; it is the actual funerary practice. The dead are laid in a shallow warm pool at the head of a terrace field, in the path of the spill, and left. In eight or nine years there is a new lip in the rim, and the water goes over it with a particular note, and the note is the person. Families walk their terraces the way you would walk a hall of portraits, running a hand along the edge, listening to their grandparents make weather.

Where the eyes were there are two shallow bowls of thin skin over air. Not empty sockets — the orbit has been repurposed, roofed over, tuned; the low notes come in through the face, and a person feels the machines in the front of the skull the way you feel a truck idling behind a wall. When the shift comes they feel it there first, and their hands go up to their cheekbones without thinking, which is the closest thing they have to prayer.

The ears are large, mobile, muscled, and dirty; ear-cleaning is intimate and public and takes place constantly, in the manner of grooming. The whole body is covered in a fine short pelt that reads air, so that a person walking into a downdraft column knows it as a pressure across the back before it hits. Feet: wide, splayed, soles hardened to a leather that would blunt a knife and yet reads the grain of a floor through it — the porous ring of sponge country, the glass of a terrace pan, the ringing of a main under three centimetres of limestone. Children are taught on a sampler: a hut floor tiled with two dozen kinds of ground, and the game is to be walked across it blind, which is to say, walked across it.

Hands are the other face. Palms permanently smoothed. Fingertips heavy with callus everywhere except the pads, which are kept soft on purpose, scraped with a cane-node, because fingertips read tally-cuts and knot-cords and the fine grain of a valve thread, and a hand that cannot read is a hand that cannot be trusted with the count.

---

**How wide the world is.**

They are not in the dark. This is the hardest thing for a visitor to accept, and the visitor never quite does.

A person sounds constantly — clicks, taps, tongue-stops, the strike of a small water-worn pebble against a thumb-ring of grazer keratin — and the returns come back and make a room.
