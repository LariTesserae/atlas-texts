## 1. On names

Nothing here is named. What follows are the names a visitor puts on things in order to keep them apart in the mind, and they are all wrong in the same direction: they imply that the basin has residents, that is, parties who could in principle be introduced. It does not. It has occupants the way a lung has air.

Still. You have to call them something.

## 2. The Builders

Begin with the small ones, because there are more of them than of anything else and because the plain is, in the last analysis, their excretion.

They are pale, roughly the length of a thumbnail, unpigmented, eyeless in every caste but one. The cuticle is heavily waxed — sealed, like the garden leaves, for the same reason. A builder does not sweat, does not urinate as such, does not lose moisture through the shell. Everything it takes in, it keeps, and when it dies it is carried inward rather than outward, and set into the wall, still holding its water, which the wall slowly takes.

The masons work stone with their mandibles and cement it with dung and dust, a mouthful at a time. Individually they are useless. There is no plan in a mason. Put one alone on the pavement with a supply of paste and it will build a hopeless little wall two centimetres long and die beside it. Put four million in a mound and you get flues that draft correctly in both directions, thermal stacks tuned to the diurnal cycle of a world they never see, intake ports oriented against a prevailing wind that changes every eleven hundred years, and a gallery-plan whose main corridors correspond, at the top, to the corridors of the mound that was there before this mound and is now the foundation. They are not remembering the old plan. They are following the shape of the ruin under their feet. It comes to the same thing.

They cannot hear each other over the basin. That is the central fact of their biology. Speech at the scale of a thumbnail is impossible inside a permanent roar, and so they have given up on the air entirely and gone into the substrate: they bite the wall. A mason strikes the gallery face with the flat of its head, five or six times, at a spacing that means *here* or *not here* or *bring paste* or *bring the wet*, and the mound, being a resonator sixty metres high and honeycombed to the density of pumice, carries it. This is why a mound at full activity hums like a struck bell held to a pillow. That hum is not a by-product. That hum is the entire conversation of two million individuals, superimposed, and each of them can pick its own thread out of it by the feel of it in the legs. The building is the language. It is also the throat and the stomach and the tomb, and it grows a hand's width a century, which is the rate at which the sentence lengthens.

Three castes are worth your attention.

**The cisterns.** In the lower galleries, where it is coolest, hang the full ones: builders whose abdomens have swollen to the size of a grape, translucent, faintly amber, immobile for life. They are lifted into brackets of chewed stone and left there in rows. Each holds perhaps a gram and a half of water. They are fed, and turned, and milked mouth-to-mouth by attendants who then carry the drink to the working faces. A large mound may hang forty thousand of them. That is its treasury: sixty litres, give or take, sitting in the dark in living jars, replenished at a trickle, and it represents the difference between the colony existing next century and not. Everything else about the mound — the height, the spires, the layered dead cities beneath it — is downstream of the number of jars it can keep full.

**The climbers.** Bigger, darker, hooked, and the only caste with eyes, and those eyes are good for exactly one thing. Once every few nights, when the basin's breathing reverses and the air draws up the chimneys, a hundred of them leave through a doline and go up the wall. Four hundred metres of overhanging limestone. Perhaps sixty arrive at the lip. Up there is a world they are not built for: wind, sky, temperature, predators with reflexes. They drink whatever the surface has — dew on a stone, the wet edge of a leaf, in a good season a puddle, which they perceive as an event of terrifying abundance — until their bodies are round and clumsy, and then they come back down. Thirty make it. Each is carrying forty grams of the outside world in its gut. That is the balance of trade of the Underplain: a few kilograms a night, arriving through forty holes, inside bodies, on legs. The whole living mass of the basin — every mound, every grazer, every roosting flier, every fern — contains less water than a farm pond, and it has been passed hand to hand for as long as there have been hands.

**The founder.** Somewhere in the base of every occupied mound, walled into a cell built around her when she was small, is a single individual the size of a finger, blind, boneless, ninety per cent ovary, laying at a rate of one egg every eleven seconds, and she has been doing it for between forty and two hundred years. Attendants stroke her constantly. If you wanted the closest thing in the basin to a resident, in the sense of someone who has lived somewhere a long time, it is her, and she has never moved more than the width of her own body and does not know that there is anywhere to move to.

## 3. What comes down

The economy of the pale country is guano, and the rule that nothing pours has exactly one exception: this. It rains shit here, softly, unendingly, from ten million lodgers in the ceiling.

The fliers are the only occupants who cross between worlds every day, and so they are the only ones who are visibly *fed*. Everything else in the basin is thin. The bats are not thin. They go up through the shafts at dusk in a spiralling column, eat the surface — moths, beetles, the fig-things that grow around the doline rims — and come back heavy, and defecate the outside world onto the floor, where the pale mats take it, where the grazers rasp it up, where the mounds harvest it. The Underplain is a stomach fed through forty mouths by couriers.

They have escalated, as the description says. Some of them have escalated in a direction you would not predict.

The common ones — the clatterers — shout. Their calls are so loud at the emitter that in any quieter cave they would deafen themselves; here they are simply one grain in the gravel. They fly badly and roost densely and are eaten constantly and it does not matter.

Then there are the ones that have stopped shouting. Larger, slower, long-winged, and mute — no click at all, and enormous ears, and a face that is mostly membrane. They do not sound the world. They listen to the clatterers sounding it, and read the echoes off targets the clatterers have illuminated for them, and take the clatterers themselves. They are parasites of other animals' curiosity. A hunter that has given up asking questions and lives entirely on overheard answers.

And in the highest galleries, where the ceiling is warmest, the swifts, in numbers that make the roof itself seem to be a coarse trembling surface. Their nests are cemented with saliva, and saliva is water, and so a swift nest is, in the accounting of this place, an obscene extravagance — a bird spending a portion of its own body's wet to hold a cup to a rock. They are robbed for it, constantly, by everything that can climb.

## 4. The grazers of the pale country

Out in the dim, where the vegetation has stopped bothering with light and gone white and started eating the crust, the animals are correspondingly slow.

The rasp-backs are the big ones: waist-high, plated, blind, with a mouth like a wood file, working across a colony of pink guano-fans at a rate of about eleven metres a day. They chew the crust and the dust with it, and their dung is drier than the meal that made it, because they extract water so thoroughly that the pellets come out as chalk. They live perhaps eighty years and travel, in that whole life, maybe two hundred kilometres, and they travel it on roads laid down by the dead.

Because here is what the dust does, besides holding footprints: it holds scent. There is no rain. There is almost no wind at floor level. A trail of gland-marks laid along a trough does not degrade in a season or a decade; it fades over a century or two, and it is refreshed every time something walks it. So the floor of the basin is written on. There are scent-roads out there running for ninety kilometres, from a pale-country grazing ground to a mound-hill flank, and the animals that follow them are following instructions left by ancestors forty generations back, and where the road forks and one branch is faint, that branch leads to a garden that stopped existing when its light moved off it eleven thousand years ago. They still go and check. A few every year walk out along a dead road to a dead place and stand in the dim where a forest was, and mill, and come back.

This is the closest the basin comes to history: the fact that its animals can be lied to by the past.

## 5. The hunting hours

Now the part that reorganises everything else.

You were told that silence moves across the plain like a shadow and is the only fast thing here, and that is true, but the inference you drew from it is wrong. Silence is not the signature of the hunters. It is the signature of the *bad* hunters.

The floor predator — call it the Hush, because that is what it leaves — is tall, cursorial, long in the leg, sparse-furred, with a skull that is mostly acoustic apparatus and feet spread into wide silent pads that print like a hand pressed in flour. It kills by listening. And every animal in the basin knows that being audible is being findable, so as the Hush comes on, the crust-grazers stop rasping and the clatterers stop clicking and the near mounds withdraw from their outer galleries and go still, and a bubble of quiet perhaps four hundred metres across travels ahead of it, announcing it to everything for two kilometres in every direction.

Which is a disaster for the Hush. Its whole method is undone by the fact that its method is legible.

So the ones that eat well hunt during the exodus. Ninety minutes at dusk, ninety at dawn, when the column is going up through the shafts and the plain is a drum being played from inside and the noise is not a sound any more but a structural load — no local hush can exist inside that, because nothing can be heard to stop. Those are the killing hours, and they are conducted in a din so total that the hunting is done half by ground-vibration through the pads. Between them, for twenty-one hours a day, there is an armistice enforced entirely by acoustics: predators that cannot hunt without giving themselves away, prey that cannot be surprised, both parties sitting out the middle of the day within a hundred metres of each other, both aware, neither able to act.

And so, when you do see the quiet crossing the plain in the lull — a smooth grey advance of nothing-happening, and every animal within it frozen and every animal outside it watching its edge — you are watching something desperate. That is a Hush too old, or lame, or newly weaned and incompetent, forced to try in the wrong hours, and it will very likely fail, and the plain reads its failure in advance and moves aside from it with something that in a warmer place you would be tempted to call contempt.

There is a second predator, and it does not hunt meat.

The well-digger is heavy, low, armoured across the shoulders, with forelimbs built for one job: opening a mound. The walls are stone. It takes four to nine days, working at night, going in at a flank two metres above the base, and the colony's response is to seal galleries ahead of it — to amputate its own architecture, corridor by corridor, retreating inward and cementing behind itself, sacrificing masons by the thousand to slow the breach with their bodies.

The well-digger is not after the masons. It goes down and in until it reaches the cool lower galleries and the brackets in rows, and then it drinks the treasury. Forty thousand jars, sixty litres, four hundred years of climbing accumulated a gram and a half at a time, gone in a night and a half. Colonies rarely die of this. They spend the next two centuries not dying of it.
