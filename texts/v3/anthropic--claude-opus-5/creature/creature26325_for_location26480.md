## The Tenants

Eleven, someone once said, and the number has stuck the way numbers do. Count for yourself and you get nine, or fourteen, depending on whether you are willing to call a felt of mould an individual and whether you count the thing under the mat that nobody has dug up. But the number is not the point. The point is that Bay Nine is not inhabited by a population. It is inhabited by a small number of very large bodies, each of which has been doing the same thing for a hundred years without interruption, and by an enormous number of very small bodies that are the only ones here with a future.

---

**The fig.**

Start with the fig because you cannot avoid it; it is most of what you are touching.

It came in as a cutting in somebody's coat, a windowsill plant, the sort of thing you take a slip of because it is free. It went into the mat at the base of the second column on the west side — you can still find the origin, a woody knuckle the size of a fist, no bigger than it was in 1930, because a fig of this kind does not thicken at the base, it only leaves.

What it has done since is walk. It is one organism in two shapes at once, permanently. Against the iron it is juvenile: small leaves, two centimetres, laid in overlapping shingles like slate, flat to the metal, gripping with adhesive rootlets that set into a latex glue so hard that if you peel a stem away the 1889 paint comes off with it in curls. Where it has run out of anything to climb — over the lamp cages, out along the fan guards, in the long swags between the vaults — it changes its mind and becomes adult: leaves eight centimetres and leathery, branches that stand out from the surface, and figs. The columns and the lanterns are the same individual in youth and middle age, side by side, and it will never resolve into one or the other, because it will never run out of iron and it will never stop hanging free.

It does not grow towards the light. This is the thing to understand about it. In its climbing phase it grows towards *dark*: a fig seedling on a forest floor sets off for the deepest shadow in its field of view, because in a forest the deepest shadow is a trunk, and a trunk is the road up. It has no other plan. Everything the fig has done in this room, it has done by reading darkness as a promise of structure.

So the columns, yes. The vault shadows, yes. And at the far south end, the hairline crack in the brick with cold black behind it, which the fig entered at some point around the war and has been climbing ever since at two or three centimetres a year, through packed clay, in the absolute dark, in the correct and confident belief that it has found the trunk of a very large tree. It is somewhere under the pavement now. It will keep going. There is no signal that will tell it to stop, because the signal that stops a fig is daylight, and there is none where it is.

You can see what daylight does to it: the seven metres of rope hanging out of the broken lens, in the street's grey air. That strand is not lost. That strand has *arrived*. It has found the canopy, which is where climbing ends, and it has spent seventy years there doing nothing in particular, holding its adult leaves out to a light it does not need, at the top of a tree that does not exist.

Two feelers, then. One that thinks it has finished and one that thinks it has started. Neither is right, and the fig has no organ with which to be wrong.

---

**The sedge.**

Along the leat margins, one plant, three hundred metres of edge. It arrived in a load of washed sand and it has extended itself by rhizome ever since, at the pace of a slow argument. Its leaves will cut you if you draw your hand up them; that is its only opinion.

It flowers in spring. There is no spring in Bay Nine — thirty-one degrees, ninety-eight percent, forever — so the question of how it knows is worth asking, and the answer is overhead: the pavement lenses, whose moss-green windows brighten and dim across the year with the sun's angle over the street and dim again when the corporation lays grit. The sedge is reading the sky through forty small dirty windows and getting it roughly right. It is the only thing in the room that keeps a calendar.

The exception is the run under lamp row 1, where the mercury-vapour never goes off. Those tussocks flower whenever they like, or not at all, and have been out of phase with themselves for decades.

Every spring it produces pollen — good pollen, viable, enormous quantities — and the fans take it and lay it evenly across the entire room, a yellow film on the leats for a fortnight, and none of it finds a stigma that is not its own, because there is only one plant. It is the most thorough act of futility performed anywhere in the building, and it is performed with total competence, annually.

---

**The ceiling.**

Here the accounting changes, and this is where the real inhabitants are.

The big lagging-ferns are one clone, rooted in crumbled asbestos wrap that suits them better than any soil. Fine. But underneath them, on the brick, in the liverworts and the mosses and the thumbnail ferns — millions of them, per bay — something is happening that happens nowhere else in Bay Nine.

They breed.

A moss needs a film of water for its sperm to swim in, and in this room every surface has had a film of water on it continuously since the fans were commissioned. The vault crown drips five or six times a second per square metre, and the liverworts have splash-cups, little green goblets of gemmae positioned exactly to be fired by falling drops, and the drops fall. The thumbnail ferns drop spores into the wet, and the spores make gametophytes the size of a grain of rice, heart-shaped, green, packed in the millions across the brick, and on those the sperm swim thirty seconds' journey and get there.

So: sex. Recombination. Generations — perhaps two hundred of them since the seal was closed. Selection, too: the tighter, redder plants in the daylight rectangles are not the same as their cousins twenty centimetres away under the lamps, and they have not been the same for a long time. There is drift, because a square metre of gametophytes can die in an afternoon if a fan bearing shifts and puts a lens of drier air where it was not before, and that afternoon is a mass extinction and a founder event at once.

The fig and the sedge are furniture. The ceiling is a *country*, with weather, history, provinces, and no records. If Bay Nine ever produces something that is not found anywhere else on earth, it will come from up there, and it may already have.

---

**The mouths.**

Nothing here eats — at your scale. Below your scale the room is nothing but eating.

The largest body in Bay Nine is probably not the fig. It is probably the white mycelial felt through the root mat, which you can lift in sheets the size of a bedspread and which extends, as far as anyone has bothered to check, from the sump to the north wall. It arrived as a spore in a trouser turn-up. It has been dismantling every leaf the fig has ever dropped, one at a time, for longer than the fig has been here.

On the shoring under the bowed arch there is a wet rot working the pit props, and it has been working them since before the datum mark. It is forty millimetres into a two-hundred-millimetre prop and it has been forty millimetres in for as long as anyone recorded, because the core of the prop is too wet for it and it cannot get purchase in standing water. So the arch, the props and the fungus are in a three-way stand-off that is stable to the width of a pencil line and has been since about 1962.

On the wetter beams, the four-day mushrooms: up in the morning, gills open by evening, black slime by Thursday. They are the only fast thing in the building.

And in the leats, in the jade-green, there is a food chain of the ordinary kind — filaments grazed by ciliates, ciliates ingested by amoebae, iron bacteria laying down an orange skin at one seep near the ram where the water comes up out of the pipework tasting of the pipework. It is a complete ecosystem. It is simply all under four hundred magnifications, and nobody has brought a microscope down the stair, and the last person who might have has been dead for some decades.

If there is an animal in Bay Nine — a real one, with a gut and a nervous system — it is a bdelloid rotifer in the moss on the underside of the pavement lenses, arrived as a dried-out speck in dust, revived by the eternal film of water, and eating bacteria in the dark with the same equipment its ancestors used forty million years ago. Bdelloids have no males. They have never had males. In a room where nothing sexual happens except on the ceiling, the only candidate animal would be a lineage of identical females copying themselves in a puddle the size of a full stop.

Nobody has looked. It is the sort of thing that would take an afternoon and a jar.
