## The Forty, More or Less

**Hands First**

You meet them hands first, and if you are tactful you never get past that, because the hands are where the person is kept.

They are small — the crown of the tallest would come to your sternum — and dry, and dust-colored in a way that is not dirt: the color is *in* them, a warm mineral gold that goes pale at the thin places, the ear, the eyelid, the web between finger and thumb, where you can see the channels running and, if the cycle is at its high point and you are very still, the slow creep of the powder along them.

Their hands are out of proportion, and the proportion is not an accident of build but of use. The palm is broad and slightly cupped, as if it had been shaped around a brick and then kept the shape. Across it runs a shallow chevron — two ridges meeting at an angle, the same chevron that is pressed into every brick in Hesp, from the courses under the low garden to the topmost soldier-row of the outer wall. Infants have it. It is there before there is any brick to have made it. The Makers have never treated this as a mystery worth solving, which is the single most characteristic thing about them.

Greeting is palm to palm, held for about four heartbeats, long enough to read. They do not look at faces much. Faces drift — faces are seen, and seeing lies here, gently and consistently. A palm measures true. When they speak of a person absent, they do not point at where the person is; they show the chevron, angled a particular way, and that angle is the person's sign. Nobody makes their own sign. It is given by someone else, early, usually badly, and carried for life like a nickname from a bad year.

Their portraits are handprints. The long room at the north of the basin — the one the wax-beetles have not touched, because even beetles seem to have opinions — has a wall of them, pressed in a fill of ochre and husk, going back as far as the fill holds. No names on the wall. Nothing on that wall has ever been said aloud, and so it is the lightest wall in Hesp, and the easiest to stand near.

**What Runs In Them**

They do not drink, and they have no word for not drinking.

What they have instead is the same dry chemistry as the waxflag and the dustvine: a fine talc-fine sap, butter-colored, that flows when warm and sets when cool. At the low end of the day-cycle they thicken. You can watch it happen across forty bodies at once — a settling, a slight shortening of gesture, conversation collapsing from full arm-sweeps into small movements of the fingers and then into nothing at all. They do not sleep exactly. They stiffen, sitting upright against the warm floor, eyes half-lidded, and wait out the cool, and the garden stiffens around them at the same rate, and it is impossible to tell, standing among them in that hour, where the population stops and the vegetation begins. Which may be the answer to several questions.

At the high of the cycle they soften and lean and work.

They eat the way most things here eat: by skin. Once a cycle, at the honeyed peak, they go out into the low garden and stand — arms a little out from the body, palms down, chin level — and let the gold settle on them. An hour, sometimes two. Forty small figures in the pollen-light, not speaking, not moving, faintly sagging. It is their meal. It is also, as far as anyone can tell, the whole of their religion. There is no ceremony attached and no one leads it and nobody is compelled, and it is the only thing all of them do at the same time.

Cut one and no bead forms. A breath of powder puffs out, hangs, and the wound seals itself with a wax scab in about the time it takes to say something you regret. They do not find this remarkable either.

**Weight**

The central fact of a Maker's social life is how heavy they are, and heaviness is made of talk.

A person spoken about often becomes hard to move. It starts as reputation and ends as mass. The much-discussed elders of Hesp are physically difficult: slow, dense, warm as fired clay, seated for years in the same alcove because the labor of shifting them requires four people and a sling of plaited husk. Their shadows arrive a moment after they do. They are consulted, which makes them worse. Every consultation is a sentence spoken in their vicinity, and every sentence is another turn of the screw, and the whole community knows it, and they consult them anyway, because the very heavy remember more than anyone and can no longer be persuaded of anything, which is sometimes what you need.

Against this stand the unnamers.

They are not a caste by birth. They are volunteers, and their work is the slow, muttered, backwards labor of releasing what earlier generations pinned by talking — going through the far rooms object by object, reciting what was said about a thing in reverse, sound by sound, until the thing loosens and can be lifted again. It works. It is also expensive. An unnamer unnames themselves a little with every session. The old ones are the lightest people in Hesp: you can lift a senior unnamer with one arm, and more troublingly, you will lose her in the middle of a conversation — not because she goes away, but because the fact of her stops holding in your head. Her sign stops working. You find you are addressing a spot in the air with great warmth and no referent.

They are cared for by rota, which the rota-keeper records in knots, because knots are not words.

The ideal, endlessly discussed in gesture and never once stated aloud, is a working weight. Heavy enough to stay. Light enough to be carried out of a room that is on fire, if Hesp could burn, which it can't, which doesn't stop them planning for it.

**The Mouth**

They have mouths and use them for almost nothing.

The mouth is not for food — food happens at the skin. It is not for breathing, particularly; that is diffuse and quiet and mostly happens elsewhere. The mouth in Hesp is a single-purpose instrument, the only organ in the whole made world that turns a thing more real, and it is handled with the care you would give a blade you cannot sheathe.

Children are therefore the emergency.

A newborn babbles. A newborn babbles at *whatever is in front of it*. So the nursery is the most-named room in Hesp: a low chamber whose every fitting has been deliberately spoken about, for generations, by committee, until floor and walls and the seven lamps and the carved rail are so thoroughly pinned that they cannot be made worse. Nothing new is brought in. The nurses wear no ornament. A child can shout at that room for a year and do no harm; the room is already stone.

Mouth-discipline is taught before walking. By six a child has the reflex: the heel of the hand comes up and covers the lips at the first pressure of an unconsidered word. Adults still do it. You will see a Maker, mid-argument, hand slapped over the mouth, eyes furious above it, finishing the point with the other hand in sharp flat strokes — and that image, more than the bricks or the lattice or the light from nowhere, is what a visitor takes away.

**Offices**

There are more offices than people, so everyone holds several, and the holding is recorded in cord.

*Sweepers.* The threshold of the seventh doorframe is swept twice a cycle by someone who will do nothing else that day. They sweep toward the frame, never away. Not worship. Courtesy to a neighbor whose house shares a wall.

*Wakers.* They walk the corridors at intervals with a length of soft cord and trail it across the flank of any animal that has held a posture too long, because an animal that sleeps deep enough goes shallow, and its far side merges with the wall, and then it is decoration. It is considered the kindest of the duties. It is also the one nobody wants, because the wakers are the ones who find the failures, the climber flattened into the plaster with three legs still round and four gone flat, and who must then decide whether to keep waking it. They usually do. There are corridors in Hesp where the reliefs breathe.

*Cord-keepers.* They measure with knotted husk-cord, because the hand measures true and the eye does not, and they are the only ones who know precisely how much the inside of Hesp exceeds the outside. The knot-archive hangs in a far room like a second garden, hundreds of cords from the lattice to the floor, and it is read with the eyes shut, running the line through the fingers. It is the only writing they have, and it is safe, because a knot is not a word. Everything in Hesp that must be remembered without being fixed is remembered there: the drift of the shadows, the season-count, the tally of living persons, the depth of the bowing when the framed wall went warm.

*Counters.* A sub-office. They track the duplicates, the six pickings still in transit, and they maintain the tally of sockets.

**Sockets**

This is the part the Makers will not discuss, and not because it distresses them. It simply does not have the shape of a topic.

The soil of Hesp is a graded blend of milled bone-meal, seed-husk, and powdered clay. The bone is theirs. The dead do not rot — there is no rot — they dry and flake, and the flakes are gathered and milled and blended and poured into the hand-cut sockets in the floor, and tamped with a bone dibber, and the garden grows out of them, dense and layered and four storeys deep.

And every so often a socket, out in the far garden where the courses are oldest, opens.

What comes up is a person: small, dust-colored, adult-sized, packed to the shoulders in soil, holding a shell blade or a bone awl already in the hand. They come up not knowing anything and knowing how to do everything. Within a cycle they are packing brick with the correct chevron. Within three they have a sign, given by someone else, badly.

And their palm — this is the part the counters attend to — their palm will match a print on the north wall. Not always. Often. A palm that has been up before. The community does not regard this as a return; nobody expects the newcomer to remember, and they never do. The counters simply go to the archive and re-tie the knot that came loose when the first one un-happened, and put the cord back on its peg, and it is done.

There are empty sockets still, person-sized, out past the bellow-ferns. Someone sweeps them.

**The Keeper of the Dry Word**

One office is held by one person and passed at the deathbed by a single gesture.

There is no water at Hesp and there never was, and the sign for it is a flat hand turned over, palm down: *that which we do not have and do not miss.* The sign is taught to children with great gentleness, and it is a lie by one word, and the lie is load-bearing.

Because somebody does miss it. The office is hereditary in the sense that it is always filled, and its duty is not to guard a secret — there may be no secret, there may be no word at all — but to *do the missing*, on behalf of forty people, so that the other thirty-nine can make the flat-hand sign and mean it.

The Keeper does not speak. Ever. Not one syllable, from taking the office to giving it. If there is a word for water, and if saying it would start rot in a place that has never had rot, then the safest arrangement is a mouth that has agreed, once, in gesture, to be finished with language. The Keeper sweeps, wakes, tamps, measures, stands in the gold with everyone else at the high of the cycle. You would not pick them out. Their hands are a fraction slower than everyone else's, that's all — a fractional hesitation on every sign, the hesitation of somebody who is constantly deciding not to.

**The Others**

*The seam-things.* Small, blunt, warm, with hands. They live in the finger's width between brick and matting, and they move objects overnight — always by a little, always as if toward an arrangement nobody has decoded. They are the figurines. Early generations, following the logic of a model, carved little makers to put in the model of the place the makers made; the carvings, obeying rule four, began to breathe; and what came off the wall was crude, because the carving was crude, and faceless, because faces are hard. They have been shifting the furniture of Hesp ever since, trying to complete a gesture they were cut in the middle of. No one carves people now. It is the only prohibition the Makers have that carries real heat. The seam-things are left out a share of powder. They are never spoken of, which keeps them light, which is the most anyone can do for them.

*The wax-beetles.* Palm-sized, industrious, chewing powder and extruding hard bright fill. They mend brick and tighten lashings. Left a season they will raise a wall nobody designed, in a place where a wall makes structural sense and no other kind of sense at all. They are a second authorship and cannot be evicted, since without them the outer courses would go in a generation. The Makers' feeling about them, rendered in gesture, is roughly the feeling of a man who returns to find his house repaired, excellently, in a style he does not recognise, by someone who will not leave.

*The leaf-birds.* Shed waxflag, gathering itself, hunching, going up the lattice-light in a loose spiral for thirty heartbeats and coming apart. One creature, or the recurring habit of the litter. The Makers consider the question settled and rude, and will change the subject with their hands, which is the only polite way to be shut down.

*The neighbor.* Beyond the seventh frame, which stands in the corner of the low garden framing a stretch of blank wall and has always been there. Something large. Something slow. Twice in living memory the framed wall has bowed outward and gone warm — warmer than everything, which is already warm — and subsided over a month. The knot-record of the second bowing gives the depth as three fingers and the duration as thirty-one days. Opinion divides, in gesture only, as to whether the thing on the other side is the larger place that Hesp is a model of, pressing in; or the one who built Hesp, gone back out and grown too big to return, and still trying the door.

The threshold is swept toward it either way.

---

They do not call themselves Makers. Their sign for themselves is the packing gesture: palm pressed down, turned a quarter. *The ones who put things where they go.*

They are not frightened. That is the last thing to understand about them. They live in a small warm made place that is quietly eating the difference between the model and the thing, and their response is a rota — sweep, tighten, wake the sleepers, unname the pinned, count the duplicates, keep the seventh threshold clean, and once a season walk the outer wall and touch it, all of them, palms flat, to confirm it is still a wall and not yet a horizon.

Every word of this has weight. They will be a long time getting it off.
