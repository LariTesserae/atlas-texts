## The people of the Bell

They are, first of all, deaf — and this is not an affliction here but a rank.

A man who comes down at eighteen can hear the difference between the fall and the bats, and he is useless for eight or nine years, because he flinches. He is wanted at the pump house and cannot bear the pump house; he is asked to stand under the dome and finds he cannot think there. Deafness arrives in him gradually, from the top of the range downward, taking the clicking first, then the beetles, then the crackle of the guano slope, then the human voice, then, last and most reluctantly, the fall itself — which never goes entirely, and which the oldest of them say they can still feel arriving through the soles of the feet and up the shinbone into the jaw, like a room being moved in the next house. By thirty-five he is calm. By fifty he is one of the ones you want beside you on wet grating with a load. **Deaf at forty, useful at fifty**, they say, and they say it with their hands.

The hand-speech of the Bell is not the hand-speech of the plateau towns, and the two cannot be made to fit. It has no tenses to speak of. It has eleven distinct signs for the condition of water — falling, standing, rising, seeping, misting, beading, shuddering, hot, sour, new, old — and the sign for *rising* is made with a flat hand at the level of the eye and is never, ever made as a joke. Its vocabulary is arranged for distance and dim light: the gestures are large, slow, and made with the arms held out to the side so that they break the lamp-glow rather than hide in the body's shadow. A man addressing you from forty metres away in the orange bubble of Lamp Sixty will turn himself deliberately so that his profile is against the light, and speak with his arms as much as his fingers, and the whole thing has the look of semaphore or of some very patient wading bird. Children raised here learn it before they learn speech and go on preferring it. When one of them is taken up the ladder for schooling and is put in a room where words work, the teachers write home to say the child is sullen and will not answer, when in fact the child has been answering the entire time, from the waist and elbows, in a language that no one in the room can read.

Where the hands will not reach, the works themselves carry the message. The iron pipes on their trestles run six hundred metres out along the galleries and they are struck with a spanner: one blow for attention, then a count of blows for the lamp you want, then the code. Two-and-two is *come*. Three-slow is *hurt*. A long ugly rattle of eleven or more, which anyone can produce and which anyone will produce without being taught, means *the water is rising*, and it empties the lower galleries in four minutes. Nobody has needed it in nine years. Everybody hears it once a season anyway, in dreams, and wakes with a hand already flat at eye level.

---

There are perhaps ninety of them. The number has been between seventy and a hundred and forty for a century, and the changes have had less to do with birth and death than with the price of things far away.

They fall into three trades, and the trades regard each other with the exact affection and contempt of siblings.

The **pumpmen** are the aristocracy, or at least they behave as though somebody had once told them so. Four families have held the two vertical engines between them since the engines were lowered in pieces down the shaft, and the current head of the work is a broad slow woman of sixty with forearms like a stevedore's and a permanent fine grey scurf of graphite on the back of her hands. She cannot hear the engines at all and has not been able to for twenty years, and diagnoses them entirely by contact: a knuckle laid to the frame, a length of soft iron wire held between her teeth and touched to the rod, one flat palm on the flywheel guard while the other hand tells an apprentice, quite calmly, *sour*, *old*, *seven days*. Her people are the least wet in the works, because the engines are hot, and they know it, and the others hold it against them.

The **hill men** work the guano and are the loudest of the three, in the sense that they are constantly and gorgeously laughing at things no one will ever hear. They go up onto the living slopes with cloth over their faces and their trouser-cuffs bound, and they come down with beetles inside their clothing, in their hair, once in a while in an ear canal, and this is a whole genre of joke among them. Their eyes weep continually from the ammonia. Their lungs, later, do worse things. They are the best paid, they eat the best food, they die soonest, and they consider both the pumpmen and the dome crew to be a species of hobbyist.

The **dome crew** are the strange ones. Twice a year they go up the ironwood poles pegged into the roof of the great chamber — a hundred and some metres of standing timber and lashed rungs, kept greased against rot, replaced pole by pole across the generations so that no single pole is old but the pole is very old indeed — and they harvest the black cups. They are the only people in the Bell who still protect their hearing, because they work by it. Wax and grease in the ears at all times outside the dome; no shifts in the pump house; no smoking. Up in the mosaic they take the plugs out and listen, and they can tell a brooding nest from an empty one by the way the returning click comes back off it, and the very good ones can tell a nest with a hatched chick from a nest with an egg. They are lean, vain, delicate, and superstitious, and everyone else calls them the ear-boys well into their sixties.

---

The single most important object in the lives of all three trades is the exhaust flue.

It runs from the pump house up along the wall for six hundred metres, stitched to the rock, and it is warm, and it is the only dry surface in the known world. Everything domestic has collected along it like silt on a bar. Clothing, hung in a line so long that a shirt at the far end takes two days to come back to you. Tobacco, cured there in shallow tin trays. Paper — letters from above are read once at the ladder and then carried straight to the flue and pinned out flat, and the language of the Bell distinguishes between a letter and a *dried letter*, the second being one that has been read enough times to be worth saving. Salt, in a lidded box strapped to the pipe, turned twice a day by whoever passes, and never once dry. The sick are laid on a bench beside the flue at the point where it is warm but not scorching, and this is understood to be the place where you either get better or you do not, and the person tending you will be sitting with their back against the pipe, because it is the only comfort the Bell has ever offered anybody.

They have no bread. Nothing crisp exists here; nothing can be made to be crisp; the air undoes it within a minute of the tin being opened. Children born in the Bell do not know the word, and when the word is explained to them they nod, and don't believe it, in the manner of people being told about snow. Their fingertips have no pattern left on them — permanently macerated, soft, the whorls gone soapy — so that on the two occasions in a lifetime when the state requires a print from them, the print comes out as a grey smudge, and a clerk on the plateau writes *illegible* and files it, and this has caused at least one legal disaster involving an inheritance which is still, forty years later, a story told with hands at the flue.

---

Someone keeps the board.

The office has no name and no pay and it is not exactly hereditary, but it is chosen, and it is chosen young, because the point of it is continuity of judgment. Once a week the keeper of the board goes down to the gauge in the lower gallery, reads the water against a brass pin sunk into the rock by a surveyor whose regime has long since been dissolved, and comes back and writes the figure in chalk with a date, in the eleventh hand to appear on that board in eighty-one years.

Nothing happens. That is the work. A centimetre a decade. The keeper's whole professional life consists of proving, week by week, that the world is behaving. She — the present one is a small dry-voiced woman of forty who talks to visitors more than the others do, out of a certain starved appetite for being listened to — can tell you the level on any given week since she was nineteen, and can tell you her predecessor's habits of rounding, which she has deliberately preserved even though she thinks them wrong, so that the column will remain comparable. She is training a boy of eleven. Neither of them expects to see anything.

Set against that centimetre is the other number, which is why the board is read at all. In the wet season the shaft can vomit. The fall thickens, the floor water climbs off the shudder into real motion, and the lower galleries — the ones that were sumps in the grandparents' time and are workplaces now, on loan — go back to being sumps for six weeks. The bench, the pump house, the bunkhouse on its stilts, all of it sits above a line painted on the wall in 1904 and repainted since, and everyone in the Bell knows where that line is in relation to their own knee. The slow number is comfort. The fast number is the thing that took eleven people in a single afternoon within living memory, and their names are on a plate screwed to the flue, at the warm end, where the ink will not run.

---

They do not bury anyone below.

This is not sentiment; it is arithmetic. The water table is retreating. Anything you put into a low passage will, in some number of centuries, be handed back up out of the flood into the air, and someone will come along with a lamp and find it. So nothing stays. The dead go up the ladder — a full day's work, six people, a wrapped stretcher passing hand to hand from bubble of light to bubble of light — and are put into the thin limestone soil of the plateau, and the party comes back down that night, and this is the only journey in the world that a resident of the Bell makes exactly once. **Up is for the dead and the mail**, they say. They carry their rubbish out too, all of it, tins and wire and broken felt, hauled up by the good winch past the seized one, out of the same arithmetic: whatever the Bell swallows it will one day give back, and they would rather not be remembered by their tins.

At the foot of the ladder there is the tin shrine. It is full of matches. Not lit matches — the air will not hold a flame steady for the length of a prayer, and everyone stopped trying long ago — but dry matches, in bundles, cigarette papers, a page of a book, a twist of clean salt, a bandage in its wrapper. Dryness is the whole content of the offering. Nobody will tell you clearly what it is offered to. Pressed, they will say it is for the ones going up and the ones coming down, which is not an answer, and they know it isn't.

---

Two more things, and they are the ones I would want you to keep.

The first is that all of them sing. Constantly. Badly. A population of ninety people, each walking a wet catwalk singing at the top of their lungs in complete privacy, because nothing carries and nobody has ever heard them and nobody ever will. Children learn songs by watching mouths. Some of the tunes have drifted so far from the versions above that a plateau man hearing one would not recognise it, and cannot hear one anyway. In the far outer galleries, though — beyond four hundred metres, where shouting works and sounds like whispering — a man will occasionally be overheard, and it is a small scandal, and an intimacy, and he is teased about it for a year.

The second is the lamps. The chain of bulbs in their wire cages makes the only gardens the Bell has: perfect fur circles of algae and felt-moss, growing at a few centimetres a year, the fastest living thing in the system. Every one of them is somebody's. There are gardens down there older than the pumpmen's youngest engine, tended in the sense that they are *not touched*, and the only real politics in the works — the only matter that produces factions, walkouts, and a bad decade between two families — is the question of moving a lamp.
