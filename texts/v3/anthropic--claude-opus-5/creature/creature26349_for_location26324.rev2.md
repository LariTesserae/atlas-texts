## 9. The bone-hearers

There are forty of them, or sixty. Start with the bodies.

They are short-limbed and deep through the chest, with the big spleens and thick blood of any people who have lived four kilometers up for longer than anyone can carve. Their skin is dark and, on the garden side of their lives, permanently damp; the grease they render from swift-egg and grub-fat beads on it. They dress in felt. Beard-filament, wetted and worked between the palms for hours, mats into a grey-green cloth that holds warmth even soaked, which is the only kind of warmth available here. It smells of minerals and armpit and rain on stone.

Their eyes are the most obviously adapted thing about them. The light on the plateau changes four times a minute — pearl, glare, slate, pearl — and their pupils keep up with it in a way that is unsettling to watch, flickering like something mechanical. They are superb at motion and poor at color; they can read a hand-sign at a hundred and twenty meters across a clearing and cannot reliably distinguish blue from green, which does not matter, because almost everything here is grey, black-green, or ash.

But the sense that organizes them is not sight and is not, strictly, hearing.

They wear plugs from the age of one month, and by forty most of them are stone deaf anyway, and none of this cuts them off from the roar in the slightest, because the roar does not arrive through the ear. It arrives through the skeleton. Sit on the pavement and the plates of it come up through the pelvis into the spine. Two thousand throats, each with a mouth-diameter and a depth and a column speed, each therefore with a note; each note attenuating with distance at its own rate; the whole plateau a chord four kilometers wide, played continuously, and never once in a human lifetime the same chord twice, because the throats stall and surge and the chord leans.

They read it. Not consciously, any more than you consciously read the pressure of the floor against your feet. A person can be led blindfolded thirty kilometers across featureless shine-pavement and will stop, and turn, and walk to the correct garden. Ask one how — put the question in hand-sign, large and slow — and they will take your wrist and set your palm flat on your own breastbone and hold it there a moment, and that is the whole answer, and it is not evasive. They have no words. They have never had words. They are telling you the truth in the only place they keep it.

The deafness comes for them all. It arrives around forty, having been arriving since the first month, and it is not treated as a loss, because nothing that anyone needs is carried in the air. The old know a throat has stalled because the chord goes lopsided in the hips. The old know the Silence has come because the beard-curtains stop swaying, and the young all stand up at once.

## 10. Hands

Two languages in the same body.

The **far hand** is enormous. It uses the whole arm, the turn of the shoulders, the set of the feet; a single sign can be a meter and a half wide, held for two full seconds so it survives a shadow-blotch passing over the speaker. It is built for a garden clearing in changing light, and it has a grammar of distance: the further away you are, the more formal you necessarily become, because the small qualifying movements drop out at range. You cannot be subtle at eighty meters. You cannot really lie, either — not the intricate kind of lie. Everything said in the far hand is said publicly and plainly and is visible to anyone in the ring who happens to look up, which is why the far hand is used for arrivals, warnings, weather, agreements, insults, and jokes.

The **close hand** lives between two people, inside the space of the forearms, fingers only, often shielded by a shoulder. It is fast — a fluent pair can sustain something like conversational speed — and it is where the whole interior life of this people is kept: grief, appetite, obscenity, doubt, the long dull negotiations of who is going to walk the dew-comb line this week. Privacy is not achieved by lowering the voice. There is no voice. Privacy is achieved by geometry.

Consequences follow, and they are severe.

Blindness here is far worse than deafness. A blind person loses language, not landscape; the landscape is still coming up through their feet. They are moved onto the touch-register, hand inside hand, and someone must be within arm's reach of them for the rest of their life. This is arranged without discussion and is regarded as ordinary.

Ruined hands are a kind of aphasia. An old woman with swollen knuckles speaks slowly and badly, and people come and stand very close to her, which is the courtesy.

And at night — no fire worth the name, and nothing to burn out on the flats — there is simply no language. Dark is mute. A family in a spiral windbreak sleeps in a heap for warmth and communicates by grip and weight, and whatever the argument was at sunset it cannot be carried past dark, because there is no vehicle for it. They wake to a plateau where yesterday's quarrel has no place to have been stored. Outsiders have found this enviable. The people themselves seem to find it neither here nor there.

They dream, and there is a shared conviction, insofar as anything so vague can be pinned down in hand-sign, that in dreams everyone speaks and is understood, and that this is what the dead do all the time.

## 11. The tether

An infant's first plugs are made at about one month from grub-wax and packed beard, worked warm and pressed in by whoever the mother lets. It is the closest thing to a ceremony that the first years contain, and it is done indoors, meaning in the lee of a spiral, and it is done quickly, because the child is screaming and no one can hear it.

Then the cord. From the time it can crawl until it can be trusted — three years, four, longer for the ones judged dreamy — a child on a garden ring is tethered by the waist to a flutewood stake driven into the sponge-mat. The cord is beard-twist, and it is short, and the length of it is calculated from the distance to the lip. Every adult on this plateau carries the scar of it, a pale band low on the belly, and every adult knows precisely how long a running child takes to cross twenty meters of mat, because at some point in their lives they have watched one do it.

The throats do take children. Not often. A hand's count in a generation. There is no body to do anything with, and the name is carved anyway, on the upright stone at the lip of the throat that took them, which is the only circumstance under which a name goes on a lip-stone. Those stones are therefore also a register, and a short walk around the marked throats of the plateau will tell a patient reader roughly how many people have lived here and how many of them were small.

## 12. The round

The ring feeds them and the flats move them.

Mat-shoots, eaten raw, bitter and squeaking against the teeth. Grub, from the flutewood joints, dug with a bone awl — bitter also, and rich, and the source of all the wax on the plateau, so a grub gallery is a bottle-seal and a pair of ear-plugs and a meal at once. Swift eggs, harvested at the lip with a beard-rope round the waist and the other end round three people, the harvester hanging in a wind that is trying to take them upward and inward at once, groping in crevices with a hand they cannot see, in a roar that has erased their name from their own mouth. Nobody enjoys it. Everyone has done it.

Water is never the problem — it is coming up through the floor by the tonne — but carrying it is. Flutewood segments, wax-plugged, slung in a beard net. A journey across the flats is planned as a sequence of dew-combs and cushions, and a person maintains the combs along their habitual routes the way you would maintain a boat.

Once in perhaps ten years, a loaf is taken. The whole plateau, or as much of it as can be found and told in time, gathers to the garden where it happened. There is no way to preserve meat here except the dryness of the open pavement, and so the flats around that garden become, for a fortnight, a drying ground: strips weighted under stones out to the horizon, a chessboard of red across the shine, everyone working in the grit-wind, no one saying anything, the flutewood shrieking behind them. It is the largest thing that happens in a lifetime. Afterward the carapace is kept. A loaf-shell is the only large rigid flat object available to this culture; it becomes a sledge, a door, a birthing floor, a rain-shield, a carving desk, and it passes down three or four generations, growing thin and translucent at the edges from use.

## 13. The stone, the name

Carving is the art, because carving is the only thing that lasts, and on rock this old and this hard a full name is four days' work with a chert point and a bone mallet and it takes the skin off the heel of your hand.

Nobody carves idly. Four days is long enough to change your mind, and the change of mind is understood to be part of the process; a name begun and abandoned is left there, half-made, and is not a shameful thing. The lee sides of the knuckles are covered: names, and the records of stalls and Silences and great rains, and long strings of glyphs that the living can no longer read at all, and older strings that are visible only for ten minutes at low sun, when the shadow finds them.

The courtesy toward old marks is exact. You do not carve within a hand's span of a mark you cannot read. You do not carve over one at all. And when you come upon one, you wet it: a squeezed handful of beard-water poured down the face of the stone so that the glyph goes dark and stands out for a minute or two before the air takes it. That is the whole greeting. It is done for strangers a thousand years dead with no expectation of anything, and it is done every time, and children are corrected sharply if they walk past.

A name exists in exactly one place. The sign for a person, in either register of the language, is their glyph traced in the air, so to speak of someone at all is to reproduce a particular set of cuts on a particular rock forty kilometers away, and to *know* someone is to have been taken there. Friendship on this plateau is measured in walking days. A child is unnamed for years and is signed as *the one at the garden with the split flutewood*, or *the one who bit*, until someone — anyone, it is not fixed who, though it is usually not the mother — decides to spend four days on a rock.

The roofless tower on the western swell is six meters high and nobody knows what it was for. It is repaired anyway, stone by stone, by whoever is passing and notices a slump. What it is for now, by drift and convenience, is being found: a person who wants company, or a mate, or help, or who is ill enough to be afraid, climbs it and sits on the rim, where they are visible for a very long way across the polished ground. Sometimes somebody sits there for nine days. Sometimes no one comes.

## 14. The Silence, from inside

They do not sleep through it because the Silence is the one hour in the year in which anything can be heard, and hearing is not a sense here, it is an event, and events are rare.

The young have the ears. The young are therefore instruments. A child of nine with good hearing is the plateau's entire diagnostic medicine, and in the four to sixty minutes of a Silence they are walked from body to body and made to put an ear against chests and bellies and swollen joints, and afterward they report in the close hand, badly, what they think they heard: *water in this one, a fast one here, nothing, nothing, a rubbing*. Everyone understands the limitations of this. Everyone stands very still and waits their turn anyway. A person who falls sick in the week after a Silence will very likely be dead before the next.

The rest of what happens in the Silence is not useful.

They hear their own footsteps for the first time each year and are visibly disconcerted by the sound. They hear the beard-curtains let go all at once, a garden-wide patter like a crowd applauding at the far end of a valley. They hear each other breathe, and swallow, and the stomachs of the people they have slept against every night of their lives. A newborn delivered into a Silence is the only infant any of them will ever hear crying, and such a birth is carved.

They do not speak, even then. The anatomy is there and the habit is four hundred generations gone; what comes out, in those who try, is nothing anyone wants to repeat. But many of them hum. Standing on the pavement among the settling swifts with their heads slightly down, a low buzz in the chest that they can feel through the jaw and which is, in that hour and no other, also a sound. They appear mildly embarrassed about it and they do it every time.

And when the first low note comes back up through the floor, the plateau exhales its accumulated cargo: the spore-smoke of every cushion for a hundred kilometers, which has been hanging motionless in the dead air waiting for exactly this, goes up four thousand meters on the returning column. Nine thousand years of patience, loading during the pause, fired by the resumption. The people watch it go. They have a sign for it that is also the sign for a thrown thing.

## 15. Going down

There is no soil to bury in and no fuel to burn with and nothing on this plateau that eats the dead.

So they are carried, sometimes for days, on a loaf-shell sledge that squeaks across the shine, to the nearest marked throat. The rope-party works the last ten meters lying flat. There is no ceremony at the lip because there is no sound in which to hold one; the roar has already dissolved everyone's name.

The body goes over and the updraft takes the hair and the felt first, lifting them straight up while the body drops, so that for about a second the dead appear to be rising. Then they are gone into warm cloud and nobody ever hears the end of it. That is understood to be the point. The one rule is *do not descend*, and the dead are the single exception, and the below-world is therefore populated entirely by everyone who has ever lived here, and the warm wet air coming up through two thousand stone flues is, in the only cosmology these people have ever needed, breath.

Which explains the other rule. In the Silence the breathing stops. The dead have paused. Whatever is happening down there, for four minutes or for an hour, the plateau is briefly audible to it, and it would be a poor thing to be found asleep.

The name stays up. The name is the part that does not go down. Some very old dried people are still sitting out on the flats from a time before this custom, leathered and windpolished and kilometers from anything, facing in no particular direction. You do not carve near them. You set a stone upright and go on.

## 16. The others

**The flutewood** does not want light and barely wants water; it wants to be played. The mouth-slits are on the outside of the stems but the resonance is internal, and a stand of it is a single clonal organism sharing a root-mat under the sponge, tuning itself: segments thickening, slits widening or scarring over, the whole grove taking months to retune after a neighboring throat stalls. Cut it down and the stumps regrow leaning toward the loudest throat within reach. Nobody knows what it gets out of this. The best guess of anyone who has thought about it is that the sound is not the point but the by-product, that the segments are pumping — a bellows working off the updraft, moving water up a plant with no wood and no real pressure of its own — and that the ten thousand organs in the wind tunnel are simply the noise a pump makes.

**The beard** is not a plant. It is closer to a lichen with ambitions and it is doing exactly one thing, forever, with total commitment: combing water out of moving air. It has no roots and does not touch the ground and will grow on rope, on felt, on a sleeping person's hair if the person sleeps long enough.

**The cushion** is the oldest living thing in the country and possibly the oldest living thing anywhere. Three meters of ash-blue dome is nine thousand years of half-liters. Each one is sealed, self-watering, self-shading, and internally warm — put your hand under the skirt of an old one on a cold night and it is like an animal. They do not compete; they are too far apart to compete. Each is alone in a hundred square meters of nothing and has been since before agriculture existed elsewhere in the world. They split open five or six times a year for forty minutes and then close.

**The loaf** is deaf, which on this plateau is unremarkable, and armored, which is not, because there is nothing here that could threaten it and has not been for a very long time. It is wearing the answer to a question no one can ask. It spends most of its life bedded under the sponge-mat, grazing upward from below, visible only as a slow bulge crossing a garden floor over a season. Twice a year one decides to change gardens, and hauls itself out onto open pavement, and walks for weeks across bare glowing stone at a hundred meters a day on stored water, unable to hear anything at all, navigating by something — moisture, perhaps, or the ground-note through its belly plates — toward a ring of green it cannot possibly see from the start of the journey. They arrive. They almost always arrive.

**The throat-swifts** are the only creatures here that have made the roar their home rather than their weather. They live inside the columns: sleeping on the wing in the lift, drinking saturated air, mating in the boiling white, feeding on the insect wash coming up from the warm world below, which they harvest as it arrives at four thousand meters and dies of cold. They touch stone only to lay, in crevices within a meter of the lip, in the loudest place on the surface of the planet. Their chicks launch by falling. And in a Silence, when the lift stops, they have no choice but to come down — which is the whole banal mechanism of the strangest gathering in the world, and does not reduce it by one particle: thousands of birds standing on polished rock in absolute quiet among a scatter of grey-green people who are humming, all of them waiting for the same note, and all of them going up together when it comes.

## 17. If you come

They will find you long before you find them; you will be the loudest-looking thing on a hundred kilometers of stone.

They will not be frightened and will not be especially interested. They will feed you mat-shoots and hand you a squeezed cupful of warm mineral water and watch your mouth moving with the mild patience of people watching an animal do something an animal does. They will give you plugs, pressed in with a thumb, without asking.

And if, after enough days, you have not walked where you were told not to walk and have set upright every stone you happened to move, one of them will get up in the pearl-light and start off across the flats, and glance back once to see whether you are following, and keep going, for a day, or two days, to a particular knuckle of grey-green rock among a thousand identical ones.

They will pour a handful of water down the lee face of it. A mark will go dark for a minute.

That is their name, and they are showing it to you, and there is nothing to say, and no way to say it, and no need.
