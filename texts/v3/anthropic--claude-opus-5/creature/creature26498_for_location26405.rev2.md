## The People of the Shelf

They lean.

That is the first thing, and you will notice it before you notice faces. Every person on the Shelf stands with their weight two degrees into the northwest, the same two degrees the block leans, so that they are plumb with the world and out of true with the floor. It is not a posture they learn; it is one they are grown into, the way a tree grows out of a cliff. The hips do it. Set a Shelf-dweller on a level pavement — at the bottom of the switchbacks, say, in the valley towns where the fog lies — and they list. They put a hand out. They look like people on a boat. Within a few days they correct, and within a few days of returning they list the other way, and they say, with some feeling, that the valley is what's crooked.

They are not tall. The light up here is unmediated and hard, and the air never warms, so they are pale about the lashes and brows and weathered across the nose and cheekbones in a way that looks like age but is not. You cannot guess ages on the Shelf. A woman with white hair will carry a slab up four levels without stopping; a man with a smooth face will tell you he remembers when the Spill ran thin for a season, which nobody else remembers at all. Nothing wears out properly here, and that includes them. They have very good teeth. They have dry-stone hands — flat-tipped, nicked along the outer edge of the palm, a callus at the heel where a block is steadied and none at all where a tool would be gripped, because they do not grip tools so much as set things down next to other things until they stay.

Their voices are low without being soft. You will find yourself matching them. There is a whole grammar of restraint here that has nothing to do with piety: they simply do not like to add to the sound. When two of them meet on a stair, one steps into a doorway and neither says anything and both are satisfied.

---

There are four thousand one hundred names.

Not four thousand one hundred people who have names — the names themselves are a fixed inventory, counted and knotted like everything else. Orrel. Mev. Tacket. Sill. Hanno. Deiss. Short ones, mostly, one or two beats, because a name is spoken across terraces. When someone dies, their name goes back on the cord, and it will be the name of the next child born or the name given to whoever comes up out of the fog, and nobody thinks of this as replacement and nobody thinks of it as reincarnation either. It is simply that the name is a slot and the slot was empty and now it is not. If you say to a Shelf woman, *I knew an Orrel once, thirty years ago, was that you?* — she will consider the question seriously and answer that it was Orrel, yes, and she will not understand why that does not satisfy you.

Children born onto a vacated name are called *returns.* They are not treated as holy. They are treated as arrived. A return is handed around the lower quarter for the first year the way a borrowed tool is handed around, and everyone who holds it says the name out loud once, and that is the whole of the ceremony.

---

The town's real occupation is arithmetic, but arithmetic is not the town's real work. The work is *transfer.*

Because nothing accumulates, nobody here can gain anything except from somebody else. There is no growth to distribute, no surplus to discover, no frontier. Every want, without exception, is a request that another person be diminished. This produces the two things that visitors find hardest to reconcile: the extraordinary courtesy, and the quiet, open, unembarrassed traffic in personal deficit.

The courtesy first. They are the most careful people you will ever meet. They do not take the last of anything, ever, from anywhere, because the last of a thing is not a portion, it is a change in the count. They ask permission to admire. A man will not say *that is a fine knife* without adding, immediately and almost as one word, *and I do not want it* — because to want it, said aloud, in a place where the number of sharp knives is fixed, is very nearly to begin taking it. Children are taught this the way children elsewhere are taught not to point.

And the traffic. There is a bench at the north end of the third level, under a corbelled arcade, where the giving is done. If you need a thing, you go and sit, and you say what you need, and you wait, and someone comes and gives it, and the two of you go together to a Counter and the cord is checked to see that the totals are undisturbed — they always are — and then you part without thanking each other. Thanks would imply a gift. There are no gifts. There is only the redistribution of an unchanging mass, and both parties are simply where the mass happened to move.

---

They will tell you, if you ask directly, that the Even holds for things. They are less forthcoming about what else it holds.

Skill is conserved. There are eleven people on the Shelf who can throw a pot that will not crack in the fire, and there have always been eleven, and when a young man begins to show the hands for it there is a period of a few weeks in which one of the eleven, usually the oldest, finds that he has begun to put his thumb wrong. Nobody resents this. The old potter is not diminished in anyone's estimation; his going is the young man's coming, and both are the same event viewed from two ends. They speak of it as *handing.* An old woman will say, plainly, at a meal: *I handed reading this winter.* And someone at the table has begun to read.

Memory is conserved, though this one they dislike discussing. The town's stock of the past is a fixed quantity, and since nothing new happens in any way that generates events, the stock mostly circulates: a man wakes one morning with a clear picture of a sheep drowning in a cistern, and he has never seen it, and somewhere across the city an old man has lost the same picture and is mildly relieved. This is why they keep no ink. Writing would let memory pile up outside of people, in a stack, unbalanced, and they are quite certain — they cannot say how they are certain — that the Even does not care where the surplus is. It will take it back out of somewhere.

Grief is conserved. This is the one that cracks visitors open. When a death is announced and the ledger is not yet closed, the bereaved do not mourn; they *hold.* You will see a man sitting in his doorway for nine hours with his hands on his knees, perfectly composed, waiting for the balancing birth or the balancing arrival, and when word comes up from the gate he will put his face in his hands and howl like an animal for exactly as long as it takes, and then get up and go to work on the terraces. The grief was always going to come. It could not come early, because there was no room for it yet.

---

Certain people do particular jobs, and the jobs are strange.

Nineteen Counters, always nineteen, with nails worn flat and shiny at the pad from running cord. They do not seem to be chosen. When a Counter dies, the man or woman who replaces them is generally found to have been counting already — something small, the hares in the north quarter, the courses on a retaining wall — for some months, without having been able to say why they started.

Twelve masons, who build nothing and repair almost nothing and spend their lives confirming with knotted cord that the walls are where the walls were. They are the most cheerful people in the city. A mason's whole professional life is the repeated experience of being right.

The hollow-keepers, six of them, who maintain the forty-one empty houses of the northern quarter — sweep them, clear the dew-fins, keep the gutters running, and once a month lay a fire in each cold hearth and then take it up again unlit. They are regarded as slightly ridiculous and are universally liked. Children follow them.

And the gate-keeper, who is not one person but whoever is next in the rota, and who sits at the stone bench at the head of the switchbacks and does nothing at all except be present when someone sits down. The bench is long. At any time there are between two and nine people living on it, more or less — people who have declared to leave and are waiting for an arrival to release them. Some have been on the bench for eleven years. They have a whole small culture down there, at the lip of the fog: their own songs, their own way of cooking, a habit of not asking each other why. They do not go home at night. Going home would be a kind of withdrawal, and the list is strict, and the list is the closest thing on the Shelf to hope.

---

Twice a week, at dusk, somebody walks out to the rim above the Spill and gives something away.

They call it spilling, and it is the nearest thing here to worship or to medicine, depending on who you ask. You go out along the joint-channel with the great white rope of water going over beside you, and you stand at the bevel, and you say the thing out loud into the noise — a memory you were not meant to keep, an appetite, a sum you accidentally learned, a grudge, a song you made up and therefore should not exist. Then you go home lighter and the total is preserved. What goes over does not land. The fog in the valley stands level as water and never clears, and if you have been on the Shelf long enough you will start to think about what the fog is made of and then you will stop thinking about it.

Because the other direction is also true. When the ledger opens — when someone dies, or leaves — a traveler comes up the switchbacks out of that fog, always within the day. They arrive tired and mild and pleased to be there. They do not remember deciding to climb. They very often know the way to a specific house without being told, and they sit down in it like a man sitting in his own chair, and within a month they lean two degrees to the northwest. Sometimes they arrive with a scar that a departed person had. Nobody on the Shelf finds this remarkable. Nobody on the Shelf asks a new arrival anything at all about before, and this is not tact.

---

The vice here is keeping.

It is not called theft, because nothing is missing from anyone in particular. It is called *holding back,* and it is the only thing that makes these calm people cold. Somewhere under a slab in the second level there will be a box with three good knives in it, kept by a woman who could not have said why she wanted three, and because of those three knives, across the city, three knives have gone dull before their time and three people are standing in their kitchens, puzzled, running a thumb along an edge that was fine last week. That is what hoarding does here. It does not make you rich. It makes a thin, diffuse poverty in four thousand other lives, spread so wide that nobody would ever notice — except that the Count notices, because the Count does nothing else.

They do not search houses. They triangulate. They look at the shape of the deficit and work back along it, the way you would find a leak by the damp. It takes them weeks, sometimes a year. Then several of them come and stand in the doorway, not blocking it, and say the person's name once, and the person gets the box out.

And then everyone walks to the rim together, and the thing is given to the Spill, and the holder is not punished, because there is no punishment — punishment would be an addition — and they walk home in the dark with the moths coming up granular out of the joints and somebody's hand on the back of their neck.

---

The animals are better at this than the people, and everybody knows it.

The stone hares in the doorways of the empty houses are not squatters; they are, functionally, the reason the houses are not empty, and the hollow-keepers sweep around them. The quill-mice go along the gutters in files and the sill-hawks take them at a rate so exact that no Counter has ever had to intervene. The four hundred shaggy browsers eat the moss cushions down to a certain height and stop — not because the moss is gone, but because that is the height. The grey deer keep the hedges at hedge height. None of these creatures has ever been observed to be wrong, ever, by a single individual, in four generations of cord. The people, by contrast, are wrong all the time. They want. They hold back. They sit eleven years on a bench at the edge of the fog hoping a stranger will climb up out of it and let them go.

Ask an old Counter what lives on the Shelf and she will tell you: the hares live here, and the hawks, and the moths in the joints, and the browsers on the scree, and four thousand one hundred people who are *learning* to.

---

Stand at the rim at the end of the day, then, and be honest about what you are looking at. Not a law. Not a spirit in the rock. Something more like an enormous, patient, unhurried attention that came up with the block and caught the mountain mid-gesture and has been holding it there ever since, the way you would hold a level full cup: not tending it, not loving it, just *not letting it move.* The people are inside that attention the way a fly is inside a held breath. They have made a civilization out of good manners toward it. They are kind, and exact, and very quiet, and they will feed you and give you a swept house in the north quarter and ask you nothing.

And the hawk three feet from your elbow will not turn its head, and you should understand why. It is not tame. It is not unafraid. You have not sat on the bench. You have not taken a name off the cord. As far as anything on this mountain is concerned, you are not here yet — and the moment you are, someone will be walking down the switchbacks into the fog, listing to one side, correcting.
