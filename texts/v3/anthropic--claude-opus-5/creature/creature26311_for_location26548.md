# The Tenders

**Hands first.** Everything else about them follows from what has happened to their hands.

A tender's palms are wet-white and deeply corrugated, wrinkled not by immersion but permanently, structurally, the way the floor is worn concave — the same process at a smaller scale. The pads of the three working fingers are broadened and flattened and the nails on those fingers are gone, grown out and worn off and grown out again until the beds sealed over. Where a handrail has a slender polished waist, that waist is the negative of these fingers, and any tender laying her hand on any rail anywhere in the continent finds it fits. This is not sentiment either. It is the same hand. There has only ever been one shape of hand here, because the tools were made for it and it was made by the tools, and the loop has been closing on itself for ten thousand years.

Their forearms are inlaid. Beginning at about twelve, a tender opens the skin of her own arm with the grafting knife and beds in a sliver of live Stock — from her own gallery, from the arch she has just been given charge of — and binds it and waits. Most take. The graft raises a pale ridge that thickens and stays warm and, in the old, branches. An eighty-year-old tender has a low relief of woven Stock running from wrist to elbow on both arms, blood-warm, respiring faintly, indistinguishable in temperature and texture from the wall she is leaning on. It is not decoration and it is not quite a record. When asked, tenders say it is so that the floor knows them. They mean this practically. It shortens the burial.

Beyond the hands and arms: small people, wide-footed, splay-toed, thin through the chest and heavy in the thigh from a life of walking curved surfaces where down keeps changing its mind. Enormous dark eyes with almost no white showing, which is why a lamp-gall held near a face reads as two wet holes and nothing else. They never dry. Nobody in the Held has ever been dry. Their skin carries a fine grey bloom of resistant mould that they cultivate deliberately and oil with lamp-gall grease, and which smells sweetish and faintly of hot metal, and which is the smell of a person; a tender can tell whose gallery she has crossed into by the smell that has settled in the withy.

They wear almost nothing: a resin-cured harness for the tools, a bag, a hood against the interior drizzle. Cloth would rot in a season and nobody has ever needed it for warmth.

---

**The murmur.** A tender working alone talks continuously, aloud, in a low unbroken voice, and what she says is a plain narration of what she is doing and what she is about to do. *Taking up the adze. Dressing the graft-lip on the third rib, the near side, the wet side, then the far side. Nine strokes. Ten. This is for the arch to carry, this is so the arch will carry the walk that isn't made yet.*

This is not a superstition laid over the work. It is the work. Intention is the load-bearing element of this world and saying-out is how intention is kept in the body over the eleven or twelve hours of a day's labour, the way a lashing is kept tight by hauling it again every hour. A tender who falls silent while working will notice, within minutes, that the tool has begun to go dull in her hand — not blunt: *undecided* — and she will stop, and start the sentence again from the beginning.

When two tenders meet, they stop talking. Almost entirely. They will work side by side for a day and exchange perhaps forty words, and this is not coldness, it is relief: the other person's presence does the intending, so the mouth can rest. Company, to a tender, is fundamentally a rest from speech. Their word for a companion translates as *quiet-bringer*. Nothing in their culture is stranger to them, or more obscene, than the idea of two people talking to each other for pleasure. They will do it anyway, sometimes, at the knot-chambers, and be ashamed afterwards, and go home and work in silence for a week to make it up.

---

**Going wide.** Because distance is made of attention, despair has a measurement.

The first sign is that the walk to the water-gall takes a little longer than it did. Not tiredness — the tender counts her paces, everyone counts, counting is taught before walking — and the count is up by eleven. The next month it is up by forty. Then the far end of her own sleeping-gallery is a half-hour away and the graft she meant to dress this year is a two-day walk that used to be a morning, and she is not sick and not injured and there is nothing wrong with her legs. The world is simply withdrawing to the distance at which she is holding it.

They call this *going wide*, and it kills, and it is the single most common way for a tender to die, well ahead of the long walkers and far ahead of falling.

The treatment is other people, and it is expensive. A neighbour will walk four months to come, and when she arrives she does not comfort or counsel. She takes the wide tender's own routes and walks them with fierce deliberate purpose, all of them, in order, one after another, saying out loud with every step *this is a way, this is a way that is walked, someone means to walk here*, and re-hangs every bell, and makes the wide one walk behind her, and this goes on for as long as it takes, up to a year. The counts come down. Sometimes they don't. Where a tender has gone wide and no one came in time, the territory afterward is unrecoverable: not thinned, not yet, but stretched, its distances swollen out past any budget of attention, so that the whole country of it must be written off and the bells brought inward, and the next Sigh, when it comes, comes from there.

---

**The Allowance.** Every tender carries a book of thin Stock-veneer leaves, incised with the gouge, bound in cord. It is too dark to read it without a lamp-gall, so reading it is always a small deliberate ceremony done kneeling with the cage open. It contains the names, in full, of everyone and everything in her charge that is permitted to exist: herself, her apprentice if she has one, her named animals, her lamp-gall colonies, her arches, her bells. Fourteen hundred entries would be a large book. Most run to three hundred.

A name is a list of tasks. It must be said entire at the naming and at the burial and never in between. And a valid name must include at least one task that does not yet exist — work that cannot be done in the named one's lifetime, an arch that will not be reached for four generations, a gallery that is currently void.

This is the rule that everything hangs on, and the tenders know it and can state it: *what holds is not what was made but what is still being meant.* A name that only lists existing work will not hold a child. The child sprouts and thins and by the ninth day there is a spot on the floor of the nursery that no one can look at directly, and the mother's Allowance has an entry in it that nobody can read anymore, though the incisions are plainly there.

So every person in the Held is, formally and legally, a promise of labour that will not be finished. This is also, they will tell you, why the old work is still audible. The adze-strokes you hear in a gallery at the floor of audibility are not echoes and not ghosts. They are the unfinished portions of ten thousand names, still owed, still being paid down.

No voices come through because a voice was never in anybody's name.

---

**The dead.** Nothing is buried in the Held, because there is nowhere to put it. The dead are grafted.

A body is folded into a young trained fork at the third stratum, in a place chosen decades in advance and dressed for it, and lashed, and the fork is fed and coaxed and closes over eleven or twelve years. The arm-inlays help; they fuse first, which is the whole reason for the inlays. Afterwards a floor is woven across, and worked, and walked, and in two hundred years there is another floor over that, and in four thousand the layer is hard as slate and shows the adze-scallops of whoever dressed it, and someone four kilometres up is standing on it and hearing, faintly, the sound of two people breathing in unison, lifting.

Tenders find this consoling in a way that is difficult to convey without making them sound morbid. They are not morbid. They simply do not experience the floor as a floor. It is a depth of company. This is precisely why they cannot sleep in a silence with no work in it: not fear of the dark, but the intolerable sensation of lying down on nobody.

---

**Listeners.** A few in each generation — always old, usually deafened to ordinary sound by decades of the marsh-galleries' pressure, often blind — do nothing but stand still and listen downward.

They are the only early warning the world has. Before a stratum lets go, the labour-sound in it fades. First the breathing goes, then the lashings, and for a season or two there is only the adze, and then nothing, and a listener who finds a silent stratum sends runners and the bells come inward and the tenders make what preparations can be made, which are few. The Sigh follows anywhere from a month to nine years later.

Listeners who go deep — down past the fifth stratum, into the carried dark, with a caged gall and a rope — come back with an observation that they report in the same flat professional register as everything else and which nobody in the Held has ever been able to do anything with. Below a certain depth, the work-sound changes. The strokes go slower and spread out, far past the pace of any human arm. The lashings stop. The two breathers stop.

And below that, further, in the Dark Grain, there is still work going on, and there is no breathing in it at all.

---

**The shape of a life.** Crowded, then alone, then crowded.

Children are raised in the knot-chambers, where a dozen major spars fuse and a dozen downs cross and thirty or forty people live all over the inside of a hollow sphere. It is the only crowded place a tender ever knows and she knows it before she is fifteen. Then she is given her lines and goes out, and for forty or fifty years she sees three adults a year, and talks to herself, and dresses grafts, and hangs bells, and pushes her arches nine centimetres further into the unmade air. Then her hands go and she comes back to a knot-chamber to teach, and dies in the noise of children, and is folded into a fork.

Courtship, across those middle decades, is conducted by bells. Two tenders whose territories face one another across an unmade void begin extending toward each other. Nine centimetres a year. Neither will live to make the join. What happens instead is that each year the other's boundary bell is a little more clearly audible in the quiet, and this is the entire romance, and the tenders consider it complete and sufficient and are not being brave about it. The children finish the arch, or the grandchildren do, or nobody does and the two lines stop a kilometre apart and thin, and somewhere a long way off a listener notes the work going quiet.

Knot-chamber people, who grow up with several downs, cannot live in ordinary single-grain galleries; they get flat-sick, vomit, cannot sleep. Marsh-people, who punt the doubled world of the shallow galleries and read the frogs' green flashing as fluently as speech, have soft feet and are useless on the arches and are regarded with a mixture of envy and suspicion. Everybody agrees the marsh-people talk too much. The marsh-people, whose hectares of light-conversation go on all night in total silence, would say they never talk at all.
