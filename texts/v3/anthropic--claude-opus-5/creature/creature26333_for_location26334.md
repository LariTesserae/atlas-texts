## What lives in the Lees

Begin with the fact that they are also what settled out.

There are about eleven hundred people in Thimble and perhaps three hundred more distributed across the moor in one-room turf huts at the ends of the fog-lines, tending nets. That is the population of the plateau. It has not varied by more than a fifth in any record anyone keeps, and the records go back a long way — though not as far as the tools in the peat, which is the town's oldest open question and is discussed quietly, without heat, roughly once a generation: whether the people who left those tools were somebody else, or were us, before we forgot.

They have no answer. They also have no urgency about it. Both facts are characteristic.

## Bodies

Deep chests, and the depth is real, not posture: the ribcage is a different shape here, sprung wider at the base, and a lowland doctor palpating a Thimble child has been known to look for disease and find only a person. They breathe more often than you do rather than more deeply, a soft continuous working of the diaphragm that never quite stops, even asleep — so that a room of sleepers in the Lees sounds like something being sanded very slowly a long way off.

Skin the colour of unbleached wool, except across the nose and the tops of the hands and the outer curve of each cheekbone, where forty days a year of blue-white noon have laid down a permanent brick-dark weathering that begins at seven or eight years old and never lifts. Their faces are therefore mapped: the parts that face up are dark, the parts that face out are pale, and a stranger's face reads as blank to them, unfinished, like a wall that has not yet been pointed.

Fingers thick to the last joint, chapped in a fine crazed pattern, nails cut back to nothing because nails hold water and water holds cold. Capillary flush at the fingertips, a dull permanent rose. They lose the small toes sometimes. Nobody makes anything of it.

And the eyes, which are the strange part. Raised under a light with no direction, they have grown extraordinary at surface and useless at space. A Thimble woman can tell you the wetness of a stone at nine paces by its grey alone, distinguishing — with words for each — the grey of stone that is damp through, stone that is filmed, stone that is beaded, stone that is beaded and about to release. She cannot reliably tell you whether the stone is nine paces off or ninety. Distance in the Lees is not seen; it is deduced, from texture, from the known height of a rosette-tree, from how long you have been walking. Children are trained to it as a skill, with games, and adults still get it wrong, and everyone carries a rod — not for support. For sounding. The air lies, but the ground is honest, and a rod struck against peat, plank, gneiss or bog tells the truth up your arm.

## Breath, and what it costs

Air is the currency of speech here, and they are frugal.

A sentence in Thimble is one exhalation long. The breath that follows it is not a pause in the sentence; it is part of the sentence, and the local obscenity — the thing that will get a grown man walked away from — is to fill another person's breath with your own words. Long arguments are conducted in alternating short units, each speaker inhaling while the other spends, so that a serious disagreement resembles two people slowly passing a single lit lamp back and forth for an hour.

The volume is that of a mouth twenty centimetres from an ear. Sibilants die in this air within a metre, so the language has thinned itself of them; it runs on stops and nasals and low hums, and their names are built to survive fog at close range — Bab, Meck, Ondo, Tepp, Krull, Nane. A name in the Lees is a name you can put into somebody's ear while both of you are working.

And because two people forty metres apart cannot speak at all, everything that gets said in the Lees has first been walked. You cross the distance. You put down what you were holding, you go over, you speak, you go back. The result is an entire ethics arrived at by acoustics: nobody here says anything they were not willing to stop working and walk for. They are, accordingly, a people almost without the casual insult, and almost without the shouted apology, and it is difficult to tell which absence has cost them more.

For the rest there are taps. The plank lanes carry sound beautifully — the whole floor of the town is a transmitting membrane over running water — and so does the copper. Two knuckles on a hip-height pipe in the fog-hall will be felt in four buildings. The works run on a code of about two hundred figures, learned by children as rhymes, and the town's only music is played the same way: felt through the boot soles, tapped into the planks at the crossings on the long dusks, a slow pattern that arrives in the bones of everyone standing in the street and in nobody's ears at all.

## The manners of the turnover

Twice a day, for four minutes, the engines move and the town stops.

Children are taught it as manners and grow out of it and come back to it around thirty without noticing they have. There is no rule. There is simply the fact that nobody in Thimble talks through the turnover, and the observable consequence — a people who can be interrupted twice daily for their whole lives without the slightest irritation, who can be halted mid-gesture and resume as though nothing had occurred. Lowland buyers find this restful for one day and unbearable by the third.

There is a second consequence, less obvious. In a town where every voice is a murmur at ear-range, there is no privacy in speech; you cannot be overheard, but you also cannot arrange not to be, since anyone close enough to hear you is close enough to see you. The turnover is the only noise the Lees has. So it is under the four-minute groan of the engines, standing still, that the hard things are said here: the proposals, the refusals, the news of a death, the one sentence someone has been carrying for eleven years. The town is full of people who associate the sound of a kilometre of moving metal with the worst and best minutes of their lives, and who go quiet at the ninth hour for reasons that have nothing to do with manners.

## Heat, and who sleeps against whom

Warmth is property. Every house is built around its pipe-room, and the pipe-room is a sleeping-shelf laid along the warm wall, and the order along that shelf is the household's whole social document. Smallest children at the hot end. The very old at the hot end also. The strong sleep on the outside, as the wall.

Grow up in that and touch means something different than it does to you. Hands go onto shoulders, necks, faces constantly, with no more freight than a glance — because to get someone's attention you must reach them, and because to check a person's warmth is to check their health, and the standard greeting in Thimble is a palm laid briefly at the side of another's throat. What they do instead of touch, for intimacy, is to give someone air: to hold your own breath while they speak, so that they may use the whole silence. That is the tender thing here. That is what a Thimble man does at his wife's sickbed — nothing, apparently, for a long time, while breathing very carefully.

## What they eat

Insects out of the cushion-domes, combed out in autumn and pressed to a dark grist that tastes of iron and keeps forever. Pith cakes from the soft trunks of the rosette-trees, which are farmed on a rotation of ninety years and planted by twelve-year-olds who will not see them cut. Grain, oil, salt and sugar up the cableway, in exchange for bales. Tea, endlessly, the water for it drawn from the pools it resembles.

And the caches. Everyone has bog-holes: peat is a larder that does not fail, and a family's butter, a family's grain, a family's sealed jars of last decade's grist go down into the cold acid dark with markers only that family reads. Consequently the moor is salted with forgotten food. A cutting crew opening a new race will find a firkin of butter three hundred years old, entirely edible, and the town will discuss for two turnovers whether to eat it, and then eat it, and say very little afterwards.

They do not eat the leaners. No one has ever produced a satisfying reason. A leaner found dead is carried — sometimes twenty kilometres, by four people, at the pace such a thing requires — to the southern rim, and given to the water.

## The tally

At last light every hut on the moor shows a lamp for a count of thirty. From the rim of the bowl, two people whose job this is watch the whole plateau and count lights, and the number is tapped down into the town on the pipes, and every person in Thimble hears it in the floor before they sleep.

If a light is missing, someone walks out. It may be four hours. They go anyway, in the dark, following a leaner path, because a leaner path across a bog has never once been wrong, and because the arithmetic of fifteen hundred people is such that no one here has ever had the luxury of a stranger.

## The bank, and the one who lays out

Whatever the cuttings turn up is laid on the bank: pollen-black wood, a bone pin, a mummified bird with its feet drawn up, a shoe, an axe of a design nobody uses, a coil of something that was rope.

There is an office for this, held for life, usually by someone with bad lungs and good hands. The layer-out cleans the find, sets it on the stone shelf that runs three hundred metres along the north wall of the works, and looks at it. Anyone may come and look. Anyone may make a guess. The guess is spoken once, aloud, to the layer-out, and is never written down — this is the rule, four centuries old and enforced by nothing but embarrassment — because a written guess would be kept, and this is a place where things that are kept do not go away, and they have decided that they would rather be ignorant than permanently wrong.
