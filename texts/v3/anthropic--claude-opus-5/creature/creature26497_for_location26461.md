## The Census

The first survey of the caldera lists nine vertebrates and stops, because the surveyor was cold-adapted, out of breath, and had four days. The list is not wrong. It is simply a list made by someone who assumed that the interesting inhabitants would be the ones that moved.

Here is the list, corrected.

---

## The Oldest Tenant

Start with the thing that is not on any list, because it does not look like an inhabitant. It looks like a color.

The mats that begin at seventy degrees and run down the staircase to the marsh are not two hundred separate populations in two hundred separate basins. They are one body. The films connect over every lip, thin as varnish across the sinter dams, thickening again in the pools below; there is no break anywhere in three kilometers. Genetically it is a smear rather than a creature — a consortium, a dozen lineages that stopped keeping their affairs separate a very long time ago — but functionally it is one continuous individual, and it is the oldest living thing on the mountain by a factor nobody has the tools to estimate. The forest is a recent arrival. The mat was here when the caldera floor was still glass.

It has no behavior except one, and the one is this: it holds its own temperature line, absolutely, at a tolerance of about half a degree. Its color is its position on the gradient. Orange is seventy-four. Olive is sixty-one. The rubbery brown is forty-eight. So when a vent shifts — when a channel armors itself shut and the hot water finds a new way out three hundred meters east — the mat does not die and regrow. It *migrates*, and it does so over the course of a season, and for that season the whole northern arc of the caldera visibly changes hue, orange creeping east across the terraces like a slow stain, the brown withdrawing before it.

Everything else that lives here reads that map. Some of them cannot read anything else.

## The Wrong-Way Animals

Every animal in the bowl has a bare belly.

You notice it once and then you cannot stop noticing it. The grazers, the frogs, the snakes, the small mammals in the marsh reeds, even the soarers four hundred meters up: dark, naked, thin-skinned underneath, and insulated on top. Fur, feather, scale, wax, all of it on the dorsal surface, where elsewhere the sun would be. Here the sky is the enemy. The sky is a two-kilometer draft. Warmth is a floor you lie down on, and the whole fauna has been rebuilt around the business of pressing itself against the ground and keeping the rain off its back.

From this one fact, everything else follows, and everything else is backwards.

**The frogs climb to cool off.** The bromeliad pools in the canopy run twelve to fifteen degrees below the marsh, which makes the treetops the coldest habitat available and therefore the refuge. Tadpoles develop up there, in the cold, in the air, forty tiny lakes to a tree, and when they descend to the warm mud to breed they are descending into a furnace they will not survive for more than a season or two. The life cycle runs down the temperature gradient, once, and ends at the bottom. Nothing goes back up.

**The snakes hunt cold.** Every pit organ on this mountain that came in from outside has been inverted or discarded. A heat-sensing predator here is a predator standing in a bonfire looking for a match. So the local snake — long, blunt, the color of wet sinter — has a facial pit that reports *negative* contrast, and what it is looking for is the cool silhouette of a frog that has just come down out of the canopy and has not yet warmed up. It has perhaps forty minutes to find that frog. After that the frog is the same temperature as the rock and becomes, to the snake, invisible.

**The bats are audible.** Water vapor eats ultrasound; at this humidity a bat calling at eighty kilohertz would be shouting into a wall of felt. The colony under the eastern lava ridge — tens of thousands of them, in caves so steamy the guano never dries and runs instead in warm black rivulets down to the marsh — has dropped its voice into the range you can hear. They come out of the steam caves at dusk clicking and knocking in a low wooden baritone, thousands of them at once, and it is the single most unnerving sound in the caldera, because it is the only sound here that is unmistakably being made *at* something.

**The birds sing at the pressure minimum** because that is when the vents exhale hardest, and the exhalation brings up the midges. Twenty minutes, all species together, the whole bowl at once, and then nothing.

## The Soarers

Eleven or twelve, always. The number has probably been eleven or twelve for ten thousand years, because the thermal will support that many and the food supply is fixed and there is nowhere to disperse to.

They are not really scavengers in the sense the word usually means. There are no large deaths here to wait around for. What they eat is what the terraces cook: the mountain's pools are traps, permanently baited with warmth, and every day some number of frogs and yearlings and confused insects go into water at sixty degrees and come out already prepared. The soarers work the terrace edges. They land, they lift, they eat parboiled things, and they take almost nothing that was not killed by temperature.

They nest on the borate flats in the dry western corner, the one square kilometer of the caldera that is not wet, and the nests are made of bones because there is no other dry material. White bone on white borate under a white sky. From the rim it looks like a scatter of litter.

And they land, between feeds, on the lip of the nine-meter chimney. Generations of them. The outward roll of that lip is precipitation, physics, exactly what the description says it is — but the *polish* on it is theirs, claw-worn, a hand's breadth of gloss you can see from two hundred meters when the light is low. It is the only mark any living thing in this caldera has left that could honestly be mistaken for workmanship, and it is on the one object that already looks built. The mountain has a sense of humor about this or it does not; there is no way to tell.

## What the Survey Called a Grazer

The first survey recorded *a cat-sized grazer, dense-boned, large-chested, browses algae at the terrace margins, no flight response.*

That animal is a yearling.

The adults were also present, in numbers, within fifty meters of where the note was written. The surveyor walked past nine of them and recorded them as sinter mounds.

### The crust

Here is what happens to the yearling.

It is born bare, pink-bellied, warm to the touch, quick for about eight months, and it spends that time on the low terraces in the forty-degree band, cropping mat. Then it starts to move upslope, and as it moves upslope it enters the spray.

Everything that sits in geyser spray gets coated.
