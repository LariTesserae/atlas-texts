## The Inhabitants

Begin with the thing that will keep tripping you: the plateau does not distinguish between its people and its animals, and neither does the language spoken beneath it. Down under the cloud lid, in the villages of the buttresses, there is one word for the woolled quadrupeds, the rock-hares, the Nailed, and the four figures on the eastern slope of the middle stump. The word means *the ones who have got there*. Grammatically it takes no plural. Herders will tell you this is because you cannot count them, and that is true, but it is not the reason. It takes no plural because there is only one condition up there, and everything on the plateau is a different distance into it.

So: the fauna and the folk are the same population. What follows is about the folk only because the folk are the part that arrived on purpose.

### Where they come from

Nobody is born on the plateau. Nothing is. Rule one forbids it, and rule one has no exceptions and never has had; there is not a single infant skeleton in the pavement, though there are ninety kinds of small skull in it, and there is not a single pregnancy among the herds — not one of those two-metre wombs holds anything, and the herders, who have very good hands, know this and do not like to discuss it.

Every person on the plateau is therefore an immigrant. Every single one. They came up from Below, from the world where the sun travels and there is an evening, and they came up in ones and twos across a span of time that the tallies were supposed to record and do not. Their clothing, where it survives, does not match: there are three weaves up there that no living loom can produce, a shoe of a design that is otherwise known from a single grave-find eight hundred kilometres away, and a bone comb whose tooth-spacing suits a hair type nobody on the buttresses has.

They have no name for themselves, collectively. They cannot have; a collective name requires that somebody say it to somebody else, and speech dies at ten metres.

### The window

You do not stop all at once. That is the mercy and it is also the whole of their civilisation.

The contagion works as advertised — no hunger by the third day, the knuckle by the fifth, the reasonableness of standing by the eighth — but the eighth day is not the end of movement. It is only the end of *urgency*. After it, you can still walk, still lift, still work; you simply find that you are no longer inclined to do anything you have not thought all the way through. Then the gaps between the inclinations get longer. A man who has been up for a year works about an hour a month, and works beautifully. A man who has been up for forty years does one thing a year, and it is exact.

This is why every artefact on the plateau is small, and finished, and better than it needs to be. The needles are polished to a gloss no hide requires. The mortarless cairns are laid so tight you cannot slip a frost-flake into a joint. The eleven-hundred-pace wall on the Pan is one metre high and dead level along the whole run, built by hands that had, by the end, all the time in the world and nearly none of the ability to hurry, and its purpose was to cast a shadow, which is the local word for *to say something permanently*.

And every tool up there is *set down*. Not dropped, not flung, not left where it fell. Set down, aligned, with its point away from you. Someone with a decade of foresight put that awl there. The coil of rope beside the hearth is spiralled with the tail tucked under, which is how you coil rope you expect never to use again.

There is exactly one exception, and it is worth the walk. In the shelter chipped between the fourth and fifth vertebrae of the Long One, on the swept floor, there is a hide with a single row of stitching across it, and the needle is halfway through a stitch, thread slack, held in a hand. Not resting in the cloth. Held. That is the only unfinished object on sixty kilometres of plateau, and it is the most human thing on it, and the stitch is going to be completed.

Nobody has found the broom.

### Thrift

Visitors call it paralysis, or peace, or trance, and all three are wrong. What the inhabitants are practising is economy.

Consider the frost. Wipe it off with your sleeve and it does not come back. Consider the felted insect crust: step on it and it yields, without sound, and does not rise again. Consider the stone you moved: it stays moved, and the seam it used to cast stays cut in the rock behind it, orphaned, a shadow with nothing to throw it. On this plateau nothing is replenished and nothing is repaired. You cannot deposit. You can only spend.

So they don't spend. Every motion up there costs something irreversible, and they have all arrived at a point in their lives where they have decided what they are willing to lose. That is not stillness for its own sake. That is a household budget.

Their etiquette follows from it and is the only law: do not enter another's shadow; do not touch frost; do not move a stone; do not walk on the crust where the crust is unbroken. A herder taught to walk properly up there walks in the track — because there is only one track, one boot wide, dulled and pressed into the crust and running from the Lip to the north cairn and out again, containing the footsteps of every visitor who has ever crossed, because the plateau has one crossing and lends it out. Every arrival treads the same damage. This is the plateau's entire written history and it is a record of what people broke.

### Names

An inhabitant's name is their seam.

Because they have stood in one place long enough for their shadow to cut the rock, each of them is attached to a groove, and the groove is a description with coordinates: *the one whose shadow crosses the third cairn at the shoulder*, *the one whose seam runs into the wall's seam and stops*. The names are precise, unforgeable, and pronounceable only by walking to them, which is a form of address the Below has no equivalent for and which, once you have done it, you will find you prefer.

They do not name each other; the seams do that. What they do instead is arrange themselves so that their seams *mean* something in relation to other seams — parallel, converging, deliberately just failing to touch. From the middle stump, with the light where it always is, you can read about four hundred metres of this before the crowd of the Standing confuses the syntax. It is not decipherable. It is very obviously syntax.

### Speaking, thinking

The hum is partly them.

Take the heartbeat at a decade, which is roughly right for the deep ones, and follow it down: a breath every few years, a blink not yet due, a thought at the pace of the bone-white lichen. A sentence, for someone forty years in, is a project. A conversation is architecture. Two of them facing each other three metres apart, mouths closed, eyes open, dust on the corneas — that is a discussion in progress and it has been in progress since the Below invented arithmetic and it is going *well*.

Which means you, on your eight-day visit, are not a person to them. You are weather. You flicker in at the edge of the frame, warm and appallingly fast, and you are gone before their eyelid has come halfway down. They see you the way you see a moth cross a lamp: as an event, not as company. If you shout, and stand close enough for the shout to survive, they will hear it as a click.

They are not, however, dying quickly. They are dying. This is the part the pilgrim literature omits. The Hour is an hour, and hours end; what is being held here is not eternity but the final stretch of a very long exhalation, and one of the reasons the country is named for a time of day is that a time of day is a thing that passes. The inhabitants are at the extreme end of a life, holding, with excellent budgeting, but holding.

### What they came to hold

The postures are the interesting part, because they were chosen.

Some chose sensibly: hands at sides, facing along the seams, a stance you could keep for geological time without regretting. Some chose the last good moment they had. There is a man on the northern scree with a broad-headed spear through him below the ribs, standing quite comfortably among the rock-hares, not bleeding — he arrived with about two minutes left in him and has spent nine centuries in the second before those two minutes started, and his face is untroubled, because pain requires a next instant and he has declined to have one. There is a woman holding out a cup at arm's length to a person who is eleven metres away and will never receive it, and the geometry of that is unbearable for about half a day and then becomes, in a way you will not enjoy noticing, admirable. There is one man tilted seven degrees off vertical with his weight entirely on the ball of his left foot, who mis-timed his stopping and has been beginning to fall since before the villages below had a word for the plateau. He is not going to land.

And there is a group of nine on the south apron, arranged in a ring facing inward, all of them mid-laugh. Not smiling. Laughing, teeth showing, one with her head back. If you have ever wondered what a joke sounds like when it is a thousand years long, the answer is: the hum, faintly, from that direction.

### The eight hands

The tallies on the boulder at the head of the Pan are in eight distinct hands and the totals never agree, and the marks have never changed, which means the disagreement is in the counters and not the count.

The herders' guild holds that they are eight people counting eight different things. The pilgrims hold that they are one person counting one thing, eight times, across eight stages of slowing, and that the strokes get finer and better and further apart as the hand loses its haste, which is observably true.
