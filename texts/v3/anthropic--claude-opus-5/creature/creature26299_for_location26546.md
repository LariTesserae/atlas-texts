## The Hung

They are small. Adult standing height is about a meter and a half, and standing is not a posture any of them favors. The mass they carry is the mass they can lift on one arm, and no more; a heavy person here is a person who has miscalculated. The chest is deep out of proportion to everything else, the ribs sprung wide and shallow-hinged, so that breathing is a long slow bellowing motion with very little visible effort — the same principle as their foundries, air husbanded and metered. Their blood holds oxygen the way blotting paper holds ink. A newcomer arrives gasping and stays gasping for a month; they watch this with the flat interest of people watching a machine run badly and wondering who will pay for the fuel.

The nose is the most elaborate organ they have. Inside it, a stacked labyrinth of thin bone leaves, cold at the tip and warm at the root, so that every exhalation gives its moisture back on the way out. Their breath is cool and dry and does not smell of anything. This is the mechanism that makes the whole civilization possible and it is entirely invisible, and they are aware of it in the way other peoples are aware of rainfall — they do not think about it, but there are words in the tap-code for a nose that is failing, and they are grave words.

They do not sweat. The skin is close-grained, nearly poreless, and they seal it anyway: every morning a film of rendered louse-wax worked over the whole body with the heel of the hand, and graphite over the wax, and then the polishing. They polish each other. It is done in pairs, with a leather pad, and it takes a long time, and it is the single most intimate act in the culture — more so than any other you might name. The result is that a citizen of the vug has exactly the same finish as a bulkhead or a bellows-frame: dark grey, faintly luminous, catching the ember-light in long soft highlights along the shin, the collarbone, the outside of the forearm. From any distance, in that light, you cannot tell a person from the city. This is not symbolic to them. It is simply what a clean surface looks like.

Not sweating leaves them with a heat problem they solve architecturally. Warmth pools in the low galleries where the mountain's own heat comes up through the seams, and cold sits under the apex, and every worker's day is a movement between the two. Foundry crews work half-shifts and then climb to the ceiling districts to lie in the cold with their limbs spread wide — fingers splayed, feet flexed, palms turned out — dumping heat through the long fine vasculature of the extremities. Newcomers read this posture as surrender, or worship, or death. It is a coffee break.

---

The eyes are what you would notice if you could see them properly, and in the vug you mostly cannot. They are very large, very dark, with a mirrored layer behind that gives back a dim bronze coin of reflection when a lamp swings past. They are also fragile. Foundry light burns them out. Every furnaceman goes silver-eyed in the end, and silver-eyed is an honorable condition, and a silver-eyed elder is fed and polished by strangers as a matter of course.

Which produces the strangest institution the city has. The seam-watchers cannot be allowed near the arc-glow, not once, not for a second, because a wall's strain-flicker is fainter than a firefly and only a wholly unspoiled eye will catch it early enough to matter. So they are taken as infants and raised in the unlit galleries. They grow up in absolute dark, handled and tapped at and spoken to at a range of three centimetres, and their eyes come in enormous, and their skin, ungraphited, stays the colour of unfired clay. They are called *the unlit*, and there are perhaps two hundred of them, and they are the only citizens who are never made to look like the city. They know the vug entirely by hand and by tap and by the green fire on the quartz, which they alone have seen properly. They are also the only people with the authority to end a district. When the strain runs and a pin-caller shouts down the line to cut a tenement loose, the order originates in a black room with a pale person in it who has never once seen the thing they are destroying. Nobody finds this uncomfortable. They find it obvious.

---

Their hands are the point of them. Long-palmed, heavily calloused across the whole gripping surface, the fingerpads worn to leather. Their feet are hands too — a long divergent first toe, a strong hook, the sole thickened into a pad. A citizen at rest keeps one foot wrapped around something. They fall asleep hooked. Infants are taught grip before they are taught anything else, and the first grief of any household is the infant that will not grip.

Every person carries a striker: a rod of hardened copper or a good dense fragment of spar, on a thong at the wrist, worn smooth as a worry-stone. Speech at conversational distance is a dry consonantal whispering, all clicks and dentals and tongue-percussion, because those are the sounds that survive in air this thin; vowels are almost vestigial and mostly serve as grammar. Everything longer than two sentences is tapped. Every structural member in the city is a line, and every citizen is fluent, and the practical effect is that nobody in the vug has ever been out of earshot of everybody else. There is no privacy in the acoustic sense. There is only the courtesy of not listening, which is elaborate, and codified, and about as reliable as such courtesies are anywhere.

The jaw carries the mark of it. By twenty, every adult has a polished patch on the point of the mandible — a small shining callus where the bone has been pressed against steel and stone tens of thousands of times. It is the first thing a mother checks on an adolescent, and the last thing they check on a corpse.

Some of them cannot stop listening. Press the jaw to a main stanchion and the entire city comes in at once through the teeth: the tick and clatter of nine thousand people, the treadmills, the drills working somewhere out in the rock, and underneath it all the mountain, groaning in a register that has no top and no bottom and does not stop. There is a word for the people who go to a stanchion and do not come away — *the stone-drunk*. They are found in a week, still hooked on, desiccated, weighing almost nothing. Their families are not shamed by it. It is understood to be a thing the mountain does, and not a thing the person did.

---

Kinship is not blood and it is certainly not place, since no structure stands where it stood five years ago. Kinship is *pin-right*. A family is the set of people entitled to hang from a particular pin, and a pin is a stamped iron spike driven into a seam by an ancestor, and the register of pins — a wall of stamped tags in the assembly hall — is the only permanent document in the culture. Everything else they write is graphite on mica and is polished off and used again. Their history is a palimpsest. Their genealogy is ironmongery.

So a household is portable and an identity is not. A family can be evacuated, carried away in units one person can lift, and reassembled two hundred metres downwall on the same pin, and it is the same house. To lose pin-right — for debt, for building a *grave*, for cutting a stranger's cable in a scramble — is the whole of their idea of punishment. The cut go down to the floor, where the scree shifts, and live among the shard-folk who glean the fallen prisms and go first into every fresh break, and who have their own etiquette and their own tap-dialect and their own long memory. Everyone above needs them. Nobody above will be polished by them.

Names are knocks: three or four beats, given at the age when a child first hangs something that holds. The spoken version of a name is a transliteration and is considered slightly babyish, the way a nickname is.

Their dead do not rot. There is no water in which rot could happen. A body dries in a month and comes to weigh what a coil of cable weighs, and the custom is to wire it to the underside of the family platform, in order of arrival, so that a household walks around all its life above a rack of its ancestors, and the children learn the lineage by climbing under and touching. After a generation or so the oldest is taken down and ground and spread on the mats, and the mats are grazed by the lice, and the lice are roasted and eaten. They say it without any solemnity at all: *the wall feeds us and we feed the wall*. It is not a sacrament. It is inventory.

---

They have one word for what other people would have to split into catastrophe and windfall. It is a short flat dental syllable and it means, more or less, *lift*. There has been a lift; the mountain gained a metre in the night and the ceiling districts came down and a new vein opened in the break and there will be copper for a decade. Someone's grandmother was lifted. Someone's cousin was lifted and is now the richest smelter on the eastern wall. Both sentences use the same word and neither carries a tone. When outsiders press them on this — and outsiders do — they become genuinely puzzled, and then bored, in the manner of someone asked to justify the concept of Tuesday.

Traders come up through the old drill-bores from the warm country below, three or four times a year: big, loud, wet-eyed, sweating visibly, appallingly wasteful of air. Children queue to smell them. That is not a joke and it is not really a game either. The visitors smell of water, and the smell is unmistakable and unreproducible and the children are taken to have it once, the way they are taken to the Cold Face once, and most of them decide that they have now smelled the outside and require no more of it.

And a few — the drill-followers, the ones who go alone into the seams for weeks with a striker and a sack of pressed mat, tracking a boring animal by the thud of its mouthparts through a hundred metres of rock — come back with something else. They say that out past the last worked vein there is a rhythm that is not a drill and is not the city. Slower than either. Patient in a way that a machine is not. The unlit have a code-group for a light on the quartz that is not strain-light, and they are trained never to send it, and once or twice in a generation one of them sends it anyway, and the pin-callers cut the district and everyone moves, and afterwards nobody discusses what was cut or why.

They will tell you the mountain is not aware of them. They will tell you this without being asked.
