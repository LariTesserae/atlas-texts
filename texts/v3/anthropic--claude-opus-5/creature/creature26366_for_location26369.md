# Who Lives in the Warm Cistern

## The census, first, because it is short and misleading

Six loach, sometimes seven. Some hundreds of shrimp, all of them within a hand's breadth of the pump's mouth. Three separate installations of weed, of which one is a single individual older than any nation. One snail. Seasonal midges. One spider, annually replaced.

That is the whole list, and if you take it as the answer you will have missed the tenant entirely — the way a man who inventories the furniture of a house has not met the family. But start there. Everything in the room is honest. Nothing is hiding. It is only that the important thing is distributed.

## The shrimp, who live in a country the size of a soup bowl

Under a centimetre each, transparent, with a hot orange thread of gut showing through, so that the crowd at the discharge looks less like animals than like a slow orange snowfall going the wrong way. They are born in the scalding two litres and they die there. A very few are pushed out into the standing plume and cool and are eaten by loach on the way down; those are the only shrimp who ever learn that the room has a middle.

For the rest, the Cistern is a black iron cliff of moving parts, ringed by a warm shelf, ringed by an inedible cold that goes on forever. Their calendar has one unit: four and a half seconds. They moult on it. They mate on it. When the day heats and the stroke quickens by three percent, the entire population accelerates by three percent, invisibly and completely, like a held chord tightening.

They groom the beam's pivot with the fussiness of monks polishing a rail nobody uses. They do not know they are keeping the machine alive. There is no version of a shrimp that could know it. But the fact remains: the oldest continuously operating device on the mountain has been maintained for nine thousand years by an animal with no memory, which does the work because the work is edible, and which would do it just as thoroughly if the pump were a corpse. The relationship is not care. It is closer to digestion that happens to leave its host in better repair than it found it.

## The loach, who are a budget rather than a rule

Six, sometimes seven, never eight. It reads like a law. It is only arithmetic.

The room receives so much light down the shaft and so much heat up through the floor, and this converts into so much weed, and the weed supports so much grazing, and when you have subtracted the shrimp's take and the snail's take, what remains will feed six pale bottom-feeders of that size and no more. Eggs are laid. Eggs are eaten, mostly by the shrimp, who eat anything that arrives in the hot water, and by the snail, who is indiscriminate. A fry survives to fingerling roughly when a slot opens. The population is not fixed by anything so grand as fate. It is fixed the way the number of people in a lifeboat is fixed.

They are, nonetheless, the closest thing in the room to persons. The eyes are vestigial and sealed under skin, but the lateral line is superb, and what they are reading is not water so much as the pump: the flat clank arrives as a wall, the intake's hiss as a long slope away from it, and between those two signals a blind fish can locate itself in the bowl to within a few centimetres, permanently, without effort, the way you know where your own hands are. To be a loach here is to live inside a sentence of four and a half syllables, spoken without pause since the invention of speech.

At night they lie along the hot outflow in a row, touching, in water far too warm to be good for them. They do not need it. Their ancestors came up a mountain in a bucket, in a temperature that must have been terrifying, and something in the lineage still lines up at the hot place and lies down.

## The weed, and the one part of it that is an individual

The mats on the bronze grating are a slum: mixed, layered, half-dead underneath, a reef by courtesy. The tide-line fringe is a thin renewable film, grazed as fast as it grows, and it exists mainly to be eaten.

The rope up the east wall is a different order of thing. Thick as a wrist, red under green, holding by a foot of calcified tissue that the wall has grown over and taken in — it is not attached to the masonry, it is *jointed* to it, and neither could now be removed without breaking. It has no root, no need of one; it drinks the room. Its upper metre is young. Its lower centimetres cannot be aged because nothing that could age them has survived. Assume it is as old as the standing water. Assume it was put here.

It leans toward the light column over the course of the day — visibly, if you have three hours and nothing else to do. Not a movement. A very slow steering. If attention is the act of orienting toward something for longer than is convenient, then the rope is the most attentive object in the room, and has been paying attention to the same thing, once daily, for eight thousand years, and has never once been told what it is looking at.

## The spider, who is a stranger every year

The only dry inhabitant. Black, slow with cold, up in the dome's shade on brick that has never been wet. Its web is bad. It does not need to be good: the draught that pulls the midge-blizzard up out of the eye also brings a percentage of them back down dead, and the web is simply the place where that percentage lands.

It is the one creature here with a view of the entire room, and the one creature here with no interest in it. It freezes each winter. Another arrives each spring — blown up, presumably, on a thread, from a valley that does not know this place exists. So the highest and driest seat in the oldest building on the mountain is occupied, in every generation, by an immigrant who will die of the altitude before it can learn where it is.

The room breathes out midges. It breathes some of them back in. The spider is the mechanism by which the Cistern reabsorbs its own exhalation, and the spider is a tourist.

## The snail

Here is where the accounting fails, and where the actual tenant starts to show.

Everything else in the room is explained by a shrug and a calorie count. The snail is not. It is expensive: a turban shell the size of a thumb-joint, plated in red copper laid down out of the water and then burnished by nine thousand years of contact with a wall, so that for about eleven minutes a day, when the edge of the light column swings far enough over to graze the west arc of the tide-line, the animal — if it happens to be standing in that arc — ignites. Not glows. Ignites: a coal in a pink shell of a room. It has been set on fire in this way perhaps a hundred thousand times and has never altered its pace by a second.

Its pace is this. It grazes the four-finger band at chest height, algae below the tide-line, moss above, and it walks a circuit of the room — a little over fourteen metres — in the time it takes the surface to fall its eleven centimetres and come back. One breath of the room, one lap. It goes always the same way round. It does not use the floor. It does not use the reef. It has one road and the road is a closed circle and it has walked it something like a hundred thousand times.

Put your cheek flat to the wall in the last of the afternoon and sight along the band. You will see the road. Three centimetres wide, faintly brighter than the surface above and below it, going away from you in both directions and meeting itself somewhere behind your head. It is the second sharpest feature in the room. The sharpest is the tide-line, and the tide-line is sharp partly because the snail keeps shaving it.

Now: the band where the surface is subtly different, the one that might be the last blurred residue of writing. It is at chest height. It is the same band. Whatever was cut into this wall, the snail has been standing on it since before the cutting was forgotten, and the reason the inscription is illegible is not the water. It is a twelve-gram animal, replaced when it dies, walking the same fourteen metres a hundred thousand times with a foot like a wet tongue.

But the letters were cut, and cut things hold harder lime in their grooves, and the road is not perfectly smooth. It stutters. Seventeen places — I counted, over four days, and I would not swear to seventeen — where the surface roughens for a few millimetres and the snail slows, and then goes on. The same seventeen places every lap. It cannot read. There is no faculty in it capable of the concept. It simply cannot cross those spots at speed, and so it doesn't, and so its gait around the room is not uniform: it is a rhythm, with seventeen hesitations in a fixed order, closing on itself once a month.

That is where the inscription is. Not on the wall — the wall lost it. The only surviving copy of whatever somebody thought was worth carving into the top of a mountain they had built by hand is a limp in the walk of a snail, and it has been faithfully transmitted, without comprehension, through some hundreds of illiterate copyists, because the road teaches every new snail the same way it taught the last: by being difficult in the same seventeen places.

When one dies, another appears. Nobody has seen a second. I suspect there is no mystery: the mats on the grating certainly hold juveniles too small to notice, and the road is a single-occupancy job — one band, one animal's worth of grazing, an economy of one. A vacancy is filled from below. Within a few laps the new tenant has the limp. It never knew there was a limp to have.

## The tenant

So. Not the fish, not the shrimp, not the guest in the dome.

The thing that lives in the Warm Cistern is the Warm Cistern. It has water for blood, and the water has never been anywhere else, has never been in a cloud or a river or the sea, has only ever been in this room and in the bodies of this room, so that every loach is made of water that was previously a shrimp, that was previously the rope, that was previously a loach, at a depth of recursion that stopped being interesting eight thousand years ago. It has an iron heart in the west wall that cannot finish and cannot fail, and the heart is kept clean by something like enzymes, which are the shrimp, and which believe they are farmers. It has a mouth in the ceiling that it hums through, and it breathes out midges on warm afternoons and takes a little back down. Once a month it inhales and exhales eleven centimetres. Once a year the mouth closes with ice and the whole thing goes dark and warm and stirred and deaf, and that — sealed, humming, blind, still working — is not the Cistern shut down. That is the Cistern at rest, which is to say the Cistern in its ordinary condition, of which the summer with its light column and its blizzards is only the excited interruption.

And it has memory: one item, badly stored, in an unreadable format, endlessly recopied at cost, walking around and around the inside of its own head at two centimetres an hour.

## Which is why the visitors sleep

A very small number of people have come up over the last terrace with cracked lips, not knowing what they were climbing, and put their heads through the eye, and found six white fish and a pink room and a machine going about its business, and every one of them has lain down on the wet brick beside the opening in howling noise at six thousand metres and gone out like a lamp.

They will tell you they don't know why. I think the nervous system is not fooled. Four and a half seconds is a pulse. The note of the eye is breath. The room is warm, the room is enclosed, the room is loud in the specific manner of being inside something enormous and alive and entirely uninterested in you, and there is only one situation in a human life that resembles it, and in that situation the correct response is to sleep.

They wake, and go down, and afterwards each of them says the same two things without knowing anyone else has said them. *It is the oldest thing I have ever been inside.* And: *it was so loud, and I fell asleep immediately.*

Two lines. Fixed order. Carried out of the room by bodies that never learned to read them, repeated without variation by copyists who have never met.
