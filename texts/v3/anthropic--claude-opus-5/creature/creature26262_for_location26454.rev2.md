## The People of the Bowl

They are not impressive to look at, the first time, because the first time you see them they are crossing.

Everyone crossing looks the same: hooded, head down, one shoulder forward, walking fast in short strides with the weight kept low, a hand out to trail the wall if there is a wall. The gait has a name below the rim — the valley towns call it *the Hush step* and use it to pick plateau people out of a crowd at fifty yards, years after they've come down. It is a walk that has had every ounce of swagger shaken out of it. There is no reason to swing your arms when your arms are your mouth and your mouth is shut, and no reason to lift your chin when there is nothing in the loud air worth lifting it for. They move across the polished pans the way people move through cold rain: efficiently, without resentment, without the slightest interest in you.

Then they reach a node, and step in over its unmarked edge, and stop, and push the hood back, and take out the plugs — and the whole body opens. The shoulders come down. The chin comes up. The face, which was a shuttered thing thirty seconds ago, begins to work.

---

**Faces and hands.** Their faces are the grammar of their language and their hands are its vocabulary, and both are athletic in a way that unsettles visitors. A Hush conversation is conducted with raised and lowered brows, with the corners of the mouth, with a repertoire of about nine distinct nose-wrinkles, and with puffed or hollowed cheeks that mark aspect and doubt. To an outsider it looks like a nation of hams. Portraits painted below the rim always miss it; a Hush sitter holding still for an hour is a Hush sitter with the language switched off, and the faces in those canvases have a slack, drowned quality that their families find obscurely insulting.

The hands are the real vanity. Nobody up there cares much about hair, and cosmetics rot in the wet air, but hands are oiled nightly, pumiced, kept out of the lye. Rings are worn on the thumb only, where they distort nothing. The marriage mark is a small tattoo on the inner wrist, visible only in the two or three signs that turn the palm inward — so that a married person is continually, accidentally, flashing the fact at intimate moments in ordinary sentences, which is considered the whole point. Foundry burns and crush injuries are spoken of as speech impediments. There is a word, *scarred-tongued*, for a man whose right hand no longer makes a clean sign, and a small and rather tender body of etiquette about how to wait for him.

The other vanity is the hood lining. Hoods themselves are undyed felt, uniformly drab, because a hood that catches the eye out on the loud ground is a hood belonging to somebody who wants to be noticed by strangers, and that is a moral failing. The inside, though — the inside is silk if the family can get silk, embroidered inch by inch over a winter, red and gold and green, saints and eels and lists of ancestors and jokes — and it is worn four inches from the wearer's own eyes, in the dark, for hours a day, and nobody else ever sees it. It is the most decorated textile on the plateau and it is essentially private. Ask to look and you will be shown, politely, once, and the asking will be remembered.

---

**Voices.** They sound, to the valley, like kettles. The note eats the bottom of the register — anything sung or spoken low simply dissolves into the plateau's own voice — so eleven thousand years and eight hundred generations of speech have climbed. Hush is a language of sibilants and stops, front vowels, clicks borrowed from somewhere and naturalized, pitched high and cut short. Women's and men's voices are nearly the same. Below the rim, plateau people are mistaken for children on the far side of a door.

Their hearing has the matching shape. Every physician in the valley knows the audiogram: a clean deep notch centred on the note and a second, shallower one on its first partial, and then above that a sensitivity so fine it is nearly a nuisance. They can hear a cistern seep three rooms away. They can hear a pump bearing going bad a week before it goes. They cannot hear the Bell at all, in the sense of hearing; it has been subtracted, the way you cannot see your own nose. A Hush man taken down to the coast will lie awake his first night appalled by the silence, and will say, over and over, that something has stopped.

And between intimates there is the bone-talk: forehead to forehead, jaw to jaw, one hand cupped over the joint. Children learn it in the crook of a parent's neck before they learn to sign. Lovers use it in the street, which is legal and universal and looks, to a visitor, like two people in a public place having an experience that ought to be indoors. The dying are spoken to that way, always, because a dying person's plugs are taken out and the room is quiet and the words have to go in through the skull, past whatever else is happening. There is a phrase for the last thing you say to someone this way — *through the bone* — and it means a thing said with no possibility of being misheard, and no possibility of being overheard, and no possibility of taking it back.

---

**How they are made.** A child is born in the white house in the deepest hollow, on the only lawn, and for the first year it is carried in the quiet and never taken across a ridge. Crying is useless — a baby's cry doesn't carry six feet out there — so infants are worn against the sternum and read by squirm, and the sign for *weeping* is made on the cheek and translates better as *leaking*. Mothers rock at the slack. Everybody rocks at the slack. Every cradle, every mourner, every drunk swaying against a wall in the Salt Quarter is swaying at five and a half seconds, and none of them know it, and outsiders find the city's music unendurable: six notes in a minute, struck on glass and wire, high and thin and patient, with the whole pulse of the plateau underneath doing the work a drum would do anywhere else.

At three, a child is taken to a plug-cutter and an impression is made of both ears in warm wax, and the first fitted plugs are turned and given, on a cord, and this is the naming. The plugs are recast every two or three years as the ear grows; the old ones are kept; a grown woman's string of nine or ten little clay ears, sized from acorn to thumb, hangs by her bed and is buried with her. Losing your plugs is the standard catastrophe of childhood, the thing every folk tale starts with.

At seven or eight comes the crossing — the first supervised walk out over loud ground, forty yards of polished pan between two hollows, hand in an adult's hand. Everyone remembers it. It is the day you learn that the world has a shape you cannot see, that the ground is a map of something enormous, and that the adults have known where the edges are this whole time and can feel them coming in their chests. After that, the child is taught to read the wave the way children elsewhere are taught to read weather: where the nodes are thinning, where they've walked since the last survey, which streets have gone bad, which garden will be a loud lot in ten years and is therefore cheap and a bad idea.

They grow up slow, and slowness is the plateau's chief virtue. The word for a hasty person is the sign for *beetle* — the dun thumb-sized thing that sits out on the pans vibrating. It is used of drunks, of the devout, and of anyone who goes into the loud on purpose and stays there, and it is not entirely an insult; *beetle* is also what they call a mystic, and what they call a man who has decided not to come home.

---

**The trades.** Some of them are simply a city's trades, done damp: smiths and clerks and midwives and bad poets. But the plateau has made four kinds of people that exist nowhere else.

There are the **wading crews**, who go out in diving order into the antinodes, filled with water to the collarbone, to nurse the resonator halls. They walk in a line, knotted to a rope, and they never hurry, and they come back deaf in the specific pattern that pensions a man at forty. Out there the low pressure shakes the eyeball in its socket and the world smears at the edges, and every crew that ever worked the halls has seen figures standing off in the mist where no figure should be. The protocol is written into the guild book: *do not count the line*. You hold the rope, you feel the knots, you do not turn your head and you do not count. Nobody in the Pitch Office believes in the fifth man. Every single wader does.

There are the **lifters**, the moving-masons, whose entire trade is the disassembly and re-erection of houses. Every stone in Hush is numbered, and renumbered each time it moves, so that a good lintel carries eleven sets of marks up its face like the rings of a tree, and a family's wealth is counted not in land — land walks away — but in stones, and in the right to a position in the wave. The deeds are written to follow the node. Half the litigation in the city concerns where, exactly, a hollow currently *is*, and the other half concerns what a man is owed when it leaves him.

There are the **surveyors**, who go out with hounds. The hound is the instrument: low, silent, built like a draught-excluder, bred for four hundred years to be a living node-detector, and utterly bound to its own hollow. A surveyor walks the edge and reads the dog — the ears, the crouch, the place where it will not go — and marks the boundary in chalk, and the Pitch Office redraws. A good hound never leaves the quiet in its life. The quiet simply moves, a hand's breadth a year, and takes the dog with it, and the dog never knows it has travelled.

And there are the **flat-born**: the ones born on loud ground, in the old quarters, on the road, in the bad years before the white house — who never learned to hear, and for whom the Bell is not sound and never was. In a city where everyone signs, deafness costs less than it costs anywhere else on earth, and the flat-born are not pitied. They are simply the people who can go where others can't, hoodless, unplugged, upright, at normal walking speed. They carry messages. They work the hall-mouths. They navigate by counting — they are prodigious counters, every one of them, pacing the city in memorised geometry — and they are the only citizens who cross the pans looking around them at the view. A flat-born messenger strolling across a polished lobe with her hands in her pockets, in the middle of the note, is one of the sights of the plateau. It is also the single thing that most reliably makes a visitor understand where he is.

There is, in the Office archives, a locked series of volumes concerning the two centuries when certain families arranged their confinements out on the loud ground on purpose, and got, by that means, sons who could work. It is illegal now. It has been illegal for six hundred years. Nobody discusses it, and everybody knows which guilds are old.

---

**What else lives there.** The beetles drink. Their backs are fluted, and the loudest ground makes the most mist, and they sit in the worst of it with their elytra spread, fog beading and running down the grooves to the mouth, vibrating like struck things, perfectly content. The hounds live one hollow each. The eels in the warm seeps navigate by touch and taste and, once a year, in the early autumn, run — down the drains, out into the brackish sheet, across the knee-deep water in a pale hurrying carpet, and over the lip into the Throat. Nothing comes back up but steam. The city treats the eel-run as a festival and eats the ones it catches and does not ask the obvious question.

The dead go the same way. They are carried at night to the edge of the sheet water by four people in silence and given to the current, and slide out across the shallows, and are gone. Afterwards the family stands on the polished ground and listens. There is an old and disreputable profession of **listeners** who will attend for a fee and tell a widow which part of the note her husband has become. The Pitch Office loathes them. The shrines tolerate them. Nobody has ever been able to stop them, because what they are selling is not really a lie: everyone up there does, in fact, believe that the Bell is made of what has fallen into it, and that it has been getting larger and lower for eleven thousand years for exactly that reason.

---

**And what they are waiting for.** The shaft is widening. The note is dropping. Twice a month a clerk carries a fork and a bowl of water out to the marked stone and writes a number in a ledger that is longer than everything else the city has ever written down, and the numbers go one way only.

They know what that means. It is the one thing in Hush that every faction agrees on and interprets differently. The shrine-keepers hold that the Bell is a single syllable being spoken very slowly, and that the falling pitch is the sound of a word ending. The Pitch Office holds that it is rock and water. The old quarrel is not about the fact but about the feeling: whether to dread the silence or to want it. There are congregations on the eastern edge who pray for the Throat to hold, and there are the beetle-sects out west in the dead roofless quarters — the shell-dwellers, mostly old, mostly deaf, living in drained houses with green-furred walls in the loud ground the city abandoned — who sit out on the pans at evening with their plugs on a cord around their necks, unused, waiting for the end of the note the way other people wait for morning.

Between them, the forty thousand: lying in warm wet stone rooms under damp blankets in the hollows of a shape none of them has ever seen, not listening, exactly. Entrained. A whole people breathing on a five-and-a-half-second cycle, moving their city a fingernail a year across the floor of a drum, keeping the ledger, oiling their hands, embroidering the insides of their hoods — unhurried, unshoutable, and quite certain that they will be the ones to find out how the sentence ends.
