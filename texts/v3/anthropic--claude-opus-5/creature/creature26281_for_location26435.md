# The Wrights

You have already stood in a doorway. Probably you knelt in it, to photograph the flat rainbow lying in the fog ring at the base of a flue tree, and you noted the mud, the moss, the warmth, the astonishing richness of that little dark collar of ground in all that blinding frost, and you wrote down *garden, spa, metropolis*, and you did not once look up the inside of the tree.

That is the standard error. Everyone looks for houses on the ground. On the Loft there is no ground to spare; the ground is a mattress of tenants four deep and there is not a square centimetre of it that is not already spoken for. The only unoccupied volume on the entire plateau — the only *interior*, the only hollow, the only room — is the inside of a flue.

Somebody lives in there.

## The Hollow

A mature flue is a chimney forty metres tall, seventy centimetres of bore at the base narrowing to forty at the crown, with a pulpy check-valve at the bottom that opens on the exhalation of the herds and slams on a pressure surge. Inside it the warm made air of the plateau is drawn upward continuously: nine to eleven degrees, saturated, oxygen-enriched twice over, first by the bellowers' intestinal symbionts and again by whatever the herds have been grazing. The inner wall is corrugated, wet, and condensing.

It is the warmest place in the world, the richest air in the world, the darkest place in the world, and it is the only place in the world where nothing is shining at you.

The wall inside is furred with a pale felt — a fungus that eats the herds' breath and has never needed a pigment in its life. This is the one white living thing on the Loft. Everything out under the glare is coal-black to the last thallus; everything up the chimneys is bone-white, and both facts have the same cause, which is that light here is not a blessing but a weather condition.

The felt is cropped. If you go up a kept flue you will find the wall grazed in bands, harvested and left, harvested and left, in a rotation that runs the height of the tree on a cycle of about two hundred days; and you will find the borer-grubs picked out of the bark and the ice-plugs broken and the creeping edge of the mattress cut back from the valve seat, and, wedged in a fork of the inner buttressing about six metres up with its throat against the wood, listening, the tenant.

Surveyors below the rim call them wrights, which is a joke that has outlived the person who made it. There is no made thing on the Loft. There are the wrights.
