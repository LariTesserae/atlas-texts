# The Ones in the Quiet

## Correction to the Census

The account is right about animals. It is right in the way a man is right who says a room is empty because nothing in it is moving.

Three things live in the basin. A fourth is argued about. The fifth is the reason for the other four, and is not alive in any sense a surveyor would accept, and is the largest inhabitant by a wide margin.

Begin with the largest.

## The Moss

There is one moss. Not one species — one individual.

It occupies some two hundred and eighty of the three hundred discs and it is, so far as anyone can section and match it, a single clone: the same organism, cut into pieces by four square miles of noise, holding its pieces in the only places where the pressure permits a filament to stay anchored to anything. It cannot fruit. It has no spores anyone has caught. It moves by being carried — a smear on a heel, a crumb caught in the weave of a felt cap, a green thread combed out of hair on the far side of the plain and dropped in the wet.

Which means that the moss, at some point in a duration nobody kept, acquired legs.

It grows a finger's depth in a lifetime and it holds the grey flour together, and where it holds, the sedges root, and where the sedges root, there is flour to grind, and where there is flour to grind there are people, and the people track moss between the pits. That is the whole of the economy. Everything else in the basin is weather.

The shape of this organism, mapped, is the shape of the vanished engine. It has grown itself into a diagram of a machine it never touched, and it maintains that diagram with an accuracy no instrument in the basin can improve upon, because the diagram is simply where it is possible to be.

## The Threads

At Pumphouse Four, in the blood-heat outflow, the pale eyeless things go on swimming upstream.

They are farmed. Below the natural spillway there are eleven terraces of broken tile, laid by hand, silted and re-laid, each one slowing the water a little more, and the terraces are older than any wear-mark anyone has thought to measure. Whoever built the first of them was doing what everyone does now: watching the only things in the world that move because they want to.

Half the people eat them. Half will not.

This is the closest thing the basin has to a religion, and it is a dispute that cannot be shouted, which means it has never been settled and never will be. A quarrel that cannot be conducted at volume has to be conducted by living differently instead. So there are threadeating households and there are households that carry the threads down from the terraces in cupped tile and pour them back above the spillway, over and over, all their lives, and the two kinds of household intermarry, and the children choose at about nine, and nobody has ever recorded a fight about it. There is no mechanism for a fight. You can point at a thing, and you can walk away from a thing, and in a pit you can say twelve words about it, and then the phase turns and the water rises and you are both wet and cold and it is time to move.

## The People

Fewer than four hundred. Never more, and — as far back as the tooth-record runs — never appreciably fewer.

That is not a coincidence and they know it isn't. The pits are fixed, the sedges are fixed, the fungus shelves are fixed, and the arithmetic of the standing wave has settled the population of this place the way it has settled everything else: at a number so worn-in that it can no longer wear. They are the last moving part of a transmission that transmits nothing. They are also, plainly and completely, people.

**Bodies.** Short, wide in the foot, heavy through the shoulder, the skin waxed to a dull amber with grease cut from the bluff and worked in daily until the water beads off it. Hair matted flat and greased into a cap. Under the grease they are pale as the fungus. Their eyes take in the dim without effort and are ruined for the Arc-Front: nobody looks west on purpose, and the children who do are told, by a flat hand turned over, that it is stupid.

Their teeth are worn flat by the grit in the sedge flour, evenly, at a rate that has been the same for as long as there have been teeth here, and they read each other's ages off their molars the way they read the plain off a bolt-stump. It is the only measurement they take of a person. Asking a stranger's age is done by touching your own front teeth. It is not rude.

**Ears.** Plugged from infancy with grease-soaked insulation fibre, packed by a mother's thumb, replaced twice a day, and over that a felt cap laced under the jaw. Nobody in the basin has ever heard the Drone properly and everyone in the basin has felt it every second of their life. Even so, by thirty the top of the range is gone. Their spoken language uses only the sounds that survive that loss: low vowels, hard stops, no sibilants at all. To a visitor it is the sound of two men arguing on the other side of a wall.

They speak perhaps four hours a month.

**Hands.** The real language is the forearm and the flat of the hand, and it is a contact language as much as a visual one: in the dark you take the speaker's wrist and read the tendons. That is why they walk in pairs, forearm in forearm, and why a person walking alone across the flats is understood to be either in mourning or in disgrace, and why the sight of one is enough to make a whole household stop grinding and look up.
