## I. The one who is at home

Start with the tenant, because everything else in the bowl is arranged around it the way a household is arranged around the person who does not have to explain themselves.

It is about the length of your arm and forearm together, and it does not look like anything with a name. Honey along the flanks, sooting to near-black at the mask, the feet, the last third of the tail. The coat is coarse and always slightly wet and lies in whorls, and in strong light there is a green sheen in the black that is not pigment but structure. The head is broader than a marten's and the eye is too large for it, wet and forward-facing and unhurried. There is loose skin at the throat. There is something wrong with the way it walks, which is that it does not seem to be in the same relationship with gravity as the rest of us: it flows down a root the way water finds a gutter, and then stops in the middle of a movement and holds there for eleven or twelve seconds, one forefoot lifted, apparently thinking, and then goes on.

The plateau herders have a word for it, and the word does not mean *weasel* or *cat* or *thief*. It translates, awkwardly, as *the one who is at home*. Not the owner. Not the guest. The one who is indoors while you are outdoors.

Its hollow is the eastern fig, second buttress, a dry chamber above the bat-stink where a limb tore out some centuries back. It sleeps there through the loud part of the night and comes out at the turn of the mist, and then sleeps again in full sun on an open root in the middle of the bowl, on its side, flank rising, in front of anything that cares to look at it. It is not brave. Bravery would require some notion that there is an alternative.

Someone once sat a full season on the stone bench with a tally-stick and came away with numbers, and the numbers are the strangest thing about it. Nine swifts a week, plucked out of the dusk funnel with a lunge off the wax bark. A bat every third night. A frog most days, more in the wet. A moth when it is bored. It never gorges. It never caches. It never fails, and it never tries twice: if the first lunge misses, it sits down and grooms and lets the column go by. There is no hunger in it at all. There is a *rate*.

Nobody has seen one arrive more than three times in the memory of anyone who writes things down. What is described each time is the same: a thin animal, thin as a rope, coat in ruins, crusted at the nose and the corners of the eye, coming in a straight line across dry country where nothing of its kind can live and where there is no population within a hundred kilometers to have produced it. It does not cast about. It does not follow the water, because there is no water to follow. It comes over the lip, does not drink, does not eat, goes up the eastern fig into a chamber that is already empty, turns around once, and sleeps for most of a day.

Already empty. That is the part. No incumbent has ever been found dead of a bite. The seat is vacant first, and then it is filled, and in between there is a season in which the bowl is measurably louder — frogs up, bats up, the swift column ragged and fat — until something crosses the plateau and the numbers come quietly back down.

They are not the same animal returning. One had a milky eye. One was a third larger and had a kink in the tail. One, the herders insist, was female and heavy with young, and there were never any young.

## II. The chorus

Ten thousand black cicadas with a red seam, in four trees, on one note.

They are not singing to each other. They have taken their carrier tone from the hum of the lattice under the loam, which is barely audible to a person and evidently deafening to them, and they hold it — not near it, on it — so that the bowl becomes a single instrument with a glass floor and seven stone reflectors, and the sound stands in the air in fixed bands you can walk into and out of like rooms.

Here is the demonstration, and it never fails. Scrape aside a handful of loam, lay your palm flat on the mesh, and hold it there. Within three or four seconds the song directly above your hand sags — a quarter tone, no more, a soft ugly wobble in a patch about two paces across — and the cicadas at the edge of that patch break off and clatter and reseat themselves. Lift your hand and the note comes back up like water levelling. Your body drinks a little of the hum, and they know at once, and they mind.

Nobody has decided what this means. It is simply true that ten thousand insects here are tuned to a machine, and that the machine is not tuned to them.

## III. The ones for whom this is everything

Twelve species of frog, thumb to fist, lacquered in colours that have no business at four thousand meters: enamel green, wet ochre, one that is genuinely blue along the thigh. They breed in the beads. A female lays into the curl of a fig leaf where the condensation has pooled to the volume of a walnut shell, and the whole business of the tadpole happens in that walnut shell, and the frog that comes out of it will live and die inside a circle four meters wide and will never once encounter standing water. They have no concept of a pool. Their word for the sea, if they had words, would be a leaf.

Their entire range is forty paces. The ceiling of their world is the sheared flat top of the fig crowns, where the warm air stops; eggs laid above that line fail overnight. Every frog in the bowl is a cousin of every other frog in the bowl, and has been for a very long time, and by every rule anybody knows they should long ago have collapsed into one dull inbred species. There are twelve. There have been twelve as long as anyone has counted. Not eleven, and not thirteen.

With them, in the same absolute confinement: three kinds of bat, packed in the hollows of the old figs like wet coats on a hook. Hand-sized moths whose caterpillars eat fig and nothing else, and who die within a quarter hour on the dry side of the lip, so that the six paces of glassy rim is a wall to them as final as a cliff. Mantids the exact shade and vein-pattern of a tree-fern frond. And the ants, who farm the moss, and who have worn a road — a visible groove, a finger deep — into the fused red material that a steel knife will not scratch. That groove is the oldest thing here that anything alive has made.

Only the swifts commute. Two thousand of them go up at first light in a column that unwinds out of the bowl mouth like smoke off a fuse, and spend the day over a hundred kilometers of dead tableland, and come back at dusk in a funnel that drags the mist down with it, and the sound of that arrival is a bedsheet tearing for ninety seconds. They are the only residents who know that anywhere else exists. They do not appear to hold it against the place.

## IV. The oldest resident

Not a tree. The vine.

One individual, one root system, one animal-slow intelligence of the kind plants have, which has spent several centuries climbing all seven stones and is now the actual structure of the bowl's upper air — loop after loop between the uprights, cabled, self-grafted where two of its own arms have met and fused, holding the moss on the inner faces, holding the stones themselves for all anyone can tell. Everything with wings in the Kettle sleeps on it. The figs are furniture. This is the walls.

It flowers white, all year, and stinks of overripe fruit, and it opens three flowers. Not three at dawn and eleven at dusk. Three. One closes, one opens. In two hundred years of this being noticed by people who bothered to write it down, the number has been four twice, briefly, both times in the season after an incumbent died.

And the trees are four. When the fourth of the old ones went over — hollow, bat-eaten, sometime around the collapse of the western hut — a young fig came up in the exact center of the bowl, in the open, where there had never been one, and grew to the ceiling and stopped. Nobody planted it. The bowl replaced its furniture.

## V. The tenant who stays one night

People are not residents here, and the reason is not the noise.

Everything you own that was ever alive is on loan inside the Kettle. Wool, leather, horn, gut, dried meat, cheese, a wooden haft, a bone button, a dog's collar off the dog. Keep it on your body, keep it in your hand, keep using it, and the lattice leaves it alone with what looks very much like courtesy. Set it down and sleep, and there is a fair chance that by morning the ground is simply less occupied where your coat was. This is why the whole human archaeology of the bowl is mineral: a stone cistern, a stone bench, a ring of tumbled stones, fired clay. Nothing anybody loved survived a night here unless it was a rock.
