## The Census

You have counted the bodies and found none, and the count is honest. It is also a question of the instrument.

The living population of this basin is somewhere in the region of a hundred quadrillion individuals. Wrung out and weighed together — every cell in the water, every cell in the film on the rock, every cell in the top centimetre of the ochre — they would come to about a bucketful. Perhaps two. Less than the tyre on the fourth bench. More, by a good margin, than the sixteen moss cushions that are the only inhabitants large enough to have a shape.

So: a bucket of life, dispersed through five million cubic metres of water at a density that makes it invisible, doing the one job available here, which it has been doing without interruption for thirty-one years, and which is the reason the water is the colour it is, the pH it is, and as clear as it is.

The place is not empty. It is thin.

## The sixteen islands

Begin above the line, because that is where the animals are.

The cushions are *Pohlia nutans*, which is the moss that arrives on acid ground everywhere in this latitude and asks for nothing. Each one sits where a hairline joint in the siltstone delivers seepage at something like a millilitre an hour — not a trickle, not a drip, a dampness — and that is why they are metres apart. They are not colonising the wall. They are occupying sixteen taps.

Inside each cushion, in the water held between the leaves, there is a fauna.

Bdelloid rotifers, mostly: *Habrotrocha*, transparent, a third of a millimetre, moving over the leaf surface in the looping gait that gives them their name, extending two ciliated wheels that are not wheels and drawing in whatever is smaller than they are. Tardigrades in perhaps nine of the sixteen. Testate amoebae — *Assulina*, *Euglypha* — each in a little built flask of secreted plates or scavenged mineral grains, so that a pinch of dead moss under magnification looks like a shelf of pottery. A nematode or two. Rotifer eggs. Fungal hyphae.
