## What lives in Cell Nine

Ask a tender and she will answer with her hand, because the answer is numbers and numbers are the one thing her language does easily. Forty. Eleven. Forty million, which is two taps and a spread palm and is understood to mean *more than can matter*. Three hundred. Three. One. One.

Forty nozzles, eleven combers, the hangers, the swift column, the three green things, the Constant, herself.

That is the census. It is complete and it is wrong, in the way that a list of a body's organs is wrong about the body.

---

### The crowd

Nine thousand to the square metre is not a population, it is an upholstery. Put the lamp close and the fur resolves: pale fused shields, each the size of a thumbnail, each canted at the same shallow angle into the wall, so that the whole surface has a grain like brushed velvet and can be stroked with it or against it. The tenders stroke with. Against, the shields lift and the mouthparts come off the stone, and for one second, in a patch the width of your hand, the wall begins to set.

They cannot stop. That is the whole of them, and everything else about them is arrangement around that fact.

A miller that pauses for ten minutes is a fixture. The milk does not care what it settles on. So the millers do not sleep, and being unable to sleep they have dispensed with the apparatus of it; they do not rest, they do not court, they do not fight, they do not have eyes because there is nothing in their world that requires being looked at. They mate at the file, hindquarters to hindquarters, still grazing, still advancing, two mouths working the wall in tandem for the eleven or twelve seconds it takes. The female carries the eggs cemented to the dorsal shield in a raised comb of eighteen, which is a difficulty, because the shield is the one part of her she cannot reach; the eggs would be sintered over within the hour but for the fact that the miller directly above her in the crowd is grazing downward, and takes the deposit off her back as a matter of course, without interest, as pavement. The nymphs hatch into that traffic. Their first act, before they have colour, is to rasp their mother's shield clean, and then to turn and put their new files against the stone, and that is the last new thing any of them will do.

They are not eating rock. This is worth saying, because visitors assume a miracle where there is only a circuit. The milk carries lime and it also carries the fine dark organic thread of everything the sumps below have dissolved, and it lays both down together, a black hair in a white lamina, over and over, so that the wall in section — if you could ever get a section — would be a striped bread. The lime is the crust. The millers file through the crust to get at the thread, and the thread is the cell's own dead, returned to the cell as flooring, pumped up in the morning to be eaten off the wall in the afternoon. Nothing enters Cell Nine but heat and nothing leaves it but heat, and the millers are the mechanism by which the room continues to eat itself without loss.

They die of blunt teeth. Nothing here dies of age; there is no age. The rotary file wears to smooth, the rasping slows, the slowing is felt by the crowd as a snag in the grain, and the wall takes her, and she is a bump under the pearl for perhaps forty minutes before a comber comes and pulls her out by the shield with a sound nobody has ever heard.

### The eleven

They are the only inhabitants of the cell that anyone would call handsome. Knee-high, blind, six legs of pale banded chitin jointed like calipers and set so wide that the body rides level a full hand above the fur, the way a marsh-strider rides above water. They walk on nine thousand mouths per square metre and are never bitten, because they are not stone and the millers are not interested in anything that is not stone.

They comb by feel, and what they feel is depth. A comber's foot sinks into the carpet to a distance that reports the local thickness of the crowd, and where the crowd is thick the legs sink further and the gait shortens and the head comes down and it feeds, and where the crowd is thin the legs ride high and the gait lengthens and it moves on. That is the entire behavioural repertoire. It is a governor made of meat, and it is why the millers are *even*: not because anything counts them, but because eleven blind animals are walking a slow patternless circuit of the walls with their feet in the population figures.

Eleven is enforced from below. The combers brood in the warm outflow where the sump water returns, in among the ribbon-weed, in a sediment of larvae numbering in the hundreds and going nowhere; the adults exhale a suppressant into the flow, and a larva in that flow will grow and moult and grow and never finish. When an adult dies the concentration falls by a ninth, or an eleventh, and in the flow a larva that has been waiting nine years reads the change in the water and completes itself overnight, and comes up the outflow pipe onto the wall before the old one has been fully dismantled. It is possible that some of the larvae in the sump are older than the Constant. Nobody has looked. The book has no column.

### The forty million

The lattice is not a mist. Say it clearly: it is an architecture, and the beads are its rooms, and each room is a closed world with a population of eight to two hundred and a diameter of four millimetres, and there are perhaps four hundred thousand of them hanging motionless in the shaft from the grating to whatever is up there.

The hangers inside are commas of clear jelly with a beating band of cilia and a gut you can see through, and they spend their entire lives in orbit of the inside surface of a drop of water, feeding on what the milk brings past. They are luminous when startled and they are startled by the wall of their world flexing, which happens when the room's four notes shift by a hair, which happens never, and when an arm goes through.

Here is the thing the book does not have a column for either. Four hundred thousand ponds, isolated for a length of time that has stopped meaning anything, and no migration between them except by accident — and the accident, when it comes, is a merger. Two adjacent beads swell past their nodes and coalesce and the joined mass is too heavy for the lattice and falls, and everything in it dies on the grating in a green flash, except for whatever fraction of the sixty or eighty animals is caught by the fall in the act of budding and splits and is thrown wide enough to be caught again by the standing wave. Reproduction across worlds requires the destruction of both. It happens maybe two hundred times a shift, high up, and looks like sparks going down.

Which means they have speciated, and any old tender knows it without having a word for it. Run your arm through the lattice at chest height under Lamp Two and the wake is green. Do it under Lamp Six and it is greener, colder, and lasts a half second longer. Do it at the northeast rib, where the nodes are crowded by the shape of the ribbing, and the flash comes in a stutter, two-and-one, two-and-one. The tenders call the regions by their flicker and use them for navigation in the dark, the way you would use the smell of streets. It is the only diversity in Cell Nine and it is filed, in the book, under *hangers: present*.

### The column

Two or three hundred sooty birds turning counter-sunwise in the updraught, mouths open, taking beads. They have been in this room long enough that the word *long* has gone soft, and long enough that they no longer *could* leave. Their balance is calibrated to a four-note standing wave and a single direction of turn; a vent-swift put out into the open air over the mountain would not fly away, it would fall, spiralling to the left, unable to find a horizon in the absence of a roar. The rule that nothing leaves the cell is not kept by the double lock. The lock is a courtesy.

They cannot land. The walls would strip them; the grating is the tender's; the lattice will not bear a bird. So they sleep aloft, one hemisphere at a time, half a brain flying and half a brain doing whatever the other thing is that brains do.
