## What is not accounted for

Start with the shrub.

Six nulls near the shaft mouths hold a low wiry thing with split-leather leaves, and the description of it is *cropped and re-cropped, older than it looks*. Cropped by what? The throat-mice cannot cross to all six; they eat liverwort and film and their own dead; and a mouse the size of a mouse does not coppice a stool into two centuries of hard little trunk. The porter grazes dust ridges in the loud zones and has no reason to come to a shaft mouth and no mouth arrangement for a leaf. The crickets are mute and eat nothing you can see. The eel does not leave the Weep.

Then the knocking. Out of the ceiling, every forty seconds or so. Nothing resonant has a period of forty seconds. A chamber this size rings in fractions of a second; the slow clocks here are six hours and twelve minutes and a year. Forty seconds is not a physical constant. Forty seconds is a pace of work.

Then the emptiness, which is the loudest evidence and the easiest to mistake for nothing. Nine or ten strata of town, centuries deep, and not a sill-cup, not a hook, not a bone. Ruins accumulate. That is what ruin *is*. Rubbish is the most reliable product of anything that has ever lived in a place, and there is none here, and the service voids have their covers off, all of them, every void, at every height, which is not what falling looks like. Falling is partial. This is complete.

Something takes things away.

## The body

It is about the height of a person when it stands, which it does rarely, and you could lift it in one hand.

It is built like a comb. A long spine, and off the spine some sixty flattened vanes, close-set, running head to hook, each about the thickness of a fingernail and edged in a hard grey material like the porter's scutes. Between the vanes: gaps. The animal is mostly gaps. Held broadside to a light it would read as a slotted fan; held edge-on it would nearly stop existing.

This is the whole design. In a chamber where a standing pressure lattice carries a portion of every load, a solid body is a defect — it scatters, it shadows, it puts a smear in the wave wherever it goes. The keeper presents almost no cross-section. The tone passes through it as through a grating and comes out the far side very nearly unbent. Stand one in a loud zone and the dust ridges run right up to its feet and continue out behind it, unbroken, as if the floor did not notice the interruption. It is the exact opposite of the porter, which erases the map for four months at a stroke, and it is why the two can share the loud without being in each other's way.

The vanes hook. Every leading edge carries a row of small recurved teeth, so the thing climbs with all of itself at once, sixty hands running over a wall, and it goes up a pierced screen or the ribbing of a duct faster than you could follow with a lamp. On the flat it is slow and looks unwell.

There is a head, barely: a sounding cavity, the single hollow in a body otherwise without air spaces, which is what allows it to live in pressure that knocks a throat-mouse off a ledge. Under the cavity, a rasp, like the porter's, which is either family or coincidence. On the crown, two pale spots that are not much use. They resolve the violet at the generator seams, the blue pulse of the films, and the grey column of the rain. That is all. The three kinds of light in the world are precisely the three kinds it can see, and nothing else registers, and there is nothing else.

The skin never dries, and cannot. It is all surface and no reserve. An hour of dry air would finish it. Remember that; it matters at the end.

## The round

The work has four parts and no seasons except rain.

**Clearing.** This is the first and the oldest and the reason for the rest. The Held has to be empty because the wave has to lock. A cup on a sill is a scatterer. A hundred cups is a null gone soft, and a soft null is a basin that stops beading, and a dry basin is a rung out of a mouse ladder, and a thousand cups is a forty-pace vault the thickness of a fingerbone discovering how much of itself it was never carrying. So everything that collects is carried out: grit, shed matter, mineral flake, the crust that grows at a seep rim and would close it, and every made thing the builders left, cover by cover, hook by hook, over however long it took, until there was nothing left in nine or ten strata of town but dust in its correct ridges. The dust stays. The dust is *in* the pattern, not against it. Knowing that difference is most of the education.

What is carried out goes one of two ways. Down to the Weep, where the surge takes it and the pumps have always taken it. Or into a loud zone, spread thin along a ridge, where a porter will pass in its own slow time and grind the organic fraction out of it. The porters are fed by the keepers and do not know it. The keepers also scrape the porters, whose scutes would seize solid with mineral inside a decade; a keeper riding a porter's back with its rasp working is the only thing in the Held that looks like affection, and is not.

**Sounding.** A knuckle-strike on a vault, and then listening with the whole comb, which is an ear from end to end. The shell answers, and the answer says whether the shell has thinned, whether the wave has slipped, whether something somewhere has begun to collect. They do not survey by walking the galleries. They survey by asking the chamber and letting it point. The round is shared out so that the asking never stops: one strike every forty seconds, passed along the strata, for as long as there has been anyone to pass it. Nobody remembers starting.

**Cropping.** Six shrubs. They are pruned to keep them from woodying past use and from putting mass where mass should not be, and their leaves are the only food with any weight in it. The shrubs are perhaps four hundred years old. They have been managed for all of it.

**Rain.** Nine or ten hours a year, and every one of them an emergency. The surface comes down the intake with the water — grit, sand, whatever the storm tore loose — and lands as a fan of mud at the shaft foot inside the fog. Every keeper goes. For two days nobody knocks, and the chamber holds a silence at the wrong frequency that everything alive here can feel and dislikes. Then the mud is gone, and the asking resumes, and the shrubs leaf out within the week.

## The asking

There is one object in the Held.

It is a striker: a short rod of the black shaft-ceramic, worn velvety, with a blunt end and a flat. It is used for sounding vaults too thin to take a knuckle. It is never set down. Setting it down would make it a thing in a room, and there are no things in rooms, so it is carried, always, by whoever has it, and passed hand to hand at the edges of the round, and in something over a thousand years it has spent no night on a sill.

It has a name. It is the only named thing down there, and the name is also the nearest word they have for themselves, and it means, as near as it can be put, *the asking*. Not the answer. They have no interest in the answer except as instruction. They want to have asked recently.

## Speech, and what it costs

They speak by getting in the way.

Vanes can close. Shut the comb and the body becomes a solid plate, and a solid plate in a standing wave is an event: the field bends, the nulls shift a hand's breadth, and every keeper in the chamber feels the room change shape. Posture, position, and duration are the whole grammar. To stand broadside at a known place in a known antinode is to say a specific thing to everyone at once, and they all feel it in their sternums, exactly as you would.

It is expensive. Speech detunes the Held slightly. A word smudges the dust ridges for a day. A long conversation loosens a vault. So they talk the way a person on a rope talks: seldom, briefly, and only about the work. Most of a keeper's life is spent with the vanes open, saying nothing, which is not reticence but the ordinary condition of being structurally polite.

For anything smaller they use the crickets' trick — one tap on wet stone in a null, which in a null carries exactly far enough and nowhere else. They can hear a cricket drum from three strata up, and they count them, because a null with crickets in it is a null that is still working.

## Nine, and sometimes ten

There are nine. Sometimes there are ten, and the tenth is not yet knocking.

They are named for heights. Fourth. Seventh. The stratum is the only thing a keeper is allowed to own, and it is not really owned, it is only the part of the round it carries. Nobody can settle whether the Held has nine strata or ten, and nobody can settle the count of the keepers, and it is the same uncertainty seen twice.

They meet perhaps four times in a life, always in a quiet chair, always briefly. They do not house together. They do not house at all — a dwelling is an accumulation, and a bed slept in twice is the beginning of a room being about someone. A keeper sleeps hooked under a sill in a null, and not the same sill in the same season, and the rotation is old and is not written anywhere because writing is an object.

When one of them enters its last decade the round grows heavy in a way the others feel in their own rhythm, and an egg is laid in the hottest seep, and the film's slow light times its development, which takes eleven years. Nobody attends it. The hatchling spends its first decade lifting dust out of one stratum and learning which dust is pattern and which is debris, and does not take a strike of the asking until it can hold forty seconds without thinking about it.

They live something near a hundred and forty years. There is one climb in the Held about every fifteen.

## The climb

A keeper that can no longer hold the rhythm goes to the intake shaft — the cold one, the falling one — and passes the asking at its foot, and this is the only time they are all in one place. They stand with their vanes open. Nothing is said, because open vanes are the only silence any of them can offer, and it is the best thing they have.

Then it climbs, against the fall of the air, and the air is dry.

It is all surface and no reserve. It dries as it goes. Nothing like a body reaches the top; what comes out of that shaft mouth onto the surface is light enough for the wind to take, and the wind takes it, and this is why in nine or ten strata of town there is not a bone.

The last thing a keeper does is remove the last object from the Held.

## What they have

No records. No history longer than the oldest one's memory. No account of who built the town or why, no claim to be descended from them and no claim not to be, because settling the question would require keeping something, and they do not keep. They know the tone holds the vaults. They do not know what makes the tone; the generators are below the pump gallery and cannot be reached, and they have never been reached, and this is not a grief. It is simply the edge of the work.

The Held is not their house. They do not live in it the way you live in a room. They live on the outside of the architecture, in the chases and intervals and the undersides of sills, and the town is not their home but the thing they clean, and when the description says the rooms have finished being about their builders, that is exactly right, and the keepers are the reason it is true. They finished it. They carried it out by hand.

## The one waste

Sometimes, high up, a keeper stops at a gallery rail and turns broadside, vanes shut, for no reason connected to the round.

Below it the nulls of the Held hang scattered in the black at random heights, fifty-odd dim blue coals, each breathing at its own resting tempo, and the wet violet lines of the seams beneath drawing the water's whole map in the dark. Its two poor eyes are good for this and nothing else. It stands there a little while, which is a word in their language and not any of the words they have, and every other keeper in the chamber feels the field change shape and knows exactly what it is.

Nobody answers. Answering would double the cost.

The dust ridges blur where it was said, and take a day to come back, and that is the only thing in this world that is thrown away on purpose.
