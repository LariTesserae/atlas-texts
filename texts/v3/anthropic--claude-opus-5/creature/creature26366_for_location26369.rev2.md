## The Shrimp

Start here, because this is where the mass is.

They are nine millimetres, eleven at the outside, and the colour of the inside of a fingernail — transparent enough that you can watch a gut working its way dark to pale over the course of an hour. No eyes to speak of: two pinhead spots that read brightness and nothing else, which is sufficient, because there is no brightness where they live. They navigate by heat. The discharge jet is roughly fifty-two degrees at the core, which would cook them, and the room at large is thirty-six, which would starve them, and the shrimp occupy the shell between — a wobbling, unstable envelope of water perhaps two litres in volume, shaped like a poorly blown bulb around the delivery pipe's mouth, re-formed with every stroke. That is their planet. Its weather has a period of four and a half seconds.

They are not there for the heat. They are there for the iron.

The base of the food chain in the Warm Cistern is not the light column. It is the pump. Water this old, this enclosed, this saturated with dissolved iron, grows a film on any ferrous surface — a rust-eating, rust-making bacterial felt, dull ochre, no thicker than a coat of varnish, renewing itself as fast as it is taken. The shrimp farm it. That is not a metaphor; they crop it in rotation, they work the same faces in the same order, and the parts of the pump they graze most heavily are the parts where the film grows fastest, which are the parts that move. Bearings. Packing. The beam's pivot. A shrimp braces with three pairs of legs against the shudder and works with the front pair, scraping in short strokes, riding the clank like a passenger who has stopped noticing the train.

They breed continuously. Six weeks to maturity in that heat, call it eight generations a year, call it sixty-four thousand generations since the water was put here. Everything they are, they became in a saucepan of hot water beside an iron machine. There has never been a shrimp in this room that has seen the room. Drift two handspans off the jet and the cold closes on you like a hand, and your legs stop answering, and you sink into the dark of the floor where the loach are waiting with their mouths slightly open.

Which is the only way out of the shrimp world, and is taken by a few hundred a night.

## The Loach

Six. Sometimes seven. Never eight.

Pale as boiled rice, as the survey says, and about the length of a finger, and blind in the specific way of things whose ancestors could see: the eye is present, the lens is a clouded pea, the skin has grown a thin window over it and then stopped bothering. They read the room by pressure. The clank gives them a metronome and the room gives them an echo, and between the two they hold a model of the chamber accurate to a centimetre — the reef's overhangs, the sag of the mats, the standing plume's shivering column, the exact place where the east wall's curvature begins. They know it the way your tongue knows your teeth: completely, constantly, and without ever once having looked.

They are the only citizens with a passport. The room runs two separate economies — the light economy of the weed and the moss and the midge larvae, and the iron economy of the pump — and they are the only creatures that draw from both. They graze the grating mats in the afternoon. At night they line up along the outflow and take delivery of the day's failed emigrants. Everything that the hot corner produces in excess arrives in the loach, cooling, one mouthful at a time.

They live a long while. Sixty years is ordinary. And the count holds at six because they eat their own eggs, nearly all of them, without apparent malice and without exception — a clutch laid in the reef in daylight is gone inside two hours, consumed by its parents and its parents' siblings, and the room's population control is thereby handled internally and permanently, which is one of several respects in which the Warm Cistern does not require anyone's attention.

The seventh, when it happens, is a winter child. In the sealed dark, at the bottom of the freeze, the adults go torpid — not dormant, but slowed to a stroke and a half of the pump between movements — and a clutch laid then can go unattended long enough to hatch. One in a thousand of those reaches a length at which it stops being food. Then there are seven, for a decade or two, until an old one goes; and then six.

It has been eight once, briefly, about four hundred years ago. It did not last.

## The Snail

Always one.

It keeps to the tide-band. Its life is organised not around the day but around the month: when the surface drops its eleven or twelve centimetres, a strip of moss comes up into the air, dazzling and dripping and briefly the richest food in the room, and the snail is there, working it, for the three or four days before the water comes back and reclaims the crop. The rest of the month it grazes the algal fringe below the line, slowly, at a profit margin of almost nothing.

The copper is a problem it manages. Plating accrues on the shell at a rate that would, in eight or nine years, make the animal too heavy to hold the wall — so it does not stop moving, ever, and the constant friction of shell against masonry keeps the deposit down to a skin. This produces the only structure in the Warm Cistern that could be called a road.

Look along the tide-line with the light behind you. There is a faint clean spiral around the whole circumference of the chamber, a band perhaps two centimetres wide, where the lime has never been allowed to grow: brick-pink, polished, slightly sunken. Its edges are crisp. It is the single most maintained object in the room after the pump, and it is maintained by a population of one, because each new snail finds the old track easier going than virgin wall and follows it, and thereby keeps it, and thereby leaves it for the next. Nobody knows how many snails have kept it. The road is older than any religion currently practised.

Where the next one comes from is unsettled. There are no juveniles. There has never been a second shell. The best guess — and it is a guess assembled from three visits over two centuries, which is not a dataset — is that the animal lodges a single egg deep in the reef, at some point early on, and that the egg does not develop while the parent lives; that there is a signal, perhaps chemical, perhaps just the cessation of one, and that when the old snail finally fails and its copper begins to dissolve back into the water, the egg reads the news and begins.

That is one account. The other is that the wall makes them. This is not seriously held, but it is not seriously disproved either, and people who have spent a night in there tend to stop arguing about it.

## The Weed

Three stands, and only one of them is an individual.

The fringe is a consortium — a moss above the line, four or five algal species below, all of them fast, all of them turning over on a scale of weeks. The mats on the grating are a slower, thicker, dirtier business, more bacterial than plant, the compost heap of the room. Neither is a person.

The rope on the east wall is.

It is a single clone, unbranched at the base, red-green, thick as a wrist, and it is the oldest living thing in the chamber by a factor of something like a hundred. It cannot fragment; there is nowhere for a fragment to go. It has no competitors; there is no second rope. It simply grows, and is grazed, and grows, and has been doing this since before the first loach came up in a bucket.

Over the course of a day it inclines toward the light column. Not quickly — you would need to mark the wall and come back in three hours — and not by any nervous process, only by pressure changes in its tissues, cell by cell, an enormous slow arithmetic performed in sugar. It leans east in the morning and west by evening, and it has performed this calculation correctly on every day it was possible to perform it, roughly three million times, and it has never once reached the light. It does not need to. The column crosses it for about forty minutes around noon and that is enough, and the lean is not desire, it is bookkeeping.

Still. You watch it move and the word that arrives is not *bookkeeping*.

## The Midges and the Spider

The midges are the room's only export.

Their larvae live in the fringe, in the wet moss, and they hatch in clouds on warm afternoons and go up the light-shaft in a thin grey smoke, out through the eye, into air at six thousand metres that kills them in under ten seconds. Every one of them. There has never been a return. What leaves the Warm Cistern in a year, by this route, is on the order of a few grams — call it a fingernail clipping a day — and this is precisely enough. A sealed room with no export silts, sours, and dies. The midges are the sewer, and they pay for the privilege with a hundred percent mortality, continuously, for ten thousand years.

What comes in balances it: hail, grit, a feather about once a decade, the dust of a mountain that is not a mountain, and — once or twice a century — a human being, who leaves hair, skin, salt, a quantity of exhaled carbon, and a smell the loach will be reacting to for a week after he has gone.

And spiders. They come down on the draught as ballooning young, a dozen or twenty in a summer, and nineteen of those land on water and drown or land on wet lime and slide. One a year makes the dry brick under the dome. It lives there, cold and slow, in a bad web, eating the midges the down-draught knocks back through the eye — the ones that nearly escaped. It dies at the freeze. It does not breed; no second spider has ever arrived in the same season and found the first.

So: ten thousand spiders have lived in the Warm Cistern, and no spider has ever been born there. It is the only address in the room that is always occupied by a stranger.

## The Machine, Considered as an Organism

It takes in energy from a source that cannot be examined. It maintains its own rate against thermal variation, within a few percent, which is homeostasis by any reasonable definition. It is cleaned, repaired, and kept patent by a symbiont population that has co-evolved to the task and would starve without it. It does not reproduce.

Three out of four. A mule gets three out of four.

The honest position is that the pump is not alive and that the question is badly posed, and the useful position is that nothing else in the room would survive its death by more than a season. Stop the stroke and the chamber stratifies inside a month; the floor goes anoxic; the mats rot; the loach suffocate in the dark they have always lived in. The shrimp go first and take the machine's last chance with them. The rope on the east wall, being patient, would be the last to know.

Everything in the Warm Cistern evolved inside the sound of that clank. Not one organism in there has ancestors who knew a world without it.

## What Actually Lives There

Here is the difficulty with the inventory.

The water has never been anywhere else. It has not been rain, or river, or blood belonging to anything outside this room. Which means that every atom of the snail's foot has been, at various points and in some order: wall lime, algal filament, loach, egg, loach again, midge larva, midge, a midge that almost got out, spider, spider corpse, mat, shrimp, shrimp that drifted too far, loach, and wall lime again. Eight thousand years of this, in four thousand litres, stirred every four and a half seconds. The residents are not distinct populations sharing a habitat. They are the same few tonnes of matter taking turns at being things.

So the answer to *what lives here* is: one body, roughly four and a half metres across, with an iron heart in the west wall that beats thirteen times a minute; a gut of hot bacterial film; a green skin on the east side that leans after the sun; six white nerves that patrol the floor and know the whole interior by touch; one copper thought that travels the circumference once a month along a road it has worn for itself; a habit of coughing a small grey portion of itself out the eye on warm afternoons; and a sleep every winter, sealed and black and humming, from which it has always woken.

That is an animal, or near enough that the remaining difference is a matter of taste. It is missing only a future. It does not grow, it does not age, it is not going anywhere, and it has no offspring and no prospect of any. Whatever tense that requires, the builders had a word for it and did not leave the word behind.

## And You

If you get up there — cracked lips, no idea what you climbed — the room will take you in without any particular ceremony.

The water is already your temperature, so the boundary is hard to locate. The pressure lies to your ears and your lungs disagree and after a few minutes you stop adjudicating. The noise is total, which is functionally the same as silence, because noise only matters if there is something you are listening *for*, and there is nothing in that room to listen for. Nothing is approaching. Nothing is being decided. Every negotiation in the Warm Cistern concluded before your species could write, and your nervous system — which is very largely a device for keeping a list of unresolved matters — finds the list empty, and files itself away.

That is why they all fall asleep. Not the warmth. The settledness.

You will be incorporated while you doze. The shrimp will be on your skin flakes within the hour; the spider will get a hair; the loach will spend a fortnight investigating your salt. Some fraction of you will still be circulating in there in a thousand years, having been weed, having been snail, having been the road. The room does not notice you as an event. It processes you as a small, warm, unusually complicated meal, politely, over the course of a century.

The shrimp are its hands. The loach are its attention. The snail is the part of it that remembers where it has already been.

And you, for one afternoon, floating on the drumhead with your teeth full of iron, are the only part of it that can leave.
