## The tenders

You will meet them in clots, at long intervals, with a great deal of shining empty brick between — which is the same pattern as the figs, and is not a coincidence. Because a voice dies at arm's length, company here means contact. Four or five of them will be sitting knee to knee in the wet shade of a fig, or shoulder to shoulder along a tank step with their feet in the green water, and from three hundred paces off they look like a single dark mark on the pavement, and they are silent, and they are talking continuously.

They are small and broad-handed and the colour of wet slip to the elbow, all of them, permanently, the mud never drying and never washing off. They wear wrapped cotton the shade of unfired clay. Hair oiled, bound, unstirred by the sheeted heat. They go barefoot on brick that would take the skin off you, and their soles do not blister; strike a heel down on a tank step and it gives a clean short note, the note of a good brick, and nobody four paces away hears it.

Look at their right thumbs. Smooth. Not scarred smooth, not worn — simply blank, a bald pad, no whorl, nothing to press into a bed face.

Their work is tending. Never laying. They sweep pavement that carries no dust, and the sweeping is not pretence; they take it seriously and they do it well, and if you watch a long while you will see that the broom is being used to check the level, the way a hand runs over a table. They sight lines that are already true. They water figs that are already dripping. They wipe the float and set it down, and pick it up, and wipe it, and set it down. Every tool on the Level is set down neatly because a tender put it there in the middle of a task, and the task cannot advance, so the putting-down became the task.

They are not unhappy. They are conscientious. With no news to exchange, their conversation has refined itself into an extraordinary connoisseurship of detail: the correct wrist for a float, the exact greasiness of a good slip, whether the third course of the eastern levee is a hair proud (it is; it has always been; there is a school of opinion that says it was meant). They will discuss this with you gladly, and they will not discuss the white edge, and if you bring it up they will nod and wait, courteously, for you to finish.

They have names, but a name you cannot call across a plain is no use, so a name here is a rhythm tapped on the collarbone. Everyone knows everyone's. There are no new ones — nothing changes — except when someone walks in out of the brightness, and then a name has to be invented for them, and this is the only act of making the Level still permits. Certain tenders are remembered, as much as anything is remembered, for having once invented a very good name.

None of them can tell you about arriving. All of them will tell you, without being asked twice, that everyone here arrived.

## The ones still counting

You can pick them out at once, because they still have prints.

They come in off the white, delighted, and they walk the causeways for a while with their heads back. Then they begin to measure. They are the ones lying full-length at the canal cut with a cord, counting courses down into the wet dark. They are the ones at the parapet of the third causeway with a tally in one hand, counting the tally, and counting it again, and going white about the mouth. They are the ones who stand over a tank until the tenders come and stand near them, not touching, just near.

They dig. There are open cuts on the Level going down thirty and forty courses, timbered, bailed, entirely tidy, because the spoil rises back out of the baskets and reseats itself within a count of ten and the digger must dig the same course twice, and does, and does. They keep notes. The notes stay wet.

What happens to them is not dramatic. They stop asking after a while. They pick up a float because a float is lying there and the pavement wants a hand run over it. And one day someone taps their collarbone with a name that was invented for them and they answer to it, and their thumb is smooth, and there is mud to the elbow that will not wash off.

## The corner

At the northeast margin, under the reed shade, beside the stack of green bricks that have never dried, a woman is kneeling on a reed mat with a brick in her hands.

She is about twenty. Her hands are forty years old — the calluses are the calluses of a lifetime of laying — and they are on the hands of a young woman, unaged, unworn, wet to the wrist with slip. Her right thumb has a deep, complete, beautiful whorl.

It is the thumbprint. All four hundred million of them. You can check; people have checked for days; it is hers.

She is not frozen. She breathes and blinks and wipes her forehead with her forearm and shifts her knees on the mat and turns the brick over to look at the bed face, and adjusts her grip, and looks at the pegged line, and looks at the mortar pit. She has been about to set that brick for as long as anyone can remember.

Kneel down beside her — shoulder against her shoulder, or she will not hear you — and she is perfectly willing to talk. She says the slip has taken up too much water. She says it will be a moment. She says it in the tone of a person who has been asked a reasonable question by a great many reasonable people and has never once minded. She will ask you to hold the line. You will hold the line. It does not sag.

The slip in the pit is exactly as wet as it has been since before the tally began. Whether she knows this is the only real question on the Level, and she is the only one who could answer it, and what she says is: in a moment.

Because it is a made floor over made floor with no bedrock under it and no kiln anywhere and nothing that falls or rots or dries, and if she sets that brick, something will have happened.

There is one thing about her. Everything on the Level takes its reflection in the tanks — the sunlit version, the worn version, the version with a shadow. She has none. Lean over the mortar pit beside her and the wet slip gives back the reed shade, the white sky, your own face going old — and where she is, nothing. Just slip.

## Below

Divers who go down the deep cuts and put their faces to the lowest reached course report a change in the pressure of the air against the cheek. Not a sound. Sound does not travel. A rhythm, though: tap, tap, tap-tap, coming up through forty courses of herringbone and stretcher and clear standing water, patient, unhurried, the pressure-ghost of a wooden mallet.

The bricks get smaller as you go down. That is the well-known part. What is less often said is what they get like: not cruder. Finer. The joints narrow to a hairline and stay wet. At the deepest course anyone has brought up whole, the bricks are the size of a thumb, and they are perfect, and each one carries on its bed face a thumbprint of full adult size, whorls complete, unreduced, wrapping the little brick like a hand around a seed.

So it is not a child down there. It never was. Something is going downward into detail instead of downward into age, and the print does not shrink to fit, and whatever is laying by feel in that lightless clear water under the whole made world has hands that cannot possibly fit between those courses, and is laying anyway, and is getting smaller work done, and is nowhere near finished, and is going the wrong way.

## The citizens

The herons, the ibis, the catfish like slow thoughts, the brick-coloured crabs sidestepping the joints, the dragonflies in their soundless hundreds. They are the only inhabitants for whom none of this is a problem. They were made for exactly this place and no other, and it holds them the way a well holds a fig.

Some have the wheel-spiral on the belly, or the potter's swipe at the nape, or gills that were cut rather than grown, and some do not, and it makes no difference to anything, including to them. They brood potsherd eggs that will not hatch and do not mind. The dead heron lies in perfect feather with its eye bright, and is standing in the shallows the next time you pass, and neither state seems to it a change of address.

Pick up a crab and hold it too long and it stops sidestepping and cools and stiffens and is a brick in your hand, with a thumbprint on it. The tenders will take it off you without the least reproach, and set it down on the pavement, and wait, and within a count of ten it walks.

## The neighbour

In the tanks, in the reflection, the sun is low and orange and the joints are furred with moss and a fig has heaved the pavement out of true, and a canal has silted, and it is all centuries old, and it is empty.

Not always. Once in a long while — and every tender on the Level will describe it to you the same way, tapping it out shoulder to shoulder — an old woman walks a causeway crown over there with a stick, slowly, dragging a long eastward shadow, going somewhere in the evening. She does not look at the water. She has never looked at the water.

The tenders stand still until she is out of the frame. They cannot say why. It is not fear. It is the stillness you keep in a room where someone is asleep, or working.

Some of them think she is what the young woman at the corner would have been if the brick had gone down. Some think she is the one who is really laying it all, and the Level is the model on her table, and the water is only where the two surfaces touch. Nobody thinks she is a copy.

## And

You have read the tally. You noted the number. You are about to count it again.

Look at your thumb.

The float has been in your other hand for some time now, and it is warm, and it is worn smooth by use.
