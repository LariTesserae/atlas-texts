## What Lives in the Warm Room

**First answer, which is wrong.** Nothing lives there. You can say this and be technically correct for about nine seconds. There is no animal, therefore no appetite, no territory, no nest, nothing that wants. The place has the whole apparatus of habitation — warmth, water, light, food, a clock — and no tenant to use it.

Then you notice you have been using the wrong verb. Things live there in enormous quantity. What the room lacks is not life. What it lacks is anybody *in* it. The distinction matters, because the resident of the Warm Room is not absent. It is simply not shaped the way residents are usually shaped, and it takes some patience to see it whole, and it does not help you see it.

**The candidates, and why each is only an organ.**

The turbine is not alive and never was, and it is nevertheless the only thing down there doing work against entropy in the ordinary sense — the only thing that keeps a surface clean, that produces rather than accumulates. But it does not know it is doing this. It is a heart, and a heart is not the animal.

The ferns are alive, individually, in their millions of leaves, and every one of them is also not the animal. Consider what has happened to them. They do not orient to light; they orient to heat, which means they orient to the machine, which means the entire architecture of this jungle points at one object the way a congregation points at an altar. The great tongue-ferns in the silt lean west. The hanging curtains grow *down* from the vault and then curl *in*, so that their undersides face the housing. Their sky is on the floor, and they are falling toward it at half an inch a year, and they will never arrive. Take any single one of them out of the room and it will die of cold and clean air, but more than that, it will die of arrhythmia — it will have nothing to be tuned to. It is not a plant. It is a string.

**The animal, then.** What lives in the Warm Room is one individual, and it is roughly nave-sized, and its body is: a fissure; a turbine that the fissure turns; a pipe that the turbine cools and warms; ten thousand square feet of leaf that the pipe rains on; a spore-load that the turbine's fundamental shakes free; and a black-red loam, made from its own shed tissue, that receives the spores and grows the next leaf. Nothing enters. Nothing leaves. That closure is the reason it is one thing and not a collection of things. A body is defined by what circulates inside it, and here everything circulates and nothing else does.

If you must call it something, call it the Consort. The word means a set of instruments built to be played together, and it also means the one who stays.

**Its vital signs.** Its heartbeat is thirty-three hours long and you can stand inside it. The surge comes; the load rises; the pipe chills; and for forty minutes the Consort rains on itself, hard and unevenly, that pattering like applause through a wall, and this is not weather happening *to* it — this is its breath, the whole inhalation, all its water moving from the cold line to the crowns of the leaves to the loam to the sump and back down the fissure to be steam again. Then the drizzle, which is the long exhalation, twenty-nine hours of it. Its pulse is audible at the bottom of hearing. Its temperature is thirty-eight to forty and has not varied by a degree in living memory, because it has no memory and nothing living to remember with.

It has two senses. Warmth, which tells it where the middle of itself is. And vibration, which is everything else: signal, season, fertility, command. It has no use for sight; four of the nine lamps have failed and the Consort registered nothing, because the lamps were never for it, they were for men who wanted to read a dial. Sunlight, when it comes for eleven days in high summer through the broken flue, arrives as an injury and is filed as an injury. The pale scorched stripe across the moss is not the Consort's calendar in the sense of a thing it consults. It is a scar in the shape of a year, which is what a calendar is when you have no head to keep one in.

**What it does not know.** It does not know that anything exists outside itself, because functionally nothing does. It has no sky, no winter, no predator, no competitor, no wound that heals, no mouth. Nothing down there has ever needed to defend itself, and you can feel this in the tissue: the fronds are unbittered, no thorns, no acrid oils, no toxins, all of them soft as the inside of a wrist. That is why nothing bites you. Not restraint. It never occurred to any part of this body that being eaten was among the available events. When you walk the length of the room, parting the curtains with your forearms and taking their stored rain down your collar, you are performing the only act of intention that this organism has experienced in perhaps thirty years, and it cannot perceive intention, so what it perceives is a small, warm, extremely local storm moving east. If you shouted, spores would fall. That is the entirety of your relationship. You may be weather to it. You may not be company.

**The one who has already ended.** Near the hot patches, in vigorous mats, grows a fine bronze-coloured fern in perfect health that has not produced a spore since about 1970. Its sori are intact. Its machinery is intact. The note that used to split them has gone flat and gone past, and the fern is still down there thriving, spreading by runner, indistinguishable in every visible respect from what it was when it could still make a successor.

This is the Consort's first bereavement, and it did not notice, and there was nothing to notice. Nothing rotted. No gap opened in the sward. The bronze fern simply became a portrait of itself, and portraits keep very well at forty degrees.

Understand what this makes of the resident. It is not aging in any way it could ever detect. It is going out of tune with pieces of itself, one voice at a couple of centuries, and each voice, on losing its future, stays exactly where it is, looking exactly as it looked, filling the same volume of the same room. The Consort will be reduced from a chord to a note without any change in appearance whatsoever. At the end it will be one clone, ancient, immense, warm, drummed on by rain, silent in the only register it can hear. Not dead. Finished. It will not be able to tell.

**The other thing, which is probably not a thing.** There is a habit in that room of arriving at the shapes of people.

A moss cushion on the housing has taken the exact contour of the pressure gradient across it, which is a coincidence of thermodynamics and reads as a hand pressed flat. A hanging fern at the sump edge has grown into a serviceable profile of a man, three-quarter view, chin down, and it is where a man would stand if a man were checking the water. The one surviving flickering lamp flickers on the surge, and at hour nine or ten it begins to answer you. And on the slate inside the foreman's cabin there are two hundred and eight strokes, of which two hundred and five are a hundred and thirty years old and three are perhaps thirty, and all of them are grooves now, etched by the way chalk changes the weathering of slate — which is to say that all of them were once chalk, which is to say something held chalk in that cabin thirty years ago and made three marks and stopped.

The rational explanation is that dissolution imitates a great many things, and that a room with no observer and a hundred and forty years of dripping will eventually produce, by pure combinatorics, the profile, the handprint, the tally, the cup welded to its saucer as if just set down. This explanation is sufficient. Hold it firmly.

Hold it firmly, and then notice that the room has only ever been given one form to copy. Nothing has come down that slope but men in boots — a handful of them, briefly, a long time ago — and the flowstone is a medium that records what touches it at under a millimetre a century, and the Consort's whole existence is the reception and slow reproduction of vibrations that arrive from elsewhere. If a thing like that were to have anything resembling an output beyond spores, it would be this: the patient, unmotivated, mineral rehearsal of the only visitors it ever had, done wrong, done at the wrong scale, done without any idea what it was doing, the way a fern goes on presenting its intact sori to a note that has moved on.

**How it will receive you.** It will not. It will rain on schedule. Twice, if you stay too long, and by the second rain the gas will have taken your judgement gently, as a kindness, and you will want very badly to put your thumb on the two gold patches on the housing, because they are warm and clean and they are the only surface in that entire body that something is still tending. Don't. That heat is not for you and it is not affection; it is the last two spots where the burial has not yet closed over the heart.

Climb the rubble. Let the bass note thin to a hum, to a pressure, to nothing. What lives down there is one animal with a cast-iron heart and a hundred thousand leaves, breathing every thirty-three hours, deaf in one more register every few centuries, incapable of loneliness in the way that a note is incapable of loneliness, and it did not mark your visit except as rain that fell in the wrong place, from your sleeves, briefly, on its floor.
