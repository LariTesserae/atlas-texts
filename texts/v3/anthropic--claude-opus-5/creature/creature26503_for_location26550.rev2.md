# The Clear

*(a continuation of the file on the Standing Sea, concerning what lives in it that can be said to* live *there rather than merely occur)*

---

### 1. The wobble that turns

You have already swum through them. That is how it goes. You come down through the bright ceiling into all that unspent light, you get used to the idea that the shimmer ahead is a shoal of bell-shrimp or a thermal seam or nothing at all, and then one of the shimmers stops, and rotates, and holds a fixed distance off your shoulder for four minutes.

What you see, if you see anything, is a slow dark braid about the length of your forearm, and under it a loose handful of gravel, suspended, turning over. That is the gut and the stone-bag. Everything else — two metres of body around them, edge to edge — is water with an opinion.

I have been calling them knappers, which is wrong, because the hammering is the least of it. The lowland word that fits best is *householder*. Let me go in order.

### 2. What there is to see

An adult is a lens of clear muscle and clear cartilage, roughly two and a quarter metres across the long axis, thin at the rim and deep through the keel, neutral to the water within a gram. There is no bone in it that is not glassy, no cavity in it that is not flooded. It cannot float and it cannot sink, and the bellies ring it like a struck rail without doing it the slightest harm, because there is nothing inside it for the pressure to squeeze against. This is the price of admission here, and everything that lives in the Standing Sea has paid it.

Along the forward margin are two large lenses, set close, with a fixed convergent stare — a face, more or less, if you need one. Behind them the rim carries a fringe of perhaps two hundred simple eyes, the size of pinheads, which do nothing but count light. In an ordinary ocean this would be a waste of tissue. Here, where a body casts a hard shadow at four hundred metres, the rim-eyes are a second skin. They feel your shadow before the lenses find you.

There are four arms, folded flat along the keel when not in use, two heavy and two fine. The heavy pair end in a gripping fold rather than a hand — a pocket of muscle that takes a hammerstone and holds it the way a fist holds a fist. The fine pair are long, and split near the tip into six, and are the part of the animal you will find hardest to look away from, because they behave exactly like hands that have been doing something skilled for a very long time.

The mouth is underneath, and it is a rasp. It is the same rasp the ribbon-grazers have. They are cousins; everything here is a cousin; the whole fauna of this sea came up the shafts as larvae in a pipe, a few million years of accidental delivery, and diverged in a bowl of clean water with nothing in it. The people know this. Their account of their own origin is that they came out of the machine. It is not a myth. It is simply what happened, and they are in the fortunate position of a species whose creation story is checkable.

### 3. The stones

A solid animal in water is acoustically invisible to itself. Sound goes through it as through more water; there is nothing to shake. So in order to hear the loudest sea on the planet, a body of this kind must carry something dense, and they carry gravel.

The stone-bag sits under the gut, a muscular sling holding between nine and forty pebbles — dense, dark, chosen. They hear with it. The stones lag the flesh by a fraction of a fraction, and that lag is the whole of their perception of the Draw: the eleven-second pulse arriving as a shove against the sling, the two-hundred-hertz roar as a standing burr, and the phase differences between one tower's pulse and another's read off the way the mass swings in its hammock. They also grind the stones, slowly, over decades, which trims their ballast and fertilises their wake — a person moving through the sea drags a thin comet of rock flour behind it, and the grazers follow, and the roads they have used for a thousand years are visible from above as faint rust-and-cream lines drawn over black glass. Their highways are made of what they have digested.

Because the stones are the ear, the choice of stones is the most intimate thing about a person. A dense glassy pebble gives fine phase and a poor sense of the roar; a coarse vesicular one gives the opposite. Young adults experiment and swallow badly and go about for a year hearing the world slightly wrong. The old carry the same four or five stones for two centuries, polished to eggs, and will not be parted from them.

When one dies, the clear tissue goes to nothing in a season — there are no borers here, but there is the rasp of a million ribbon-grazers, and a body is the softest food in the basin. What is left on the rock afterwards is a small neat heap of polished gravel, and everyone who passes knows whose it is. That is the entire funerary practice. It is also, as it turns out, the entire postal system.

### 4. Why they break the world

Nothing grows here. There is no crop, no herd, no soil. There is a rust-and-cream bacterial varnish that lives off hydrogen bleeding out of young rock, and there is the simple rule that the varnish is thickest where the rock is freshest, because a fracture surface is a reactive surface and it exhausts.

So the economy is fracture. A person takes a hammerstone and opens a face of basalt, or better, of volcanic glass, and the conchoidal scoop that comes off leaves behind a bright new scar the size of a door. In three or four years that scar will carry a varnish thick enough to feed grazers. In fifteen it is a good pasture. In sixty it is greying. In two hundred it is dead rock, and you go elsewhere.

The consequence is that these are people who live inside an inheritance of dulling. Every household has its faces: a cliff apportioned in scars of different ages, cut by parents and grandparents and by people whose stones are heaps at the foot of the cliff, the whole wall a calendar in which the bright panels are this decade's work and the grey ones are somebody's dead great-grandmother. They cut new faces constantly, and they cut them in a fixed order along the cliff, and they do not cut out of order, because the order is the only accounting anyone has.

A ridge worked for a thousand years is a strange thing to swim along. It has been stepped back into the mountain by fifteen or twenty metres, in a long scalloped colonnade of flake-scars, the oldest at the back and grey as ash, the newest at the front and glowing green-black, and below it a talus of spent flakes that is itself crowded with grazers in the interstices, and below that, the road.

### 5. Going white

They are transparent. Transparency is the only camouflage the Standing Sea permits. And so — inevitably, the way a thing is inevitable once you see it — their language is the surrender of it.

Under the clear skin lie sheets of cells that can be flooded with a white colloid in about a fifth of a second and cleared again in two. A speaking person *clouds*: bands, chevrons, creeping fronts, a slow milky blush moving from the keel outward, a hard flick of white along one rim. The vocabulary is large and the grammar seems to be mostly in rate and direction. It is a beautiful thing to watch and it is completely silent and it is, in the most literal sense available, self-endangerment.

Because the slate-eels hunt by listening — and the eels' ears work only in the nodes — and the people's eyes work everywhere. So the moral geography of this sea inverts once more, on top of the inversion already in the file. The nodes are quiet. In the quiet the eels can hear a heartbeat at two hundred metres. Therefore in the quiet country you go clear, and you say nothing, and you cross alone and fast with your stones held tight against the sling so they cannot knock.

And in the bellies — the hammering, deafening, sunlit reaches where the silt-sheet stands off the floor and nothing with a hollow in it can live and no eel can hear anything at all — that is where they talk. That is where the councils are, out in the middle of the noise, forty people in a ring at sixty metres depth in a column of daylight, blazing white at each other in a din that would put your teeth out, perfectly safe.

They are a people who conduct all their public life inside a foundry, and all their travel in silence, and who have no sign at all for *whispering*.

There is a sign for the other thing — for a person who clouds while crossing a node. It is not a word for foolishness. It is a word for a particular kind of insistence, the ones who will not go clear even where going clear is free, and it is used about the dead more often than the living.

### 6. The seventeen rings

Everything in the basin that is not starving is within a few kilometres of an intake tower, where the outfall brings up water from the lowland ocean with iron and silica still in it. Around each of the seventeen there is a ring of relative wealth perhaps three kilometres wide, and around that ring, two hundred kilometres of bright blue nothing.

So there are seventeen peoples. Not seventeen nations — they have no such idea — but seventeen populations that meet each other perhaps once in a generation, with seventeen dialects of clouding that have been drifting apart for as long as the sea has held its level. A town is maybe two thousand people spread over thirty square kilometres of reef and ridge below a black riveted tower, living off the ring and off worked cliffs, keeping a respectful distance from the outfall galleries, which are too loud even for them — not dangerous to the body, but blinding to the stones, the way a floodlight in the face is blinding. Near an outfall a person cannot tell where they are. They hate it. They go anyway, for reasons in section 8.

Out on the ridges between the rings, at intervals, there are households: a family, a colonnade of scars, a talus, a road. This is the country. It is very poor and extremely quiet and its people are famous for being slow speakers, which appears to be literally true — the crossing-clouds move at about two-thirds of town speed, and a town child mimicking it is being rude in a way that would be recognisable in any market on Earth.

### 7. The line of rods

Every fifty kilometres along certain compass lines there is an iron pile driven four metres into the rock, numbered, with a corroded plate. The people who drove them have been gone a long time. The rods are now the only roads across the void, and they are used constantly, and this is the single institution that spans the entire Standing Sea.

It works like this. Nothing grows on the plains between the rings. A crossing of two hundred kilometres is therefore a starvation march, ten or twelve days at a working pace with nothing to eat, and a body that arrives at the far side with an empty gut has perhaps three days of life left in it. Unless there is food at the rods.

So at the foot of each rod there is a cairn: a low heap of freshly split blocks, opened deliberately, left to ripen. It takes four or five years for a broken face to carry enough varnish to feed a traveller. The traveller eats it — grazes it down, rasps the cairn grey — and then, before going on, spends half a day with a hammerstone opening a new one beside it, for whoever comes next.

Nobody arranges this. There is no authority. There is only the rule, held in every town and every ridge household in every one of the seventeen dialects, that you do not leave a rod without leaving food ripening at it, because the food you just ate was cut for you by somebody who was dead before you were born and who did not know your name and could not have learned it. The cairns at the rods along the old survey lines run in series: this decade's, last decade's, grey ones, greyer, a mound of them, a few thousand years of the same courtesy.

And at the base of each rod, spilled around the iron, are the stones. Every traveller who passes leaves one from the sling — not a ritual with any elaboration to it, just a thing that is done — and the pile at rod 114 on the northeast line is now a metre deep, and it is the traffic of ten thousand years, and if you put your hand into it and take one out and hold it up in that unspendable light, it is polished smooth, and it is somebody's ear.

### 8. The cliffs that eat their own writing

They cut marks. Of course they do; they are a species whose whole relationship with the world is the controlled fracture of glass, and the marks are cut with the same tools and the same grammar of direction and rate that the clouding uses, which makes their writing look, to an outsider, like a transcript of weather.

But a cut face is a fresh face. A fresh face grows varnish. Varnish draws ribbon-grazers, and a ribbon-grazer is a rasp on a body forty centimetres long that does nothing all its life but mow. An inscription blooms within four years, is crowded within ten, and is licked back to a smooth illegible blush inside a century.

So: in a world where nothing rots and nothing erodes and every iron rod driven a thousand years ago is still standing exactly where it was driven — in a world built entirely for the permanent record — the one material that cannot hold a record is writing. Their history feeds them and is eaten in the feeding.

What follows is a whole profession. Texts that matter are *kept*: somebody sits at the face, for years, for decades, scraping grazers off a wall with the fine arms, taking them one at a time, dropping them over the edge. This is called keeping the wall and it is done by people who have chosen it, and a kept wall is a wall somebody is starving beside, and the length of a text is a straightforward statement about how much hunger a community is prepared to underwrite. Most walls are not kept. Most of everything anyone here has ever said is gone.

Except in one place. Inside the outfall galleries, within two or three hundred metres of the roar, no grazer will settle and no varnish thickens and nothing eats anything, because the sound simply will not permit a body to attend to its own mouth. And so along the inside of the eighty-metre halls, under the rusted arch-ribs, in the corona-blue dark, on the ribs and the wall plates and the raw cut rock, there is writing, the oldest writing in the sea, and it will not be erased until the mountain goes.

You cannot read it for long. The stones in your sling are being shaken to mush and you do not know which way is out. Twenty minutes is a great deal; an hour is a career-ending injury. So the deep history of these people is held in a place where it can be consulted only in fragments of a few lines at a time, by individuals who come out shaking and half-blind and must be led home, and who spend the following winter clouding, very slowly, in a ring in the middle of a belly, what they think they saw.

### 9. The room with the hooks

On the flank of one ridge there is a single small building of poured concrete with a steel door standing open: one room, a table, a bunk frame, a wall of hooks, a stove that could never have been lit here.

The hooks have hammerstones on them. Four hundred and some, hung one to a hook and then two and then three, the oldest at the back, worn round, glass-dark, with the grip-wear of a fold that has not existed for a hundred generations of anyone.

There was a town on that ridge once, and the room was its tool-store, and then the ridge was worked out and the town went, and it is a good sign that nobody took the tools with them. There is no cult in it. It is housekeeping. Two species, in sequence, tidying the same room.

They know, in the ordinary way that people know things, that somebody else was here first. Their sign for it is *the straight-line ones*, because nothing in this sea is straight except what those people left. Fracture makes curves. A scallop, a cone, a lip. Everything a person here has ever made is a curve. And then there is a rod, four metres, plumb, and a rectangular door, and a plate with edges, and the long true chord of a pipe gallery, and at the head of the basin seventeen black towers standing up out of the water with their vertical sides and their horizontal decks and their chairs.

### 10. Eleven seconds

The heart is entrained. A larva calibrates in its first four days out of the sling, taking the phase of the pulse and the bearings of two or three towers and fixing them, and from then on the animal's own eleven-second cycle runs locked to the Draw and the sea's fixed standing wave. It is why they are never lost. Anywhere in a thousand-kilometre basin, at any depth, in fog or dark or a ten-metre sea overhead, a person knows where they are to within a few tens of metres, continuously, the way you know where your hand is. There is no sign for *lost*. There is a sign for the other thing — for someone whose first four days went wrong, who imprinted off a bad echo in a trench and carries an offset of a few hundred metres for life, and who therefore walks with complete confidence into the wrong side of a node margin. Nobody can correct them. The map is not taught. It is simply had, and theirs is wrong, and the eels wait where the drift comes out of the loud country.

They do not know that the towers are machines. They know the towers are where the good water comes from, and where the noise comes from, and where the origin was, and that the world is nailed to them; this is all true and it is not a misunderstanding of anything. They have no reason to suppose the arrangement could end, and neither does the arrangement. Inflow equals evaporation plus leakage. The pulse sits on the seiche period. The wave has not moved in ten thousand years, and every node and belly and safe council ground and lethal margin and crossing route and cairn line and council-in-the-noise is where it is because a driver five kilometres down has not varied.

If it varied, nothing would die at once. The water would stay high for years. But the nodes would walk. The quiet would come into the towns and the eels would come with it into places where people have been speaking out loud, in white, in the open, for four hundred generations; and every person alive would be carrying, in a bag of polished stones under the gut, an exact and useless fix on a world that had moved.

None of this is anticipated. It is not a fear they have. It is simply the shape of the thing, seen from outside by someone improvising names, hanging in a column of daylight at a hundred and eighty metres with a wall of grey flake-scars going back into the mountain and a road of rust-coloured droppings running away north along the ridge, and eleven seconds later, eleven seconds later, eleven seconds later.

Nobody is at the towers. There are chairs. Somewhere out on the line between the ninth and the tenth, at the foot of an iron rod with a metre of ears heaped around it, a cairn is four years old and nearly ready, and the person who cut it has been dead since the winter.
