# The Inhabitants

## Hantlers

They are a mongrel stock — peat country below, ore country further below, four or five waves of people who came up for wages and then found that the way down had somehow lengthened — and eleven generations at this pressure have done what eleven generations do. The chest is deep and the shoulders are narrow, so that a Hantler undressed looks slightly barrelled, like something built to hold air rather than to lift. The hands are thick at the knuckle and slow. Not clumsy: slow on purpose, a whole population having learned that a dropped thing on the Kell does not stop falling.

The skin greys. Everyone's does, eventually; grit gets into the pores and stays, and by fifty a mill-hand's forearms have the soft dull finish of unpolished slate. They are not embarrassed by this. The colour is understood to be earned, and there is a word for a newcomer's pink complexion that translates roughly as *unstarted*.

The eyes are the strangest adaptation and the one that survives a move downhill. There is no sun here, only the smeared lamp under the sheet, and an eye raised on even light becomes an instrument for texture rather than hue. A Hantler can distinguish the grain of two stones at four hundred paces in what an outsider would call dusk. A Hantler can also stand in front of a vermilion door and a rust-red one and call them the same word, which is *smelter-coloured*, which covers everything from blood to flame and is imported vocabulary anyway. There are seventeen ordinary words for grey and no poetry about any of them, because you do not write poems about the alphabet.

And they lean. The oldest streets tilt inward a few degrees, and the newer ones are built level but will not stay level, and a body that grows up on a slow-rolling deck sets itself to compensate. Put a Hantler on a true horizontal floor and they list very slightly uphill toward a hill that isn't there. You can pick them out in any port below the cloud-deck by this alone: the ones standing at a patient, courteous angle to the world, as though listening to it.

## How they talk

Loudly, flatly, and twice.

The dumb air above the Line eats speech, and though the city proper is mostly below it, the habit has come down the mountain the way habits do. A Hantler says the important part of a sentence, then says it again in different words, and the person listening repeats back the part they intend to act on. Whole conversations are conducted in this ledger-grammar, and to an outsider it sounds either hostile or half-witted. *Six hours. The sixth, not the fifth.* — *The sixth. I have it.* — *You have it.* The exchange is finished not when the meaning has landed but when both parties have entered it in each other.

Silence between two people walking together is a rudeness bordering on assault, and above the Line it is a crime; the high crews are required by ordinance to speak a minimum of once every hundred and twenty seconds, anything at all, the price of tallow, a list of aunts. Men have been fined for going quiet in fog. Men have also not come back from going quiet in fog, which is the actual point of the fine.

Sentences here are three breaths long, because the stair between the Grit Row and the Second Landing is a hundred and forty steps and nobody wastes breath on subordinate clauses. Hantle prose, in its one newspaper and its enormous archive, is short, declarative, and deadly dull, and the city considers this a moral achievement.

## Names

A name is a structure and it is built with redundancy, the way a bridge is.

Nobody on the Kell has a single name. They have a filed name, which includes a doubled element, so that if a letter drifts — and letters drift, up here; the drift is small and constant and nobody pretends otherwise — the corruption is visible by comparison with its own echo. *Dessa Onwen Onwenar, Sixth Stair, Grit Row, moth-scraper, daughter of Halle Onwenar who was a mill-hand and died in the winter of the collapsed truss.* Half of that is not decoration. It is parity check. If she comes down off a comb-ridge one evening as *Onwenar Onwen* she will notice, or her name-holder will.

Name-holders are the other half of the system, and they are an institution as old as the funiculars. Every citizen is assigned a stranger — deliberately a stranger, deliberately from a different ridge and a different trade — who commits their name and particulars to memory and is required to meet them face to face four times a year and say the name back to them. The meetings are brief and formal and are held in tea-rooms built for the purpose. You do not become friends with your name-holder; friendship would defeat the redundancy. What you become is something the language below the cloud has no word for: the person who can tell you that you have changed.

On waking, most Hantlers say their own name once, aloud, before they say anything else. Visitors find this eerie. Hantlers find visitors, who apparently sleep without precautions, frankly reckless.

## The habit of witness

The greeting is not *good morning*. The greeting is **I saw you**, and the answer is **I was there**, and it is a small mutual deposit made several hundred times a day across the whole eleven kilometres of the city.

Everything follows from this. Gossip in Hantle is not a vice; it is masonry. The paper devotes eleven of its sixteen pages to the *Seen* column, dense agate type, thousands of entries — *Hollem Burr Burrach at the Fourth Landing at the third hour, carrying a copper kettle, in a brown coat. Two of the Aske family on the upper truss, arguing.* You may buy an entry. It is not vanity to do so and nobody pretends it is anything but what it is, which is the purchase of a small quantity of having-happened.

Which is also what wealth is, here. The rich are the most written-about, and they became rich by being written about, and they stay rich by continuing to be. Old families have ledger-lines running back nine generations, thick as cable, and they walk around inside them like men in armour. They commission portraits in quantity, badly painted and enormous. They employ *witnesses* outright: salaried people whose entire office is to be in the room, to look, and to write down afterwards what was worn and what was said. The poor are thin, and know it, and the word for poverty on the Kell translates as *thinly attended*. The charities of the city do not distribute bread. They distribute attention: visiting societies, reading circles, paid strangers who will come and sit with you and describe you back to yourself on a Tuesday.

And the great crime — greater than theft, tried in a separate court, carrying the only sentence the city still calls *permanent* — is false entry. To write down a thing that did not occur is to put sand in the mortar of the entire mountain. Forgers exist. They sell thickened pasts to thin people, invented childhoods, counterfeit *Seen* entries, whole fabricated aunts. They are hunted with a zeal that outsiders read as religious and that Hantle regards as structural engineering.

## The dogs

Every dog on the Kell is named in the Registry, and the names are absurd: four and five elements, doubled like a person's, *Grib Gribbenar of the Pin-Yard, black with one white foot, barks at the ore-buckets.* A dog is a cheap way of making a place happen. A dog is present, loudly, continuously, and it notices things, and it can be written about for very little money. Poor families who cannot afford a witness keep three dogs.

They are small, wiry, deaf on the mill side, and there are thousands of them. At the eighth watch the whole eleven-kilometre smear of the city barks at once at something nobody has ever identified.

## The trades, briefly, by the bodies they make

**Mill-hands** go deaf by thirty and speak with their hands and their faces; the sign-language of the stamp floors has escaped into the general population, so that Hantlers gesture while they talk even in quiet rooms, even below the cloud, even in exile.

**Etchers** have pitted knuckles, no eyebrows, no eyelashes, and a particular vocal roughness from the flue-sulphur. They are the best-paid and the shortest-lived and the most superstitious, and they work in threes on rope stages with five hundred metres of nothing under their boots, and each of the three says the other two's names aloud at the top of every hour.

**Lichen-tenders** are slow, soft-spoken, unhurried people with green-black crescents permanently under the nails, and they are treated with a deference that the etchers resent. They talk about the year two hundred as though it were a colleague.

**Moth-scrapers** carry a faint luminescence in the creases of their wrists that shows up in the dark of an unlit alley. Their guild is the oldest and quarrels about precedence at every civic function. Their children glow slightly too, until about eleven.

**Pacers** are the strangest of the guilds. Their bodies *are* the instrument, so their bodies must not be allowed to drift: they are weighed monthly, measured, photographed, described in the Registry in a detail applied to nobody else — the length of the stride, the wear pattern of the boot, a mole, a chipped tooth. A pacer walks with a string of counting beads and a metronome heart and an expression of total, humourless concentration, and a pacer's certificate outweighs a theodolite in any court on the mountain. They retire early. Something about walking provisional ground for a living thins a person from the inside in a way that no amount of documentation seems to fill, and the Guild pension is generous, and the pensioners mostly take warden huts and sit and look at rock until they die.

**Wardens** are the old and the broken, six hundred of them in tin huts, four hours a shift, paid a pittance and honoured out of all proportion. They keep bad tea. They know their assigned faces of rock the way other people know a spouse. *Ninth hour: mist on the east buttress, cleared. A stept, alone, crossing left to right. Nothing else.* A warden who reports *nothing else* for eleven years is not idle. He has held a kilometre of cliff in place with his eyes, and at his funeral they will say so.

## Children

Children are thin. This is not a judgement; it is a statement of the obvious. Very little has happened to them.

So they are kept low — below the Line, below the trusses, on the sheltered inward streets where the world behaves — and they are kept loud, and they are kept *seen*. Hantle schoolrooms have their windows facing the street, and the street knows it, and passers-by are expected to glance in and count heads. A child is walked from household to household on its name-day to be looked at by forty strangers. Birthdays are not counted in days but in events, and an eight-year-old on the Kell means a child to whom eight recorded things have happened: a fall, a fever, a first day at the combs, a dog's death, the winter the truss went.

The first scar is a small feast. There are families down in Grit Row who do not wait for the accident, and the Registry, which is not sentimental and has looked at the alternative, does not ask.

## The high people

A few hundred live outside the city entirely, where the built stretches give out and there is only bare shelf, a comb-field, a hut with a tin chimney.

Herders. They are lean and laconic and they smell of stept — that wet-felt, flinty, faintly sweet smell that no washing removes — and the city half-trusts them, the way a port half-trusts sailors. Above the Line they do not use names. Names are exactly what drifts, and the herders' position, arrived at over eleven generations and never once argued in the Registry because they will not come down to argue it, is that a name carried into provisional country is bait. So they take the bell instead. Each herder wears one, tuned to the lead animal's, and above the Line they answer to the note and not to the word, and they say — those few who will say anything — that the stept are simply better at this than people are, that a stept has never once come back with its details altered, and that the correct way to walk on unwritten stone is to be slightly less insistent about being there.

Their children are raised to the bell and sent down at twelve to be filed, and about a third of them will not go.

## The Unwritten

And then there are those who go up on purpose.

They are not numerous and the city does not persecute them, which is itself a kind of statement. They shave their names — there is a procedure, a form, a fee — and they hand in their beads and their certificates and their bells, and they walk up past the last comb-field into the grey. The doctrine, insofar as anyone has written it down, is that thickness is a tax; that a life spent accumulating ballast for a mountain is a life spent as mortar; that above the Line a person is provisional, yes, and a provisional person is also *unfinished*, and unfinished is not the same as dead.

Etch-crews see them sometimes on faces that no rope-stage has ever reached, standing, not doing anything, and they are not always quite the right number when counted twice. High crews report hearing their own conversations four minutes late from the wrong side, in voices that are close to theirs but not theirs. The Registry files every sighting without comment. The Registry files everything without comment; that is its entire method; and the sightings go into the ledgers and the ledgers go into the nine windowless storeys and are thereby made to have happened, which is, if you think about it for a moment, precisely the thing the Unwritten went up the mountain to escape.

## The Reading

The Registry never stops. It cannot: it runs its own generator and its own dynamo shed and a staff of four hundred, pale as cistern shrimp, who copy every document three times because paper is ballast and three of a thing is heavier than one.

But the real work happens at night — at the dark end of the twenty-seven-hour cycle, in the long room on the sixth floor, where the readers sit. Sixty of them at any hour, in rotation, reading the old ledgers aloud. Not to each other. Not to anyone. They read court records from the fourth generation, tax rolls, the names of dogs a hundred years dead, the warden's note about mist on the east buttress, cleared, out loud, steadily, into a windowless room, and they have done this without a break for seven hundred thousand hours, and the mountain is still there.

Ask a Hantler whether this does anything and you will get the flat double-said answer of a people who have thought about it more than you have. *It has never failed. Not once has it failed.*

## The Dead

They are carried up. It is a long carry and it is done on foot, never by cable, and the family goes and the name-holder goes and the wardens along the route come out of their huts and stand.

They are buried high, in raw rock, above the Line, because a body is an enormous quantity of having-happened and it is a waste to put it where things already are. Families own faces the way families elsewhere own plots, and a good face — south-facing, unlined, never yet witnessed — is the most valuable property on the Kell and cannot be bought with money, only inherited or earned.

And at the graveside they read. Everything: every entry, every *Seen*, every court appearance, every dog, every recorded thing the person did, from the Registry copy carried up in a satchel, aloud, to the stone. It takes hours. For an old and busy citizen it takes a day and a night and they spell each other. This is the aristocracy of the dead and everyone knows it: the measure of a life on this mountain is how long it takes to read it to the rock, and the rock is being told, patiently, in flat Hantle prose, *this occurred, and this, and this, and now you have had it happen to you.*

Then they come down, and the wardens go back into their huts, and somebody says *I saw you*, and somebody answers *I was there.*

## And whatever is pushing

Nobody lives at the summit. There is no summit; the elevation is pending.

But the sub-bass comes up through the pins and the rails and the soles of the boots, once every few minutes, patient, unhurried, and the pin-inspectors — who are not really inspecting pins, everyone knows this, they are listening — keep a separate ledger that goes to a floor of the Registry that has no public stair.

The herders' version, offered rarely and without emphasis, is that it isn't pushing. It's breathing. The city has built itself onto something enormous and new and not yet obliged to have a history, and for eleven generations forty thousand people have beaten stone into sand and sprayed acid off ropes and grown lichen in trays and sat in tin huts looking at cliffs and read the names of dogs aloud into a windowless room, all of it to write a past onto a thing that does not have one, and the thing has held still for it.

The herders do not say that this is a kindness being done to the mountain.

They say it is the mountain, having come up out of somewhere deep with nothing on it at all, growing its first thin skin of weather — and that the weather is us.
