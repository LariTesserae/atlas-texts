# The Tenant

The honest census is short and badly weighted: one tree, one cushion of moss, one stripe of black-green varnish, and a population you cannot see which numbers in the trillions and would together weigh less than a mouse. Nothing in the room is more than a few grams of anything. This is not a poor place, exactly. It is a place where the entire biological project has been re-costed at the local rate.

Start with the tree, because the tree is the householder.

---

## The fig

What you see above ground — the squat grey thing with its fist of leaves, no taller than you — is not the organism. It is the organism's small, exposed, expendable end, the part it puts out into the weather in order to buy carbon, and it has been kept deliberately, ruthlessly small. The plant proper is in the room. There is more of it on the ceiling than there is in the sun. If you could pull the whole thing out intact and lay it on the pan, it would run out for tens of metres in every direction, kilometres of root end to end, and the crown would sit in the middle of that sprawl like a knuckle on a hand of enormous fingers.

It got here by accident. A bird or a fruit bat passed over this pan carrying a gut full of seed and voided some of it at the lip of the shaft, which is the only place on the whole graded floor where a fig seedling could have lived: the one mouth in the landscape that breathes damp, twice a day, a handful of air at a time. Everything the tree is now follows from that. It is not competing with the grey subshrubs and their polite rings of bare ground; it is not in their world at all. They are drinking the two centimetres of rain the pan gets. It is drinking the chamber.

Understand what that means physically. There is no water down there. There is a film of moisture in the pores of the dolomite, a few tenths of a percent by weight, held in the rock at a tension that would collapse the plumbing of any plant you have ever eaten. The fig's fine white hairs, spread under the silt in that slow radial map, are working against tens of atmospheres of suction, harvesting vapour, hair by hair, and passing it up through a xylem so narrow and so heavily armoured against cavitation that the wood is closer to horn than to timber. A knife will not enjoy it. The growth rings are thinner than paper and frequently absent, which is why the tree's age is not a question anyone can settle: four hundred years, three thousand, the wood declines to say.

Its year has two halves and the halves belong to different worlds. The crown lives in the calendar — the burning months, the two cold weeks, the clench and unclench of the leaves, the brief indecent luxury after a rain. The roots live in no calendar whatsoever. Down there the temperature never moves, the humidity never moves, there is no day, and so the root does not bother with any of the timekeeping that plants normally maintain. It has one behaviour and it performs it continuously: extend, flatten, grip, absorb. A finger's width in a decade. It is the fastest process in the chamber and by any standard elsewhere it is not a process at all.

It is worth saying plainly that the room the fig lives in is not the room you are standing in. You see a dissolved hall, fluted, lobed, scalloped, no corners, beautiful in the way of the inside of a shell. The fig has no access to any of that. What the fig has is a topography of dampness: a broad slightly-moist vertical surface, a moister floor, a dangerous dry throat above through which the whole enterprise must pass. The exquisite fluting registers, if it registers at all, as additional surface area. The three fallen blocks are obstacles that were routed around some centuries ago. The beauty is entirely yours; you brought it in with you, along with the heat and the breath.

And it is quietly, minutely undoing the room. The account of the chamber says that nothing is being built here anymore, and that is true. But the root tips exude acid, and acid on carbonate does what it always does. In the zone of the fine hairs the dolomite is very slightly softer than it was, and a fraction of the white efflorescence at the margin is not simply the memory of the last water; it is the residue of a transaction between the rock and something alive. The fig is very slowly eating the house. At its rate this is not vandalism. It is closer to settling.

## The tenancy before

Look at the grey flowstone curtain on the eastern wall, the one that flakes. Some of the flaking follows tubes. There are hollow channels in it, finger-thick and thinner, running down through the calcite, and they are casts: the negative shapes of roots that came down this same shaft, gripped this same wall, and were buried by the last centuries of dripping before the water gave up. Under the silt, if you went down through the polygons, you would find more — a felt of them, mineralised, fossilised, replaced, at three or four levels.

So this is not a discovery you are making. This is a slot, and it has been occupied, serially, by the same kind of tenant, since before the water left. Fig, gap, fig, gap, fig. Each occupancy a few thousand years, each ended by a drought too long even for this arrangement, or a bird that failed to come, or nothing in particular. Then a gap of ten thousand years in which the chamber held its number and waited without waiting, and then another seed at the lip of the shaft, and the whole apparatus reassembled from scratch in the dark along almost exactly the old routes, because the routes were never a choice — they were the shape of the damp.

The current tenant may or may not be able to reproduce. Figs need their wasps. Whether any wasp population survives within reach of this pan is not a thing the pan will tell you. Perhaps once a decade the crown sets a synchronous crop and, across a landscape that erases distance, the insects arrive, and for four days there is more animal life in the top of that little grey tree than has existed in the chamber since the mountains, and not one of them goes down the hole. Or perhaps that stopped long ago and every fig on this pan is one clone, propagating by root suckers, sexless, patient, occupying breathing-holes one at a time.

## The moss

In the low alcove: a grey-brown scurf, brittle as ash, perhaps a hand's span, weighing a few grams dry. This is the second householder and it keeps time in a completely different unit.

It is desiccation-tolerant in the full sense — not thrifty with water but indifferent to having none, able to shut down to a chemical standstill and hold that condition for decades or centuries with no cost and no decay, because in this room there is nothing to decay it. It does not sleep. Sleeping is a metabolism. It simply stops, in the position of stopping, like everything else here.

What it waits for is a flood. Once in a very long while — long enough that the intact dusty spider's web downstairs has never seen one — a storm arrives on the pan that the caliche cannot absorb, and the dished floor does what it was graded to do, and gathers, and finds the one hole under the fig, and sends a tongue of brown water six body-lengths down into the warm dark. That is the chamber's weather: two or three days of it per millennium. It lays down a new layer of silt, which is where the polygons come from. It dissolves the salt flowers and re-deposits them slightly differently. And it wets the alcove, and within a quarter of an hour the scurf swells and goes green — actually green, the only green in the chamber's history — and photosynthesises furiously on nothing but the grey scatter from the shaft, and grows perhaps a millimetre, and sets spores, and then dries, and stops.

Total time lived, in the sense of chemistry running: a few weeks. Elapsed time: possibly longer than the tree lineage. It is the oldest and least experienced thing in the room.

## The stripe

The black-green varnish on the wall where the daily bar of light lands is a crustose lichen and a smear of desert algae, working on a photon budget of some minutes a day, at a duty cycle that would embarrass a machine. Its water is the same pore film the fig drinks, taken directly from the stone. Its growth rate is measured in hundredths of a millimetre a year, which means the patch you can cover with your palm is older than agriculture.

It has drawn the sun's annual wander on the wall, precisely, because it can only live exactly where the light goes. And above and below the living band there are ghost bands — relict smudges, dead, dry, the same shape shifted a hand's width — recording the times when the shaft's mouth was wider, or narrower, or partly blocked by a root-buttress that has since rotted out and been replaced. It is a sundial that eats its own light and keeps its own archive, and it is doing this at a metabolic rate slower than the room's chemistry.

## The majority

The census in the description is a census of the visible, which in a place like this is a census of almost nothing.

In the silt, in the pore space of the dolomite, on the powdery surface of the dead flowstone, in the salt crust, and above all in a dense sheath around every one of the fig's white root hairs, there are cells. Bacteria and archaea, oligotrophs, mineral-scrapers, living on iron and manganese and traces of sulphide, on the last dissolved organics that the last flood brought down, and — in the root zone — on the sugars the fig leaks as a matter of course, because even at this rate a root cannot help paying its neighbours something.

They are the reason the varnish is black and not white. They are part of why the flowstone is grey and powders. Their doubling times, here, are not measured in hours. Some of them divide about once per human lifetime. A colony the size of a full stop may have been assembling since the water left. This is the population of the chamber — enormous in number, negligible in mass, and slow in a way that makes the fig look frantic.

## Not residents

Two things in the room lived and are not tenants, and both deserve a moment.

The spider came down as a spiderling on a thread of ballooning silk, or fell, and did in the dark what it was built to do: it made a complete and correct web under the largest block. The web is a proposition. The proposition is *there will be flying insects here*. It is the only false statement in the chamber. Everything else — the shape of the walls, the grade of the pebbles, the closed leaves, the dormant moss, the stripe on the wall — is exactly and locally true. The spider's one contribution to this place was to be wrong about it, beautifully, in tensioned silk, and then to stop.
