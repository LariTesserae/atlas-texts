## The Living, Ascending

### The films

Start at the bottom, because everything here does.

The grey-brown skins in the pools are not one thing but perhaps forty, and the people distinguish them by taste and by feel and have a sign for each. There is the one like cold metal that grows in a day and is worth nothing. There is the thick one, dark as a bruise, that takes a year and tastes of matches and keeps a person alive. There is the pale one, almost white, slimier than the rest, that grows only in pools within two paces of a working mouth, and which is sweet — genuinely sweet, the only sweet thing in this world — and which is given to children and to the very old and to anyone who has just come out of a bad wind alive.

They are farmed. It took me a month to see it. A pool that has been *made* has a rim a finger higher than nature built it, patched up with chalk paste so the water sits longer and stiller; and a pool that has been made will be bare on one side where a palm scraped it eight days ago, and furred on the other where the same palm seeded it. A family's pools are scattered over two or three kilometres and are not marked, and there is no dispute about them, because everyone can hear exactly where everyone else is and has been able to since birth.

Harvest is a bone scraper and a grown bowl and a squatting posture held for a long time. The sign for film is the flat hand pressed on the sternum, which is also the sign for *enough*, and for *ordinary*, and for *the way things are*.

### The nails

On the films, in some pools and not others, the flat blind crawlers. The people call them by a sign that is two fingers walking on the back of a wrist. They are the size of a fingernail and are eaten by the handful, alive, and taste of nothing much, and are the reason anyone here has teeth left at all.

Gathering them is children's work, and it is done at the throats, where the exhalation is that one degree warmer and the crawlers mass on the rims in slow crusts. This is also where the gas is thickest. Every family has lost a child to a still hollow full of nails.

### The spat

Twice in a lifetime, or less, a mouth brings up something from underneath.

I saw one. A blind eel the length of a forearm, unpigmented to the point of translucency, with a rasping wheel of a mouth and no fins to speak of, and it had come from wherever the aquifer goes, and it died in the air in about ninety seconds. It was the richest thing available to these people and they did not eat it immediately. They carried it to a silence and sat with it and passed it hand to hand and let everyone put a palm on it, and only then.

A mouth that has spat is marked with a ring of pressed clay and may never be stopped, and the family whose ground it is will be treated differently for two generations.

The other things that come up are stones: hard glassy nodules, chert or something like it, the only substance in the basin that will take an edge and hold it. There is no fire here and therefore no metal, and carbonate cuts nothing, so every blade in this world was vomited out of a singing throat by luck. They are inherited. They are named. Wealth, in the flats, is a hard thing you did not make.

### The listeners

They are the largest animal, the only large animal, and everything the people own that is not stone came off one: hide for the soles of the feet and the door of a hut, fat to grease a skin that will otherwise macerate off the bone, sinew, and a hundred kinds of tool from the long bones and the flat wide teeth.

They are almost never killed. They are *found*. A dead listener slumped against a tube alters that tube's note, and an altered note in this basin is a scream — it can be heard for kilometres by people whose entire nervous system is a map of pitch, and it will be relayed by hand-stopping to the edges of the flats within a day. So the gift arrives as a wrongness in the chord, and men and women walk three days toward a sour interval and find the carcass, and everyone who came gets a share.

I want to be careful here. The people do not regard the listeners as animals in the sense we would use, and they do not regard them as gods either. The sign for a listener is one of only two signs made on another person's face, and it is the same sign used for the falling of the note. What I think they mean — and I had sixty signs and a bad accent, so discount this — is *a decision already made, elsewhere, that you will live inside*. A herd walking through the drone-quarter is not a misfortune. It is the future arriving on four legs, moving like something being poured, and taking two hundred voices out of the chord that your grandchildren will not know were ever there.

### The people

There are perhaps two hundred of them in an area the size of a small country.

They are short and very deep in the chest, long-armed, with hands that are enormous relative to everything else, because the hands are the mouth. The skin is bleached the colour of the floor and is wrinkled everywhere, permanently, the way a hand is wrinkled after an hour in water, because there is no hour of any life here that is not spent in water. They are greased head to foot with listener fat and it is the only smell in the basin that isn't stone. The soles of the feet are horn, three-quarters of a centimetre thick, and are the most valuable thing a person owns and the last thing to be repaired before travel.

Their front teeth are notched and worn flat in a way I did not understand until I saw a woman testing a tube she was thinking of stopping. You bite it. You set your incisors against the wall and the note comes up the jaw into the base of the skull with a clarity you cannot get any other way, and if you do this several times a day for fifty years your teeth wear a groove that fits a tube wall exactly.

The eyes are large, pale and nearly useless, and nobody looks at anybody. This is the thing that unsettles a visitor most, more than the sound. Speech is touch, and the face is not for speaking, so a group of people in conversation stands shoulder to shoulder in a knot, all facing outward into the mist, hands moving on each other's forearms and backs and sternums. I spent a season among them and could not have described any one of their faces.

They sleep in contact. A palm on a companion is not affection, it is a held-open channel; it is the equivalent of being spoken to occasionally through the night. To wake alone is the worst ordinary thing that happens to a person here, and there are signs for it, several, and they are used the way we use words for grief.

### Names

A name is a pitch, and the pitch is a place.

You are named for the mouth nearest where you were born, whatever note it happened to be holding that year, and thereafter your name is not something anyone says. It is somewhere anyone can walk. To speak of an absent person you take your companion's hand and turn it uppitch or downpitch and give the interval; to speak *to* them you find their mouth and cover it in a rhythm they will recognise, and every mouth in this basin is a telephone, and the stopping-patterns are personal, and a child learns its parents' patterns before it learns to walk.
