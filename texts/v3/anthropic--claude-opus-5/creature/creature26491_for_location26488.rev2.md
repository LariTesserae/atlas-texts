## The People

A hundred and thirty-one at the last count, which was taken in a flowering year, as all counts are.

They are a small, pale, long-fingered people with excellent night vision and bad sun tolerance, and they look, to a visitor coming down off the rim, unwell — which they are not. They are simply lit wrong. Everything about their colouring has been arranged by four centuries of flat green light coming down between columns: skin like the underside of a mushroom, eyes on the light end of whatever they started as, a squint that arrives at about thirty and never leaves. Up on the gravel in open daylight they hold a hand at the brow without noticing they are doing it. Some of them have never been over the rim at all, and of those, several have declined the opportunity on the grounds that they have heard what it is like.

They are not thin. The fibre trade pays, in its slow way, and the rim allotments do well enough in grit. But they are undersized by about a head compared to the carters who bring the flour, and there is a bow to the older legs that the Company physician attributes to damp and lack of sun and treats with the bracket fungus, boiled, which tastes of pond and works.

Their hands are the parts of them that have been most thoroughly rewritten. Grafters' knuckles thicken from a lifetime of tying wet cord in cold; tappers carry a single scar on the ball of the thumb, always the same scar, always in the same place, received in the first year of the trade and functioning thereafter as a qualification. The lacquer crew are amber to the wrist and cannot be scrubbed back, and the whole family of a lacquer cook smells faintly of iodine and cut grass for as long as he holds the post, which is generally until he dies. That smell is considered attractive. Marriages have been made on it.

## Voices

They speak. It is the first thing outsiders get wrong.

What they do not do is *voice* — the vocal folds barely close. Pan speech is a fast, precise, breathy whisper pitched deliberately underneath the drip, so that the drip masks it at twelve metres and carries it perfectly at two. It is a dialect built for unvoiced transmission: vowels flattened almost out of existence, consonant clusters doing all the work, which is why the names sound to a stranger like small mechanical events. Skeff. Orst. Hulm. Kesk. Prett. Sithe.

Children are given hand-signs before they are given names and speak, on average, at four. They are not slow; there is simply no hurry, and nothing they want that a sign will not get them. A Pan adult can conduct an entire working day — a shift, a meal, an argument, a reconciliation — without once closing the folds.

The sign language runs to two hundred and eleven standardised forms and perhaps four hundred colloquialisms, and is made almost entirely between the sternum and the hip, with the forearms. It uses the face hardly at all. This is not reserve. It is fog. A face is unreadable at six metres in the morning; a forearm against a grey coat is legible at fifteen. For the same reason the signs are large, slow, and unrepeated — repetition is an insult, meaning *you are not watching me*.

Affection is manual. The greeting is two fingers laid on the other's forearm, held slightly longer than necessary. The idiom for love is borrowed whole from the second rule: *I would lacquer you before dark.* They sign it more often than you would expect from a people this dry.

Deafness is not a disability here and never has been. The deaf are over-represented in the trades that have to happen on the rim, and the smiths, who are deaf by fifty from the hammer-shed, are regarded as competent professionals with an unfortunate accent.

## Everybody Owns a Distance

There is no coinage in the Pan. Coins clink. Money is written — the debt ledger is the only currency, and it is a real currency, transferable, discountable, quoted in kilos of retted fibre. Outside money is taken from visitors at the rim station and held in a felt-lined box, which is the single most foreign object in the basin and gets looked at.

A family is a number. Because the charter grants position relative to the leading edge and not ground, the deeds read as distances, and after four hundred years the distances have become the surnames. House Nineteen. The Forty-Sixes. Old Ninety, meaning the family and not the metre. Status is entirely legible as geography: the forward houses are the new nodes, soft-walled, undepleted, warm, with the first pull of the morning lens; the back houses are the silvered dry ones among the sap-cell racks, where the drip is louder and the water comes through the taps last and tasting of the racks. A family that prospers *buys forward*, a metre at a time, against the pin. A family that fails *goes back*. Bankruptcy has a direction and a speed, and everyone can see it happening from across the basin, which is thought to be the main reason it happens so rarely.

The ledger's first page has been re-copied every nine years for as long as there has been a ledger. It lists, as principal creditor, the Stand — the plant, the clone, the one organism — with a balance that is not a number but a phrase, carried forward unpaid since the founding. Nobody in the Pan would call this religion. They would call it the first page.

## The Order of a Life

You begin at the rim and end under the trailing edge, and in between you go southeast at one metre twenty a year, which is the speed of the plant, so that in a hundred and twenty metres of life you do not really walk anywhere. The basin walks through you.

Births happen above the leading edge, in the birth-shed on the northwest gravel, because labour is loud and a shift is a shift. The child comes down into the Pan at the first fog after, and is given its sign-name there, by the Listener, in front of whoever is not on shift. Everyone in the Pan has therefore been carried into it. They make something of this.

At six you turn beetle trays. At ten you gather shed sheaths and work the retting in the Thread, which is filthy and freezing and universally remembered fondly. Somewhere between eleven and thirteen you make the squeakless crossing — the whole basin barefoot, side to side, on marl, without raising the chalky noise — and after you have done it you are permitted to carry tools. The young graft, because grafting wants a straight back, a long reach and an indifference to consequence. The middle-aged tap, card, cook lacquer, keep the lamps, run the racks. The old keep the ledger, read the fines, and listen.

Grafters tie the leading edge for houses they will not live in. This is stated plainly to them at fourteen and again at every flowering, and it is the nearest thing the town has to a creed. A grafter signs the inside of every node she trains, in the callus, where it will stay for four hundred years and then rot into peat, and any adult in the Pan can tell you, without looking it up, the name of the woman who grew the room they sleep in and roughly when she died.

## The Back

The dead nodes have no valves. They cannot clamp. The ruins are therefore the only place inside the basin where noise is free, and the town's entire suppressed register lives down there among the racks and the standing fog.

Quarrels go to the Back. Courting goes to the Back. Grief goes to the Back — mourning aloud is permitted nowhere else and is not thought shameful, merely located. Children are sent to the Back to scream twice a week, formally, in a group, under the supervision of someone's grandmother, in what the Company minutes call *release* and everyone else calls the shouting. The far ruins smell of brine felt, wet pith and old amber, and tick continuously with drip, and are not frightening to anyone raised here.

Each person, once, in the flowering of their sixteenth year, gives the plant one object. It is pressed into a fresh wound at chest height and lacquered before dark and left, and from that day it walks away from its owner at one metre twenty a year, and may be visited. This is rule five, and it is the reason the ruins are a museum of deliberate offerings rather than a rubbish tip: the bicycle frame, the lamp bracket, the spectacles in profile in the amber, the tools whose use has genuinely been forgotten because the giver chose them for private reasons and told nobody. Prying at any of it is the offence for which visitors are expelled. There is a scar on the node wall beside the spectacles where, nine years ago, somebody tried.

The dead go in at the very back, into the peat, unmarked. What is recorded is the metre-count. *How far is your mother?* — *Eleven metres.* A widow's first year is called the first metre. Distance is how they hold time, and a very old man in the Pan can tell you that his father is ninety-four metres behind him and his wife is thirty-one, and he will say it the way anyone else says a date.

## The Listener

There is one salaried post in the Company whose entire duty is to lie awake.

The Listener is old, usually a woman, usually someone whose hands have gone. She sleeps through the day and is awake for the dawn surge, and she listens to the long groan that runs around the basin — the rope through the ring — and logs it. Pitch, duration, whether it goes clockwise or against, whether any node comes in late. Four centuries of these logs exist. They are the Company's only real science and its only real scripture, and three times in recorded history a Listener has caught a failing quarter of the Stand from a change in the groan nobody else could hear, and the ledger duly notes what that was worth.

She also names the children. Nobody can now say why those two duties are held by the same person.

## What They Are Bad At

Four hundred years. They are magnificent at four hundred years. They can tell you what the town will be doing in eleven flowerings' time and they will be doing it.

Four minutes undoes them.

The protocol for fire is a red shutter on the Turn's mast and a walk. The protocol for a fall is a walk. There are two cases in the Listener's log within living memory of a person dying in the time it took the basin to arrive without running, and both entries are short, and both are read aloud — signed aloud — at every flowering, and nothing has ever been changed as a result.

The single most discussed event of the last generation is the woman at House Fifty-Three who screamed when her child went off a walkway. The Works lost the shift. The fine was assessed at the standard rate and entered in her name, and then paid within two days by anonymous subscription, in full, from about ninety separate accounts. Both facts stand in the ledger. The town regards this as a complete and satisfactory resolution and becomes uncomfortable if you press it.

The oldest document they hold is a fine, in fibre, levied against a man for laughing. His name is in it. His descendants are still at House Nineteen, and once every nine years, on the first night of the flowering, somebody chalks the sign for *funny* on their door. It is the only joke in the Pan older than anyone alive, and it is told by three strokes of chalk, in the dark, by a person nobody sees.

## The Loud One

The rim station employs an outsider on a nine-year contract: a factor's agent, a carter's clerk, someone from downcountry with a normal voice, whose job is to meet the wagons, argue over grades of fibre, take delivery of flour and salt and cod-liver, and absorb all the shouting so that none of it comes down. The post is well paid and mildly disreputable. The holder lives on the gravel, may not sleep in the basin, and is not invited to the flowering.

Children go up to the rim on dares to hear him speak at full volume, the way children elsewhere go to see a bull.

## Eleven Days

During the flowering every valve opens to the spikes and the clamp reflex is suspended. For eleven days, once in nine years, the Pan cannot punish noise.

They know this. It is in the charter. And what happens is that the town sits in the dark under the rotten-honey smell with the moths coming off the gravel in thousands, and the lamps dying one rack at a time, and holds its court and strikes its balances and marries its people and drives its pin by candle, and sings — under the breath. Because they cannot do otherwise. The young ones try, most flowerings; two or three of them go back into the ruins and attempt to shout, and it comes out wrong, thin and shapeless and shocking, and they stop.

The exception is the very old, who are past caring. There is usually one. He stands out among the spikes on about the ninth night and sings in full voice, badly, a song the town has otherwise only ever heard whispered, and a hundred and thirty people lie in their nodes in the dark and listen to it the way you would listen to weather.

Then the flowering ends, and the valves shut, and the Turn takes up its stroke, and somebody goes round with the felt.
