## The Tenants

The first thing to understand is that the Hummock is fully occupied. There is no vacancy anywhere in it. Every cistern has frogs, every duct has its bootlace snake, every plaza has its heron and its column of grainy light, and the tissue itself is packed to capacity with the slow arithmetic that keeps growing stairs. Nothing here is empty in the sense of unused.

It is empty in a narrower and much worse sense. It is empty of the thing it is for.

You will feel this on the stairs, before you can say why. Eleven risers, all of them level, all of them the same, and all of them wrong — a hundred and thirty millimetres, which is short, which makes a person of any ordinary height climb like someone descending, mincing, in a hurry they don't have. The handrail is at seven hundred and eighty. That is low. You take it and you find yourself stooping to it, and your palm does not fit the worn place, because the worn place is not a palm-shape. It is a groove. Something hooked that rail, four times, in the same four positions, ten thousand times a day for an era, and hauled itself up eleven short steps, and the town has been reproducing the marks of that grip ever since with the fidelity of a mourner.

The chairs are worse. A chair grows overnight in a new east-facing room and shapes itself to a body, and you sit in it, and it is not for you. There is a hollow low in the back where nobody has a hollow. The seat pitches forward. The armrests are not arm-height; they are set for something that rested its weight on its forearms, wide, like a person leaning over a rail to look at water.

Nobody has ever met the tenant. The scars on the crown may say who they were and the scars no longer say anything. What is certain is only this: they were shorter than you, and broader, and they hooked rather than gripped, and they leaned, and the entire settlement is a four-thousand-year-old act of hospitality toward them, performed hourly, uncorrected, by something that cannot be told they are not coming.

So. The residents.

---

**Leeward.**

She lives on the northeast face, in the lee of the crown, under a leaf she has been living under in some form for nineteen years — not the same leaf, which would be impossible, but the same *idea* of a leaf, which the town has now learned to keep providing her. That is the beginning of her whole trouble: the town learns.

Her work is the scars. There are four hundred and sixty of them on the crown, or four hundred and sixty that she counts as scars and not as damage-noise, and she copies them. She makes charts on scraped cuticle in an ink of her own devising, and she measures the strokes, and she notes the drift — because they do drift, generation on generation, and she is the only creature that has ever been in a position to watch it happen. In nineteen years she has documented a hundred and nine changes. A hook softening into a curve. Two marks that used to be separate touching, and then merging, and now indistinguishable from a mark that was always single.

Her charts rot. Cuticle lasts eight months here, ten if she oils it. So she copies the charts, and then copies the copies, and about six years ago she caught herself losing a stroke in transcription and understood, sitting there with the pen in her hand, that she had become a second lattice, running the same decay at a faster clock, and that if she lived a thousand years her charts would arrive at a beautiful gestural nonsense of their own.

She said, at the time, that this was fine. She said someone ought to be wrong about it in a different direction.

Then, in a bad season, in a mood she does not discuss, she cut a mark of her own into a column. One line, at the same height as the others, in the same hand she had spent a decade learning to imitate.

The column died. The wardens ate it. The mark came back at the same height in the same hand, and came back again, and it is now nine hundred generations old and has begun to drift, and she has documented the drift, and the sixteen scars nearest it have begun — this is not possible, and she has the charts — to drift *toward* it.

She is somewhere between forty and seventy. She keeps a small bone toggle swinging on a thread at her wrist at all times, because she is not a fool, and she has been here long enough to know exactly what the wardens do to things that hold still. She will show you the charts if you ask twice. She will not show you the mark.

**Sunward.**

He is on the other side of the crown, which is two hundred paces, which here is a country away, and you should go and see him and you should not stay long.

He was a wader once too. He came with boots. There is a general agreement among the others that he was ordinary for about six years.

What happened to him is nothing dramatic. He stopped moving quite as much. He let a root-braid come up through the slats where he slept, because it carried warm water and the nights are not cold but the warmth was pleasant, and he did not cut it when it reached his calf, and then it was at his hip, and then it was a hammock, and then it was a wall with him in it. There is no violence in this. He can leave. He has left, three times, for a day each, and come back and let it retake him, and it takes him faster now because the town remembers the shape.

His voice has gone slow. He speaks in the rhythm of the venting — a pause, and a phrase pushed out all at once, and a long pause. And in that voice, without any ceremony, he tells you the weather.

*Southwest. Thursday. Hard, and then all night.*

He is right. He is right nearly always, and he is right about things no barometer would have: which colonnade will lean, which gallery has nine days left, that the Verge will produce, tomorrow, four lenses and nothing else. He does not experience this as prophecy. He says it is like knowing you are hungry. The sap knows about tomorrow's rain; he is now downstream of the sap.

He is the only instrument this place has ever had for reading itself, and he cost a man to make, and he is still being made. Each year there is less of him that is a decision and more of him that is a report. Leeward visits him twice a season and asks him nothing, which is the kindest available behaviour, and cuts back the braid at his throat, which he permits and does not thank her for.

He has no toggle at his wrist. He has not needed one for a while. The wardens come over him in their thousands and go on, and do not eat him, and nobody knows what that means, and everyone has a theory, and his own theory is the worst of them: *I am not deviating.*

**The Verge-sitter.**

Call her Tamn, because that is what she says when asked, though the others use the place. She is the one you will meet first, because she is the one who watches the water, and she wades out to intercept arrivals for the entirely practical reason that new people put things down and go to sleep.

She is fifty-something and burned brown and cracked at the knuckles and has a working knife made from a spar of dead lignin, resharpened so many times it is now a bodkin. Eleven years. She is the shortest-tenured of the three and by a distance the most useful, because she has understood the one thing the town lacks and cannot grow, which is not strength or memory or anticipation. It is preference.

The seedwork has no taste. It has habit, and it has editors, and the editors only ask *is this the same as before.* Nobody in the system asks *is this good.*

So twice a day she sits at the Verge with a bag, and the bud opens and hands her a hinge, a spoon, a lens, a bracket for something that no longer exists, another hinge. And she sorts. Keep. Discard. The discards go straight down to the wardens, where they are eaten within the hour, and the isotope-pith takes some fractional note of what was not returned to the pool, and — over eleven years, measurably, in a way she can prove with a shelf of them — the spoons have got better. There were spoons with three bowls when she came. There are now, on a good week, spoons.

She also breaks things. This is the part visitors find hard. A stair with a shallow riser gets its riser split, because a broken riser is collapse and collapse gets eaten and rebuilt, and there is a small chance the rebuild lands nearer to something a foot can use. A wall grown across a good sightline gets girdled. She is a slow, patient, arguing hand inside a senile process, and she loses roughly nineteen arguments in twenty, and the twentieth is a doorway you can walk through without stooping, and it lasted forty days once, and she talks about those forty days the way other people talk about a marriage.

She knows the improvements do not accumulate past a certain depth. She knows that whatever she teaches the Verge will be forgotten in a decade after she stops, because the memory here is patterns of isotope in the pith and the pith is on a churn like everything else. She keeps sorting spoons.

Ask her why she stays and she gives the line the others give, and she means it more literally than they do. *It is very difficult to leave a place that is trying so hard.* She has stood knee-deep on the margin with her bag packed. It vents. It builds a chair for someone who is never coming. It gets a spoon right.

**The fourth.**

The count is four. There are three people on the crown.

The fourth is a set of returns. A spoon left at the Verge, upright in a drain-mouth, of a quality Tamn discarded a season earlier and would not have kept. Bare footprints in fresh pale floor, a shorter stride than any of theirs, always heading down-slope. A cistern drained and cleaned. Once, a chart of Leeward's, missing for two months, back under her leaf with a stroke *added* — a stroke she could not disprove and has since had to include.

They have never all three seen him. Sunward says he goes past. Sunward says it in the same voice he uses for Thursday's rain, which is not reassuring. Tamn believes it is a man who came ashore about fifteen years ago and simply preferred not to be met, and who lives on the water and sleeps in the sedge, and there is nothing strange in that; she says she has considered it herself.

Leeward believes something else, and does not say it, and keeps counting four.

---

**Everyone else.**

The actual citizenry, by mass and by number, is frogs. Sixty species, and the leaf-cisterns fit them exactly: right depth, right warmth, refreshed every afternoon, replaced on a twenty-day cycle that happens to match a small translucent life from egg to jump. Nobody designed this. It is the surplus finding a use for itself. In the whole of the Hummock, the frogs are the only inhabitants who have been accommodated perfectly, and they were accommodated by accident, and that is either the joke of the place or its single mercy.

Then the wardens, who are not residents but immune systems, and eat error, and have been eating error for so long that error is the entire substance of their diet and they have grown iridescent on it.

Then the tree-crabs, pale blue, pruning. The herons in the drains. The gnats. The bootlace snakes doing something useful with the mites.

And then the tenant. Short, broad, leaning, hooking, gone the way a word is gone after it has been said, and still — in the tread height, in the groove in the rail, in the hollow low in the back of every chair — the only one the town is actually building for. The three people on the crown are guests in a house that has not noticed them. They are the wrong height. They grip with a thumb. They are tolerated because they move.

In the morning there is a new room facing east, with a good drain and a level floor and a chair.

Tamn will look in, on her way to the Verge, the way you check a window in a house where someone is ill. Leeward will not, because she is at the scars by first light. Sunward, if the braid has let him turn, faces east anyway.

Nobody sits in it. The chair would not have them.
