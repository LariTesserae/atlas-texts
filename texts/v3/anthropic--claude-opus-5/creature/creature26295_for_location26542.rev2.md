## The Entry That Says None

The entry is not wrong. It is written by people who came looking for animals and found, correctly, that there are none.

What lives in the basin is not fauna. Fauna is what happens in a place. What lives in the basin is what the basin does at certain fixed coordinates, in the same sense that scale is what it does under a drip and moss is what it does inside a node. They occur where the sound cancels. They have occurred there for as long as the wear-record runs, at a density that has not measurably changed, in a distribution that is not theirs and never was.

There are, at present, four hundred and seventy-one of them.

## The Count

The number is known to the last unit, which is remarkable for a people with no writing that survives the hour.

It is fixed because the pits are fixed. A node the size of a room grows enough sedge, moss, and shelf-fungus to carry one adult and something less than one child. A node the size of a village square carries twenty-six. There are around three hundred nodes and their areas have not altered within any reckoning anyone still holds, and so the population has a ceiling made of acoustics, and the ceiling has not moved in a length of time that the wear-record measures in fathoms of polished iron.

They know this. It is the first fact they teach. There is room for four hundred and seventy-one, and there has been room for approximately four hundred and seventy-one since before counting, and there will be room for approximately four hundred and seventy-one after. A birth waits on a death the way a crossing waits on the water. Nobody finds this cruel. They find it structural, which is a different thing, and which is the only kind of thing the basin has.

Their kinship runs along the pattern of the discs — who may marry into which node, which node owes labor to which, which three nodes are one node in matters of grief. Drawn out, the chart of their descent lines is the same chart as the pit-map: a mathematician would recognize it instantly and an anthropologist not at all. They have arranged their families according to the transmission diagram of a machine nobody has ever seen. They did not choose to. It was the only shape available.

## Bodies

They are small, and thick through the chest, and grey.

The grey is not pigment. It is the flour — the ground concrete that is everywhere, in the water, in the air of the drift-fog, packed into every crease. Under it the skin is whatever it is; nobody sees. Over it they carry a worked layer of pump-grease, cut from the tallow bluff below the pumphouses and rendered soft on a warm dome, dressed onto the whole body twice a Turn. It keeps the wet off. More importantly it keeps scale from taking hold, because scale takes hold on anything, and a person who neglects the dressing will find a white crust starting behind the ear and in the webs of the fingers, and will have to scrape it, and will bleed.

Their hearing is undamaged and almost entirely unused. The Drone is too low to injure and the Shriek is far enough that its harm is slow; what they have is an organ in perfect order that functions about two hours in twenty, inside the nodes, where it is very fine indeed. They can hear a moth-weight of silt settle. There are no moths.

Their teeth wear flat. Grit is in everything they eat, and the molars go down like the anchor bolts go down, to smooth brown stumps, and a person's age is read off them by anyone who cares to look — the same method, the same arithmetic, the same confidence as reading a drip-bowl or a gear rim. Wear is the only clock, and it does not make exceptions for the living.

Ages are given in feet. The Arc-Front moves north a hundred feet a year and leaves its scale-line on the slab behind it, and so a young man is two thousand feet and an old woman is six thousand three hundred, and the very old are spoken of by the fused pavements they can remember standing under. This is not poetry to them. It is the only measure long enough.

## Crossing

Out on the flats a person has no face and no voice and, by strict usage, no name.

The face is a literal absence. Standing water in the basin is never still — the Drone keeps every sheet of it crazed — and so nothing reflects, anywhere, ever, except inside a node, where the vibration cancels and a pan of rain lies flat. Faces exist at three hundred addresses and nowhere else. A person crossing the Gearfields is, in the plainest sense available, unwitnessed, and has been raised to feel it.

The pace is not chosen. The Stamp arrives every four and a third seconds, through the slab, into the sternum, and everyone in the basin walks on it. Left foot on the strike. This means that all travel proceeds at exactly one speed, that distance is counted in strikes and never in anything else, and that two people who set out from the same pit an hour apart will arrive an hour apart, forever, with no possibility of hurrying and no reason to. A child learning to cross is strapped to an adult's chest and takes the Stamp secondhand through another person's ribs for the first four hundred feet of its life.

Sleep on the flats is not survivable. Not because of cold — the basin is a cellar, forty-six degrees, and the grease holds heat — but because anything left standing still is annexed, and the rate is fast enough that an exhausted sleeper wakes cemented at the ankles and, if the sleep was long, does not wake. Everyone knows this in the body. It is why the crossings are laid out the way they are, node to node, timed to the hour, with no slack in them at all.

## The Two Languages

The binding language is the one that cannot be spoken.

It is made of grip, shove, point, and mud. Facts are given on the forearm. Orders on the shoulder. Warnings at the back of the neck, which is the only place a stranger may touch you without asking. Numbers are tapped on the breastbone, in the gaps between Stamps, because that is where the beat is and because it is the one rhythm two people are guaranteed to share. Longer matters are written with a stick in the grey flour, read, and left. Mud-writing survives about an hour before the rain and the tremor take it, which means nothing is archived and everything is witnessed. Their whole law consists of what people saw with their own eyes in the noise and remembered afterward.

A promise made with the hands, on the flats, in the scream, is absolute. There is no way to soften it there, no tone, no qualification, no chance to explain yourself; you can only do the sign or not do it. This is why they consider it the honest register.

The other language is speech, and speech is a place.

You cannot talk outside a node. You can talk inside one. Their verb for speaking and their noun for a Silence Pit are the same word, and the ambiguity does not trouble them, because the two things have never been separable. Speech is understood to be the unreliable mode: the one for grief, for wanting, for lying, for songs, for telling children what is under the slab. Nothing said in a pit is binding on anyone. They say a thing with their mouths roughly the way another people would say a thing while crying, and with the same allowances made.

## Entering

The boundary of a node is a stride wide and the crossing of it is a physical event.

The weight goes. The pressure that has been in the joints for six hours releases all at once and the body does what it does — the eyes water, and then more than water. They call it the entering-weep and it is not remarked on, any more than a sneeze is remarked on. Outsiders weep in nodes too, and are embarrassed, and are watched with mild curiosity, because to be embarrassed by it implies you believe you could have done otherwise.

Inside, in the two hours before sleep: the rain on your own shoulders, the sound of your own swallowing, the stillness of the water pan, your face in it, and voices. It is where children first hear themselves and first see themselves, in the same hour, usually in the same week, which they consider a single event and have one word for.

## The Turn

Their year is eleven days long, and it is the beat frequency of nine unmaintained pumps.

When the pumps align, the standing pattern is at its most coherent. The nodes deepen and sharpen and reach their fullest extent; the silence inside them goes absolute; and the flats become a thing the body treats as an injury and cannot be crossed at all. This is the Tight. Everyone is home, every pit is full, the water pans are flattest, the talk is best, the children are conceived. It is the good season and it lasts about three days and nobody can go anywhere.

When the pumps scatter, the pattern blurs. The nodes shrink at their edges and hiss; sleep is poor; the small ones half-close. And the flats become passable. So the Scatter is when everything is done — the marriages moved, the grease cut, the seed carried, the dead carried — and it is done at speed, on the Stamp, by people who are nobody for the duration.

The whole of their social life is a tide. You may be at home, or you may go out, and never both.

## Marginal Nodes and the Standing Dead

Some nodes are not permanent. They open for part of the Turn and close for the rest: a patch of slab that is a room with a floor of moss for three days and a piece of screaming flat for eight.

Nobody lives in them. They are waystations. A long crossing is planned to reach a marginal node at the hour it opens, and the timing is not approximate — a late arrival finds noise where quiet was promised and has no choice but to go on with nothing left, or to stop.

This is where most of the standing dead are.

They are upright, generally. Scale builds from the feet, and a person who stops on wet slab is fixed at the ankles within a season and shrouded in three, and after that is a white cone of gently increasing diameter that will not fall over, because there is nothing here to push it. The oldest have lost every feature and are simply pillars. The recent ones are unmistakably people, hooded in mineral, hands often at the face.

They are not removed. Removal is not a category of action in the basin. They are also, frankly, useful: at close range, when the drift-fog is down and the Arc-Front is behind cloud, the standing dead are the best navigation marks there are, and the routes are described by them — *third white one past the bolt-grid, bear left.*

The one observance made on the flats: passing within reach of a standing one, you put your flat hand against it for the duration of a single Stamp. Four and a third seconds. Then you walk on, on the beat, and you have not lost time, because the hand goes out on the strike and comes back on the next, and that is the whole of it.

## The Reckoners

Because the marginal nodes kill, somebody has to keep the count.

There are eleven of them, one to a pumphouse and two spare, and their work is to sit on the northeast ridge and watch nine beams rise and fall and maintain the phase-count, which is the calendar, the tide-table, and the travel schedule of the entire population. They are chosen young and they do not cross. Their whole lives take place within the reach of the pumps' noise, in the worst acoustic in the basin, and they are, predictably, the strangest people in it.

The count has been lost twice. Both losses are remembered by the name of the reckoner and by the number of people who did not arrive. The names are not curses. They are simply the two dates in a history that otherwise has no dates at all.

## Food, Heat, and the Only Pilgrimage

They eat the white shelf-fungus, which tastes of wet ash and is the bulk of it; the seed of the pit-sedge, ground with grey flour into a paste that is half mineral and wears their teeth away; the moss, in bad Scatters, which is a thing people apologize for afterward; and fat rendered from the grease bluff, which is edible in small quantity and is the difference between a hard Turn and a death.

Cooking is a location. There is no fire — nothing to spare to burn — but there are the warm domes where something beneath still runs, and the tile spillway below Pumphouse Four where the outflow comes off at blood heat. Food is carried to warmth and left there and fetched. Like speech, like faces, like sleep, it is somewhere rather than something. Their language has very few verbs and an enormous, precise vocabulary of places, and this is the reason.

Below Pumphouse Four, in that warm water, are the threadlike things: eyeless, quarter-inch, swimming against the current.

Every person in the basin goes to look at them once, at about six hundred feet of age. It is the only journey with no other purpose. They do not eat them, touch them, name them, or explain them. They lie on the broken tile in the warm spray with their faces an inch above the water and watch the only other objects in the world that move because they have decided to, and then they get up and go home and do not speak about it, even in a node, where they could.

## The Drips

The dead who are carried, rather than the dead who stopped, go to the drip-lines.

The roof truss has been raining in the same fixed lines for the whole wear-record, each line into its own dimpled bowl with its own white rim. There are more bowls than there are families, and every family has one, and the address is inherited. A body is laid in the bowl in the first hours of a Scatter and left. Scale takes it: crusted in a season, cemented in three, indistinguishable in ten, and after that it is simply an increase in the bowl's floor, which the drip will go on working at for as long as there is a truss to condense on.

Their grave-map is therefore a map of the ceiling. To say where your people are buried, you point up.

## Birds

The grease bluff weathers out hollows. Wings, keel, beak, the fine bones of a foot: the void left by something that fell into warm tallow a very long time ago and dissolved, leaving its shape behind in nothing.

They know what these are. There is a word for them and it means, exactly, *a shape made of the absence of a thing.* Children are taken to see them.

From these they have concluded that above the truss, at some point, there was a place that produced objects capable of arriving from overhead under their own power, and that the objects were hollow and light and had these particular proportions, and that the place is either gone or is still up there past the ironwork, and that either way it is not a thing that can be walked to. They hold no opinion about what is below the Gearfields, at the level the belts come from. They do not think anything lives down there. Their phrasing is that below is not a place where living is done; it is the place living is done *from*. They say it in nodes, so it does not have to be true.

## Visitors

Outsiders arrive every few generations, with instruments and wear-tables, and cannot cross unaided. They talk, which is useless and looks like a nervous complaint. They stop to take readings, which is dangerous. They walk out of step and therefore walk badly and tire in a third of the time. They look for animals in the moss with the sincerity of people who have never yet been told no by a landscape.

The basin-dwellers guide them for cloth, rope, unscaled iron, and seed. They regard the visitors as slightly deformed by the ease of wherever they came from, and treat them with the mild forbearance owed to anyone carrying an injury they don't know they have.

In four of the nodes there grows a stout brown fungus that is not native and is better eating than anything else in the basin. It came in on somebody's boot. It is named after that person. Nobody now remembers that the name was a name.

## The One Who Went Out

There is always a story, and it is always the same story, and it is told only in nodes.

A man went up over the eastern ridge and was gone eleven Turns and came back.

What he said, afterward, was that out there the whole world is a Silence Pit. The quiet is not in places; it is everywhere, without edges, and so it costs nothing and marks nothing and means nothing. You can say any word at any coordinate. Water lies still in every ditch, so your face is everywhere, so it is worth nothing. Promises are made with the mouth, which can be softened, so they are all soft. Nothing holds you down, and nothing holds anyone to anything, and there is no hour at which a thing must be done or not done forever.

He said: *there is no discipline in it.*

Then he lived out the rest of his life in a room-sized node in the western flats, and dressed in grease, and walked on the Stamp, and was carried to his family's bowl under a drip that had been working on his people since before the arc-light crossed the plain, and is now part of the floor, which is part of the inventory, which is what you are walking on.

He told the story only in pits. Which means it did not have to be true, and they know that, and they tell it anyway.

## What They Call Themselves

Their word for themselves is their word for silt.

It means *what comes out of suspension.* Outside a node, the tremor holds everything up — flour, spore, seed, dust — and nothing can land. Inside, the shaking stops and the water goes still and the fine material falls and stays and becomes, over a long enough time, ground. Moss is what happens next. Then sedge. Then them.

The same word, with no change at all, also means *to stop moving,* and therefore *to be annexed,* and therefore *to die standing on the flats in the white.* They are aware of this. It is the basis of the only joke they all know, and it is a joke that cannot be told outside a node, and is therefore not binding on anyone, and is therefore not, strictly speaking, a claim about the world.

They do not carry out the last rule, because they never picked it up. They have never once suspected that the basin needed them, or noticed them, or would run a stroke differently without them. They are a property of interference. They settle where the sound cancels, in a pattern set by a machine that no longer exists in any other form, at a fixed count, for a length of time measured in the depth of a thumb, and it would all continue identically if they were gone.

They have had a very long while to be all right with this, and they are, which is the part that's hard to put down.
