## The owners

It is a mistake, and the people themselves will correct you on it, to say that the people live here. The clappers live here. Everything else is a tenant.

Take one out of the bed — you will need to dig, and it will fire against your palm hard enough to bruise bone — and you have in your hand something not much longer than a finger: ten legs, plating the colour of wet horn, a rasp of gill along the belly, and the hammer. The eye is nearly gone, a fleck of pigment with no lens behind it, because there has never been anything here worth looking at. The whole animal is an apparatus for the delivery of a single collapsing bubble, roughly twice a second, for about eleven months, and then it dies and is eaten by its neighbours and its socket is occupied before the tide turns.

What matters is not the individual. Four hundred million to the square kilometre means that a bed is not a population but a surface, and the surface has weather. The barrage is not uniform; it moves. Waves of intensity cross the beds at about the speed of a slow walk, pressure-fronts, shimmers of concentrated firing that travel and break and interfere, and a person standing barefoot on the flat can feel those fronts arrive through the soles and can tell from the interval and the direction where the tide-line is, how fast it is coming, and whether the bed under the next rib has been disturbed. The children are taught to read this before they are taught anything else, and old people who can no longer stand are carried out and laid down with their feet against the stone so they can check.

The people's dead go into the beds. The clappers reduce a body in about six days. So the noise is not merely the condition of the world; it is also where everyone's mother is. They do not make more of this than it is, but the phrase for dying is *went into the noise*, and the phrase for the noise is the same phrase.

## The grazed and the grazing

The plated grazers drift over the mat-fields in the thousands, slow grey dishes from the size of a boot to the size of a door, mouth on the underside, mineral tongue rasping, leaving behind them a clean stripe of polished gneiss that will re-skin in about a season. They are the whole economy. Their shell plates are the walls of the houses and the bowls and the spoons and the ear-caps; their hides are cordage; their fat is the blue flame; their flesh, fermented in lined pits because nothing in this world will ever dry, is what a winter is made of. A grazer's shell large enough to roof a crawl-entrance is inherited, named, repaired, and argued over across three generations.

The long segmented swimmers come with the flood, four metres of articulated dark, backs breaking the sheet-water. You feel one before you see it: a bow-wave of pressure crossing your shins. When a long-swimmer is working, every tap-line on the rib stops. It is the one silence on the Floor that is not an alarm — it is discipline — and it is why the code has no filler, no *hello*, nothing to say when there is nothing to say.

Overhead, and there is nothing else overhead, the leathery gliders ride the standing air off the rib-tops and drop feet-first into the pools. Their heads are mostly eye and mostly blank where the ear should be. They are silent, they have always been silent, and the people watch them the way other peoples watch weather: a glider circling and refusing to drop tells you the seep beneath it has turned, and a whole band will move camp on that evidence alone.

## The people

Short, wide, heavy through the chest, with fat laid under the skin the way it is laid under the skin of swimmers. Pale to the point of translucency when young, going to a grey leather by thirty. Very little hair, and what there is kept greased and bound flat. Large dark eyes that are good at pewter and useless at anything else; a lamp brought down from outside blinds them for a day. They grease themselves against the cold wet with rendered grazer fat, and the mat-luminescence takes to the grease, so at night a person walking is a smear of low green moving at knee height, and a band at work is a constellation, and this is how you find your own people in fog: not by looking for a shape, but by looking for the particular way a body you know disturbs the water.

They are, all of them, more skin than anything else. The roar is a pressure on the chest wall and the sinuses and the fine hairs of the forearm, and they read its texture the way you read a face. A line of adults standing along a rib with their palms flat on the stone, not moving, is a band listening to thirty kilometres of country at once. They can tell you the distance of the tide-line by the weight of the sound on the sternum, to within a hundred metres. They cannot tell you anything at all about the pitch of a voice, and most of them have no idea that such a thing exists.

## Four speeches

They have four languages and no words.

The **far speech** is the tap: knuckle or iron nodule against quartz, received by pressing palm or jawbone to the speaking-knobs, those shallow dishes polished into the ribs by generations of striking. It is slow, public, formal, and enormously old. It carries names, tide-counts, weir reports, deaths, the movements of long-swimmers, and nothing intimate whatsoever, because everybody with a hand on the rock for a kilometre in either direction is listening.

The **middle speech** is the hands in air with the face fully involved — fast, dense, capable of anything, and useless in fog, which is most days.

The **near speech** is the hands into another's hands, palm inside palm, used in fog and in the dark and inside the houses. It is the language of children, of lovers, of the dying, and of anything shameful. Two people speaking near-speech stand with their heads down and their four hands folded together, quite still, for as long as an hour, and to the eye nothing is happening at all.

The **cord speech** is tugs: walking roped in fog, one pull, two pulls, the long drag that means *stop*. It has perhaps forty signs and every one of them is an imperative.

Because both hands are language, a person never fills both hands in company. Everything is carried by headstrap, shoulder yoke, or dragged on a hide sledge; a man eating holds his bowl between his knees. To take someone's wrist and hold it is to interrupt; to hold both wrists is an assault, and there are killings recorded in the tap-code that began exactly that way.

## Names

A name is a knock — a rhythm, three to seven strikes with a signature in the spacing. It is given at the fitting of the first ear-caps, which is the largest event in a life: shell caps ground to the shape of a particular child's ear canals from a grazer taken by that child's parent, seated with wax, and then every member of the band takes the hammer in turn and beats the new knock into the rib while the child lies with their jaw against the stone until they can feel the shape of themselves coming up through the rock.

Knocks age. A reader can hear arthritis in a knock, can hear grief in it, can hear that a man has broken the second finger of his striking hand and is compensating. Knocks are never reused. When someone dies, the band assembles on the rib and taps that person's knock all together, once, and then it is retired forever, and this is why the code is so crowded with dead rhythms that a new child sometimes has to be given a seven-strike name.

## The year of ears

They plug from infancy and they are all deaf by twelve regardless, and in between there is the window, and the whole society is organised around it.

A child who can still hear is the band's alarm. They are posted on the knobs with a cord at the waist, and the adults working the mat-fields look up at them every forty heartbeats, all day, for years. The first face-sign a child masters is not *mother* or *hungry*; it is the particular widening that means *there is a hole in the roar*, and it must be produced instantly and without thought, and children are startled deliberately in training until it is reflex.

The turn comes when a hole opens somewhere on the flat and one posted child's face changes and another's does not. It is noticed within the hour. The caps of the child who did not flinch are taken out and laid in the clapper bed, and the tap-phrase for it means *the ears went first*. The child is then an adult: which is to say, a person who can stand inside a silence and work.

## The readers

The very old go up onto the rib and stay. They cannot see well enough to sign and cannot be spared for the fields, so they lie with the jaw against a speaking-knob, under a hide, for most of the rest of their lives, and they receive. A reader holds the whole territory: every knock, every weir's condition, every band within eight ribs, the tide-count going back sixty years. They relay, they correct, they refuse to pass on gossip or pass it on maliciously, and they lose their teeth to the stone, one at a time, which is considered the honourable version of losing teeth.

## The quiet house, and the singing

A shell dome is built for one purpose, which is not warmth and is certainly not dryness. Inside, keyed into the weeping rib, with the crawl-entrance turned away from the sea, the roar drops to a bearable pressure, and this is the only quiet anyone owns. To offer a stranger the deep end of your dome is the largest hospitality there is. To be denied it is exile in miniature.

And in that quiet, in the dark, they sing. Not through the air — there would be no point, and half of them have never heard anything through air. They press forehead to forehead, skull against skull, and hum, and the bone carries it, and the other person feels the song in the roots of their own teeth. It is the only sound on the Floor that has ever been made for pleasure. It can only ever be received by one person at a time. Mothers do it to infants; the old do it to each other; it is what is done for someone who is dying, all night, in relays, a queue of the band waiting their turn to put their head against the head of the person who is leaving.

## The three quiets

**The working quiet** moves. A predator crossing a bed shuts it down in a spreading ring, and you feel the ring pass over your feet, and you go up the nearest rib and take the children with you.

**The sour quiet** does not move. A seep turns, the water goes bad, and the bed beneath it simply stops and does not start again. They mark such places by laying a cairn on its side, and the sideways cairns stand for decades, and no one grazes there.

**The third quiet** has no tap-phrase. It does not spread and it does not sit; it is a hole that is suddenly entire, the whole bed gone at once, with no ring. There is a hand-sign for it, made inward, made once, after which the hands are rinsed. People have come back from it and given accounts, and the accounts are not repeated. The readers will not relay them. When a reader feels the third quiet open somewhere on their stone, they tap a single strike — just one, which in no other context means anything — and the entire rib, for kilometres, goes still and stays still until the next tide has come in and gone out again.

## The visitor

You should know that when you walk out onto the Floor you announce yourself. You do not know how to place a foot, and the bed under you pauses in a small ring about four metres wide that travels with you wherever you go. To everyone lying with a jaw to the rock, you are a wrong quiet, walking.

They will come to you the way they come to anything wrong: from upslope, spread wide, unhurried, carrying nothing in either hand. They will look at your ears first. Then they will take you up the rib, fit you with a borrowed pair of caps from a dead child, push you ahead of them through the crawl-entrance into the only silence anyone here will ever own, and sit you down in the blue light of a fat lamp.

And after a while one of them will lean in and put their forehead against yours, and you will feel, in your own skull, something that is unmistakably addressed to you — patterned, warm, deliberate, entirely without meaning — and you will not be able to answer, and they will not expect you to.
