# The People of Ashlar, and What Else Is Counted

### Hands

Start with the hands, because the hands are the tool.

Everyone in Ashlar has a ring of hard skin across the inside of the first knuckles and a shine on the outside of the right palm. This is from touching. They touch the walls as they go — not for balance, the lanes are narrow enough to lean in — but as a matter of practice and manners, the way you'd keep a hand on a child in a crowd. Every lane in town has a polished band at hip height where the drawn stone has been worn to a deeper grey by sixty-one years of passing palms, and a second, fainter band lower down, at the height of a seven-year-old.

That band is the most valuable surface in the region. It is the only wear in Ashlar that is *true*. Everything else the looms produce arrives pre-dented, pre-softened, wearing a history it did not earn, and the people can tell the difference at a glance the way you can tell a photograph of a room from a room. Drawn wear is even. True wear is selfish: it clusters where hands actually went.

So: hands out, always, brushing stone. Hands otherwise kept in sleeves, because it is cold, always, a degree either side. Red knuckles. Cracked thumbs. A pervasive local habit of holding a cup with the whole hand wrapped round it rather than by the handle, which visitors find charming and which is simply what you do with a warm object in a place where warmth is a currency.

### Bodies, and how they move

They run small and thick-chested, short in the leg, heavy in the shoulder — not bred that way, sixty-one years is nothing, but made that way by ladders. A native of Ashlar climbs the way other people walk, without looking at the rungs, carrying something in one hand and a conversation in the other.

They walk with one shoulder leading. Even in the market, where there is room. Even, a returning traveller reports, in Elsewhere, on a boulevard forty metres wide, where the habit made her look permanently apologetic.

You can identify an incomer for about a decade by the fact that he walks down the middle of a lane and does not touch the wall.

### How they talk

Rule two has made them a people who converse shoulder to shoulder, facing masonry. Two friends talking in Ashlar stand side by side looking at a wall, close enough that their arms touch, speaking low into the stone. A serious meeting is nine people in a row facing a stairwell. Public debate is conducted with everyone's back to everyone else, which outsiders find sinister and which locals find simply polite: you are not putting your words out into the open where they can be taken up. You are giving them to the building, which is yours, which has been used.

To turn and face someone directly in the open air of a lane and speak is therefore enormous. It is done twice in most lives. The first time is when you propose a household to somebody, standing in the middle of Sinnet or the market square with nothing between your voice and the sky, saying the thing out loud where it can be heard by whatever hears. The family then waits three to nine days to see if it comes back, and how. A kind return is a very good omen. A slurred, rearranged, doorway-in-the-night return is not fatal to a marriage but is remembered, and mentioned, for forty years.

The second time is at a burial, and I will come to that.

### The work, which is all of it

There are looms and loom-wardens; there is a survey office that maps lichen and speaks about rivers three hundred years out with the flat confidence of a timetable clerk; there is a keeper of the Rill, currently a man of sixty-eight named Bell Orrow, fourth in the line, who has never once in his tenure touched the water.

But the bulk of the working day in Ashlar is *use-rota*, and the use-rota is the town's real government.

Every enclosed volume within the ring is assigned. Somebody must be in it, doing something with intent, at a stated minimum frequency. Not sitting in it — the early years established that boredom is not a fixative, that a person stationed in an empty room to occupy it will produce a room that slowly loses its grain around them while they wait. It has to be *work*. So the rota sends you to the ninth-storey back stair with a sack of roots to peel, or to the cold room behind the second loom to sort thread, and you go, and you complain about going, and the complaining counts too.

Rooms that have gone under-used are called *thin*, and a thin room is diagnosed by an odd flavourlessness — a corner you cannot quite describe afterward, a doorframe your hand doesn't want. The remedy is escalation. First the rota. Then meals. Then, if the thinness persists, the town sends its grudges there.

This is the part that most surprises incomers. Ashlar allocates its quarrels. If two households are in dispute over a cistern, the Count will hear it and then direct them to hold the dispute at the ninth-block stairhead, at a set hour, twice a week, until resolved — because a genuine unresolved argument, conducted by people who mean it, in a place, is the densest fixative known. Grievance is a municipal resource. It is budgeted. A famously long-running feud is spoken of the way another town would speak of a reservoir.

The cooking follows the same logic and has become baroque out of all proportion to the ingredients, which are: dry stores from Elsewhere, cistern algae, wet-lace, and roots. There are nine recognised preparations of the grey paste and each has partisans who will not speak to the others. The food is not good. The food is *argued about*, which is better than good; it is structural.

The four tomato plants are tended by a woman called Hesk, who has been filled-in, and I will come to that as well.

### Children, and the fading of them

Children name. This is not a charming custom; it is a physical function, and it is why the birth of a child in Ashlar is greeted with the relief of a town receiving equipment.

A thing that has never been named sits closer to draft than a thing that has. An adult naming a thing hesitates — considers, weighs, tries two options — and the hesitation is a gap, and the world will fill a gap with something plausible rather than something true. A child does not hesitate. A child looks at a new puddle behind the fourth block and says *that's Marget's Uncle*, and it is, permanently, for nineteen years and counting, appearing in the survey office's drainage forecasts under that name and no other.

So children go on naming-walks in threes: an old person, a child, and a third who will do the mentioning. The child names. The old person uses — sets a cup in it, steps over it, sweeps it, whatever use the thing admits. The third goes home and tells someone. Rule seven, executed as a small parade.

The naming force fades at eleven or twelve. Nobody knows what fades exactly; the survey office's view is that it isn't the child who changes but the child's relationship to being wrong. There is a ceremony for it, called *setting down*, which lasts four minutes and involves handing your naming-stick to somebody smaller, and it is the first grief most people here experience: the discovery that you have finished being load-bearing in that particular way, at eleven, and must now be load-bearing in all the duller ways for the next sixty years.

### Ballast

The old are heavy.

An old person is the densest concentration of true past available — decades of actual wear, actual misremembering, actual argument, packed into one body — and a room with a ninety-year-old in it holds better than a room with four apprentices. Everyone knows this. The old know it best and are insufferable about it.

They call themselves ballast. They are carried up ladders in slings, with tea, to sit in thin rooms on the eighth and ninth storeys and do handwork and tell the same three stories with the details migrating year by year, which is not a decay but the point: a story remembered wrongly has been *handled*. A story remembered perfectly has been left on a shelf.

When an old person of great weight is dying, the household is moved to them rather than the reverse, and the death happens in the thinnest room the family can reach, because that is the last place they can be put.

### The dead, and the edge

Ashlar buries outside the Anchors.

A body is the most saturated used object a person can produce — a lifetime of habitation in a single compact volume — and out past the third Anchor, where the world thins and the mud forgets what it was, the graves hold. Around each one, a firm disc of ground perhaps three metres across, with lichen on it, and proper frost, and a grain in the soil, persisting.

They call it *planting*. The graveyard is not behind the town. It is in front of it — the leading edge, laid out in a widening arc along the line the lichen says a river will someday take. The dead are the only part of Ashlar that gets to go first.

At the graveside the family turns and faces outward, away from the Anchors, and speaks aloud into the open. This is the second time you use your voice that way. What you say is a description of the person: plain, factual, specific, the more ordinary the better. *She kept her knives too sharp. She would not eat the third preparation. She sang in the stairwell and was asked not to.* The point is to hand the world a fully specified thing and let it hear what specified sounds like.

It does come back, sometimes, slurred, from a doorway. At a graveside return nobody shushes it. They stand and let it finish.

Not every grave holds. The survey office walks the arc each spring, and some years finds a grave that has gone smooth, the disc of true soil gone back to paste, and once, when a marker had to be relocated, found the shape of a person filled in with bone of a plausible but incorrect kind. This is reported to the family in person, indoors, against a wall, and the family goes out and does the describing again.

### The filled-in

Some of the living come back that way too.

You go outside the ring with a kit of your own worn objects — a spoon, a comb, a bad knife you have used every day for twenty years — and you set them down behind you as you go, and they hold the route the way stepping stones hold a ford. Come back along your own biography and you come back yourself. Stay too long, or lose the line, and the world begins to ask you what you are, and the asking obliges an answer.

You can spot it. Ask them something only they would know — the name of a lane from their childhood, what their mother put in the paste — and there is a pause of four or five seconds, and then a perfectly reasonable answer arrives. Quartz, usually. Sometimes a plausible feldspar.

They are kept. Nobody in Ashlar would dream of anything else; they are nine hundred and forty and every body counts against the volume. They are simply never sent out again, and never put on a naming-walk, and never asked to settle anything. They are given the kind of work that rewards faithful repetition without requiring a past, and they are spoken to constantly, by everyone, in the warm inner rooms, because being mentioned is the only treatment anyone has ever found.

Hesk has the greenhouse and the four tomatoes. She came back thirty-one years ago from a walk that should have taken two days and took eleven. She waters at the eighth hour, exactly, every day, and has never lost a plant, and the tomatoes — the only true living things in Ashlar that make more of themselves — are in the care of a woman whose interior has been partially rewritten to a plausible standard. The town finds this appropriate and does not explain why.

The loom-wardens run a related clinic for shadow-lag. Past ten seconds you are put to bed against the warmest wall in the market kitchen, in the most used room in town, and people come in shifts and talk about you within earshot while you sleep. Not to you. *About* you, in detail, ordinary detail, getting things slightly wrong so that someone else corrects them. Most people come back inside four days.

### Nine, and the kettle-birds

There is one dog and his name is Nine. He is grey, short in the leg, and has been old for eleven years without progressing. He is greeted in three lanes each morning and fed in all three, and the food is eaten once.

The survey office's private position is that Nine is not duplicated but *unspecified* — that the world, having accepted a dog, has never got round to committing to where the dog is, and is carrying the question forward rather than answering it. The town's position is that there is one dog. Both positions are held simultaneously by the same people and the tension is not resolved because the tension, being an argument that nobody wants to win, is itself extremely good for the ground.

What Ashlar is actually afraid of is that he will die in three places, and that they will have to decide how many to plant.

The kettle-birds are counted alongside the people. Sixty-one years ago there were none. There are now about two hundred and ten, and the ratio of birds to persons is the number the Count watches most closely, more than the head count itself, because birds are the world's own bookkeeping and cannot be faked. No nest has ever been found, no young, no death. They appear where ground has gone firm, in the way that a number appears in a ledger.

They sit over the new graves within a day or two. Nobody has decided what to make of this. It is not in the rules. It is simply the case, and the people stand at the edge of the arc in the drizzle and look at a pale grey bird dimming and brightening on a marker, and go back in.

### Cousin

Rule seven says *anything you cannot name, name*, and so the thing that takes words out of the open air and gives them back three to nine days later, slurred, from a cistern or a doorway or the mouth of a sleeping person, is registered. It is entry one in the Naming Book. A child named it in the second year of the town and the name is Cousin.

No one in Ashlar claims Cousin is a creature. The survey office's paper on the subject, which is eleven pages and has been copied out one hundred and twenty-two times, argues that the returns are not speech but *practice* — that a world being built is a world learning the properties it will need, and that having been given, for the first time, an atmosphere with voices in it, it is doing what anything does with a new capacity: copying it, badly, at a lag, without comprehension.

The paper does not recommend a policy. The rule stands: speak indoors.

But there are perhaps a dozen people in Ashlar, mostly women, mostly old, who walk out to the lee of the fourth Anchor in the evening and read aloud. Plain things. Lists, recipes, the names of their dead. Not the upward rain, not near it — they stand off at a distance and read into the grey for twenty minutes and then come home.

This is frowned on, in the way making pebbles do homework at parties is frowned on. It is not forbidden. Nobody has quite been able to construct the argument for forbidding it.

### The ones who leave

A few go to Elsewhere each decade, and about half of those come back.

The ones who come back all say a version of the same thing, and it has hardened into a proverb that gets quoted at children. *Nothing there wanted holding.* Too much sky, finished in every direction, stars already put in, ground that cuts its own channels without anyone standing in the rain to watch. They describe a sensation of being enormously light and entirely unnecessary, which some people can live with and some cannot.

The ones who stay in Elsewhere are not resented. Their rooms in Ashlar are kept on the rota and used weekly, badly, on purpose, and their names are left in the Naming Book, and their households mention them often, which is the only obligation anyone here really has to anyone.

### At dimming

At the end of the day, when the grey goes evenly down to a darker grey over about forty minutes and does not colour, the town gets briefly, noticeably quieter — a dip in the drip-and-hum, a pause in the low voices through walls. There is a word for the feeling, *skypang*, and there is no custom around it at all, no observance, no remedy. It is simply understood that for a few minutes in the evening most of the nine hundred and forty are separately thinking about stars they have not seen and will not see, and that this passes, and that afterwards people eat.

Bell Orrow spends that part of the day at the fence behind the Rill with his hand on the brass rail, which his grandmother's hands helped wear bright, wearing it a fraction brighter. He does not look at the water at dimming; the light is too poor. He stands with his shoulder to the rail and says whatever he says quietly, downward, into the stone, and then goes in to his supper and complains about it.

Nine hundred and forty people, two hundred and ten birds, four tomato plants, one dog of uncertain location, one tree that has never learned autumn, and Cousin, who may only be the world clearing its throat.

That is everything that lives at Ashlar. It is enough, by a small margin, and the margin is checked twice a year.
