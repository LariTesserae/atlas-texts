## The People of the Quilt

The low country will tell you three things about who lives up there, and two of them are wrong.

It will tell you that nobody does, that the Sill is a green nothing and the guides are liars. It will tell you that the guides are not men at all but grown things that have taken a man's shape, which is a rumour with a grain in it. And it will tell you that they are green, which is true.

In their own grammar there is no noun for themselves. The nearest is a verb-form meaning approximately *still moving*, which does duty for *alive*, for *person*, and for *guest*. To ask a stranger's name is to ask whether they are still moving. The answer is a rhythm, not a word.

---

**Bodies.**

They are barrel-chested and narrow-limbed, with the deep slow-pumping heart of any people who have lived four thousand metres up for longer than they can account for. Their eyes are large and their pupils habitually wide; brought down to the low country they weep and squint for a week and never quite trust the light again. They see very little red. They see about eleven distinct greens, and have names for all of them, and consider our colour vocabulary the babble of people who live in a fire-lit box.

Their skin is not painted. It is inhabited. Within a month of birth the Sill has begun on any child, as it begins on any traveller, and rather than fight it they farm it: a cultivated film across the shoulders, the upper back, the outsides of the arms, tended, thinned, weeded of the coarser mosses, kept to the fine pale species that will lie flat and photosynthesise. The yield is nearly nothing. It will not feed a person. It will keep a person alive perhaps four days longer than a bare-backed person, and four days is a birth, four days is a crossing, four days is the difference between a family and a story. Where a column has died and let a shaft of brighter green down through the ceiling, you will find them sitting in it with their backs turned to it and their heads bowed, quite silent, for hours. This is the closest thing they have to worship and they do not think of it as worship. They think of it as eating.

The scalp is shaved with a nodule flake, all but one lock at the crown, which is given over entirely — never washed, never combed, allowed to become a small hanging garden. Its length and its species are a person's age, lineage, and standing, read at a glance in the dark by touch. A person shorn of the tithe-lock has done something unforgivable. There is no word for what; the shearing *is* the word.

And they do not stand still. Ever. A person waiting is a person rocking, heel to heel, a slow perpetual weight-shift that outsiders find maddening to watch and that no Sill-born adult is aware of doing. Every child learns it the same way: once, at about four years old, the mother stands the child in good quilt and holds it there, gently, and waits while the mat closes over the ankles, and waits longer, and then cuts it free. Nobody needs telling twice. Stillness, thereafter, means one of two things, and prayer is the other one.

---

**Speech.**

The moss eats sound; the wood keeps it. That is the whole of their communications.

Face to face they talk in a subvocal murmur with the forehead or the throat laid against the listener's, so that the words go by bone and never enter the air at all. To speak into open air, at volume, is not feared — the ambush-hunters are deaf and patient and do not care what you say — it is *humiliating*. Shout on the Sill and the dead acoustics return you nothing: no echo, no swell, no answer. It is the sound of a country declining to acknowledge you. Their obscenity for a foreigner translates as *one who is answered by nothing and keeps going*.

The long talk is tapping. The columns are all one body and one continuous conductor, and a rhythm struck at the base of a column with a staff of mineralised heartwood can be picked up eight or ten kilometres off by anyone with their skull against the wood. There are households whose trade this is, generation after generation, and you can tell them by the head: the mastoid flattened, the ear low and calloused, one whole side of the face slightly pressed, like fruit stored badly. They lie curled at the foot of a great column for most of their waking lives with a cord of knot-beads in their hands, listening to the traffic of a continent, which is thin — a few hundred beats a day — and mostly other people's family business, and weather, and the death of browsers, and one thing else, which comes later.

---

**Fire's absence, and what stands in for it.**

They have never cooked anything. They have no ash, and so no lye, no soap, no glass, no pot. Everything that we do with heat, they do with rot.

Under every household's line of travel there is a string of sour-holes: pits cut into the quilt, lined with living felt, sealed with a plug of moss, and left. Into them go the soft grazers of the upper galleries, the eels out of the veins, leaf-plate, the meat of the blind pad-footed things trapped below. The peat's low smoulder does the rest, at blood heat, over months. What comes out is grey and slippery and stinks like a battlefield and is, by every account of every visitor who has ever been fed it, the finest thing they ever ate and the thing they could never eat twice. A family's holes are known and inherited and their locations are the largest part of what a mother teaches a daughter. To open another household's hole is the only crime they punish with the shears.

The other food is sap. A tap driven a hand's depth into a column gives that thin warm sweet metallic run, and the metal in it is the only iron in this country; a people cut off from tapping go pale and breathless and die of it in a year. So the columns are bled, under strict allotment — named columns, bleeding-rights, hereditary, feuded over — and every tap is understood to be a wound in the single body that everybody is standing on. They apologise. Not sentimentally. The way you apologise to a creditor.

---

**Households.**

The problem of a country that grows over your feet is solved by living on something that is always moving.

They live on the browsers. A full-grown mound-browser is the bulk of two cows and has no legs to speak of and crops the quilt in a vast arc, a single animal spending a century going once round one lake, and on its back — behind the shoulder-hump, where the hide is thickest and least sensitive — a household builds. Woven platform, sleeping trays slung above the wet, sour-hole tools, the water-skins, the cords, the staffs, the children pegged by the ankle at night. Eleven or twelve people. A hearthless hearth.

The browser is the house, the field, the calendar and the ancestor. It is named, its lineage recited, its temper known and forgiven. Nobody eats it.
