## The Census, Which Will Be Wrong

Count first, because it is the natural thing to do, and because the count will not tell you who lives here.

Nine hundred vertebrates, give or take a season. Two hundred and six bats in the high pocket. Something like eleven thousand geckos, though nobody has ever counted geckos and lived to finish. Three species of frog in the hundreds, four kinds of snail, eleven pillars' worth of ants, a whip scorpion here and another eight meters on, rat snakes to the number of perhaps thirty, blind shrimp beyond arithmetic. Fifty-one kinds of plant, of which nine are found nowhere else on the pan and four are found nowhere else at all.

All of that is true and all of it is, in the sense that matters, weather. Those animals *occur* in the room the way rain occurs in a valley. They arrive, they eat, they are eaten, they contribute their calcium to the shelf by the pool. Residency is a different thing. Residency means you have changed the shape of the place and the place has changed the shape of you, and that the exchange has gone on long enough that neither party could now be described without the other.

By that measure the room has three tenants and one ghost.

## The Tree

The fig is the landlord, and like most landlords it is largely absent from the part of the property that bears its name.

Above, on the caliche, it is a shrub. Knee-high, wind-flogged, gray, indistinguishable from the other stunted things that gnaw at that hardpan; a goat would strip it in an afternoon if a goat came. That crown is not the original. The original died some unknown number of centuries ago, and the tree simply grew another one out of a layered root, and then another, and possibly another after that. No living tissue in the whole organism is more than two hundred years old. The organism is a great deal older than that. Somewhere between nine hundred and fourteen hundred years, judging by the silt lamination under the pillars, and there is no way to check, because a fig keeps no rings worth reading and this one has spent its life making architecture instead of wood.

Downward it is a cathedral in the process of becoming a building. It came in as a seed in a bird's gut and fell seven meters into a warm wet dark with no competitors, and it has been answering that windfall ever since. The root curtains are not searching for anything; they found everything long ago. What they are doing now is engineering. Where a curtain reaches the floor it thickens and stiffens and takes load. Where two curtains touch they fuse — figs are promiscuous with themselves — and become a truss. Every slab the dome has dropped in the last thousand years has been caught, cradled, and slowly digested by root; there are four of them up there now, wrapped and suspended, being turned into part of the ceiling they fell out of.

It bears fruit on the pillars, low down, in the dark, which is not how figs usually behave and is the single most consequential decision in the room. And in the afternoons, when the heat column leans east out of the slot and bends away over the hardpan, what is leaning out is the tree's advertisement — a thread of scent running seven meters up a chimney and then a hundred meters downwind, thin as a wire, and once a year read correctly by an insect two millimeters long. More on that.

One more thing about the fig, and it is the saddest fact in the room. On the largest pillar, at the height of a standing adult's shoulder, there is a hollow worn by hands. The tree has been trying to close it for a very long time. Bark was rubbed away faster than it could be replaced, so the tree kept a callus rim standing around the wound, year after year, generation after generation of hands, the way a lip forms around a persistent injury. Then the hands stopped. The rim has begun to grow inward. It is closing at perhaps a millimeter a decade, and the hollow is now a shallow dish where it was once a socket, and in eighty years it will be a scar, and in three hundred it will be nothing at all.

## The Colony

Up the same pillar runs the highway, and the animal that runs it is one animal.

The ants are small, dark, unremarkable, and there are between four and five million of them in a nest that occupies the hollow heartwood of two fused pillars and a fissure behind them. The colony has been continuous — one unbroken chain of daughter queens, no gap, no re-founding — for a length of time comparable to the tree's. It is the only political entity the room has ever had. It farms the fig's scale insects, mines the guano beneath the bat pocket, retrieves the drowned after every flood, and dismantles anything that dies faster than the beetles can get to it. If you want to know why the floor is soil rather than corpses, this is why.

Its stridulatory file — the little washboard on the waist that ants of this tribe use to buzz for help — is smooth. Gone. Not silenced; erased, the ridges never laid down at all, an absence you can see under a lens as a faint polished panel where the instrument should be.

So when you disturb the nest, nothing shouts. There is a chemical, and then there is a freeze. Four million animals stop moving inside two seconds, in a wave that goes up the pillar and out along the fig curtains and across the wet north wall, and holds, and holds, and after ninety seconds resumes from the outside in. It is the most impressive thing the room does and you will not notice it unless you happen to be watching the highway when it happens.

## What Silence Leaves Behind

Look, while you are waiting, at the state of the room's abandoned equipment.

The male crickets have wing covers with no file on them. In the embryo the ridges begin to form on schedule and are resorbed at the last instar; the species keeps building the instrument and keeps taking it apart, and has been doing so long enough that there is no obvious reason for the detour and no pressure to tidy it up. The frogs have vocal sacs — full, functional, elastic sacs — with no slits from the mouth cavity to inflate them. Two pale pouches under the jaw that no frog here has ever filled. The largest species uses them as fat stores.

The bats have kept everything. Larynx, membrane, the whole apparatus, in perfect order, because it is also the apparatus for swallowing, and you cannot delete one without the other. So a pup born in the high pocket is born able to scream, and does, at four or five days old, once.

What happens then is the only piece of teaching in the room. The mother folds a wing membrane over the pup's face and holds it there.
