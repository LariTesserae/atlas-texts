## The census, first, because it is easy

Take the count at dusk and you will fill a page in ten minutes. Mason-ticks: uncountable, so you count a hand's-breadth of vent-lip and multiply, and the number you get is silly, and you write it down anyway. They are plated like small grey shields, they walk with a clicking gait you can hear if you put your ear to the stone, and they rasp the froth off the rim in tidy arcs, so that a well-grazed vent looks scalloped, tooled, deliberate. They will also graze a scab if you let them. It doesn't hurt. It feels like being read.

Sheet-fliers: nine or ten resident over the basin, more coming up on the warm afternoon air from the shelves below, hunting by the simple method of falling. They have no faces to speak of. Folded on stone they are indistinguishable from a puddle drying.

Frog-things: the seeps, all of them, all the time. Pale as boiled rice, wide-mouthed, entirely committed to water, and appallingly loud in a rhythm of about two beats to the second, which happens to be the resonant interval of the eastern bells, and which is therefore not a coincidence, though nobody can say what kind of thing it is instead.

Eyeless swimmers: every pool, hanging at mid-depth like commas.

Waders: four. Sometimes five. Never more, in ninety years of notebooks, which is one of the two or three facts about this place that make careful people go quiet.

That is the census. It is not the answer to the question.

## The seeds

A thousand metres down, or a hundred, the thing that actually occupies this address is at work. Whether it lives is a matter of where you like to put the word. It has no edge, no organs, nothing you could point to and call a body except the mountain, which is the slow part of it. It eats an older, duller mountain. It drinks cloud. It puts down lattice at the speed of a fingernail and it does not stop for night or weather or war, and it has never once, in the entire measured history of the Shelf, made a mistake — no false start, no abandoned buttress, no correction. Every arch it has drawn in blue haze has arrived as rock, on schedule, to the palm's width.

You cannot befriend it. You cannot get its attention; there is no evidence it has any. The mast has been talking to it for longer than anyone alive and receives, as far as anyone can tell, no reply, unless the mountain is the reply, in which case the conversation is going extremely well and will be finished in eleven thousand years.

This is the tenant of record. Everything else here, including the waders, including the notebooks, is a lodger.

## The four hands

**The first** installed the mast and wrote like a man submitting invoices. Tables of vent temperatures. Sketch of the guy-anchor pattern, dimensioned. A single sentence in eleven years that isn't a measurement: *Rain again at 14. Astonishing racket. Slept badly.* He left, or was taken off, mid-page.

**The second** was a naturalist and a hoarder, and because nothing rots here, her collection is intact. There are pressed lichen-jellies between waxed leaves in the back of book four that are still faintly warm. There is a sheet-flier spread and pinned inside a glass sandwich, ninety years old, supple, ready. She named nine species and got eight of the names wrong in ways later hands crossed out with visible affection.

**The third** stopped counting. You can see it happen across two pages: the columns thin, the margins fill, and then the columns stop and the margins take over. She is the one who wrote the rules. She drew the haze — hundreds of drawings, stairs and ribs and chambers, some of which have since arrived and can be compared, and the comparison is exact. Near the end she was drawing things that will not arrive for four thousand years. Her last entry says she is going out to stand where the eastern lobe will be a chamber, to see if she can feel the room. The books after that are tidy, dated, and in a different hand.

If you take the walnut-sized weight out of its cloth and look through it at the great eastern buttress, you will see the plan resolve, and you will see pins four, five and six glinting in it at their correct intervals, and about a metre past pin six, a shape that is not a pin. The fourth hand has never written that down. That silence is the most human thing in the fieldhouse.

## The one who is here now

She is not old. She looks older than she is, in the way that people do who have been living in a place that never touches them. Nothing here has weathered her — no wind worth the name, no sun, no dust — so her face is smooth and her hands are catastrophic. Every edge on the Shelf is a new edge. She has been putting her hands down for three hundred and forty days. The scars run in fine white lace across both palms and the backs of her fingers, and she can now tell you the age of a lobe by how much it hurts to hold: a week, a season, a decade, that one there is older than the fieldhouse and will only scrape you.

She is barefoot. Boots are an inclusion problem. Boots shed — thread, grit, flakes of sole — and everything shed here is permanent, so she gave them up in the second month and her soles have gone to horn. She keeps a tin. In the tin: hair, nail parings, the crumbs of two years of bread, one tooth. Whatever leaves her body goes in the tin, and the tin goes down the mountain with the supply runner, and the runner takes it without comment because the last three keepers had tins too and the discipline is older than any of them.

She eats froth boiled to a glue with cane pith for texture. It has no taste and no waste, and she has thought about salt so often and so precisely that she has stopped allowing herself to. Once a week she takes a frog-thing from the west seep, and apologises to the seep, which is sentimental, and she knows it, and she does it anyway. She keeps forty mason-ticks on a warm slab by the door, not for food — she tried; it isn't worth it — but because they sing, and because a slab of singing ticks by the door is an alarm system: when they stop, something is overhead.

That is the reflex the Shelf gives you and it never leaves. Not a flinch at noise. A flinch at *quiet*. Her whole body is tuned to the absence of sound, so that a lull in the ticking before rain makes her look up, and the sudden hush of the ticks drops her into a crouch before her mind has caught up. Downslope, in a town, she would be the woman who ducks when a room goes silent, and cannot explain it, and would have to say: where I lived, silence was a wing.

She sleeps in the trough between the two rains, four hours, and wakes by bell. She knows the bells. The low shells on the eastern rim mean the night's condensation has come through; the octave of clonks in the stacked lobes means the basin is filling in order, west to east, correctly; the fingerbone tubes mean a seep has shifted a hand's width, which means the plan turned, which means she should go and look. She goes and looks. She still takes the measurements, and the measurements still go down the mountain, and she believes that they matter, mostly.

She can see the haze now without the walnut. This began around day two hundred and she recorded it once, drily, and never again. It means that when she walks the basin she is walking through architecture: through a doorframe that is nine years away, under a vault that is four hundred, past the pale suggestion of a stair going up into a room that will exist when everyone who has ever heard of her is not merely dead but unmentioned. She has stopped finding this lonely. It is closer to living in a house with a very large family who all keep different hours.

She has not chosen her inclusion. The rule says: whatever you leave, you leave forever, so leave something worth looking at. The first hand left a brass rule, which is honest. The second left the flier in glass, four metres deep in the north lobe, spread, immortal, and it is the single most beautiful object on the Shelf. The third left whatever she left. The fourth has been turning the question over for three hundred and forty days, taking things out of her pack and setting them on the plank floor and putting them back, and this is the actual work of her tenancy, and she knows it, and it is why the last sentence in the notebook stops where it stops. She went out to see the waders. The frogs started, and the bells started, and she understood something about finishedness, and came back in, and did not have a word for the second half of the thought, and has been out there since, most nights, trying to find it.

## Who the threshold is for

Look through the weight at the plan and go far out — past the second doorway, past the stair, out to where the haze is barely a tint. There is a summit. There is, below the summit and set back, in eleven thousand years, a small square room with one window.

It is not a chamber. It is a *room*. It has a floor of planks in the plan, which the seeds cannot make and therefore must expect, and a door that shuts, and a threshold, and the threshold is drawn worn.

Whatever is building this mountain has drawn the wear before the stone. It has budgeted for a resident. Not a species, not a population — one, at a time, for a very long time, coming and going enough to hollow a step. The ticks came without being told. The waders came. The four hands came, and the fifth is coming up out of the cloud right now, that being you, with your clean hands.

So: what lives here?

A mountain, mostly, thinking at a rate you would not recognise as thought. Under it, uncountable small composers. On it, a shouting animal world that arrived unbidden and is somehow in time with the bells. And in a single grey room with a kettle and a shelf of notebooks, a barefoot woman with ruined palms who has learned to duck at silence, who is presently outside in the dark deciding what to leave, and who — whether she has worked this out yet or not — is the part of the plan that the plan cannot make for itself.

The threshold is smooth. It was always going to be smooth. There is room on the shelf for one more hand.
