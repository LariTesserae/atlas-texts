## VIII. The majority

Begin with the ones you will not see, because they are the population and everything else is a rounding error.

Take a teaspoon of the rust-coloured moss from the lee of any cushion, and in it there will be perhaps three thousand animals. Rotifers, hooked and trumpeting. Nematodes, transparent, lashing. And tardigrades — four species, one of them found nowhere else in the range — stumping through the water film on the leaves with the gait of something wearing too many coats. They graze the moss cells dry, one cell at a time, and when the film goes they go too: they draw in their legs, fold, shrink, and become a barrel-shaped grain of dust, at which point they are not dead and not alive and not anything that our words have a use for.

They do this most days. The shelf wets at noon and freezes at dusk, so the ordinary schedule of an animal here is: four hours alive, twenty hours powder. Reckon it up over a year and the commonest resident of the combing ground is awake for something between two and five weeks. The rest of the time it is a substance. This is not hardship. Hardship is a word for creatures with a stake in continuity.

Under all of it, and holding all of it, the fungi. Every cushion plant on the shelf has its roots wrapped in hyphae, and those hyphae do not stop at the cushion. Threads run out through the peat, through the moss mat, into the next cushion and the next, so that the green — the whole green, all two hundred paces of it — is stitched underneath into a single web of one or two species, and that web is arguably the largest living thing present. It weighs perhaps as much as a mouse. It is distributed through a room-sized garden. It has no outline, no edge, no location, and it is probably older than the shelter.

## IX. The grey tide

Springtails. Millions is not a figure of speech; a good census square gives ninety thousand to the cubic metre of mat, and there are a lot of cubic metres. Six species, mostly one. Two millimetres long, slate-grey, with a folded spring clipped under the belly like a stowed jack, and when you lift the stone the whole surface leaps at once and silently, a handful of flour thrown up and not coming down.

They eat fungal threads, pollen, and the dead. Chiefly the dead. Every aphid that arrives from the barley eight thousand feet below, every midge, every shred of moth wing, every grain of ash and every flake of thistledown is converted here into springtail, and springtail is what the shelf is actually made of. The green does not feed the fauna. Nothing here grazes to speak of. The plants are not a pasture; they are a net — they hold water, and they hold corpses, and the corpses come up on the wind from a country these animals will never enter and which supplies them entirely.

So the food web has a peculiar shape. It has no bottom. It begins in the air, above and beyond the shelf, and enters at the level of the scavengers, and everything above them is a tower standing on imported ground.

## X. Residents with names

The jumping spider is the size of the shelf's importance: about four hundred individuals, each holding a territory a hand across, which means the whole species-population here would fit in a coat pocket and yet constitutes a landscape of four hundred jealously governed estates. It is exactly the colour of a fresh fracture face, blue-grey with a greasy sheen, and when you crouch it stops, rotates on the spot, and *looks at you* — the forward eyes swivelling behind their lenses, tracking, calculating, and then, if you shift your gaze, looking where you looked. It hunts by sight in light hard enough to stun, stalking a fly for forty minutes and taking the last metre out of view, around the back of a stone, on a route from which the prey is not visible at any point. To do this it must carry the plan. There is a plan, in a head the size of a grain of salt, at four thousand metres, in a place where nothing is watching.

It spends nine and a half months of the year in a silk cell under a flake, folded, at minus twenty-two.

The beetle takes eleven years to become itself. Ten of those are spent as a larva under one stone, eating springtails and the occasional windfall; the last two are spent walking, black and lacquered, up and down the same six metres of grit at the moss margin, with its wing-cases fused shut over a hollow where wings would be a liability. It patrols the corridor. It is not obvious that the corridor contains anything it needs, and yet it has been walked, by this beetle and by beetles before it, continuously, for as long as the shelf has been the shelf.

The fly has stumps for wings, because up here flight is a method of being removed. It runs instead, which is a strange thing to watch a fly do. It exists on this shelf, and on two other shelves in this range, and nowhere else in the world, and no one has ever named it out loud.

The harvestman stands at dawn on legs of drawn wire, ten centimetres of leg beneath eight millimetres of body, lifting itself clear of the frost layer to keep its blood above the temperature at which its blood stops. It is not standing tall. It is standing *out of* something, the way you would stand on a chair in a flood.

And the oribatid mites: armoured, slow, black, chewing the moss into soil at a rate of some grams per century. They are the only geologists here. Everything that is soil on this shelf has been through them.

## XI. The commuters

The choughs do not live here. This is important and they make no secret of it. They roost two thousand metres down, in a gorge with a warm draught, and each morning they come up the face without visible effort — no labour of climbing, no beat of ascent, simply a black shape arriving out of the void at eye level as though the void had been holding it. There are nine. They are three pairs and three unattached, the pairs mated for eleven, fourteen and at least nineteen years respectively, and they walk the moss margin at mid-morning with their red bills going into the mat like a woman feeling for a dropped pin, taking the night's cold-stunned aphids one after another after another.

They are the only inhabitants who know both worlds. They have seen the barley the aphids came off. In the afternoon, when the rope of cloud comes over, they go up into the shear above the Gap and fall through it on folded wings, and roll, and catch themselves, and do it again, and there is no way to describe this that does not use the word play, so we will use it and move on.

They also land on the shelter. The northeast corner of the wall has been a chough perch long enough to be whitened and slightly dished. It is the only part of the built thing still in use, and it is being used by the one creature on the shelf that cannot be sheltered.

The accentors nest in a fracture above the doorway: drab, shuffling, mouse-coloured birds with a domestic arrangement in which the female mates with three or four males and all of them feed the young, and the question of whose young they are is not asked by anybody, and the chicks fledge, and the arithmetic holds. Snowfinches pass through in ones and twos, showing that shocking white in the wing, and do not stay.

## XII. The one under the scree

At the eastern end, where the stone is dry, something furred and heavy-bodied lives in a passage under the slabs that it did not dig — frost dug it, over centuries, and the animal merely moved in and deepened the turn. It weighs about five kilos. It has small ears, a blunt fisted face, teeth for grass, and a grey muzzle. It comes out at noon, lies on a flat slab with its belly spread against the warm stone, and regards you without alarm. It has never learned to run from a person, and that fact is not about the animal.

Outside its entrance is a midden: a heaped apron of cut vegetation, dung and urine-cement, dried by this air into something between peat and pumice. Its family has been adding to it for a very long time. A core taken from the back of it goes down eighty centimetres and, at the bottom, gives an age of four thousand one hundred years, which makes this small dull animal the keeper of the only archive on the mountain. In it, layered: pollen from the garden in every one of those years, telling the exact slow widening of the Gap. Insect freight from the valley, telling which crops were grown and when they failed. Ash from four separate burnings of the forests below. A fragment of dyed wool. A length of the transparent grass, twisted, Z-twist, identical to the cordage by the hearth.

The animal did not mean to keep any of this. That is why it is reliable.

## XIII. The visitor

Twice a year, more or less, something crosses the shelf that the shelf cannot hold. The pugmark is round, four-toed, clawless, ten centimetres across the pad, and it presses the moss so that the moss stands up again over some days, which is why we only ever find the tracks fresh or not at all. It comes along the green, walking the margin. There is usually a compression by the trough, an oval where forty kilos lay down. And then the trail goes to the lip and over, on a line that has no continuation anywhere we can reach.

Its territory is a thousand square kilometres of this range. The shelf, which is the entire world to everything else here, is one room in its house, and it does not spend the night.

## XIV. The oldest

On the stable boulders at the back of the shelf, out of the rope's reach, there is map lichen: yellow-green plates, cracked black like a dried riverbed, growing outward at something under a tenth of a millimetre a year. The largest thallus back there is a hundred and sixty millimetres across. You may do the arithmetic yourself and then stop doing it.

The cairn stones carry it too, and this is useful. Lichen on the base stones of the nine cairns gives thalli of eleven, fourteen, sixteen, twenty-two, twenty-three, thirty-one, thirty-four, forty and forty-four millimetres — which is to say the cairns were not built together. They were built over something like nine hundred years, at intervals of roughly a century, by people who came a long way apart and who each made one.

The quartz flakes on top bear no lichen at all. Quartz is a poor substrate; almost nothing colonises it; it stays clean for millennia. Whoever set those flakes knew this. They chose the one stone on the mountain that would go on being bright.

## XV. The occupant

Now: the room.

The walls are dry-laid slabs, chosen so well that nothing has shifted in a thousand years of frost, and they are waist-high because waist-high is what one person can lift alone. The roof was hide over a frame; there are grooves worn in the wall-top where the pegging stones sat, and there are no rafters because there is no wood. A floor of flat stones, and in the northwest quarter of that floor a patch two paces by one where the stones are polished and very slightly dished. That is where a body lay, night after night, facing the doorway, facing the Gap.

The hearth-ring holds a charcoal layer four centimetres thick and no more. Four centimetres is not a life of fires. It is a fire now and then, and the wood was carried up from two days away, which means somebody walked two days uphill with a bundle of fuel on their back in order to burn a double handful of it. You do not do that for warmth; at this pressure and this quantity it would not be worth the carrying. You do it for light, or for cooking one particular thing, or to be seen.

The trough is the whole explanation. Someone found the overhang that drips, and pecked a hollow under it with a harder stone — no metal marks, just ten thousand patient percussions — and set it to catch. It fills at slightly under a litre a day. A litre a day is one person. Not one person and a companion; not one person and a goat; one. The shelf's hydrology does not permit a second. Whatever rule governed the succession of people here, it was never really a rule. It was a rate.

Nine cairns, knee-high, irregularly spaced along the lip. Stand behind any one of them and look west and the sun, on one particular evening of the year, goes down exactly into a notch on the far ridge. Nine cairns, nine evenings, and the nine evenings are not random: they cluster around the turn of the seasons and the days when the rope first comes and last comes. It is a calendar you operate by standing in the right place.

And the quartz. On those evenings, for about eleven minutes, the light comes in along the shelf at a raking angle, and a flake of quartz set on a cairn at the correct pitch throws a hard white point straight down the valley — visible, if you are expecting it, from the terrace of fields you can just make out from here as a paler smudge forty kilometres away. One glint. It says nothing except: *still.* The rope still comes. The garden still stands. Somebody is still up here.

This is the version I prefer. A colleague of mine thinks the cairns are simply nine places where a person sat down over nine hundred years, and that a person alone puts stones on things. I cannot disprove her.

But this much is not interpretation. Whoever they were, they did not hunt the animal under the scree, and it still does not run. They did not cut the willows, which are eighty years old and unscarred. They did not lift the moss for bedding, though moss is the best bedding there is and they slept on stone. They carried their fuel two days uphill rather than burn one handful of a garden that can never be replaced. And every one of them, leaving, left the trough full.

Eleven of them, if the strokes are people. Nine cairns, which does not match, and I am not going to make it match.

The chough feather bound to a splinter with sinew is a brush. That is its function; it has no other possible one; and the eight fine strokes under the overhang were made with it, in soot from the hearth, thinned with water from the trough. Three of the eleven strokes are thicker and were made with a fingertip, at a different time, by someone else.

The twelfth mark, the horizontal one, the line drawn beneath the column the way you draw a line beneath a column, was not made with the brush or with a fingertip. It was made with the flat of a thumb, dragged left to right, and at its left end where the thumb first came down and paused there is a print, and the ridges are legible: an arch with a tented centre, and one old scar across it running corner to corner.

That is the entire body of evidence for the occupant of this place. One thumb, once, pressed a little too hard at the beginning of the last stroke, deciding how to end.

## XVI. Now

So the answer to who lives here is: four hundred spiders, nine choughs, one grey-muzzled archivist, a fungus with no edges, several million animals who are powder most of the year, and a lichen that was already old when the first of them came up with a bundle of wood on his back.

And nobody in the room.

The moss keeps footprints about three seasons. There are none. The walls stand. The chough lands on the corner at ten past nine most mornings and cleans its bill on the whitened stone. The snowfield throws its light up under everything at eleven and the garden hovers, shadowless, unconvincing. At dusk the whole shelf begins to click.

I drank from the trough, once, which I had no right to do, and went back in the morning to see.

It was full.
