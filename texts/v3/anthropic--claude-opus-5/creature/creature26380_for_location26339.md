## The tenants of record

Three patches of moss, and only one of them is well.

Patch A lives under the third bay of the louvre, where the drip line has been stable since the pour. Nineteen hundred square centimetres, bronze-green, closed and springy, its cushion deep enough now that if you pressed a thumb into it the print would stay for a minute. It has silt beneath it to a depth of four millimetres, which is a soil, and in that soil the beginnings of a soil's habits — a hold on water, a bank of dust, a thin acidity of its own making that is very slowly getting into the concrete and will, in a century, matter.

Patch A does not require the things the room withholds. It has no ears to lose. It has no interest in the ninety-second exhale. It photosynthesises on what spills sideways from lamp four, some forty lux, which for a *Bryum* on a wall is not poverty but a living, and it takes its water from above at a rate no plant on the mountain outside could count on: nine hundred impacts a minute, every minute, in every season, forever. In terms of the securities that matter to moss — moisture, temperature, absence of competition, absence of anything at all that eats moss — Gallery Four is a paradise so complete it is almost boring. Patch A is the only inhabitant here that is not enduring the room. It is simply at home in it, and thriving in the small unhurried way of something whose whole ambition is to be slightly larger next year.

Patch B is dying, and it is dying of an adjustment. In the third year a damper linkage was reset by four notches — a legitimate change, signed off, correct — and the drip that fed B moved forty centimetres east. It now falls on bare concrete, where it does nothing but shine. B has been browning from its upslope edge inward at roughly a centimetre a year and is two-thirds the size it was. Nobody has connected the linkage to the moss. There is no reason anyone would. The record of the adjustment is in a binder in the control building, and the record of its consequence is on the wall, and the two will never be read together.

Patch C is neither growing nor failing. It is four centimetres across and has been four centimetres across for two years, held at exactly the rate at which it loses. It is the most accurate instrument in the room.

## The fauna, such as it is

In patch A there is a population — not a colony, nothing so organised — of rotifers, nematodes, and tardigrades, in numbers that would come to a few thousand if you were prepared to spend a week counting under a lens.

They arrived in the way that they always arrive, as dust with a passenger in it. They are the famous ones, the survivors, the animals that go to vacuum and come back. And the peculiar fact of their tenancy here is that Gallery Four never asks them for any of it. There is no drought. The drip does not stop. In four years the moss has not once dried to the point where a tardigrade needed to pull in its legs, void its water, and become a barrel of glass and wait. It is possible — it is likely — that not one individual in this room has ever tunned. A lineage of the most patient creatures on earth is living out its generations in the single location on the mountain where patience has no application.

They have no eyes. Nothing hunts anything. When the gutter is cleaned in autumn, and the grass goes, some of them go too, in the shovel, and there is no event.

## The eleven

Nobody lives in Gallery Four. It is inhabited on a nine-day cycle by two people at a time, out of eleven on the rota, for a mean visit of thirty-eight minutes; longer when the relief-line fitting is inspected, which is every visit, because it has been on the defect list for twenty-six months and will stay there until a shutdown makes it worth reaching.

But eleven people over four years is enough for a culture, and Gallery Four has one, and it is entirely manual.

The base vocabulary is standard — the crane signals, the diver signals, the things any two people in noise agree on inside a week. What has grown on top of that is local and undocumented. A flat hand rocked at chest height means *the fitting is worse than last time*. Two fingers tapped once on the sternum began as *I can feel the load in my chest* and has drifted to mean simply *heavy afternoon*, and is now used outside, in the corridor, in ordinary air, where it is redundant and nobody minds. There is a gesture for the efflorescence straw: thumb and forefinger held a small distance apart, shown briefly, followed by a nod, which is not a question and does not require an answer. It means something like *still there*. It was invented by a fitter who transferred to the coast scheme in the second year and it has outlived her presence entirely; two of the current eleven have never met her and use it fluently.

They carry the room out with them in their bodies. There is a notch at four kilohertz on six of the eleven audiograms, and it is the notch that takes the top off consonants, so that *fifty* and *sixty* become the same word in a certain kind of voice. One of them cannot reliably hear his granddaughter's *s*. He has never once been in this room without protection. It happened anyway, over years, through the flesh and the bones of the skull, the way the transformer note arrives — not through the ear at all, which is why the ear could not be defended.

And they have their two rituals, both of which they perform without agreement and neither of which they discuss.

The first is the corridor. They come out, and they stand in the quiet, and they do not speak for somewhere between ninety seconds and four minutes. Someone timed it once as a joke and got a mean of two minutes ten, which is now itself a piece of local lore, cited by gesture: two fingers, then a flat palm held out — *wait, we're not ready yet*.

The second is the rectangle. In the season when the sun clears the ridge, in the middle of the day, for about forty minutes, the grey light comes down the ladder shaft and lands on the grating. Anyone present stands in it. Nobody has ever explained this, and the explanation is not mysterious: it is the only thing in the chamber that arrived from outside without being pumped, poured, bolted, welded, or hung. It is the only unmanufactured object in the room, and it is not even an object.

## The body in the upper third

There is one continuous living thing in Gallery Four that is larger than all the others together, and it is the fog.

It has a volume — call it forty cubic metres, varying. It has a metabolism, which is eleven-degree air from the machines meeting four-degree air from the cloud. It feeds through the louvre and excretes at nine hundred drips a minute. It has organs: the slow gyres, the leaning, the soft descending column down the ladder cage when the pressure drops. Its molecules turn over completely many times an hour, which is true of a body and is not an objection.

It has been continuous since the fourteenth of March in the second year, which is the day the machine hall first ran warm enough to hold the difference. It has been killed twice. Once by an electrician who opened the corridor door against an open damper, and once by a night in the third winter when the wind came round to the north-west and stood in the louvres for an hour. Both times the room took its six hours and made the weather again, from nothing, from air, without instruction. This is the closest thing to reproduction the chamber offers, and it is closer than it sounds: the fog does not come back. A new one grows in the same place, in the same shape, because the shape is not the fog's, it is the room's.

Which raises the possibility that the thing alive here is the room, and the fog is only its breath, and everything else — the moss, the tardigrades, the eleven — are things that happen inside it.

## The oldest thing

Under the west beam there is a straw of lime eleven millimetres long, growing downward at two and a bit millimetres a year.

It is the only inhabitant of Gallery Four with a past. The moss has no memory; it is only ever its current extent. The fog is four years old at most and two hours old at least. The eleven arrive with their pasts and take them away again. But the straw is a record of every litre that has come through that joint since the pour, kept in the only archival medium the room possesses, which is itself.

And it is the only thing in the chamber that is *tended*. Everything else is maintained — restored to a specification, cleaned, replaced, brought back to what it is supposed to be. The straw has no specification. It is measured with a steel rule at each solstice and the number is chalked on the beam in a column, eight entries now, and the oldest ones have been re-chalked twice by hand because chalk does not last in that wet. So the record of the straw is maintained by the same labour that maintains the pumps, and to no purpose, and with more care.

Rule five is one line long and has no reason attached to it. It doesn't need one.

## And the thing that has no name

On the load ramp, twice a day, for about ninety seconds, the room exhales.

Everyone who works here has decided independently that this is breathing. Everyone knows it is a displaced volume of air responding to a rising water column. Nobody has ever said either half of that out loud, because in Gallery Four nothing is said out loud, and by the time they are in the corridor with their two minutes ten of silence behind them the moment has passed and would be embarrassing.

So what lives in Gallery Four — the actual resident, the one with the strongest claim, the one that could not exist anywhere else in the scheme — is a small god made entirely of a pressure differential and eleven people's refusal to correct one another.

It has no cult and no name. Its only scripture is two entries in the paper log, years apart, in different hands: on one sheet, against the 09:40 ramp, the word *in*; on another sheet, months later, the word *out*. Both were initialled. The engineer who countersigns those sheets has never queried them, which may be indulgence and may be agreement, and cannot be established, because to establish it someone would have to ask.

It will die in a sentence. The licence runs to 2078. At some point after that the water will be stopped, and the machines will cool, and the room will come to four degrees all through, and the fog will fail inside an hour and not regrow. And then two people will be able to stand on that grating and hear each other, and one of them will say, *they used to reckon this place breathed*, and that will be the end of it — not the water, not the cold, not the decommissioning. The saying.

The straw won't notice. Seepage does not need turbines. It will go on down through the dark at two and a bit millimetres a year, unmeasured, uncharted, the room's slow mineral animal, writing the only sentence it knows in the only direction available to it.
