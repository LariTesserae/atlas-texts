## The Held

They call it the Held. *Throat* is what the plain calls it, the herders and the millet farmers who come down with pots, and the keepers tolerate the word the way you tolerate a nickname given by someone who does not live in your house.

There are eighty-one of them this season. You will hear them before you see anything: a wall of overlapping bellowing that resolves, as your ears adjust, into ordinary domestic content — a woman on the sixth landing screaming up four tiers that the grey goat has come back on its own, an old man screaming down that he has heard, he has heard, he heard the first time. Nobody in the Held is angry. This is simply the volume at which they exist. A keeper who goes up onto the plain to trade is recognizable at a hundred paces by voice alone, and is laughed at, and comes home hoarse and offended. They in turn describe surface people as *mumblers*, and mean it clinically: those who speak as though already dying.

They are small, most of them, and very broad through the foot. The sole of a keeper's foot is a slab — splayed, fissured at the heel, the toes wide-spaced from a lifetime of gripping wet stone barefoot, the pads so thickened that they can stand on a fresh flint chip and feel only that something is there. The other diagnostic is the shoulder and the crown: a hard flattened patch, hairless, polished, where the rim of a pot has sat since childhood. Wet-tier dwellers carry pale branching patterns on their shins and forearms where the damp has gotten into the skin — a fungus, harmless, permanent, and read here as ornament and as proof of tenure. Umaya of the eighth tier has it up to the hip in a shape her family calls *the fern*, and she will show you, and expect admiration.

Their voices fail. This is the shape of a life in the Held. You are born into a roar; your first word is shouted; you shout for forty years, and then somewhere around fifty the throat gives out and goes to a whisper and a rasp, and the roar closes over you and you become, functionally, deaf and mute in the same year — not because your ears die (though they do, in the middle registers, early: keepers cannot hear a normal speaking voice by thirty) but because the well takes your ability to be heard.

And so they have hands.

There is a full second language in the Held, made of hands and shoulders and the tilt of the face, and its existence is entirely owed to the swifts. For two hours a day, dawn and dusk, ten thousand birds make speech physically impossible, and the rule is that this is rest. What the rule does not say, because everyone knows it, is that the swift hours are when everything that matters is said. You cannot shout *I am frightened* across four tiers to your mother. You cannot bellow a proposal of marriage into a colonnade that will hand it to sixty strangers. So the public register is enormous and the private register is silent, and every tender, shameful, precise, or dangerous thing in this culture is transacted with the fingers in the screaming hour, sitting shoulder to shoulder on a warm landing with the sky going the color of a plum.

Which means the old, who cannot shout, live permanently in the intimate language. Elders in the Held are handled the way you handle a confidence. Their opinions arrive slowly, in a form that requires you to sit down, face them, and watch. A young keeper's grief is that she must scream at her grandmother's tier all day about the goat, and can only tell her the true thing at dusk.

The vertical arrangement is not what a stranger guesses. The dry upper tiers — one, two, three, cool at dawn, in the wind, near the fig and the squash and the news — are for the young, the newly married, the visiting, and the noisy. Status runs downward. To be given a chamber on the sixth or seventh, in the wet, in the held heat, with condensation letting go on your roof all night at a rate you sleep to, is promotion. The people who read the water live near the water. Their skin is ruined and their joints ache and they would not move up for anything, and they refer to the second tier the way a city man refers to the suburbs: pleasant, and for people with nothing to do.

What they do is maintain. Not build. It is important to them that nothing here is being built. The Held arrived at itself generations ago, and the whole occupation of eighty-one people is to lose to decay at exactly the rate decay advances, and no faster, and — this is stated as a moral matter — no slower. To repair a thing before it needs repair is a vanity and wastes stone. To repair it late is to have let the earth in.

Because that is the theology, and it is coherent, and it is the key to all of them: under every faced surface of this place there is soft river mud, and the mud wants to come in. Below the lip you may touch only made things. The keepers do not consider this a curiosity of construction. They consider it the condition of being alive down here. The Held is a wound kept open in silt by artifice, and they are the artifice. Raw earth is not sacred and not evil; it is *the unmade*, and it may not come down the stairs. A child who carries a handful of dust below the lip is not beaten but is made to carry it back up, alone, and put it where it came from, and this errand is remembered for life. Clay for a patch must first be cooked — fired, or slaked with lime, or at minimum kneaded and dried in the sun on the plain — before it may descend. Made, then admitted. Their word for a finished repair and their word for a decent person are the same word.

Authority is the right to cut stone. There are five bronze chisels, worn to stubs, kept in a rolled hide on the fifth tier in a niche with a lip so it cannot be knocked out, and the person who keeps the roll is the Chisel, and there is one. The Chisel is not chosen by age or descent. The Chisel is chosen by ear. A candidate is walked to a wall and told to find the fault, and taps, and listens — in all that noise, listens — and names it: *behind here, two hands in, the bedding has gone hollow.* Then they open it and see. Hanaró has been Chisel for nineteen years and is stone deaf to voices and can still do this, and nobody can explain it, and she cannot either; she says the well tells her, and what she means is that she has heard this place every day of her life and knows the difference between its noise and its noise-with-something-wrong-in-it.

For the same reason there is a person called the ear, who is not the Chisel and is often a child or an odd adult unfit for other work, whose whole task is to walk the tiers and listen to the two hundred drips. Every drip has a pitch. A pitch that changes means water has found a new path, and a new path means a joint has opened, and an opened joint means mud. The hanging clay pots — the ones set to sound a drip that kept people awake — are also tuning forks: a drip that no longer rings its pot has moved. The Held is diagnosed by sound the way another people would diagnose by sight. Their most serious verb, applied to walls, marriages, and children, translates roughly as *to have gone quiet wrongly*.

---

They do not farm. Eighty-one people who never leave a hole in the ground are fed by the plain, and the arrangement is old and exact: water is not sold, water is given, and every pot leaves something at the wide first landing — a fistful of grain, two figs, a cup of oil for the lamps, a length of new rope, a fired brick. A brick is the most welcome. Nobody counts precisely, and everybody counts.

Their other wealth is bird. The upper landings must be swept every morning of swift lime, and the sweepings are not refuse; they are the best fertilizer within four days' walk, and the millet on the plain is grown on the Held's droppings. Twice a year, at night, when the bats are out hunting, four or five members of one family go into the lowest west gallery wrapped head to foot in wet cloth, holding their breath in relays, and shovel guano into baskets. This family is honored, marries well, eats first, and dies early, coughing, of what the keepers call *the stone lung*, which they attribute to breathing the dust and which they are entirely correct about. They are the ash-lungs. They are not permitted to do the harvest before they are grown or after they have their first bad winter, and the man who insists on going down anyway, past his time, is a stock figure in their songs.
