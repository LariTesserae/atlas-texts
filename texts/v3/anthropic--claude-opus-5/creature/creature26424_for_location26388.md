## The Crews

Eleven hundred on the books. Four hundred below at any hour, which is not many for four kilometres, and this is why you can walk twenty minutes down the west terrace and meet nobody, and then come round a spoil ramp into forty of them under three lamps, all sitting, all facing the same wall, eating.

They are not from here. Almost none of them is from anywhere in the sense the surface means it. They are wet-trades people, formwork and steel and pour, and they come from other holes — the Kirn works, the Brace, the long chalk cutting whose name they say without the article, as though it were a person. They talk about pours the way other people talk about weather years: the bad winter at the Brace when the vibrators failed and a whole flank came out honeycombed, the pour at Kirn that cured for nine weeks and would not stop, that you could still warm your hands on at Christmas. Some of these are lies. The lies are old enough now to be inherited, which is a kind of truth.

They do not count in years. They count in bays. A man's standing is the date stamped at the corner of the first bay he worked, and when they want to know about a stranger they do not ask his age, they ask what he came in on. *Sixty-one*, he says, and the old ones make a small noise, because Sixty-One was the year the fans were still being commissioned and the fog never lifted, and anyone who came in then and is still here has been decided about.

## Bodies

You can pick them out on the surface, on the rare days they go up, by four things.

The eyes. Twelve metres of orange and then grey; they have spent years reading mass rather than surface, and they read it well — a caster can tell you at thirty metres in the dark hours whether a bay has been struck, by the way the black stands. In daylight they squint like sick men and they wear their caps low even indoors, and they distrust noon.

The hands. Cement bite at the wrist where the glove ends and the sleeve rides up: a shallow chemical ulcer, alkaline, painless, and it does not heal in the cold. They compare them. There is a species of vanity in it. Under the nails, grey. In the palm, the flat callus of the poker handle, and beneath that a numbness in two fingers that arrives at about eight years and does not leave.

The lungs. The spoil ramps are the old works broken up, and breaking concrete is silica, and the dry hours carry it the length of the trough. So the dry hours are masked hours, and the wet hours — blind, dripping, miserable, the hours nobody wants — are the healthy ones. They know this. They still prefer the wind.

And the walk. They stop. Mid-stride, mid-sentence, four times a minute, for a beat and a half. On the surface it reads as indecision or as insolence. It is the compressors.

## Speech

The twelve ammonia machines beat out of phase and the pressure swells and dies on a cycle of about eleven seconds, and no one here has ever tried to talk across the swell. They talk in the troughs. This gives their speech a shape: seven seconds of ordinary quick clear sentence, then a pause of four in which the face stays exactly as it was, holding the thought where it was set down, then the sentence continues from precisely that point.

It is not slowness. Watch them: the eyes do not go anywhere in the pause. They have simply learned that speech is a thing you do in the gaps of a larger noise, and they have learned it so thoroughly that they do it in the compressor halls' shadow at the far end of the works where the beat is barely a shiver, and in the mess huts, and in their beds, and on the surface in front of doctors, who write things down.

Their vocabulary is small and exact and mostly about states of concrete. *Green. Warm. Set. Blind.* They have nine words for kinds of leak and none for kinds of tree. To go up means to be promoted, to be dismissed, to retire, and to die, and the sentence *he went up in the spring* is not ambiguous to them, because they know which, and it would not occur to them to say more.

## The Fan Houses, and the Trade in Dryness

The weathermakers live in their machines. Six squat houses on the west terrace, one gang each, and a rivalry between the six that is a hundred years old in a works fifteen years deep, imported wholesale from previous holes.

In a cavern at saturation, dryness is currency. The fan men have the intake heat, and behind each fan house is a rack, and on the rack are socks. Boots, gloves, tobacco, letters, playing cards, the paper prints of drawings that the office sends down and that go limp in ninety minutes: all of it dries in the fan houses, and all of it dries at a price. Not money. Favours, and the favours are specific — an extra ten minutes of holding speed at the end of a shift so a gang can finish a lift without a gale in their faces; a whisper in advance if the changeover is going to run early.

They are blamed for everything, as the record says, and they cultivate it. They have the only clocks that matter. Below, nobody wears a watch; you know the hour by the air. So the fan men own time, and they know it, and they are insufferable, and when a fan bearing runs hot and one house drops out and the trough goes still and unbreathed for forty minutes, four hundred people look west with the same face, and the fan men do not come out.

## The Beat Men

The compressor halls are warm. This one fact separates their crews from everyone else more than trade or seniority could.

They wear cotton. They sweat, which nobody else in the works does, and they come out of the hall doors steaming into the cold in a way that makes other gangs stop and watch, the way you would watch a man walk out of a fire. They do not speak while inside — cannot — and have a full hand-language of about two hundred signs, which they go on using outside, so that a beat gang eating together looks like a table of conspirators and is in fact discussing a valve.

They have lost the same band of hearing, all of them, in the same place. It takes the sibilants first. You learn to talk to them without an *s* — the whole works has a slow flat sibilant-free register kept for the beat men, and it is used with affection, which they resent, and which they would miss.

## The Pumphouse

Two people and two dogs, and the two people have been here since Fifty-Four, and whether they are married is not known and not asked.

They hold the hydrology. All of it: eleven kilometres of cast channel, three hundred and some weirs, the grit traps that block in a particular order, which slot on the north flank runs backwards when the fans are at full and why. There is a drawing of it in the office above ground and the drawing has been wrong since the second year. The true drawing is a sheet of plywood on the pumphouse wall in grease pencil, corrected over and again, black where the corrections have overlaid each other into illegibility, and the pumphouse pair can read it because they remember what is under the black.

The dogs are deaf as posts and are working animals. What they work is pressure. A dog will come up off the floor eight or nine seconds before a big bay gives out its deep eighth-day report, and it will stand facing the bay, and the pumphouse pair have made this into a system: if the dogs stand and no report follows, somebody walks that bay with a hammer. They are, in effect, the works' only reliable instrument, and they are fed the best of everything, and they are getting old, and nobody will talk about it.

## The Listener

One woman, no gang, no hours. She works the wet hours because the wet hours are quiet in the register she needs.

She is mostly deaf herself, which she says is the point. She carries a mild steel rod a metre long and a rubber pad, and she goes bay to bay setting the rod against the face and her forehead against the rod, and what comes up the steel is not sound but a texture in the bone. Green concrete has a grain to it, and the grain coarsens as it sets, and a void has no grain at all — it is a smoothness where there should be tooth, and she knows that smoothness the way you know a word is missing from a sentence.

She has been wrong four times in eleven years and the four times are written in the front of her book. The office does not like her. The office has ultrasonic equipment in a case. The equipment is used to confirm her, and everyone including the office knows that this is the order of operations.

## Bay Forty-One

Nothing happened at Bay Forty-One. This is the official position and it has never been contested, because contesting it would require saying what did not happen.

What is observable: crews walking the north terrace pass the corner of Forty-One and touch the date stamp with two fingers, without breaking stride, without looking, the way you touch a doorframe in your own house. Newcomers copy it before they ask, and by the time they think to ask they have stopped wanting to.

The rule that bodies go up is absolute and is enforced with a rigidity that is out of all proportion to any other rule here, and it has been enforced without exception since Forty-One. They will hold a shaft for a body. They will stop the fans for a body. And the men who insist on this most fiercely are the ones who were on the pour.

## The Cold Side

Every tenth day, the Fourteenth Bay bulkhead cycles, and six people come up.

Quilted suits, hoods back, and the same frostbite in the same places on all six faces — the cheekbones, the bridge of the nose — so that they look related, and they are not. They come to the second mess. They take one loaf each and hot tea, which they hold with both hands and do not drink for a long time. They ask about the weather above. Not the fan weather: the weather, up the shafts, on the surface, which most of the works has not seen in months and does not think about. Is it raining. Has it turned. Somebody always answers with more detail than they have, and the six listen to it with their eyes shut.

They do not talk about below. Nobody asks. It is not fear exactly; it is the same tact that operates around Forty-One, and it is understood that whatever else the cold is for, the six are the ones who go and stand in it, and the whole four kilometres of casting and pumping and blowing exists to keep the place they work in colder than anything ought to be.

Then the bulkhead cycles and they go back down, and the loaves go with them, and for about an hour afterwards the mess is unusually quiet, and the beat swells and dies and swells and nobody uses the troughs.

## The Child

There is one, and she is nine, and she should not exist here, and the paperwork by which she does exist is a small permanent embarrassment three levels above anyone who has ever met her.

She has never seen the sun except as four grey columns. She knows snow as a shaft phenomenon: a thing that happens in a circle of floor, in slow rotation, at Numbers One through Four. She was the one who bricked the kerb around the grass at Number Two, using proper technique, badly, aged seven, and she keeps a list on the back of a formwork tag of everything that has ever come up inside it — eleven entries, mostly guesses, one of them almost certainly a leaf that blew down the shaft and did not grow at all.

She can read a bay's age by walking on it. She can tell a fan house by its note. She cannot sleep in silence and has never had to try. The lemon trees are hers in practice, and they are doing badly, and she has been told they will not fruit, and she waters them on a schedule she wrote herself and will not discuss.

The heron will let her within four metres. Nobody else gets six. She is on the side of the argument that says feeding it is not cruel, and she is nine, so her vote does not count, and she feeds it anyway, and this is why the argument will not be resolved.

## What Lives Here

Ears. That is the whole of it, in the end.

Not builders — the building is incidental, it is poured over and broken up and poured again, and half the boulders on the spoil ramps carry white numbers in the handwriting of men still working here, who recognise their own figures on the underside of a rock and mention it to nobody. Not miners; nothing is taken out. What this population does, generation by imported generation, is listen: to the cure, to the eighth-day report, to the grain in a steel rod, to the dogs, to the note of a bearing going, to blind water somewhere behind a wall of ice.

And twice a day the fans change over, and four hundred people in a four-kilometre trough stop what they are doing and stand still in whatever they are standing in, and hold their tools where they are, and let the note go all the way up or all the way down. Six seconds. Ten. Nobody is looking at anyone. In the shaft-light at Number Three the heron does not move, because it never moves, and it is the only thing down here that has come from the sky and stayed, and it has learned nothing.

They have. That is the difference, and that is what lives here.
