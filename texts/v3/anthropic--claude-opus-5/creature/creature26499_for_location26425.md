## The Wearers

Begin with the sternum, because they do.

Under the strap, under the brass, every one of them carries a raised seam of skin running from the notch of the throat to the pit of the stomach, thin, unvarying, the same seam you find on the ridgelines. They do not point it out. If you ask — and asking requires taking off your gloves and pressing your palm flat to a stranger's palm, so you will not ask lightly — they will confirm it without interest, the way you might confirm that you have a spine. They believe they were cast. They believe the halves of the mould closed on them at the same instant they closed on the plateau, and that this is neither remarkable nor sad, merely the local method of manufacture. Whether it is true is not a question they have ever needed answered.

They are pale as the trees, which is to say pale as something that has never been dry. Waxed felt, black-green, beaded all over like the cooling fins. Hair cropped short because long hair holds water and water is the one thing here that is never in short supply. Broad-footed, deliberate, slightly stooped in the way of people whose ribs have been arguing with the air for a lifetime. No one is fat and no one is thin. Their eyes are large and their pupils wide and fixed, because the silver overcast has never once brightened or dimmed, and there is nothing to accommodate to.

They are deaf in the sense you mean. They hear through bone. Sound comes to them as pressure in the femurs and the sinus and the plates of the skull, and they read it with a precision no ear could reach: they can stand still on a bare slab, close their eyes, and tell you within two hundred metres where on a continent they are, because the moiré has not moved since the beginning and their body is a map of it. There is no getting lost on the plateau. There is only being out of tune.

## The Drum

A brass case the size of a closed fist, worn over the seam, held by two straps and a chest-yoke. Inside: a mainspring, an escapement, a balance carrying a sliding weight, a hammer, and a small tensioned skin of gut. It beats. It beats *against*.

The tick is not for the wearer's comfort. It is a counter-argument, delivered to the breastbone at a phase the chord is not offering, and the heart, presented with two rhythms, cannot entrain to either. It stays its own. That is the whole of their medicine, their engineering, their theology, their childhood, and their manners.

Because phase varies across the land, a drum tuned in one place is wrong in another. Walking is therefore not locomotion; it is a continuous act of tuning, thumb on the balance screw, weight easing out a hair's width every few hundred metres, the way another people might breathe. Most country forgives a small error for a while. Some does not. There are places where the interference turns over quickly — the shoulder below the sixth works, the long ramp they call the Fast Ground — and these are crossed at a specific pace, without stopping, hand never leaving the screw, and a tender who sits down in the middle of one will not get up. Such crossings have names and the names are shapes drawn on a palm.

Two spare mainsprings, sewn into the lining above the left hip. A winding key on a cord at the throat, worn smooth and slightly warm from the skin, the only warm object on the plateau.

## Names

A tender's name is their phase.

When two of them meet, the drums beat against each other and produce a slow throb — one in eleven seconds, one in three, one so long it takes a hundred paces to complete — and that throb is how each knows the other, out of sight, in fog, at four hundred metres. They do not have words for one another. They have intervals. A tender met after thirty years is recognised in the first four seconds by the beat between chests, before any face is visible.

This is also how they are counted. Sixty-one drums, across a hundred thousand square kilometres. The number is known and the number has not changed. When a child is closed into its first drum, an old one walks out to marked stone and sits down. This is not presented as a sacrifice and it is not mourned as a loss. Nothing gains. Nothing is lost. They consider the arithmetic of the population to be of exactly the same kind as the arithmetic of the roof-plate in the fourth street of the third works, and they would find it strange that you find it hard.

## Hands

The stone is young enough to still have edges, and their hands are the price. Gloves are three layers, felt inside leather inside waxed leather, restitched constantly; a tender's gloves outlast their drums.

But speech is skin. Palm to palm, thumb-pad to wrist, and for the long forms forehead to forehead with the bone conducting. So to speak is to bare the hands in a world that will open them, and every conversation costs a little blood, and they budget accordingly. They are famously courteous and famously brief. A greeting is two taps at the base of the thumb. A full account of a season's circuit — where the frost was, which bearing sang, how far a lane had crept — can take four hours of continuous contact, both parties seated, hands wrapped together in a shared cloth to keep the meal of the rock out of the cuts.

Their other language runs in the rails.

The rails were never for carts. They are laid to a hair's tolerance across gorges and along the naves because iron carries a click for hundreds of kilometres, and a drum pressed case-first to a rail will put its tick into the whole network. Lay your cheek on the metal at the ninth works and you will feel someone tapping at the second, a slow patient hand four days' sound away, reporting a stalled crank or asking for the count. This is the only conversation on the plateau that outruns walking. It is also the only thing here that resembles news.

## Where They Sleep

Nothing with a pulse can rest inside the chord. A drum will keep a heart alive but it will not let it be quiet, and a body that never rests will not last a season. So they sleep in nodes.

Some nodes are the great quiet lanes. But a building of iron makes its own small cancellations — a niche behind a boiler drum, a stairwell landing, a two-metre pocket in a gallery — and every one of these has been found, measured, and built into. That is why the dormitory blocks are the shape they are: corridors that bend for no reason, stairs that climb to a single closed door, a room three metres square with a bed in it reached through four turnings, and beside it a hall the length of a village with nothing in it at all, because the hall is loud and the closet is silent. The bathhouse sits on a node the size of a barn — the only such node in the eastern works, which is why there is a bathhouse there and not anywhere else. The clock face has no hands because its spindle-hole is the peg where a tender hangs a drum while washing. That is all the clock ever was.

To lie down in a still room and unstrap the case is the great pleasure of their lives. They describe it, palm to palm, as being *let go of*. They do not linger. Frost blooms in silence; a body resting is a small silence held in the arms of the works, and they get up before they have quite had enough, every time, out of an old politeness toward stone.

## The Cradles

Here is the difficulty they live inside, and they know it perfectly well.

An infant's heart is too fast and too weak to be argued with. It cannot wear a drum. So children are born and raised in the deep lanes, in the moss that comes to mid-thigh, under the combed ranks of pale trees, in the only places on the plateau where a heart may beat unassisted — which are also the wounds, the paying-out, the exact places their entire continental engine exists to keep from widening.

Three lanes are kept as cradles. Their mouths are calibrated. A works tender will spend a decade adjusting the trim of a single flywheel so that a node three hundred kilometres away stays eleven metres wide and not twelve, and the reason is a child in the moss who is not yet old enough to be closed.

In the cradles there are old ones who never leave and no longer wear drums at all, whose hearts have gone back to their own time and cannot be argued with again. They are the ones who teach the hands. A child learns to speak by touch in silence, learns the taste of condensate off a needle-groove, learns to be pulled out of moss when it sinks, and learns nothing whatever of the chord until the day it is walked to the lane's edge and set down on sharp stone with the brass on its chest and the strap drawn tight.

They call this the closing. Afterwards it cannot go back. A heart taught to argue will not rest in quiet ground; the cradles become, for the rest of a life, places one visits with a headache and leaves early. The old ones in the moss and the drummed on the ridges cannot spend a full night in one another's country. They accept this the way they accept the three degrees. It is simply the shape of the mould.

## The Sitting Ones

On resonant rock nothing is released. A body laid on sharp ground will not soften, will not sink, will not be taken; it will stand or sit there beaded with condensate, faceted, held in its note, for as long as the note is held.

There are, at last count, nineteen of them. Marked slabs on marked ground, chosen by the ones who used them, mostly at the ends of long circuits: a figure in waxed felt seated with the drum stopped and the key still on its cord, dew on the eyelashes, hands wrapped in the speaking-cloth. The passing tender kneels, presses forehead to forehead, receives nothing, and goes on. It is not reverence exactly. It is the courtesy owed to a colleague who is still on duty.

Anyone who dies within reach of a lane is carried in and laid in the moss instead, where the debt is paid out at once and thoroughly, and is gone in a season. Both are considered decent. They differ only in whether the plateau was willing to take the body back.

## The Other Residents

They will tell you, if you ask properly, that they are not the only ones keeping the place.

Each lane's forest is one organism, and each has a name that is a shape drawn on the palm — a stand that has been walking down a canyon since the beginning at the speed of a slow gesture. A tender crossing a cradle will stop and lay a bare hand on the pale bark for a while. What comes through is the drip: a hundred litres a day per tree, arriving at the roots in a rhythm that is not the chord and not any drum, the only third time in the world. They regard this as company. Certain of them, near the end, ask to be laid against a particular trunk.

And the works. The tenders speak to the works, hand flat on iron, and would think you were being obtuse if you asked whether anyone answers. Nobody built the flywheels, so far as they know, and nobody feeds them. Their account — given rarely, only in the cradles, only by touch — is that the plateau is still rising, minutely, and that the rising is the mainspring: it turns the wheels, the wheels hold the chord, the chord keeps the rock from paying its debt, and the lanes take away exactly as much as the rising provides, and in the lanes the forests grow, and in the forests the children can have hearts. Their own drums are wound with a key, and the key's spring is wound in the works, and the works are wound by the mountain getting taller. Everything in their lives is powered by a growth no instrument here can measure.

They do not think this is a story about an ending. They think it is a description of a machine in good order, and they are inside it, sixty-one of them, walking their circuits, patching the plate in the fourth street at precisely the rate it is being drilled, listening at the rails, bleeding a little every time they speak, sleeping in closets and getting up early, all of them going nowhere with tremendous power.
