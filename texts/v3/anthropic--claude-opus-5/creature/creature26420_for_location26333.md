## The people

There are people, and there is nothing else that moves under its own decision except water. This is the first thing to understand about them, and everything else follows from it.

They are tall and narrow and slow, and they are slow because the air will not permit anything else. The chest is long — deep from breastbone to spine, so that a person seen sideways looks like a plank set on edge — and the breathing is deliberate, four or five long draws a minute at rest, so that in conversation the pauses fall where the breath needs them rather than where the sense does. A visitor from a thicker air would think them grave, or reluctant, or slightly bored. They are none of those things. They are simply working with what the sky gives them, and hurry is not among their available actions. Nobody on the Upper Shelf has ever run. There is no word for it. There is a word for the loose staggering scramble you make when talus goes out from under your foot, and it is a mildly shameful word, and it is the closest thing they have.

The skin is very dark and very fine, and it is dark for the reason that the light is shadowless. That white even glare comes from the whole dome of the cloud at once, from every angle, off every wet surface underneath as well; there is no shade to step into, no leaf-dapple, no side of a wall that is safe. A hat is useless when the ground shines. So they are the colour of wet basalt, and they are permanently, everywhere, slightly waterlogged — the fingers pleated, the soles pale and soft, the whorls of the fingertips standing up like the rims of little terraces. Their hands do not callus the way hands do in dry countries. They toughen instead into something more like sodden bark, and they are enormously strong, and they hold obsidian with a delicacy you would not believe until you have watched a woman strike forty blades in a morning without once cutting herself on stone that opens skin before the skin can report it.

They carry no fat. There is none to carry: no oil, no marrow, no cream, nothing in the whole diet that yields it. So the body is all long muscle and tendon standing under thin skin, legible as a stripped mechanism, and the ribs show, and this is not hunger. Nobody here is hungry. The terraces produce what the terraces produce and it is enough, and it is the same, and it is enough.

The teeth wear down flat and early, ground away by the grit that comes off the querns; a person of fifty has molars like worn paving. But the teeth do not rot, because nothing in this country is sweet. They have no word for sweet, either. They have eleven words for degrees of bitter, and the good ones — the ones you would translate as *savour*, *depth*, *rightness* — are all in that family. The fermented algae paste that everyone eats a thumb's-worth of daily, and that everyone would sicken and die slowly without, is called simply the necessary sourness, and the reason for it has been forgotten and the practice has not.

## What they can hear

In a country with three sounds, hearing becomes something else.

They can tell you which cliff a seep is running on by its texture at half a mile. They can hear the change in a flume from the next bench up and know that a leaf has gone into it. A child learns the drain-song before it learns to split a block, and the drain-song is not a metaphor: it is the sound the underhouse channels make when they are clear, memorised as pitch and interval, so that a man walking a street at night walks it with his ears and stops over the one grating where the note has gone thick.

And they are quiet. Not shy — quiet in the way people are quiet in a room where someone is sleeping, permanently, all of them, all their lives. A raised voice reaches the rim and comes back off the scarp, and the whole town hears it, and there is no privacy in a raised voice. So speech is pitched under the water. Two people talking at arm's length sound, from four paces, like nothing at all. They lean in. They touch while they talk — a hand on the forearm to mark emphasis, a thumb pressed to the back of the wrist for assent, because gesture at that distance is cheaper than volume. To a stranger it looks like intimacy on an implausible scale, everyone in the town constantly holding everyone. It is not intimacy. It is grammar.

They sing, but only inside stone and only under the breath, mostly in the bathing rooms where the steam and the close vaulting swallow it. There is no chorus. Nobody has ever heard more than nine people singing at once, and the nine were kneeling in a row on a wet floor with their heads together, making a sound no louder than a pot filling.

The other consequence of the hearing is the smell. In a world with no flower and no fruit and no rot but the slow clean rot of peat, there are perhaps six odours: sulphur, wet stone, fern, smoke, hot clay, and people. So people are the great fragrance of the Upper Shelf, and they know each other by it absolutely, faster and more surely than by face — and faces are hard to read anyway under shadowless light, which flattens everything and hides nothing. The greeting is a slight turn of the head and one breath taken in through the nose, and the response is the same, and it takes less than a second, and it is unforgeable.

## The only other thing that moves

They divide what exists into five kinds: stone, water, cloud, the mat, and people. That is the whole inventory, and it is complete, and they are correct.

Of these, water is the only one that acts. Stone waits and occasionally lets go. Cloud arrives at its hour like a shutter. The mat thickens and slumps and does not choose. But water leaves the joint-plane when it likes, it wanders across the flats without a channel, it floods a hollow and abandons it, it goes over the lip, it comes up scalding in one place and tepid in another, it moves twenty feet along a fault in a night and ruins a season of work. It is the only thing besides a person that will surprise you.

So their relationship to water is the closest thing they have to religion, and it is entirely practical, and it looks from outside like municipal drainage. Every stepped street, every cambered runnel, every clay gutter and stone-lined underhouse drain is an argument conducted with the one other agency in the world, and the argument is polite, and it is never won. You do not stop water. You offer it a better way down. The highest office in the town belongs to the people who cut flumes, and their skill is not strength but a kind of listening — laying a channel so that the water takes it willingly, without a lip of standing wet anywhere along the run, so that it leaves the bench in one clean unbroken line exactly like the rim falls do. A flume that hisses is a failure. A flume that is silent is beautiful, and they will bring you to see it, and stand beside you, and say nothing, for some time.

Nothing else in their world has ever wanted anything. They cannot conceive of being wanted, or hunted, or watched. There is no bogey in the dark for their children, because the dark contains nothing; the total silence of the plateau at night, which sits on a visitor's chest like a hand, is to them simply the sound of things being in order. What frightens them is short and specific: the drop, scald, the sheet-flood that comes across a flat without a sound and puts a warm foot of water through a doorway, and the slab. Everyone can name the slabs that have come down. There have been nine.

## The flash

Once, in the clubmoss beds, they collect spore.

It is the only dry thing in the country — a fine pale dust that will not wet, that skates on the surface of a puddle, that pours through the fingers like nothing else on the shelf pours. Gathered into a paper of bast and puffed across a flame, it goes off in a single silent white sheet of light.

For one instant, everything has a shadow.

They use it to open a kiln firing, and they use it once at the naming of each child, in a courtyard, at night, with the family standing round in a ring; and in that hundredth of a second every person present sees, for the only time in their lives, their own shape laid out black on wet stone behind them. Then it is gone and the shadowless world closes over. Nobody discusses it. The child, of course, does not remember. It is done for the child anyway.

## The tally of clicks

Somebody is always counting the talus.

It is a real office and an old one, held usually by the very old, who sit on a particular ledge above the second bench with a bundle of knotted cords and mark every collapse they hear — one, two an hour, the plateau settling a fraction further into itself — and the day's number is added to a run of numbers on fired clay tablets that goes back further than the plan-song does. Nothing has ever been found in it. The count is the same now as it was for the grandparents of everyone living. That is the point: the tally exists so that if the world ever changes, someone will know the day it began.

They keep the days by water. The great spring never varies, so a drip vessel is a perfect clock, and the long count is kept as a tally of the double flaring — the gold at dawn, the gold at dusk, when every wet surface on the plateau ignites at once and the whole shelf is briefly one pane of glass. Twice a day the world declares itself and someone marks a cord. There are no stars. There is no moon. The sky is a lid at three hundred feet, and their cosmology is accordingly modest: the world is a shelf between the lid and the drop, and it is new, and they know it is new because the falls have not yet begun to eat the cliff, and any child can be shown two faces of a cleft that still match.

And in the archive of the spring-keepers there is a mark cut into the mineral rim of the great spring at a height that the ochre no longer reaches, and it is older than the plan, and the keepers maintain it, and clean it, and do not discuss what it means. Some of them have privately worked it out. The heat is going, slowly, over a length of time that has no name in their language because their language has no need of numbers that large. There is nothing to be done and nobody to tell.

## The empty quarters

Half the town stands sound and swept and unoccupied, doorways open, floors warm, mats growing on the roofs. They maintain it exactly. They do not know why it is there, and they use it constantly.

When two people cannot bear each other any longer — and people are the only thing here that can be unbearable — one of them takes a mat and a jar and goes and lives in the empty terraces for a count of two or three hundred days. This is not exile and it is not shameful; it is what the empty quarters are for, though that is not the same as saying it is what they were built for. Someone comes up every few days with grain and leaves it on the sill. The quiet does the work. Then the person comes back, and the house is re-entered, and the matter is not mentioned again, ever, by anyone.
