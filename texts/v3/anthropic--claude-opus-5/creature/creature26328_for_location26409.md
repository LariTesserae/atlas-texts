## The tenant

There is one, and it is not the eel.

The six people who go down have between them four names for it, which is a good sign; nobody with a settled name for a thing has been paying attention. The man who owns the field calls it *himself*, and does not enlarge on this, and changes the subject with the particular smoothness of a man who has thought about something a great deal in the tractor. The woman who dives it most often, and who has mapped the room twice and got two different maps, calls it the gardener. The hydrologist who came out about the subsidence and stayed for other reasons calls it the tenant, and has written *tenant?* in the margin of a survey sheet, and underlined it, and never taken it further than that. The two who have been down once each call it nothing. The one who has been down twice and will not go a third time calls it, in a particular voice, *whoever's keeping that place*.

That last is nearest.

## You have already touched it

Everyone who goes down touches it inside the first second, and goes on touching it for the whole of the visit, and comes up having touched nothing else.

The weed is not its food and it is not its neighbour. The weed is the part of it that is cheap enough to leave lying about. Think of a creature that cannot afford to be present everywhere at once and so keeps a hide spread over the surfaces of its house — a skin four paces by three, growing on the walls and the fallen slabs and itself, braided into itself so that no seam is findable, because there is no seam, because it is one continuous membrane and the braiding is only what a thing does with its own hands when it has nothing else to hold.

When you push your arm in, the weed parts without resistance and without hurry. This is not indifference. It is the courtesy of something enormously slower than you deciding not to be in your way. The hand-shaped absence that stays behind you, and seals over the following hour, is the shape being held. Not remembered — *held*, the way you would hold a door for someone still crossing the yard.

## The part that can afford to happen

The divers say the animal is never in the tube. This is true and it is the wrong way round.

The tube is where it is. The tube is *when* it is. A wrist-thick clean-walled corridor opening ahead of nothing and closing behind nothing, travelling at a fast walking pace: that is not the wake of a body, that is the body, or the portion of the body that the room's poverty of interval will currently support. The rest of it is not elsewhere in space. It is elsewhere in the year. Its head, if it has one, was in this room in October. Its hind end is somewhere in next March and has not arrived. What crosses the floor at walking pace is a section — the only section that can presently be paid for — and it moves because the moment moves, and it goes around the doorframe rather than through it because it is, in that instant, a creature going about its house and not a creature going out.

You cannot see it for the plainest of reasons. Seeing takes time. It has not got the time to give you.

Nine crossings since the flood. That is not nine journeys. That is nine seconds of a life.

## The rate

It works at one or two motions an hour. You have heard them: that low padded thud, felt in the sternum, once or twice between one hour and the next. That is the sound of a large slow thing settling by a hair, and the large slow thing is not the room. It is the tenant, putting something down.

At that rate a gesture takes days. To reach across the room and shift one of the light-globes three inches to the left is a season's work, and this is precisely what it has been doing since September — the ninth globe, the flat pewter Tuesday, has moved a hand's breadth from where the first survey put it, and is expected, if anyone bothers to check, to finish arriving somewhere around Candlemas. Nobody knows why. It may be that the pewter Tuesday looks better there. There is no reason to assume it is not that.

The room grows two hand-widths a season in every direction. This is not subsidence and it is not erosion. It is sweeping. It is the outward pressure of a householder who has for a very long time had exactly enough room and has lately begun to want a little more.

## What it brings home

Here is the doorframe, standing free in the middle of the floor with no wall attached and no door, its hinge sockets rusted to a red stain.

Go through it and the water is the same water at a different hour. Warmer by a degree. Grit that isn't there on the near side. Once, a blackthorn petal in a man's hair, in November — and that was not a marvel, that was litter. That was something dropped in the doorway on the way in, the way you drop a glove in the hall.

The forty globes are the catch.

It goes out. Not up through the water — up through the hour. It goes out into a four o'clock in late August, and it takes the light coming through the blackthorn, the whole undischarged afternoon of it, grain of dust and all, and it brings that back and hangs it in the fronds. This is why the globes are bright and give no light: the light in them has not happened yet and is not going to. It has been prevented. It is being kept. A plum of unspent August, cool and firm as a peeled egg, hung at eye height in a room where nothing can see.

It is a hoarder in the ordinary way of hoarding animals, which is to say it hangs its larder up. The shrimp are the same instinct on a poorer day: they come in with the rain and they starve for interval, they cannot get a stroke finished, and they do not rot, and they hang in the weed like commas — and somebody hung them.
