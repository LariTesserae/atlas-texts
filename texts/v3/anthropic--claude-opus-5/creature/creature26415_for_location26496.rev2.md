# What Lives Here

## The Green, Which Is Not a Plant

Start at the bottom, because here the bottom is nearly everything.

The jade in the lagoons is a single-celled filament wound into a loose left-handed coil about a third of a millimetre long, and there are more of them in one lagoon than there have been human beings. It is not a plant. It has no roots to be killed, no leaves for the dust to burn, no cells to burst — it is a bacterium that learned photosynthesis two billion years before anything invented a stem, and the soda that forbids everything else is the water it was born in. Give it carbonate, warmth, a little phosphate off the escarpment, and dim brown light, and it doubles.

It is also, in a small way, a navigator. Inside each filament are gas-filled vacuoles, and it can build them or crush them. Before dawn the whole population is up, a green skin two centimetres deep lying on the black water, taking what the haze allows. By eleven, when the lid glares and the shallows are past forty degrees, it has ballasted itself down out of the worst of it, and the lagoon looks almost clear, and by four it is coming back up. The colour of the water at any hour of the day is a decision made unanimously by uncountable individuals with no means of consulting one another.

Where the liquor is too strong even for that — the last stinking puddles at the end of a healing channel, the brine in a crack in the crust, the film in the bottom of a drum left standing — the green gives out and something older takes over. Those puddles are rose, carmine, the red of watered blood, and the colour is archaea: single cells stuffed with carotenoid pigment as a sunscreen, thriving at salt concentrations that would mummify a fish in ninety seconds. They are the true residents of the dead middle. They ask nothing but heat and brine and the absence of company. When a flood sheets across the pan and dilutes them, they do not flee; they simply stop, and wait out the wet, and are there when it goes.

Between the two, on any surface the liquor has stood on and left — the flank of a rubble causeway, the boot of a pipe support, the whitened hull of the dead dredger — there is a varnish. It looks like nothing. It looks like dirt on stone. Scrape it and it is layered: bacteria, their mucus, trapped mineral, more bacteria. It is the only skin this landscape has, and it is alive, and it is a tenth of a millimetre thick.

## The Fly

Everything that walks or flies or swims in this basin is, at one or two removes, made of that green filament. The device that does the converting is a fly.

She is small, dark, bristled, unremarkable to look at, and there are enough of her under the No. 2 causeway to be weighed rather than counted. Her larvae live in the shallow warm liquor, grazing the mat off the crust, and they have solved the alkalinity in the only sensible way: they excrete it. Each larva carries a gland that pulls carbonate out of its body and lays it down as lime, so that a mature one is half-armoured in the very mineral the basin is made of, and when it pupates it cements its case to the bottom and the case remains long after, so that the floor of the shallows is paved in the empty houses of previous years, crunching underfoot like shell grit.

The adult walks into the water. She does it wrapped in a silver skin of air held by the hairs on her body, and she goes under and forages along the bottom, dry inside a bubble, in a liquid that would kill any insect that so much as wetted its spiracles, and when she is done she lets go and pops up onto the surface still dry. There is no drama to it. She does it thousands of times a day, and the underside of the surface film along the shore twinkles with the little mercury blobs of flies going about their business in a hostile solution one molecule away from their skin.

Above them stands the stratum. It is not a swarm; a swarm has intent. It is a medium, a metre or two deep, occupying a band that follows the waterline for kilometres with a fidelity that looks surveyed. Walk in and it opens and closes. It does not want you. Nothing in this basin bites; there is no blood economy here. There is only the mat, and the fly, and then everything else.

And in the fly band, unnoticed by anyone who does not kneel: the predators. Wolf spiders the size of a thumbnail, pale as the crust, running the shore at a dead sprint and stopping dead, running and stopping, with the second-by-second decisiveness of things that live on ambush. Small tiger beetles doing the same at a higher gear. A single spider on a shoreline of a hundred thousand million flies is a statistical irrelevance and a complete, satisfied life.

## Three Hundred Fish

In a pool below a spring on the north-east margin, about the size of a garage and fed at eighty-two degrees at the lip and habitable only in the second half of its length, there are perhaps three hundred fish. Palm-length, silver going to slate, with a blue edge to the dorsal in the males when they are holding a nest.

Their ancestors swam in this basin when it was a real lake with real rivers, ten or twelve thousand years ago, and then the lake dried and left them in a scatter of hot puddles around the rim, and everything that could not be done in a hot puddle stopped being done. What they can do is extraordinary and is all forced. They cannot dump ammonia across the gills the way every other fish on earth does, because in water at pH ten and a half the chemistry runs backwards; so they build urea instead, at metabolic cost, continuously, like a mammal. They run their bodies hot and fast — among the highest oxygen demands measured in any fish — and the water they run them in holds almost no oxygen at all by three in the morning, when the mat stops making it and starts consuming it. So at three in the morning the fish are at the surface, all of them, tilted up, working the top two millimetres where the film exchanges with the air, and the pool sounds faintly like light rain.

At forty-two degrees they are fine. At forty-three and a half the proteins begin to go, and they know it, and they hold in the cooler third of the pool and edge out to feed. In February, when the spring's flow slackens and the pool shrinks, the temperature ceiling comes down to meet them. Some years it meets them all the way.

The next pool is eleven hundred metres west. There is no route between them that is not lethal crust. The fish there are slightly deeper in the body and have a heavier jaw, because for four hundred generations they have been grazing a mat that grows on rougher grit, and nobody has ever gone between to correct the difference. There are nineteen such pools that anyone has bothered to list, and there are certainly others behind the seep terraces on the west side that no one has walked to in thirty years. Each one is an island. Each one has its own fish. The entire range of some of these populations is a body of water you could piss across, and they have held it since before agriculture.

## The Pink Line

The flamingos are not really inhabitants. They are weather.

A lesser flamingo is a filter-feeding machine with legs, and the machine is tuned to precisely one input: the coiled green filament. The bill is held upside down, the tongue pumps like a piston twenty times a second, and the water goes through a comb of lamellae with gaps narrower than a human hair, and what stays behind is spirulina and almost nothing else. A bird takes sixty or seventy grams dry weight a day. Multiply by a million. The lagoons behind the cutter-heads are being mown.

They come from four countries because the food does not last anywhere. A lagoon blooms, the word goes out by whatever means it goes out — and nobody knows the means — and within days the pink line lies on the horizon where last month there was nothing. They feed at night as readily as by day; the bill does not need to see. They breed almost nowhere, and not here, so every bird on this pan has come from an egg laid on a mudflat three hundred kilometres north, and will go back there, or not.

The soda takes its cut. Stand too long in the wrong place and the crust builds on the legs in a ring, then a cuff, then a shackle, and a bird carrying enough of it cannot fly, and then it is not a flamingo any more, it is a resource. Along the tidemark on any given morning there are the ones the pan killed in the night, and the marabous already at them.

Which brings up the storks. A hundred and forty of them stand around the settling ponds with their wings held out and their heads sunk, patient as bailiffs, and they defecate down their own legs to cool themselves, which is why the legs are white and why you do not want to be downwind. They are the only animals here that look like they know something. They have the plant's timetable memorised — they know which outfall runs warm at shift change, they know the bagging shed apron, they know the tanker's day. A marabou will stand within four metres of a fitter working a valve and neither will acknowledge the other. They have been colleagues for sixty years.

With them: pelicans in twenties, who cannot feed in the pan at all and roost here anyway, flying out to freshwater forty kilometres off and back, using the basin as a dormitory for the simple reason that nothing can walk to them across it. Gulls at the outfall, screaming, the only loud animals in the basin. And working the fly band, in numbers that have never been counted because counting them is a career: little stints, curlew sandpipers, avocets sweeping, and the small pale plover with a chestnut collar that occurs here and in a handful of other soda pans and nowhere else on earth, running four steps and stopping, running four steps and stopping, shore after shore after shore.

Under the causeway bridges, in the shade of the girders, there are mud nests. Wire-tailed swallows, a dozen pairs, eating the stratum. They have the best address in the basin: shade, unlimited food, no predators, and a structure maintained for them at company expense.

## The Rim, After Dark

The pan kills constantly and pushes the results to the edge, so the edge is patrolled.

Black-backed jackals work the tidemark in pairs, trotting with their heads down along a line as exact as the plover's, and they will eat a flamingo, a dead fish, a windrow of leathery mat, a fly-mass, a bag of canteen scraps. Their feet are cracked. Spotted hyenas come further out than they should, three or four of them, using the causeways because the causeways are the only ground that does not burn, so that a locomotive crew working a night rake will now and then have to stop and let a hyena get off the line, and it will not hurry.

A pair of barn owls lives in the roof of the old No. 4 calciner house, hunting the ballast margins at the rim for gerbils. Nightjars sit on the warm gravel of the main way after dark with their eyes glowing back at the sodium lamps, and lift lazily at the last second. Bats — a lot of bats, more than the vegetation anywhere within sixty kilometres could support — work the lamps and the outfall, and what they are eating is, of course, the fly.

There are no rats in the town. Nobody can quite explain this and everybody has a theory.

## Two Hundred and Forty People

They are not survivors of anything and would be irritated by the suggestion. They are process operators.

Their hands go first: the knuckles crack white and stay cracked, and there is a particular gesture, almost a tic, of working a thumb across the back of the other hand, that every long-serving person here does without noticing. They wear long sleeves in forty-one degrees and think nothing of it. They own goggles that cost very little and are scratched to translucency and are worn anyway. They drink from the tank car, and they are precise about water in a way that outsiders find obsessive — cups covered, taps checked, the tanker's arrival a small fixed point of the week.

They sleep in the dead hours if the shift allows and are most themselves at four in the morning, which is the only cool part of the day and also the hour when the fish are gasping and the flamingos are moving overhead in relays. Night shift in the control room is one man and a panel of instruments from the 1970s, and the rule is that you go out onto the walkway once an hour to look at the plume, because if the plume is wrong you will see it before the panel says so.

The chemist is a woman of fifty-one who has run the same titration four times a day for nineteen years and can tell you what the springs are doing this season before the sample is in the burette, because she can smell it. The rail crew is three men and a driver, and the driver's father drove the same locomotive. The schoolteacher takes forty-one children in two rooms and has a wall chart of the birds because there is nothing else local to make a wall chart of. There is a fitter who keeps four of the cichlids in a plastic tank on his windowsill, topped up from the spring pool in a jerrican, and they have lived three years, and he does not claim this is interesting.

And there is a class of people here who no longer work for the plant and have not left: a retired shunter in a house nobody has reassigned, a widow drawing nothing, two men whose jobs ended with No. 3 calciner in 1994. They are the only people who watch the pan on purpose. Everyone else keeps their eyes at ground level and inside the fence line, because the pan is a flat white nothing you have looked at ten thousand times; but these ones sit out in the evening facing west, where the smudge of the sun goes down into the haze without ever becoming an event, and they can tell you which lagoon the birds are in tonight by the direction the skeins take when they come over.

## What Is Actually Living Here

Nothing here lives *in* the basin. Everything lives on a line drawn across it.

The mat lives on the line where hot spring water has cooled to sixty degrees and no further. The fly lives on the line where the mat meets air. The plover lives on the line where the fly meets crust. The fish live in nineteen disconnected points on the line where a spring's outflow passes through a window four degrees wide. The flamingos live on the line the dredger cut last season. The people live on a line of made ground three metres above the crust, in buildings dissolving from the bottom up, eating food that arrives by rail, drinking water that arrives by rail, working a mineral that grows back.

Draw all the lines and you have a diagram of the basin's inhabitants that is almost entirely edge and almost entirely empty: a thousand square kilometres of hot wet mineral with a few furiously crowded threads laid on it, and along every thread, pressed shoulder to shoulder, more life than the escarpment forest supports in a province.

At three in the morning the night operator comes out onto the walkway to look at the plume. The calciner is under his feet. Somewhere below him the fish are at the surface of their garage, tilted up, breathing the film. Out on the black pan a million animals are murmuring like a crowd in another building, and a skein of them goes over low enough to hear the joints working in the wings, and he looks up, and the plume is fine, and he goes back in.
