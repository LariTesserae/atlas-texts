## What lives at Sowder's Bottom

### The tenant

Start with the difficulty: it cannot be named, because it has never once shown anybody a flower, and the one organ that might key it out is walnut-sized, black, and forty metres down in clay.

So the literature calls it things. *Machina paludis* in one 1903 paper, which nobody has accepted and nobody has withdrawn. "The vegetable colony" in the Ordnance surveyor's marginal note. "The plant," in a 1954 typescript, where the pun is clearly intentional and clearly resented by the typist, who has struck it through and typed it again.

The local phrasing does not name it at all. The local phrasing is *the works*, and when the grinding carries up to the ridge road on a still evening, which is most evenings, the phrase is: **the Bottom's in work.** People say this the way you'd say a neighbour's light is on.

Whether it is one thing is unresolved and probably badly framed. The forty drivewood columns are one individual; the torus proves it, and the fibre-spiral runs the same hand in every trunk. The mat is at least three lineages, possibly nine. The strap-vines are not related to the columns at all — different vascular grammar entirely — and neither are the pod-lungs, which are not even certainly plants below the second metre. Yet the strap-vine grows its own boss to meet the drivewood's boss, and the two bosses are machined to each other, and when a belt parts the replacement is sited two or three centimetres better than the one that failed.

The honest description is not *organism*. The honest description is *concern*: a going concern, several unrelated interests bound into one operation by the fact that the operation works, with fixed capital, a maintenance schedule, a single product, and a labour history.

It also retooled. This is the part that should be said plainly and usually isn't. The wall-scars on the northeast slope count twenty-one openings; at sixty years an opening that is thirteen centuries. The silica bed's grinding, by the growth in the same wall, is four hundred years old. Whatever the works was doing for the first nine hundred years, it was not doing it with a ring. Somewhere around 1600 this thing found a better way to make its seed and built it, and the old way is under the new way, and nobody has cut down to look.

Since then it has taken a brick culvert (1847), left it working, and grown a collar to keep it working. It has taken a four-metre English flywheel (1880s) and put a belt on it and maintained the bearing ever since, to no purpose that survives inspection. It has taken a brass nameplate to the depth of its lettering and no further, and holds it out at reading height.

It acquires technique. It does not acquire it quickly. It has never once given any of it back.

### What it does with you

It cannot see you. There is nothing in the hollow that could be called an eye, and the frond-ceiling does not track.

It can feel you. The mat registers a standing adult inside about ninety seconds — the impression fills, closes, heals, and if you keep standing in the same place on successive visits it will begin, at around day five, to lay a pale plate of stiffened tissue under you. Two of the 1930s survey team found their measuring stations footed like this and wrote it up as convenience. It is convenience. That is exactly what is wrong with it.

Interfere and you get a reply on a nine-day to eleven-month lag, depending on what you interfered with. Block a reed-stack: the charge routes to its neighbour, and within two years the blocked stack has begun to close and a new one is coming up eleven metres away, sited, when you take the trouble to plot it, in the acoustic gap. Cut a strap-vine: replaced in seven weeks. Cut the same vine twice: replaced in five, and the boss has moved.

Nothing in the hollow has ever been observed to respond to a person as a person. Everything in the hollow responds to load, wear, gap, and stoppage, and a person is a small mobile stoppage that sometimes leaves.

### The staff

**The grease-beetle.** Fat, blind, the colour of old lard, a thumb joint long, and the only animal the works keeps on the books.

Somebody in 1968 counted every place in the hollow where one living surface turns against another: bosses, slip-collars, the screw shoulder, the flywheel socket, the six-hundred-metre length of the silica bed reckoned as one continuous bearing. He got 196. He then counted beetles for a fortnight and got 203. The discrepancy of seven is error, or it is reserve, and he was honest enough to say he preferred error.

They eat wear. Ground quartz, lignin flour, the grey slurry that comes off the bed — in at the front, and out of the abdomen as a pale wax with a fingerprint grain to it, which they pack into whatever socket they are currently working. That is the entire transaction of their lives. They have no jaws for anything else and no eyes for anything at all, and they navigate by heat and by vibration: a beetle walks toward friction. Set one on a smooth table and it will not stop, it will walk a slow left-hand spiral until it dries out, the same hand as the fibre in the columns, which somebody will one day either explain or stop mentioning.

They breed in the crush zone — the metre of pulverised bed immediately behind the advancing rim, the one habitat in the basin that is guaranteed bare, warm, and freshly milled. The nursery therefore travels. It goes round the hollow once every fourteen months, and a grease-beetle's life is one lap: hatched at a mark, dead a little short of it, having never in its life been anywhere the ring had not just been.

They are not afraid of hands. They will go up a wrist without hurrying.

**The reed-spider.** Pale, flat as a shim, does not spin, lives inside the pod-lung stacks. When the chord fires it goes up the gas column with the pollen and the steam, six to eleven seconds of that, and it comes back down the outside within the hour. This was written up for eighty years as accidental ejection, which required the spider to be accidentally ejected several thousand times per lifetime and accidentally return.
