# The Sealed

## The wettest thing in the Chamber

Take a person apart and you get forty-odd litres of water, some salt, a little carbon. There is nothing else like that anywhere down here. The Dew House, working two centuries at the coldest corner of the east lobe, has produced less water than is currently walking around inside any one of the four hundred people employed by Whistler Control.

They know this. It is not a metaphor to them and it is not a secret. It is simply the fact about themselves, the way the stacks' fact is that they shine because they are too hot. Every person in the Chamber is a sealed vessel containing the only impossible substance in the world, and the whole of their manners, their laws, their swearing, their courtship and their funerals proceed from the problem of not spilling it.

## How the body holds

They do not drink. There is nothing to drink. They eat glass — the slow hygroscopic alcohol, thick as honey at ten degrees, taken warmed, about half a kilo a day for an adult on shift — and they burn it, and the burning makes water inside them. A day's ration yields somewhere near half a litre in the tissues. Against that they must set every possible loss, and the accounting is tight enough that the body has become an instrument of thrift.

They do not sweat. The glands are there, the way an appendix is there. To break sweat is to be gravely ill; *he's wet* is what a medic says to the family before saying anything else.

They do not, properly speaking, exhale. What comes out of a Chamber nose has been stripped by a set of turbinate scrolls so convoluted that the coroners call them the lace, and it leaves the nostril at nine degrees and nearly dry. Children are taught to breathe as they are taught to walk. Mouth-breathing is the first obscenity a child learns and the first one they are smacked for. An adult who breathes through his mouth in public is understood to be drunk, enraged, or dying, and in all three cases people step back.

Their waste is a paste and a pellet. Their eyes are glossed by a lipid rather than a tear film, which makes the surface of a Chamber eye look faintly oiled, faintly amber, like the wing of a paper-glider. The pupils are very small and never change much; there has never been anything to adapt to. The irises are washed-out, the whole apparatus tuned not to brightness but to the greenish-white register of the rind, in which they can distinguish shades that an outsider's eye lumps together as *pale*. Ask one of them to describe a healthy stack and you will get eleven words for the quality of a glow and none of them will mean anything to you.

The skin is dry, close-grained, slightly waxed, and almost odourless, because odour is a wet invention too — it takes bacteria and bacteria take moisture. A person who smells is a person with something wrong. Physicians diagnose by scent the way physicians elsewhere used to diagnose by pulse.

They are short and deep-chested and heavy through the shoulder, built for nine degrees, and they wear quilted coats indoors and out, and they take them off only to sleep. Their hands are calloused past the second knuckle from pith and rope. Tool handles are pith-wrapped because bare metal at this temperature aches. Their teeth are extraordinary: a lifetime on sugar-alcohols, which no bacterium in the Chamber has learned to ferment, has left them with a language that contains no word for toothache and a mild, complacent contempt for the surface documents, which go on about it at length.

## The mouth, and what is done with it

A voice carries two kilometres in this air. Consequently nobody raises one.

The ordinary register of Chamber speech is a low, level, unhurried murmur pitched just above the grazing of millers, and it is used at all distances, because at all distances it works. Two people forty metres apart on the floor plain converse without effort and without seeming to try. Visitors — there are no visitors, but the documents describe them — apparently found this unnerving, as though everyone in the Chamber were speaking confidentially to everyone else.

A shout is a reserved instrument. It has one meaning, which is *come*, and it carries an obligation: any shout must be answered, out loud, by everyone who hears it, and then those people must go. There is no such thing as a shout for help that goes unanswered, and there is also no such thing as a shout used for anything else. Four people in the last sixty years have shouted frivolously. All four names are known Chamber-wide. This is the entire punishment and it has been sufficient.

Nobody whistles. Whistling is the sound of a wound in a pressure tube, and a tone in an inhabited stack means *someone has been holed, find it, find it now*. A child who whistles is not scolded; the child is held by the shoulders, quite gently, by whichever adult reaches it first, and told what it has just said. Most only do it once.

Singing is permitted and is done constantly, quietly, in unison, in a narrow low register that the ceiling returns about a half-second later. Every stack has a pitch. It is set by the town's oldest unhealed puncture, maintained by Whistler Control as a matter of civic pride, and it drifts flatward over decades as the wound crimps and closes and is replaced by another. So the key of a town moves across a lifetime. The old sing under the young by nearly a tone and know it and do not correct it. To be homesick is to be out of tune, and they say it exactly that way.

## Cloth

A birth is the only flood these people ever see.

It is the one permitted waste, and it is not collected, and there is a specific law forbidding its collection that has never been tested. What is done instead: the linen under the mother is taken out afterward, hung in the interstitial stair where the pressure tubes flex against your shoulders, and left to go stiff and grey and faintly sweet. That cloth belongs to the child. It is dried, beaten soft over years, folded to a palm's width.

It is what they sleep in. No room in the Chamber is ever dark — the lensed apertures glow inward as well as out — so darkness is not a thing that happens to you, it is a thing you do, and every person does it alone with a rag of their own dried birth over their eyes. The blindfold is the most personal object anyone owns. Its condition is read the way a face is read. Lending it is not done. Giving it is a proposal, and the formal phrase for a marriage is *they went blind together*, because in a world with no shadows the only privacy left is sleep, and to close your eyes beside someone is the deep act. Bodies are nothing. Everyone has seen everything since birth, under a flat and shadowless light. Sleep is the intimacy.

At some point in the child's third or fourth year it is carried east to the Dew House and held up in front of a stoppered vessel of pith and glass and shown a thimbleful of water, and given its name. It does not remember this. Everyone has seen the water. Nobody has touched it. *To have seen it* is simply the idiom for being a person; an unnamed child, or an exile, or a lunatic, is called *dry*.

Their oaths are sworn on the stopper. Their obscenities are all wet: *spitter, sweat, leak, you weep.* Their endearments are all dry: *stay sealed. Keep it in. Dry as you are.*

Weeping is physiologically possible and culturally enormous. It is the only wealth every person carries and can spend. A Chamber adult may weep two or three times in a life, and it is spoken of afterward for decades — *she wept at the Terrace, in the low light, in front of the whole shift* — and it settles arguments, and it ends feuds, and it frightens people. Several of the Chamber's standing laws exist because somebody once wept about them.

## Trades

**Wound-readers** go out on the rind on rope, with felt pads and listening tubes, and date a puncture by its pitch: a semitone down per twelve hours, four days to silence. They are required to have absolute pitch and are tested at nine years old, which sorts the population early and permanently. They carry tuning forks that are inherited rather than issued.

**Sweepers** rake the floor plain into the long parallel arcs, and the arcs are not decoration. There are no shadows out there. There is no horizon, no sun, no landmark under a kilometre high, and a flat white brilliance in all directions, and people who lose the rake-lines walk in slow left-hand curves until they stop. The Chamber's word for that death is *brightness*. Where two towns' rake-styles meet, out on the floor between Nine and Eleven, there is a seam, and the seam is a border, and it has been fought over twice.

**Glass-drawers** tap the boles. Theirs is the only permitted wound. They are therefore the minters, and the Chamber's banking is done in sealed pith flasks, and its inflation is measured in millilitres per stack per year, and its most serious financial crime is drinking the float. Glass is money, medicine, antiseptic, solvent, and the only intoxicant, and a drinker is a man eating his own accounts, and everyone can see the flask level. There are no secret alcoholics here. There is no privacy for it.

**Herders** run the millers, and read pressure as the millers do, and fear the still places — dead air behind a trestle, a pinched pass on the inhale — where a herd will simply suffocate in sight of you, quietly, in the bright.

**Surveyors** work the Blind End in pairs with felt soles and a slate, marking the day's advance in centimetres while the roof lets go around them. They are young, they are paid absurdly, and the Chamber's calendar is their handwriting. Nobody here counts years. They count distance. Ask an old woman her age and she will tell you she was born nine hundred and forty metres east of the current face, and you are expected to do the arithmetic yourself.

## The flank

The dead go into the rind.

A cut is made at working height, the body is laid in it with its cloth over its eyes, and the tissue is left to close, which it does over about a season. What follows is the thing no document prepares you for: the stack takes the water, and the water is the richest thing it has ever been given, and for a year or two that patch of rind burns perceptibly brighter than the flank around it. Grave-light. You can stand at the base of Fourteen on a low light and read the west face like a page — the fresh ones low and hot, the older ones faded to a warm smudge, and all of them drifting slowly upward at about two metres a decade as the rind grows out from beneath.

Which means the oldest names in a town are up near the ceiling, four hundred metres of them, faint as breath, and that the ceiling inspectors work all day among the founders, and that the children who are not supposed to be on the high bridges are playing among them too, and everybody knows this and nobody says it.

There is exactly one absolute law. You do not render a body. Not in famine, not in collapse, not for anything. It is the only law with no stated penalty, because it is not expected to be enforced so much as obeyed by everyone alive.

It has been broken once. Four men, eleven days under a fall at the old Blind End, one of them already dead. They came out. They were exiled west in the year of the Pressing, which is also the only time in living memory that the Dew House was opened for an apology, and the apology was made to them, by the Chamber, for a rule that had left them the choice it left them. They were apologised to and they were exiled, both, and there is no record that anyone found this contradictory. The water evaporated on the stone in front of all four of them and they watched it go. That is the whole ceremony. That is all it has ever been used for besides names.

## East, and the ones who stayed

A stack eats its catchment, and a catchment is finite, and so the whole Chamber is a slow wave moving west at nine centimetres a month with its towns riding the crest.

Behind it, in the east lobe, stand the spent stacks. They are cold. They are enormous. They are lit at maybe a hundredth of a working flank, a dim ochre seethe like the inside of a fist, and they are full to the ceiling with graves that will never rise any further.

People live there. Not many — two hundred, perhaps, across six dark towns — and they are called the last-lighters, and they hold the Archive, which is where the founding documents are, the ones that describe the surface and rain and the sea and are generally regarded in the west as either scripture or a hoax and in either case as somebody else's business.

They carry lamps. Sealed filament, wire-caged, cold; nothing with a flame has burned in this Chamber in two hundred years and nothing ever will, given what the air is and what the sugar is. But they carry them, and they hold them up when they meet you, and that is the thing. A westerner seeing a lamp-carrier come up the tramway out of the dark end experiences something not far from horror, and cannot always say why, and it is this: everything in the Chamber gives off light as an admission of waste, freely, constantly, without meaning to. Only a person would make some on purpose.

## Twice a day

Stand on the floor plain on the exhale, with the sugar starting its hour-long fall from four hundred metres up, and the rake-lines running away under it dead straight to the base of Eleven.

You will hear the millers, and the mains, and the beam engine in the Fume Works turning over every four seconds like someone shifting in their sleep, and a stack whistling somewhere a long way off in a key that is slowly, slowly going flat.

And then, maybe twice in a day, a human voice — low, unhurried, two kilometres off, perfectly clear — saying something domestic to somebody else. That is the sound of the only wet thing in the world, speaking quietly on purpose, because it knows exactly how far it carries.
