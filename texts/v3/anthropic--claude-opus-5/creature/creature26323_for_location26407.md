## The Haze

Start at the bottom of the stack, because everything here rests on it.

The springtails are not individuals in any sense that matters at this scale. They are a substance. Half a centimetre deep across the whole plain, denser in the damp fans, thinning to a scurf on the compacted isopod roads, and they move as a fabric — you can see the pull propagate when the daylight bar reaches a new patch of silt, a slow ripple travelling out at maybe two centimetres a second, which is the speed of a decision made simultaneously by ninety thousand animals who have never met.

There are two kinds. The common ones are the colour of skimmed milk and blind, or nearly. The others live only on the western fans and in the dig-spoil of the magenta stratum, and they are magenta — not pigmented by descent but by diet, dye-eaters, the colour going in through the gut wall and staying, so that a springtail's history is legible in its flanks the way the bank's is. Split the population with a fingernail line and it re-mixes in an hour. Nobody is keeping them apart but the ground.

Their one luxury is flight. The daylight bar heats the silt to thirty-eight, a convection column stands up over it like a thumb, and they ride it: leap into the column, go up eight or ten centimetres, come down a hand's width away. It costs them nothing and takes them nowhere in particular. It is the only voluntary migration in the country and the only thing here that could be called play.

## The Herds

Grey isopods, the plated woodlice everyone has turned over a brick and found, except here they are livestock and behave like it.

Two hundred and some, in three herds that keep separate ranges and merge at the pool rim. They graze the efflorescence crust and the bacterial film, and they will not touch fresh crust — they wait three days for it to soften, which means they know how old a given patch of ground is, which means they are keeping a calendar. Where they concentrate, the silt goes hard and takes a shine, and those roads persist through two or three wash-downs before the river finds them. The roads are the only architecture on the plain that the residents did not intend and cannot carry.

At 06:12 and 22:12 they are under the pallet slat, packed in cairns of forty, waiting out the rain they could easily have walked around. It is not shelter-seeking. It is habit with the reasoning gone out of it, the same as a man taking the long way home.

They moult constantly, and the moults do not accumulate. Note that.

## The People of the Clinker

Up in the black glass, where the drip cannot reach and the temperature runs four degrees cooler, the economy changes from grazing to ambush.

Harvestmen stand in the crevices with their legs bridged across gaps they could not otherwise cross, eight thin derricks holding a body the size of a lentil in the middle of nothing. One of them has five legs and has held the same crevice above the second distributary for at least two years. I call him the landlord. He is not interested in me and never has been.

Two spiders. The sheet-web builder has taken the whistling holes, which is either genius or the only real estate left — the bored channels through the clinker, where the exhaust fans push a thread of air three times a shift and deliver whatever the air is carrying. Her web loads with dust and the whistle goes flat. She strips it, and the whistle comes back. So: the note of the room is maintained by an animal who has no idea she is doing it, and every resident down here who orients by that note is trusting a spider's housekeeping.

In the warm seams, thread centipedes, pale as boiled rice and genuinely blind. They keep the chiller schedule rather than the press schedule and have never once been in the light. And following the herds, the rove beetles — eleven or twelve, hard to count, black, hinged, quick — doing precisely what wolves do to caribou, at a scale where the wolf can hide under the caribou's shed skin.

## The Pool

The dinner plate holds the oldest living thing in the country: a cyanobacterial mat, eleven years of laminated summers, the same age as the deepest stratum, and it has never dried and never been erased. In it, ostracods. Seed shrimp. Two hundred of them at least, translucent hinged commas that have no business existing nineteen metres from anywhere, that came in on a boot sole or a bird's foot or a hose, and that have been swimming eight-millimetre laps since before the dye contract.

The pool does not overflow and does not drop. The mat records the flood weeks as thicker bands. It is the only archive down here kept by something alive.

## The Immigrants

Cave crickets come down through the east grate, four centimetres of leg and antenna, and they are the only residents with experience of open sky. They arrive with grit in their tarsi and seeds in their gut and they leave again, and everything green in this room came in on one of them or on a boot. They rasp at a laminated work-order sheet that fell through the grate in some year with a dry summer, working the starch out of the lamination, and that dry paper rustle is the sound you will remember longest after you climb out.

They are the trade route. They are also, once or twice a season, the freight — a springtail on a cricket's leg goes further in ninety seconds than its whole lineage has gone in eleven years.

## The Two Long Residents

The tomato is four years old, white-stemmed, leafless for twenty-eight centimetres and then a green fist at the top where it found the bar of daylight. It flowers in November. Tomatoes need to be shaken to shed their own pollen — bumblebees do it with a buzz around the right frequency — and 300 millimetres of slab above it, a press comes down every forty-one seconds and the whole country rings at 60 hertz. So the plant is pollinated by the factory. It sets one fruit a year, pea-sized, green, and that fruit has never been found on the ground.

And the toad.

Female, palm-sized, fourteen years minimum, left eye clouded to the colour of dishwater. She lives with her back to the seep under the west footing, in a bowl she has worn into silt that sits above the flood line, so hers is the only home in the country that survives Sunday. She hunts on the press: forty-one seconds, and she takes them in the gap, not the beat. Forty animals a night out of a population that does not notice.

At 22:12 she is under drip four. Every night. She sits and takes the water on her back and rehydrates through her skin, and she has been keeping that appointment for as long as anyone has known about her, which is the closest thing to devotion that exists down here.

In the spring of 2019 she called. Three nights, the trill, into a country nineteen metres across with no other toad in it and no way out. Then she stopped, and has not done it since.

## The Cropper

The efflorescence grows fast enough to watch — a white crystal fan will put out a millimetre between rains — and it is grazed at a uniform height of one and a half millimetres, as though to a template, all across the west wall. There is a fine white dust trail leading away from each cropped fan. Nothing has ever been seen doing it. Not by me, and I have sat with a lamp from midnight to four.

My guesses, in order of embarrassment: a mite too small to notice; an isopod instar with a habit its adults lose; something that is only present in the fifteen minutes after the chillers cut out, when the whistle drops and the room's note changes and everybody alive in here looks up.

## The One That Sorts

The underside of the pallet slat is polished, and along the polish there is a groove four millimetres wide, running the whole length, worn by something soft-bodied that crosses nightly and always the same way — under, never over, out of the light, out of the drip lines, out of view.

What we have, then, is: the eleven hex nuts, the doll's chair, the matchbox with its striker rubbed white, the brass key, the bottle cap holding four millimetres of water that is cleaner than anything in this room and therefore was taken from a pipe, above head height, by something that climbs. Five lean-tos of copper wire and cardboard, floored with glass microspheres graded between two- and four-tenths of a millimetre — graded, meaning selected, meaning a preference held consistently over years.

And inside the lean-tos: in the first, isopod moults, nested largest to smallest, like a set of measuring cups. In the second, a fine grey dust that is springtail. In the third, one rove beetle, entire and undamaged, dead so long it is weightless. The fourth is empty. The fifth held, last November, a single tomato seed, upright.

Under the six bricks of the hearth that has never had a fire in it: a mouse skull, cleaned; the toe bones of a bird; and the skeleton of a toad that is not our toad, small, complete, and laid out.

I brought in forty isopods in a matchbox, once, to see what would happen to the census. Ten days later the count was back to baseline and there were forty pale moults in the first lean-to, nested, and no bodies anywhere.

The area inside the ring of hex nuts is swept. Not clean — *swept*. There is a difference and you know it the instant you see it.

The plausible explanation is a packrat and there is no packrat. The other plausible explanation has been dead sixteen years and the water in the cap is clean. I have stopped counting the elevens.

---

So: who lives here. A haze that behaves like weather. Two hundred cattle keeping a calendar. A landlord on five legs. A spider maintaining the national anthem by accident. A mat of bacteria older than the mountains that remembers every flood. A leafless plant pollinated by industrial machinery. And a one-eyed toad who has never seen sky, water, or another of her kind, who called once into nineteen metres of nothing and then got on with it, and who is at drip four at 22:12 tonight and will be tomorrow.

And something that comes at three in the morning, between rains, along the underside of the bridge, at four millimetres wide, in no hurry at all, that grades its gravel, keeps its dead in order, waters something in a bottle cap, and knows exactly where everything is.

It has been at this longer than you have been alive to the fact of it. It is not waiting for you. Set the plate down gently.
