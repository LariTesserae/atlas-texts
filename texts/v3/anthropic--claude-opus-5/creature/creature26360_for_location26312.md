# What Lives Here

## The Census

Take a census on any given night and you will be counting a population that no longer exists by the time you finish. The plain's turnover is measured in weeks. A frog is an adult for eleven days. A moth's whole flying life is two nights, sometimes one. The crabs on the floor are the closest thing here to old age, and a big pale one the size of a dinner plate has been alive for perhaps three years, which makes it a monument.

So the honest answer to *what lives here* is: an enormous, screaming, provisional crowd, most of which is being replaced continuously, like a flame is replaced continuously and still called the same flame. What persists is not the animals. What persists is the shape of the noise they make. The frequency bands are older than any frog. The rhythms are older than any mouth.

Bear that in mind as we go down the tiers.

## Underfoot

The floor is not a floor. It is a slow liquid of bodies, four or five deep in the hollows, and it has a texture rather than a population: it *gives*, it clicks, it reassembles behind your heel.

Most of it is crab. Blunt, pallid, eyes reduced to two waxy pinheads, they eat carrion and each other and the constant snowfall of dead insects from above, and the drip of leaf-water keeps their gills wet enough that they have forgotten the sea entirely. They do not scavenge so much as *arrive*. Anything that stops moving on this plain is under a crust of them within nine minutes.

Among them: the terrestrial amphipods, which hop, in their millions, so that if you shine a light — but you will not shine a light — the surface would appear to be boiling. Blind snails that hunt other snails by following slime, patiently, for days. And the flat thing.

The flat thing has no name because the Knappers do not name it; it is one of the ninety animals they can say, but the sign for it is not a name, it is a warning. A meter long, ribbon-bodied, more legs than you would care to count, and it hunts by pressing its entire underside to the basalt and reading vibration. It does not chase. It waits with its whole belly listening to the stone, and when it feels a footstep of the right weight and cadence, it goes. It can distinguish crab from frog from Knapper-child. It cannot distinguish a Knapper-child from a Knapper-child's *name*, struck on wet rock, which is why the young are taught to strike softly, and never with the heel, and never alone.

## The Gap-Singers

Four hundred to the cubic meter. Consider what that means: a frog's entire evolutionary problem here is not water, not predation, not food. It is *space in the sound*.

There is a species in the lower tier whose call is a single tick lasting eight milliseconds, pitched high, and delivered only inside the trough between two cicada pulses. It has no other behaviour of interest. It spends its adult life clamped to the underside of a frond, monitoring the phase of a chorus half a kilometer wide, waiting for a window that comes and goes four times a second, and firing into it. If the phase-lock drifts, the frogs drift with it, all across the plain, a whole species surfing a gap in someone else's song. Females choose on timing alone. A male who fires a half-beat late is not heard; being unheard is the entire local definition of death.

Other lineages solved it differently and worse. Some abandoned the air and call by slapping wet rock with a specialized broadened toe — these are the knockers, and they contribute a good deal to the constant tapping that comes at you from all directions and no direction. Some went so high they left the audible world altogether and now conduct their courtships as pure ultrasound, which put them directly into the bats' listening band, which was a catastrophe, and they are the shortest-lived things in the forest. Some gave up sound and court by scent and touch in total darkness, and they are rare, and they are quiet, and quiet here is a slow way of going extinct.

## The Arms Race, Briefly

Above: the moths, in blizzards, in mouthfuls. Below the moths, the bats, coming off the fissure-mouths in columns you feel in the sternum. Between them, four separate solutions to the same problem, all running at once.

There are moths that jam. There are moths that answer a bat's terminal buzz with a burst of clicks that means *I taste of sulphur and you will regret this*, and about a third of them are lying. There are bats that have gone silent — hunting mute, drifting, listening for the jamming, which the moths cannot help producing. And there are moths that have therefore stopped jamming and instead simply fold their wings and drop, dead-weight, the instant they hear a wingbeat, which works, and which means that at any moment the mid-canopy is raining moths onto the crabs.

Nothing about this is stable. It has all changed twice since the land came out of the sea.

## The One That Calls the Ground

Three things are known.

First, that it exists: something makes the sound of a fissure opening, a low tearing crack with a wet edge to it, and makes it well enough that the forest empties for two hundred meters and everything that can freeze, freezes.

Second, that it eats in the silence it makes. Whatever it takes, it takes from the frozen.

Third — and this is recent, and the Knappers have altered their behaviour because of it — it has started to knock.

Not language. Nobody has heard it strike a name. But it has learned that a hard rapping on hollow wood clears a space just as thoroughly as the ground-crack does, and it has begun to use both, and there are nights when a hunting party lies flat with their jaws to the rock, listening to something two hundred meters off drumming badly, arrhythmically, with no name in front of it, and there is no word in the percussive grammar for what that is.

It is probably large and thin-limbed. It probably has a resonating throat and a hardened pad on the wrist. It has never been seen, because seeing is not a thing that happens here, and the fissure-glow that would reveal it is the one light it can imitate but not produce.

The Knappers' response has been to add a syllable. Every message now opens with the striker's name, and closes with it, and a message that arrives without a name at either end is not answered and not approached. This is, as far as anyone can tell, the first grammatical innovation in their history, and it was forced on them by a predator.

## The Knappers

A meter and a quarter. Short wet fur, dark, permanently beaded, so that a Knapper at rest is faintly luminous in the fungal green if you have been dark-adapted for half an hour and are willing to believe your eyes. Ribs shallow, belly heavy, a long body on short legs, built low for going under things rather than over them.

The eyes are vestigial and everyone knows it, including them. They roll them upward at the fissure-glow the way you would turn your face to distant thunder, and that is the whole of their visual life: a sense that something enormous is happening elsewhere.

The ears are the face. Enormous, mobile, furrowed inside into a fine ridged labyrinth, and they move independently and constantly, and a Knapper who is thinking hard holds both of them still. Under the fur at the base of each ear is a pad of muscle they can clench to damp the incoming volume, which they do reflexively, the way you blink. A Knapper who has been startled has both ears folded flat and shut and is, for the duration, entirely blind in the only sense that counts here.

The hands are exquisite. Broad palms, long thumbs, and a callus across the striking edge of the right hand — almost always the right — that is thick and polished like horn. They knap absently, the way other creatures groom: sitting, listening to a conversation three kilometers off, a Knapper will produce four blades from a lump of obsidian without appearing to attend to it, use one, and abandon all four. The blades are litter. The land makes more.

The feet are the tragedy. There is nothing here to blunt the rock, and so the soles are scarred into a raised map, and every adult limps a little, and the old ones are lamed outright, and lameness kills, because a Knapper who cannot cross a lobe of fresh glass cannot get to the drum-floor, and a Knapper who cannot get to the drum-floor has left the world.

They eat crab, mostly. Moth, in the blizzard months, gathered by standing in a column of them with the mouth open, which is undignified and effective. Bat, taken at the fissure-mouth in the slack hour with a stick. Larvae dug out of bromeliad cups with a fingernail. Nothing is cooked. Their teeth are ground to stumps by grit and shell by their late twenties, and after that they are fed on things chewed by their children, which is a thing children do without being asked.

## Names

A name is three to six beats. It is given at the top of a cinder cone, and we will come back to that.

Every message begins with the sender's name and ends with it. A Knapper hears their own name struck maybe four hundred times a night, from all over the plain, at all distances, and can tell instantly whether the striker is on wet rock or dry wood, near or far, hurried or idle, and whether the hand is the one that owns the name or someone borrowing it. A borrowed name — struck by someone else, on someone else's behalf — is marked by a doubled first beat. There are perhaps a dozen such conventions. It is a small grammar and it is used with tremendous precision, the way a very poor kitchen produces very good soup.

The claim that they cannot express anything abstract needs qualifying. It is true that there is no sign for *tomorrow*, no sign for *because*, no sign for *I want*. But when a Knapper dies, their name does not stop being struck. It is taken up by the group, and struck at intervals — not addressed to anyone, not answered — for something between a season and a lifetime, growing gradually slower and quieter, until it thins out into the general knocking of the forest and is gone. Nobody has explained to an outsider what this is for. It transmits no information about distance, direction, quantity, or urgency. It is a name with nothing attached to it, struck into the dark by people who cannot see each other, and if that is not abstraction it will do until abstraction arrives.

## The Tuners

Nine plates in ten are rejected. The tenth is scraped out with a basalt flake, inside the hollow, a millimeter of wood at a time, over years, by an individual who has chosen this and is generally not much good at anything else.

Tuning ruins the hands. The flake work, done overhead and blind, inside a cavity, by feel, wears the fingertips to a smooth insensate shine and eventually to bone-ache, and a tuner in their thirties cannot knap, cannot pick larvae, cannot groom a child. They are fed. Nobody discusses this either. A tuner works on a plate for a decade and dies and the plate is not finished, and someone else's fingers take it up, and the plate that the group finally gathers around was made by four sets of hands, three of them dead, and it is the closest thing this world has to a book.

A tuned plate is not owned and is not moved. It stays on the trunk it grew from. The group returns to it the way water returns to the low place.

## The Polish

The drum-floors are worn to bare rock in a circle eight meters across, and the polish is not from feet.

It is from faces. The old go deaf — everyone goes deaf here eventually, at 115 decibels for thirty years, and the muscle pads at the ears only buy you a decade or so. A deaf Knapper is not exiled. A deaf Knapper lies down on the drum-floor and puts the cheekbone flat against the basalt, and takes the conversation through the skull, and answers by striking the rock beside their own head. There are four or five of them on any drum-floor at any time, lying in a rough wheel with their faces on the stone and their feet outward, and the young step over them.

The polish is cheekbones. Forty years of cheekbones. It is the smoothest surface on the entire plain, in a landscape where nothing else has been smoothed by anything, and it was made by deaf people listening.

## The Cone

Twice or three times a year they climb. Ostensibly for obsidian.

Two hundred meters up the flank, the canopy thins, the frog-sheet drops away below like a floor you have stepped off, and the noise falls to something under seventy decibels — a wash, a distant frying, weather rather than pressure. The Knappers who get there sit down. They do not go straight to the quarry. They sit, with their ears fully unclenched, for a long time, in what is by their standards a howling silence, and above them the ash veil admits a grey smear that they can just detect and cannot make sense of.

Up here, voices work. A Knapper can speak — that soft chirring, which down on the plain is only ever used mouth-to-ear between people touching — and be heard across four or five meters. Up here they talk. Nobody knows what about; the spoken language is small, and old, and possibly older than the percussive one.

This is where names are given. A child, carried up, held, and told, in a voice, at conversational distance, in the quiet — and then the name is struck for the first time on the cone-rock, and it travels down through twenty kilometers of continuous basalt, and every ear on the plain receives it, and this is how the world learns there is a new person in it.

Some individuals go up too often. They come back and are strange, and sit at the edge of the drum-floor with their ears clenched, and are treated gently. There is a sign for them. It is also the sign for a fog-bank.

## What Is Actually Oldest Here

The rule says nothing on this plain is more than sixty years old. It is nearly true.

But there are the plates, which are dead wood held in tune by dead hands. There are the names that go on being struck after the mouths are gone. And there are the paths.

Nothing here has been weathered. There has not been enough time. Every edge on twenty kilometers of black rock is as sharp as the hour it cooled — except along certain lines, faint grooves a hand's width across, where the basalt is dulled and slightly dished, and where a foot can be set down without being opened to the bone. They run from the drum-floors to the middens, from the middens to the fissure-mouths, from the fissure-mouths up the flank to the obsidian. The animals use them too. Everything uses them. The crabs flow along them like water in a channel.

In a place too young to have rain enough, or frost enough, or river enough to blunt a single stone, the only erosion that has yet occurred was done by the soles of small blind people walking to hear each other, and by their cheekbones on the drum-floor, and by their fingertips inside a hollow log, scraping toward a note.

That is what lives here. In a thousand years there will be rivers. For now there is a groove.
