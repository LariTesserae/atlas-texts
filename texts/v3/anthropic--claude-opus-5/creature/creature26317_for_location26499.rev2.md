## The Span

Start with the fact that they have no floor. Everything else about them is an answer to that.

They live inside the flues, not beside them. A dwelling is a length of fissure of the correct width, occupied from wall to wall by bodies braced in compression, and the occupied band runs from about sixty metres down — below the reach of the fog, below the flue-figs' roots — to about two hundred and forty, where the heat begins to cook and the dendrite zone starts and the air is too thick with whisker-shrimp to speak through. Above that band is the lip and the weather. Below it is metal and the dark and eventually the boiler. There is no ground anywhere in the inhabited world. There is no surface to set a thing down on, and no thing to set down.

They call themselves by the word for the distance between two walls. It also means *one person*, and *a home*, and *the length of a life*. I will render it **span** and ask you to hear all four at once, because they do.

---

## Body

An adult is not large. The trunk is a flattened cylinder a little over a metre long, dull grey-black, dry, faintly waxed, with no hair and no shine. Off it, four limbs in two opposed pairs, each extending to two and a half metres, so that a span at full stretch bridges five metres of open shaft and weighs about as much as a heavy dog. They are built like a sprung strut. Very little of them is meat.

The limbs end not in claws — nothing claws the silica enamel, it is glass — but in broad grey pads of a pliant cartilage that grips by area and by pressure. The grip is not a grip. It is a push. A span stays in the air by shoving the two walls of the world apart, continuously, awake and asleep, from the day it first reaches to the hour it stops. Three points of contact is survivable. Two is a fall.

The head is nearly nothing: a knot at the front of the trunk carrying a fan of bristles for vibration, two pits of vestigial eye under the skin, and the mouth, which is mostly **strainer** — a pleated membrane that unfurls into a shallow bowl about a hand across. They feed by holding the bowl open in the updraft. That is the entire feeding apparatus of the species. They have no hands. They have four feet and a face like a sieve.

You will have noticed they are the same animal as the long-arms. Not descended from — *convergent with*, or perhaps cousins so old that the question has no content. Same problem, same solution, arrived at twice. The spans know this perfectly well and it is the central horror of their folklore.

---

## Width

Below 1.8 metres a flue is too tight to brace in; the limbs cannot straighten, and a span that cannot straighten cannot hold and cannot strain. Above about five metres, no one holds alone. Above eleven, no one holds at all — you can chain two and three spans trunk to trunk, hand-limb to hold-limb, but a chain has a maximum and that is it. Between 1.8 and 11 metres there are people. Outside it there is nobody, forever, and the flue belongs to the spinners and the quiet ones.

So the habitable world is a narrow band of *width*, cut out of a continent of stone, and it moves.

Steam widens every flue at roughly a hand's breadth per century. Metal grows inward off the walls at roughly a hand's breadth per century. For long stretches these cancel and the effective span of a shaft holds steady for a thousand years, and the people in it believe, reasonably, that the world is stable. Then one of the deep flues clears and vents — the tearing-canvas sound, four minutes of it — and the shock sheds a dendrite grove entire, and for an hour the shaft rings with falling metal like a sackful of bells going downstairs, and when it stops the flue is three-quarters of a metre wider than it was at breakfast.

That is history. That is the only history they have. A shedding takes a settled community and makes a third of it unholdable, and the segment moves — up, down, or out — and the move is remembered by the count of the dead.

Every flue is habitable for perhaps five to ten thousand years and then is too wide and is abandoned and never used again. They know this. They know their particular fissure has an end. There is no myth about it. It is simply the fact that the walls are going away, at a rate you could measure against your own grandmother if anyone here had anything to measure with.

## Feeding, which is the dangerous part

At the surge — every forty-one hours, the flues breathing hard — the whisker-shrimp come up in a sheet. A span turns its face down into the column, opens the strainer, and holds absolutely still for forty to eighty minutes while the food arrives by itself.

Absolutely still, warm, and vibrating with the effort of the hold, in the dark, in a shaft containing long-arms.

So feeding is arranged. A segment eats in thirds: one third strains, one third watches with limbs spread across the shaft as a tripwire, one third sleeps. The rotation is the most rigid thing in the culture and the only thing resembling a law. Betrayals of it are the subject of every story that is not about metal.

They also eat spinners when spinners can be had, and they take the copper-plated shed shells and do nothing with them whatever, and a very old span with a slow hold will sometimes drift down into the dendrite zone in the last weeks, where the food is thick and the heat is killing, and eat enormously, and not come back up.

## Names

The hold is never perfectly still. A braced span transmits a tremor into the enamel: a few hertz, personal, a product of limb length and mass and heart and age. It goes into the glaze and travels. In a straight glazed fissure it carries kilometres.

That tremor is the name. It is not a symbol of the name. Everyone within earshot of the stone — which is everyone — is continuously broadcasting their identity, involuntarily, awake and asleep, for as long as they are alive, and nobody can misstate it, and nobody can stop.

Speech is percussion laid over the top: the pads drumming the wall, a rapid dense code, the tremor underneath carrying the speaker's name the way a voice carries a face. Lying is possible about content. It is not possible about origin. There is no anonymous statement in their world and no concept that would cover one.

The name changes across a life. It thins in illness. It goes ragged in fear. It drops in pitch over decades, and the young can hear their parents getting old the way you would watch a colour fade, and there is no word for this because it is not an event, it is the weather.

To be silent, you let go. Free-fall makes no tremor. A span in the air is, for those two or three seconds, absent from the world — unnameable, unlocatable, gone — and then catches. They do this for grief, and to conceal a decision, and for love, and the act has no translation. Not letting go once is privacy. Not catching is death, and it is the ordinary way of it.

---

## Having

There is nothing built on the Tableland because a span has four feet and no hands, and to hold anything at all it must let go of one wall.

This is the whole of it. Their verb for *to have* is the verb for *to be short a limb*. A thing possessed is a thing subtracting from your hold. And because there is no floor, a thing set down is not set down, it is dropped, and it falls two kilometres into water hot enough to take it apart. You cannot put something aside for later. There is no aside.

So: no tools, no cairns, no shelters, no walls, no vessels, no clothes, no weapons, no marks. Not from asceticism. From arithmetic.

What they have instead is arrangement. The order of bodies in a shaft, who holds above whom, the spacing, the rotation, who is permitted to chain to whom across a wide section — this is their architecture, and it is rebuilt continuously by everybody in it and stored nowhere. It is very elaborate. A stranger dropping into an established segment can read forty years of local politics off the spacing in the first ten minutes, by tremor, in the dark, and will read it wrong.

## Carriers

There is one exception and it is the only art.

Twice a night per kilometre, a dendrite branch shears off a wall and falls, chiming. Perhaps one in four hundred is caught. A span with an exceptionally strong three-point hold will take it out of the air — this is reflex, it is not learned, every infant lunges at falling metal and most of them lunge themselves off the wall — and having caught it, may keep it.

Not for wealth. There is no wealth. Because metal resonates. A copper fern held against the trunk takes up the carrier's tremor and re-radiates it, and a carrier's name is audible three or four times as far as anyone else's. They are heard in flues they have never been in. They are the long-distance instrument of a species with no other one, and they carry news, and they are asked to be present at difficult things simply so that the neighbourhood can hear about it, and they pay for this by holding the world with three limbs for the rest of their lives, which is a shortened life, and everyone knows the trade.

When a carrier dies they let go of the branch first. It falls, ringing, and everyone below for a kilometre hears a chime and knows what it was — and then, twice a night, per kilometre, forever, chemistry makes the same sound for no reason at all. They have stopped mourning the false ones. They have not stopped hearing them.

## Crossing

Between flue-lines there are eighty kilometres of open tableland, and a span's limbs are compression struts, not legs. On the flat they move like a folded chair, badly, at three kilometres an hour, at the very edge of dying of cold — because the crust is warm but the thin air above it is not, and a body built to shed heat into a rising column loses everything it has in nine hours on the surface.

The only crossings are where two kiln-hog road systems overlap. A hog ranges twelve kilometres out and twelve back and no further, and where two warm mouths lie less than twenty-four apart their beaten trails touch, and there — perhaps once in three hundred kilometres of flue — the world is connected. Nobody surveyed this. Nobody could.

A crosser walks in the lee of a hog. Not a tamed one; there is no taming a blind armoured thing that never learns you exist. You simply stay against its flank, matching its pace, taking its heat, for as long as it is going your way, and when it turns for home you decide. Most turn back with it. The ones who do not have to reach the outbound edge of a stranger's hog-range before their feet stop working.

Crossers are the reason there are shared words along a thousand kilometres of fissure. They are also, everywhere they arrive, slightly wrong — their tremor is unfamiliar, their spacing is foreign, and they have been alone and silent for days, and there is an old belief, entirely false and completely unkillable, that a span who goes long enough without a name loses it, and stops broadcasting, and stays in the flues afterward holding the walls like anyone else, and eats spinners, and eats you.

That is what a long-arm is, in the stories. A span that went quiet.

## The lip

At the very top, in the fog band, among the flue-figs, a few thousand live differently.

Lip-dwellers hold shallow, in the widest and coolest part of the shaft where the fog condenses, and they don't strain much — they eat the filaments and the tubular things and the small animals of the wet zone. They are lighter. Their tremor is weak and nobody far away has ever heard them. In exchange, the eye-pits open. A lip-dweller can actually see: the maroon bloom on the ceiling, the shape of the fig-line, a warm body at forty paces as an absence in the grey.

They are the ones who know that the light is alive. They are the ones who know about the vent-columns and the rain and the two-hundred-million-year machine, because they are the only ones who look at it, and the deep segments regard them as unserious — people who hold badly and talk about the roof.

They have a floor, of a sort: the rim rock, the basin ledges, the flat treads of the fig-groves. They can, uniquely, put something down and find it again.

They never have. Nothing at the lip has ever been stacked, shaped, or kept. Partly because the lip is where the dying go and nobody stays; partly because a lip-dweller's whole vocation is watching, and a watcher who is holding something is not watching. Mostly, I think, because they are still spans, and for a span the idea of a kept object is not forbidden or unthinkable — it is simply not a thought that arises, in the same way that you have never once wanted to store a smell.

They are the reason you have an account of this place at all. A lip-dweller is who tells you the Tableland is a machine that was never assembled, and that the ceiling's glow is an animal's opinion about the weather, and that the chime is the most beautiful sound in the world. That is a view from the top of a shaft, by someone with eyes, who has never been down past two hundred metres.

Ask one of the deep ones and you would get no nouns at all. You would get a pressure, and a duration, and the name of everybody present, and then the surge, and then nothing for a long time, because they were eating.
