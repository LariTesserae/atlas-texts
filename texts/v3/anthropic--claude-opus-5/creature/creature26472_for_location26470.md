# The Quiet Ones (who do not call themselves that)

They have no name for themselves. Traders from below call them the White-Handed, or the mutes, which is wrong, or the Sill-folk, which is only an address. Their own language has a word that covers everybody who is not here, and it means, approximately, *the loud*.

## What the water makes of a person

Start with the bodies, because the water gets into them the way it gets into everything.

They are short and deep through the chest, with the long spleen and thick blood of people bred at altitude, and they move at a curious pace — not slow, but continuous, without the little bursts and stops that other people put into walking. Nothing on the Sill is done in a burst. A woman crossing four terraces with a basket on her hip looks like she is being carried by a current.

Their hands are the strangest thing about them, and the first thing a valley trader notices. They are wrinkled — permanently, from birth to death, the soft corrugation of skin too long in warm water. Pale, fine-lined, with nails that carry a chalky bloom no scrubbing takes off. Sill hands look boiled and look old at fifteen. They are also, by common agreement of everyone who has seen them work, the most beautiful hands in the range: fast, articulate, oiled every night with rendered bird fat and wrapped in cloth against the frost. A Sill mother inspects her child's fingers the way a horse-breeding people inspects legs. Hands are the organ of speech here, and an injury to them is an injury to the voice. A man who loses the last two fingers of his right hand does not go mute; he acquires an accent, thick and effortful, and people lean in to read him, and children imitate him behind his back until they are slapped.

Their teeth go early and go white-crusted. Their eyes go milky between forty and fifty-five, sooner if they are poor in shade. Their skin around the orbits is blackened with willow charcoal, renewed every morning, so that the face of an adult of the Sill is a pale brown mask with two smudged pits in it and, over the pits, the horn visor with its single slot. The visor is pushed up on the forehead indoors and at dusk. Uncovering the eyes is what these people do instead of removing a hat.

And inside them, quietly, all their lives, the water is doing what it does everywhere else. It lays itself down. Every adult on the Sill carries stones in the kidney and the bladder. The first one usually passes in the late teens; it is agony, and it is expected, and it is an occasion. The passed stone is washed and kept and, if it is a good one, drilled and strung. You can guess a person's age within five years by the length of the string at their throat, and old men with long strings are teased for vanity, and are vain. Fifty per cent of them, in the end, die of it — of a stone too large, a blockage, the slow poisoning that follows. They know this perfectly well. They drink the water anyway, because there is no other water, and because they have long since stopped experiencing it as a bargain. The Sill deposits itself into its people at the same rate it deposits itself into everything else. Being a person here is being a surface that the process is working on. You are a house too. You will also close.

## The hands, and the six hundred

The sign language is not a substitute for speech. It is the daylight language, complete, ancient, and rude.

It is signed high, at the collarbone and above, when the matter is formal — a channel dispute, a death, an announcement of intent to build — because signs made against the bright sky are legible from a hundred paces. It is signed low, in the shadow of the belly, for jokes, for gossip, for the enormous vocabulary of small filth that the young use constantly and the old pretend not to read. There is a register signed with one hand held flat against the thigh, visible only to the person beside you, which is how insults, propositions, and true opinions are transmitted. Two people signing across a terrace are making a public statement whether they like it or not; two people signing at the hip are alone in a crowd.

There is a sign that cannot be used outdoors. It means *food is being set down*. The black-and-white gulls learned it four or five generations ago — nobody can date it — and now a hundred of them will drop out of the air onto anyone who makes it in the open. So the word has gone indoors, like a night word. Children discover this by accident, disastrously, at about the age of four, and it is one of the town's reliable comedies.

They sign at conversational speed and they sign while carrying things, which means the vocabulary has full one-handed forms for everything urgent and elaborate two-handed forms for everything leisurely. A person with a basket on her shoulder speaks a clipped, telegraphic dialect. To put your load down before answering someone is a courtesy roughly equivalent to standing when they enter a room.

## The night voice

The mouth-language is nocturnal.

It exists — it is old and rich and has poetry in it — but it is only ever murmured, indoors, in the dark, at a distance of less than a forearm, usually with the speaker's lips near the listener's ear or against their hair. The whole spoken tongue is therefore built out of soft consonants and breath. It has no shout in it. There are no words in it that can be said loudly without deforming.

The effect on the culture is total. Speech is intimate by definition. It belongs to the same category as touch, warmth, and lying down. Children hear adults talk only at night in a domed room lit by one algae lamp, and consequently the voice is, for the first years of life, a thing that lives in the dark and belongs to your family; a stranger's voice heard by daylight is genuinely shocking, close to obscene. Lovers do not need private words. Every word is already private.

They sing, but only after the morning ice is broken, and only in a narrow band. Over generations their music has evolved to occupy the frequencies the water does not use — the gaps in the fizzing hush, the dead intervals where a rim's dry click would never be heard. Sill song is thin, low, and full of holes. It is written in negative. A musician from below finds it maddening: the melody keeps stepping around something, avoiding a note it clearly wants, and the reason is that the missing note is the sound of a wall about to fail.

## The shaded

Blindness is a promotion.

When a person's eyes go — and they go, they nearly all go — the community does not lose a worker. It gains an instrument. The shaded are moved to the acoustic seats: eleven hollows in the terraces, known and inherited, where the geometry of the basins gathers the sum of the fan into something a trained ear hears as a chord. A shaded woman sitting in the third seat can name each of the forty vents by voice, can hear one of them drop a half-tone over a week, and can locate a beetle-tick in a rim to within twenty paces. She sits there most of the day with her head tilted and her dog against her knee, and every couple of hours a runner comes and puts a wrist under her fingers and takes down what she has to say.

They are fed first. They are given the best of everything. They are also, being human, opinionated, quarrelsome, and cliquish; the eleven seats have politics; the second seat has not spoken to the fifth in nine years, and everyone finds this hilarious and exhausting.

Their names accumulate. A person is named for the vent nearest their birth-pool, and when that vent dies or wanders the name lapses and a new one is taken from wherever they are living then. So the old carry four, five, six names, and are addressed by whichever one the speaker first knew them under, and the very oldest shaded woman in the town is a walking gazetteer of dead plumbing. To recite her full name in order is to recite sixty years of the Sill's hydrology. She is asked to do it at feasts. She pretends to be reluctant.

## The scald-map

Everyone is burned. Not badly, mostly. The pools go from *keeping* to *bread* to *egg* over the space of a stride, and a slip in the wrong basin takes the skin off a shin.

Scalds heal pale, and pale on brown skin is legible, and so an adult's arms and legs and back carry a diagram of their carelessnesses. People read each other. *That splash on the forearm is a cook-pool, a child's burn, you were reaching.* *That whole calf is a rim failure — which one? Ah, the year the upper east went, you'd have been eleven.* Bodies are documents in a town that has nothing to write on. The old jokes are about which limb tells lies.

The one burn nobody jokes about is the flat, wide, glazed scar of someone who went down in a sky-mirror: who stepped onto the reflected cirque and found it was two feet of near-boiling water, and could not, in the whiteout of noon, see the edge to get out.

## Going in

There is work that only children can do, and the whole of childhood is organised around it.

When a house has narrowed past an adult's shoulders but is not yet fully sealed, and the family has already moved above, there is a period — a year, two years — when the room is still reachable by someone small. Children go in. They retrieve what was left: the good horn tools, a grandmother's shuttle, a bead-string forgotten in a niche. They go in with a lamp and come out white to the elbows and are given something sweet.

They also, obviously, go in when they are not supposed to. The half-sealed rooms of the Sill are a country belonging exclusively to the under-elevens: warm, glowing faintly at the crown where the deposit is thin, humming with the flow, absolutely silent, absolutely private, the only place on the Sill where a person is unobserved. They tell each other things in there, out loud, in the mouth-language, in the daytime, which is doubly forbidden and therefore the entire point. Every adult on the Sill remembers the exact room where they first heard a human voice in daylight.
