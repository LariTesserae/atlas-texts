**The Two Hundred**

Start with the hand, because the hand is the oldest instrument here and the rest of the body is mostly an arrangement for getting the hand onto a pipe.

The palm is broad and slightly cupped, and the heel of it — the mound below the thumb — carries a pad of thickened skin the colour and close grain of the floor outside. It is not a callus in the ordinary sense; it does not soften with rest. Matching it, at each temple, is a patch where the skin lies unusually thin and flat over bone, hairless, faintly polished. Put the pad on a flue trunk and the temple on the back of the hand, and the column speaks through you: the pressure as a fullness behind the eyes, the flow as a grain, a wrongness in a valve seat as a burr that seems to be located somewhere in your own jaw. Children discover the trick on their own at about four. By fourteen the skin has answered. Whether that answering is inheritance or use, nobody has settled, and the question is considered a little indecent, like asking a mason whether stone is heavy by nature or by habit.

They are tall and narrow, the attendants, long in the forearm and shin, with the light build of animals that walk far on very little. Skin grey-brown, close-grained, faintly waxy — the same four words the rock gets, and this is not a coincidence so much as a convergence: two things that have been polished by the same long abrasion. Hair fine and ash-coloured where it grows at all. The eyes are large and pale and stay wide, and they are not useless — an attendant two hours dark-adapted can walk a slag-lit corridor without touching the wall, and can read a casting's temperature by colour to within twenty degrees — but they are a secondary sense, used the way you use peripheral vision, and they weep constantly and quietly in the presence of a lamp.

The pulse is slow, near forty, and remarkably steady, and it is a piece of equipment. A trained attendant times a valve stroke by counting their own heart against the note. Each person's rate is entered in the ledger as a personal constant, verified at each ninth-day weighing, and when it begins to drift — as it does, past a hundred and twenty or so — they are taken off the standard, which is the local phrase for retirement, and the timing work goes to someone younger and the older one goes to the tallower rounds and the long walls. They live to about a hundred and sixty. Everyone eventually acquires the grey, a slow stiffening of the lung from flue leakage, and the grey has a curve too, and the curve is known, and people speak of their own remaining years the way they speak of the district's: precisely, without drama, as a number worth getting right.

---

They are the same temperature as everything else. This is the fact that organizes their inner life, and outsiders never get to the bottom of it.

Touch between them carries no warmth. A hand on a shoulder transmits texture, pressure, pulse, and nothing else — there is no comfort in it of the kind you mean when you say *comfort*, no borrowed heat, no animal reassurance of a warmer body near a cooler one. They cannot warm each other. They have never been able to. So the affections have gone elsewhere, and where they have gone is into pace.

To love someone here is to walk their circuit at their speed, for years, until the two of you take a rail junction in the same number of strides and neither has to wait at a valve chamber for the other to arrive. Households are formed along the lines: two or three or four adults whose rounds interlock, whose shifts hand off cleanly, who sleep in the same dead-air room between doors and rise in an order that has not changed in decades. When one of a household begins to slow — the grey, the drift — the others slow with them, and the roster is adjusted, and this adjustment is the most public statement of feeling that the culture permits. Everyone reads it. Everyone knows what it costs, to the gram.

They speak in three registers. The hand: fingers on a forearm, a compressed grammar of pressures, used at contact range for nearly everything. The rail: the counting notches on the handrails are also a telegraph, and a message travels the district in the time it takes to strike it, arriving thin and clean over kilometers of dead-straight steel. And the mouth, which is for children, for the swearing-in, and for emergencies. In still air on a level plain a voice carries absurdly far. To speak aloud in the open is very close to raising an alarm. Visitors talk constantly and cannot be made to understand why every attendant within three kilometers stops walking to listen.

Their language has a full apparatus for rates, and for rates of rates, and a tense — properly a mood — for processes too slow to be observed within a life but known to be underway. Most of their important sentences are in it. It is the mood of the ledger. It is also, awkwardly for translators, the mood in which they say *I love you* and *the rock is cooling*, which are grammatically the same kind of statement and are not felt to be very different kinds of statement.

---

They eat what the tallowers eat, only later and with more steps in between. There is no plant on the Flat and there never was. In the sour galleries under the reduction houses, condensate is run over trays for months at a stretch and something precipitates: a pale mineral cake, faintly sweet, faintly metallic, holding its water bound up in itself so that chewing releases it slowly. A ration is weighed out and a ration is weighed back in, and what the body does not use is returned to the charge chutes, and the loop closes to three decimal places. Nothing is thrown away because there is no away. An attendant who loses a token, a stem, a mouthful, reports the loss, and the loss is written down, and that is the end of it, and it will still be legible in four hundred years.

They are counted as carefully as everything else. Two hundred is the figure the plant needs; two hundred is the figure there is. A birth is authorized against a projected death, and the authorization is a folded slip entered in the ledger, and the wait for one can be twenty years, and mothers keep the slip for life and are buried — burned, rather, charged — with it in hand. Occasionally a child arrives without a slip. The child is not blamed and is not treated badly; the child is a discrepancy, and discrepancies are handled the way all discrepancies are handled here, by being measured. The district must run very slightly warm for that life's duration to feed it, and the amount by which it runs warm is calculable, and it appears in the great curve as a bump with a name on it. Such people are called the surplus. Any of them can go to the Assay House and be shown, with courtesy and without reproach, exactly what they cost the world's remaining warmth. A disproportionate number of them become Reckoners. They have known since childhood that the curve is not an abstraction, because there is a place on it where they live.

Children are the one loud thing in the Sixth Draught, and they are indulged in it, on the theory that the note is better learned by first being drowned out. They crawl the map before they can read it: junction tokens under the palms, counting notches at head height, the smell-difference between a north trunk and a flue return. At eleven, the Showing. A furnace door is opened fully, once, for three seconds, and the child looks straight into it with no shield at all, and screams, and is blind for two days afterward, and remembers it perfectly for a hundred and fifty years. That is what you are keeping in, they are told, and nothing further is said, because nothing further needs to be.

At fifteen they are sworn to a round and given its name. Names here are posts. A person is Achel of the North Trunk, and is the ninth to be so called, and the eight previous Achels are in the ledger with their notes and their corrections and their personal constants, and when this one dies a fifteen-year-old will be the tenth.
