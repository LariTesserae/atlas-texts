They are people. It needs saying first, because at any distance greater than ten metres they are not obviously people — a figure coming up the lane is a dark vertical smear with its hair standing straight off its skull like smoke off a snuffed wick, moving at that heavy, patient, sleepwalking pace, touching the wall every few steps, silent. You mistake them for something else exactly once.

Four thousand of them, in the crescent town and the fruit terraces and the two outlying steadings up the shoulder. They came from somewhere; they have a story about it, tapped rather than told, in which the first ones came *down*, and it is not clear whether the story means down a mountain or down through the Shine, and the difference has been argued about for a long time by people who have no way to settle it and a great deal of time to braid while they argue.

---

**The body.**

Barrel-chested, all of them, even the thin ones, even the children — a chest like a held breath, which is what it is. Long torsos, short strong legs, feet splayed and thickened to a horn on the sole from a lifetime of walking polished basalt. No one here is fat. No one here is cold, either, so nobody needs to be.

Their skin is pale, and paler on the parts that face up, because the tea-coloured light that reaches them is still light and still does something over sixty years. They are marked: vent-pigment rubbed into scar lines, ochres and a rare true blue, on the shoulders and the outsides of the arms where they can be read by someone standing beside you rather than in front of you. Most marks are inventories. This many terraces, this many children, this many descents into the scald.

The heart is slow. A resting pulse here would alarm a physician from a dry country; they have simply moved the whole metabolism down a gear and left it there. Spleens are enormous, and there is a word for the small hot squeeze under the ribs when it empties into the blood at the end of a long breath-hold, and children boast about feeling it before they actually do.

Hair is the vanity. It floats, so it is worn long and dressed with basalt beads at the tips to keep it lying along the back, and the beadwork is the only fashion the town has. Men's beards drift up over the face, which is intolerable, so beards are either scraped off with a glass flake or plaited down and weighted in the same style as the hair. An unbeaded man is either in mourning or in disgrace, and you can tell which by whether he has also given up his belt.

---

**The eyes.**

Here is the thing nobody warns you about: they cannot see far, and they never could.

Water is unkind to the human eye. Up close, their sight is superb — a knapper can read the grain of a glass rind at arm's length, and children are drilled young at squinting the pupil down to a pinhole until the trick becomes involuntary — but past a certain distance the world simply thickens and goes. The far side of the basin is a green idea. A person across the terrace is a posture, not a face. They know each other by gait, by beadwork, by the rhythm of the walk, long before they know each other by looking.

So the town is a near-world, and everything that must happen at distance happens by sound. This is why the tapstones are not a convenience but an organ.

For close work there are eye-cups: bone rings the width of a thumb, faced with a scraped membrane of lung-fruit skin, holding a little pocket of air against the socket. They leak. They fog. They must be blown out and recharged every few hundred breaths, which is a gesture you see constantly in the masons' quarter — the quick tip of the head, the silver blurt, the resettling. Through them the world snaps to a hard clean edge and everyone goes briefly ugly, every pore and pock of them, and there is an old joke about marrying without eye-cups.

Inside an air-bell you see clearly for free. That is why the bells are where the fine work is done: the pigment-grinding, the carving, the cord-inspection, the arguing over maps. It is also why a person who spends too long in a bell comes out half blind for an hour and has to be led, and why the bell-keepers' families are known, unkindly, as the fumblers.

---

**The breath.**

Every person over the age of four wears a lung-fruit on a short tether at the left hip, and the tether is the length of a forearm so that the fruit rides at the mouth without being held. You take it the way people elsewhere take a step. Three or four pulls a minute, more if you are working, less if you are old and resting in the warm.

This makes their speech — all of it, tapped or signed — arrive in packets. Say a thing, breathe, say the next thing. Every sentence has a gap in the middle of it. Visitors find the rhythm maddening; the town finds continuous talk indecent, a kind of leaking.

The fruit goes slack around midday. There is a general softening of the lanes at that hour, everyone drifting back to their own vine, a communal shift of attention like a tide in a place that has no tides.

To offer your fruit to someone is the single most loaded gesture in the culture. It is how you say: *I will be short of breath so that you are not.* It is how marriages are made — not with vows but with one bladder hung between two people for a day, in front of witnesses, until it goes limp and they hand it back to the vine together. The word for household and the word for one bladder are the same tap.

There is a taste to a fruit near the end of its charge, flat and sweetish, and a person who has been through a bad year will say they have been tasting the bottom of the fruit for months, and everyone will understand.

---

**The voice.**

They laugh out loud. It is the one sound they make on purpose with their mouths, and it is not a sound: it is a burst of silver going up, a fistful of bubbles that clamber over each other toward the Shine. You see a joke land three lanes away as a little rising smear. The sign for *funny* is two hands opening upward, fingers fluttering — the shape of your own laughter leaving you.

Otherwise: knuckles on stone, a hard dry click made far back in the throat, and the hands. The hand-language is fast and low, held against the chest, and it uses the face enormously — the brows, the mouth-corners, a particular narrowing that means *you are exaggerating*. Faces here are more legible than faces anywhere dry. Nobody has ever needed to learn to lie without moving their eyebrows.

And for distance, the tapstones, which everyone can read and only about sixty people can properly play. The tapstone players are mostly old, mostly women, mostly half-deafened to everything else by the ticking, and they are paid in fruit and deference. A good one can say a paragraph in eleven strikes. The best of them can strike a name so that the person it belongs to feels it in the soles of the feet before they have consciously heard it.

Everyone has such a name: a rhythm, given at the first air, struck once at the last.

---

**The belt.**

Flat stones, drilled and strung on plaited cord, worn low on the hips. It is the only garment that matters. Everything else — the netted pouches, the harness, the mat-cloak for cold work — is tackle.

You are fitted by a Weigher. There are four in the town and they are quiet, unsentimental, enormously powerful people, because a Weigher knows what you actually weigh, which here means what you actually are: how much fat you have lost this season, how much muscle, whether you are pregnant and since when, whether you have been eating your terrace's fruit instead of tending it. Weighers visit pregnant women every eight days and add a stone or take one, and are the first outside the family to know. Weighers also weight the dead. It is the same job. They would look at you blankly if you suggested it was two.

A child's first belt is one small stone, and the ceremony is simply that the child stops bobbing. An adolescent's belt is over-weighted on purpose for a year or two, to teach the ankles. An old person's belt goes heavier, not lighter, as the flesh thins.

To have your belt cut by another is murder. To cut your own is the recognised way out, and it is not forbidden, and people who do it are spoken of gently, and children are taught the sign for it early so that they will not use it carelessly. Exile — rare, perhaps twice in living memory — is not expulsion. It is the confiscation of the belt. The town does not have to do anything after that.

---

**The trades.**

The **fruit-tenders** are the aristocracy, and are boring about it. Terraces are inherited, subdivided, fought over in tapped litigation that can run for years. A tender's hands are stained olive to the second knuckle and smell — taste, rather; smell means almost nothing here, but taste is a distance sense, and a tender is tasteable at several paces — of cut bellvine, bitter and green.

The **masons** are the artists, and know it. No mortar will set, so everything is fitted, and a mason's reputation is a wall that has stood through three ground-swells. They wear eye-cups constantly and have the permanent ring-bruise around the sockets to prove it. They are also, by ancient arrangement, the ones who open a house when its family goes up.

The **knappers** work the glass rind and bleed constantly and are fatalistic about it. Every knapper has a hand like a map of a river delta.

The **bell-keepers** live half in air. Two families to a hearth-bell, in rotation, forever. They are pale even by local standards, they cough, they see clearly, they are the only people in the basin who know what smoke smells like, and they are the only people who are ever genuinely dry, for a few hours at a time, up in the top of the bell with the flies.

And the **counters**. The ones who go down into the scald for shrimp, on a count of forty, wearing nothing but a belt and a smear of grease. They come up boiled pink, shedding skin in sheets for days, and they are done by thirty-five, and they are given the best of everything and the front of every gathering, and nobody's parents want their child to be one. Their mark is a band of blue across the collarbones. They taste the vent field on the way down — the bitterness thickening pane by pane — and they navigate by it, the way a dry-country traveller navigates by landmarks.

---

**Birth and death.**

The only two times a person is in air.

They are born in the bells. All of them, always: the mother goes up through the hatch into the family's pocket, or into the quarter's if the family is poor, and the child is born into a cupped room of condensation and lamplight and its own first noise. The noise matters. It is the only occasion on which a human voice here works properly, and the whole household crowds the hatch to hear it, and an old woman strikes the child's name on the floor-stone twelve times, and after that the child will never again make a sound anyone can understand.

At the other end: the great unsettled question, and the town's one real division.

The **Cairn** families weight their dead and build over them, out on the low shelf where the pillows are oldest, so that the grandmothers stay in the warm with everyone else, under stones anyone can come and sit beside. The **Cut** families take the body up to the last lane before the cold pane, and a Weigher opens the belt, and the whole family watches it go — turning slowly, not gracefully, hair streaming down instead of up — until it thins out into the green and is gone, toward the drift-beach and the Shine and whatever is or is not on the other side.

Both are practised on the same shelf, sometimes on the same day. Nobody has ever proposed making everyone do it one way. The proposal would be regarded as obscene, and also as pointless, because the question will be answered eventually, for everyone, by the mountain.

---

**The others.**

The **fish** is called the grandmother-fish and is not eaten. There is no doctrine about this, only a strong and universal disinclination, and a practice in some families of feeding one particular blunt pale individual at one particular pillow for decades, and of knowing it, and of not mentioning to visitors that you know it.

The **eel-children** live in the frozen bubbles of broken pillows, blind and finger-thin, and are a toy. Small children coax them out with scraps and try to get them to lie along the arm. They will not be held. There is a whole genre of lullaby-taps about this.

The **flies** are luck. No one knows how they got into the bells. They are not swatted, they are not encouraged, they die and are replaced by more flies, and the bell-keepers speak of them with the offhand affection of people describing a difficult relative.

And up on the abandoned shelf, where the cold pane has come down and the houses stand open and emptied with their roofs unstopped, nothing lives at all. Adolescents dare each other up there — the cold hits like a hand, the lung-fruit at the hip has no local cousin to refill from, and the light is clean and pale and far too much. They stand in somebody's great-great-grandmother's doorway for as long as they can stand it, which is not long, and come back down into the warm shaking and delighted and unable to explain.

They will not be able to explain for another four hundred years, when the shrine breaches and the town finds out whether the songs about birth were right, or the other ones.

Meanwhile a tapstone goes twice and scrapes. Everyone in the lane stops. Hair stands up off four thousand scalps like smoke; four thousand pairs of hands pause on four thousand lengths of cord; the glass ticks quietly from every direction at once, the way it has since the rock was made.

Then they breathe, and go back to their braiding.
