## first, the answers that are true but not the answer

Ask anyone in the villages under the pier who lives down there and you will be told: frogs. This is correct in the way that saying *hair* is correct when you are asked who lives in a house. The drum-frogs are numerous, they are loud, they are the part of the resident that a visitor can count. But no frog down there is more than three years old, and the pockets they inhabit have been continuously occupied since before the tally column reached the water. The pocket is older than any tenant of it. That is the first hint that you are looking at an organ and calling it a creature.

The eels are the next answer, and the more knowledgeable people give it, because the eels are large and undeniably individual and there is a sense in which they run the place. But they are not the resident either. They are its long memory, and I will come back to them.

The room itself is the third answer, offered by the sort of person who likes to say that a mountain is alive. It is nearer. But the room is only the shell, the pot, the instrument-body. Something lives *in* it, and it is not distributed through the whole volume, and it is not the volume.

## the ring

The resident is a band four hands wide.

It is a torus of living tissue, twenty-odd paces across, lying against the inner skin of the dome at the level where the water meets the wall, and it rises and falls a cubit twice a day because that is its way of feeding. Everything in the source-description that seems to be a list of separate species — the felt, the frogs, the midges, the shrimp, the crickets, the snails whose shells are in the walls — is a description of one animal's parts, made by someone standing inside it and looking outward at the pieces.

This is not a metaphor I am reaching for. Consider what the parts do. The algal felt grows only in the band. It is drowned at high breath and grazed by shrimp; it is exposed at low breath and grazed by crickets and the frogs' prey. The midges hatch off the felt in sheets and are eaten by the frogs, who live in the courses immediately above the band, and by the swiftlets, whose droppings fall back onto the felt and are the animal's entire food supply from the outside world, because the three moss circles where the light lands are decorative, not nutritional. The snails eat the felt and die into the flowstone, and the flowstone is the animal's skeleton, and it is laid down fastest exactly at the band and nowhere else in the room at that rate. Nothing crosses out of the band except the swiftlets, who go and come back, and the eels, who reach in.

Take away any part and the whole band dies within a season. Nobody has tried this. It has been tried for them: the drains have clogged before.

## how to see it

Stand on the ring-walk at low breath with your lamp shaded and let your eyes go. What you will notice first is that the walls above and below the band are, biologically speaking, bare stone — clean, weeping, wearing nothing but flowstone. There is no gradient. Life does not thin out toward the band's edges; it stops at them, on both sides, at a definite line you could rule with a straightened rope. That kind of edge is not made by conditions. Conditions blur. That edge is a hem.

The second thing you will notice, if you are patient the way the white birds are patient, is that the band moves as a unit and slightly ahead of the water. The felt swells a finger's width before the tide reaches it. The frogs shift pockets an hour before the drowning. The animal knows the breath from the inside; it is not responding to the water, it is riding a rhythm it holds in itself and the water agrees with. Which is only to say: it breathes, and the pool breathes with it, and nobody has ever established which of the two is the cause.

## the voice

The note is not the dome's. The dome only makes the note *possible* — a hollow that size, weeping at that rate, would hum whatever lived in it. What lives in it has spent two thousand seasons selecting for the hum. Every frog in every pocket has ancestors chosen by how well the pocket answered; every pocket has been enlarged and glazed and tuned by the flowstone that the snails' shells feed. The resident has been carving its own larynx out of the wall it lives on, slowly, from the inside, using the dead bodies of its parts as the material.

The note is therefore what the animal is doing. It is not communication. There is nothing else of its kind to communicate with — or so we assume; see below. As far as anyone can determine, the note is simply the shape the animal makes in the air, the way a snail makes a shell. When the drawers put their foreheads together and hum, they are speaking the local dialect of a body they are standing inside, and they know it, which is why they do it with their eyes shut.

## the middle

The bubble of nothing in the centre is not empty and it is not deliberate in the sense of decided. It is the hole of the ring. You are standing in the aperture of a creature shaped like a mouth twenty paces wide, and what passes through the aperture is its exchange with the sky: mist up the middle, rain down the whole height, continually, the only traffic the middle ever carries. The reason nothing lives there is the reason nothing lives in a throat.

The falling rain you can see in the light-shafts is the animal breathing out and having its breath handed back to it by the cold of the dome. It has been rebreathing the same water for a very long time. The faint tea-colour is what accumulates in a body that never fully voids.

## the white ones

Five or six, always, standing in the shallows with geological patience: these are not birds, and they are the only part of the arrangement that unsettles me.

Nobody has seen one arrive because they do not arrive. They accrete. They are flowstone, growing bird-shaped, at the animal's hem, at the rate the room lays down everything else — grain by grain, over years, from the ankles up. Nobody has seen one leave because leaving, for them, consists of thickening past the point of resemblance, listing, and becoming one of the pale stumps on the shallow paving that the drawers step around without comment and have no word for. Count the stumps sometime. They are in a rough ring at the hem, and they are numerous, and the older ones are half-swallowed by the floor.

What I cannot tell you is why the count of standing ones holds at five or six. Accretion has no reason to be regular. Either the room has a habit that runs deeper than chemistry, or the animal is making them on purpose, and if it is making them on purpose then I do not know what for, and neither do the drawers, who will discuss the eels all night and go quiet about the white ones.

## the ones below the tideline

The eels are not part of the ring. They are its predators and its only individuals. There are, by the drawers' reckoning, between thirty and forty; none is small; no living person has seen a young one. They have no way in and no way out and they do not breed, and the arithmetic of that has one solution, which is that they are the same eels. They are what the room has instead of records.

The tally column supports this. It runs from head-height down to the water and continues below it for an unmeasured distance, which means only one thing: the water is higher than it was. The animal has been climbing the wall. Its old positions are in the flowstone rind, and where a block has spalled you can read them — dead bands, stacked, each one a fossil hem of the same continuous creature, each a few fingers below the last, the whole sequence like growth rings stood on end. The drawers have no idea they are looking at a life history. They see banding in the stone.

Somewhere down there, under eight feet of tea-coloured water, are the strokes of people who stood at a hem that is now drowned, in the same room, doing the same afternoon's work with a bone blade, and were as convinced as anyone of the number ninety-one.

## the arrangement with the drawers

The animal needs one thing it cannot do: it needs the four drain-mouths kept open, because the drains are what hold the water inside the cubit of its breath. Flowstone seals them. Flowstone is its own skeleton. Left alone it entombs itself, the pool rises past the hem, and the band drowns and has to be rebuilt higher — which it has done, by the count in the rind, twenty-nine times, each time losing most of itself and most of whatever a body like that has in place of experience.

So twice a season, four or five people come down ninety-one steps carrying a coracle in pieces, and spend an afternoon scraping the throat of the drains, and are given water and eels for it, and go up. They cut nothing. They build nothing. They have been performing the single act of husbandry that a creature the size of a hamlet requires, for longer than they have had a word for the creature, in the belief that they are maintaining a cistern.

I would leave it there, except for the rootless weed.

It drifts. It is not a plant and it is not quite an animal, and it is the only thing in the room that is not fixed to a surface, and it goes, patiently, again and again, tumbling, to the drain-mouths — the four holes that the drawers have just been paid to open — and waits at them with the current, and is turned back, and comes again.

Something in there is trying to get out. Something in there thinks there is another room.
