## The People of the Chord

### First Sight

You will see them a long time before you meet them.

On a floor seventy miles wide and flat as poured milk, a walking person is a spike of black against the glare, doubled at the ankles by their own reflection, visible for the better part of an hour. So there is no such thing as coming upon someone. There is only the long approach, and the long approach has rules.

At first sight, you stop and turn square to them. At perhaps half a mile, you sing your name — two notes, held, the first taken from the nearest throat so they can hear that your reference is true. They sing back. Then you walk toward each other, slowly, for forty minutes, in full view, with nothing to do and nothing to look at but the other person getting larger, and this is considered not awkward but the pleasant part. People rehearse the approach. People remember particular approaches for fifty years. At a hundred yards you begin the shouting, which is formal, vowel-heavy, pitched carefully into the gaps between the local notes so it doesn't get eaten. And then, finally, you close the last step, put your mouth against the stranger's ear, and say something small and true.

That is the whole shape of their society, if you want it in one gesture: a very long visible approach, and then a secret at the end of it.

### Bodies

They are small and spare and wet. Nobody up here is ever dry below the knee; the feet are broad, bleached, cracked at the heel and horned at the sole, and children are taught to walk with a low shuffling skim that does not splash, because splashing wastes your own quiet and there is little enough of that. The legs are corded. The rest of the body is thin in a way that is not quite starvation but is always, always adjacent to it.

The eyes are deep-set under heavy lids, and every face from weaning onward wears a smear of dried film-paste under the eyes and across the cheekbones, green-black, reapplied each morning, cracking by afternoon. It cuts the glare off the mirror. It is also the only pigment in the country, and so it is also the cosmetic, the mourning-mark, the signature and the insult, depending on where you put it.

The hair is long on everyone, because hair is the only crop. It is grown, oiled with flatling fat, and cut at intervals that are occasions. It bleaches pale at the ends from the sky and stays dark at the root, so a person's head is a rough calendar of their own years, and a cut is a harvest and a small grief.

The teeth are the thing that tells you how old they are. The film they scrape carries dissolved stone in it, as the water does, and so every mouthful is a mouthful of very fine grit, and the teeth of a forty-year-old are worn flat as thumbnails and the teeth of a sixty-year-old are gone to brown stubs at the gum. The old are fed on paste soaked soft in a gourd overnight. There is a phrase they use that translates, badly, as *the only weather is in the mouth* — and it is not a metaphor. On the Made Ground nothing erodes. Nothing except them, from the inside, slowly, with every meal.

And their ears. Everyone's hearing is spent, and the currency it is spent in is comfort. The warm dry galleries are high inside the throats, close to the column, and to sleep there is to sleep inside a drum. The people who live well go deaf early; you can tell a gallery-born grandmother because she will take your face in both hands and put your mouth in her ear like a cup. The people with the finest hearing at sixty are the ones who spent their lives out on the cold open mirror with nothing over them, and everybody knows what that cost, and nobody says so.

### Two Voices

They have one language and two registers, and they are not really the same language.

The shouted register is public, loud, and carries. It is made of long open vowels and hard glottal stops, and it is tuned — every speaker unconsciously slots their voice into the local gaps so as not to fight the throat. It is used for everything that can be overheard, which is everything that matters to the group: trade, argument, praise, the passing of the beat-count, the announcement of a death. It is a beautiful register and it cannot lie well. Too many people can hear it.

The eared register is what they save. Mouth to the rim of the ear, breath on the lobe, almost no voicing at all — a language of consonants, clicks, and lip-shapes, felt as much as heard. It is physically impossible to use it on more than one person at a time. Every genuine thing any of them has ever said has been said to exactly one listener, at a distance of two inches, in a voice that nobody three feet away could detect. They do not experience this as tragic. They experience an audience as a kind of indecency.

There is also the sign, for distance. Broad slow arm-work, readable at a mile, and always doubled in the water beneath the signer, so that every statement is made twice and the reflection is part of the grammar: a sign made high is public and a sign made at the waterline, where your hand meets the hand coming up to meet it, is a vow. To touch your own reflected fingers while signing is the most serious thing a body can do here. Marriages are made that way, across half a mile of mirror, with the two of them each touching a hand that isn't there.

And nobody hums.

You will be told, politely, at the rim, before you come up: don't. It is not superstition and it is not etiquette. A hum is a sustained pitch produced by a body, and the entire structure of their world is the proposition that sustained pitches do not come from bodies, they come from the plateau, and they do not change. A hum near a listening-station is treated roughly the way you would treat a man shouting *fire* in a crowded room, except that there is no fire in this country and never has been, so the comparison fails and the thing itself has to stand alone. Children who hum are corrected hard and fast, with a hand over the mouth, before they are old enough to be told why.

### Names

A person is named for the interval between the two throats audible from the place they were born. Two notes, sung, always in the same order, low then high. That is the whole name.

This means names are absolute and unforgeable — everyone here has perfect pitch, not as a gift but as a condition, the way you have a sense of which way is down — and it also means the stock of names is finite and small. There are only so many pairs of throats close enough to hear together. So names recur, constantly, and nobody minds, and a name is understood to be a slot rather than a self. To say *she was a low-third out of the eastern diamond* is to say something true about a woman and also about nine other women dead before her. They find our practice of unique personal names slightly grandiose and very sad.

### The Three Archives

Here is the central fact of their lives, and they will not tell you it, because to them it is not a fact but a temperature.

They live in rooms that refuse to remember them. Sleep in a gallery every night for thirty years and the gallery will be, on the last night, precisely as new as on the first: no soot, no stain, no dish-worn sill, no hollow in the floor shaped like your hip. Spill something and it is gone in a week. Cut your name into a threshold, bleeding your hands to do it, and in eleven days the pale pith-grain runs smooth and continuous through where your name was. The throats do not tolerate them. The throats do not *notice* them. This is a house that has never once admitted that anybody came home.

So they keep three archives, and only three.

**The skin.** Every wound is precious because it is legible. A scar here stays bright and stays forever; there is no soil, no rot, no spore, nothing to infect a cut, and the faintly sweet mineral water closes flesh cleanly. They therefore read one another's arms and shins the way we read a face. A burn is the mark of someone who has been below. A hook-ring of small white pits on the forearm means you have carried a body up.

**The mouth.** Teeth are the clock. You are as old as your molars are flat.

**The mat.** The mat is the only made thing anyone owns, and it is woven from the owner's own cut hair, and it is the only object on the entire plateau that ages. It yellows. It greases. It frays. It stains and holds a smell. A great mat, the kind hung across a gallery threshold by a family that has held that gallery for six generations, is two arm-spans of matted human hair in bands of different shades — this pale stripe is a drought summer when everyone's hair went brittle, this dense dark band is a year when four sisters were all cutting at once — and it is read aloud at deaths, band by band, by someone who can still remember which band is who. It is history, deed, genealogy, and obituary. It is also the only thing in seventy miles that can be destroyed. That is why they love it so much.

### The Holders

What they mostly do is listen, and the listening is not vague.

Two throats whose notes lie close together will, where the sound fields overlap, beat — a slow throb of loudness, a wah, wah, wah, from the interference. The rate of the beat is exquisitely sensitive: if one of the two notes went flat by an amount far too small for any ear to hear on its own, the beat would quicken, and a trained person would know within an hour.

So there are stations. Twenty-odd of them across the plateau, each an unremarkable spot on the open mirror where two fields cross, each held by one person at a time, sitting on a rolled mat, in the wet, in the glare, doing nothing. They count. The count is passed at handover, shouted across the last hundred yards: *eleven in a breath, eleven in a breath, eleven.* It has been eleven in a breath at that station since before anyone's mat had a first band.

The beat is also the clock. Distances are given in beats, cooking times, gestations, the proper length of a silence after bad news. The whole civilization is timed off the interference pattern of two buildings that nobody built.

Holding is the most honored work and the worst life. You are cold, you are alone, you are out of the warmth, you are going blind from the glare and you are keeping your hearing sharp specifically in order to spend it. Holders are fed by others. Holders are not expected to marry. And twice in the remembered history of the Made Ground a holder has come running in with the beat gone wrong, and both times it was a head-cold, and both times the person was fed and honored and thanked and never listened to again. *Soft ear.* There are perhaps a dozen soft ears alive at any moment. They are treated with enormous gentleness and everyone avoids them.

### The Carriers

About one child in twenty is born without it — without the automatic, absolute, effortless pitch that lets the rest of them know where they are with their eyes shut. Such a child cannot be a holder, cannot navigate, cannot be trusted alone on the mirror, cannot hear their own name properly.

Those children go down.

The carriers are the tone-deaf, and they are the only reason anybody up here eats. They walk the rim tracks down through the vapor into the drowned hot country below, where things burn and rot and grow, and they come back up with fiber, seed-cake, salt, dried fish, shell blades, and news. They are the only source of any of those, and news most of all, in a country where weather hangs instead of arriving and nothing whatsoever happens. A carrier coming up over the rim is mobbed by children — partly for the cake, mostly for the smell. They stink of smoke and loam and animal and decay, an entire vocabulary of odor that does not exist on the plateau, and for the first day back they are followed around and sniffed at like a fire.

They are indispensable and they are faintly unclean, and they know it, and they have their own way of being, and they do not, as a rule, tell the holders about the six days.

Because this is what happens to a carrier. You spend a season below, among the noise of weather and the many pitches of a living country, and your ear forgets. Then you come back up over the rim, and for six days — six, everyone agrees on six, it is one of the few things all carriers agree on — the plateau sounds *flat.* Sour. A hair under. The whole chord sagging. And then on the seventh day it doesn't anymore, and you cannot get the memory of it back no matter how hard you try, and you go and eat and sleep and say nothing.

### What the Flat-Sayers Say

And some of them do not say nothing.

The argument is simple enough to state and impossible to settle. Every ear on the plateau is calibrated to the plateau. If the whole chord went flat together, slowly, over centuries, at a rate of nothing at all per lifetime, there is no instrument, no memory, no holder, and no beat-count that would ever detect it — because the beat only measures the two notes against each other, and they would be falling together, in perfect step, forever.

The flat-sayers hold that it happened long ago and is still happening; that the Made Ground has been failing since before the first mat; that the carriers' six days are not a decalibration but the only moment of true hearing any of them ever get, and that what a carrier hears on the first night back is simply the world, and that the surrender on the seventh day is the ear agreeing to lie.

They are not persecuted. There is not enough society up here for persecution. They are simply not given stations, and they tend to live far out, and they tend to be the ones you meet in the open with no throat in sight, walking a long line for no stated reason. Everyone else finds them exhausting in a way that is hard to distinguish from fear.

### The Dead

There is no ground to bury in — the floor will not take a spade and would close over the hole anyway — and there is nothing that burns.

So the dead are carried up. It takes two or three people the better part of a day to bring a body up the inner flutes, in the dark and the roar, through the galleries, to the crown-basins where the water gathers before it falls. They unbind the hair and cut it and keep it. Then the body goes into the column, and it descends the whole hollow height of the throat, through the constrictions and over the baffles and around the spiral galleries, and what comes out at the base, days later, comes out as nothing at all: as milk in the water, as dissolved matter, as the faintly sweet solution that the throat drinks back up through the floor and lays down, layer on layer, as more floor.

The mass that falls is the mass that climbs. They know exactly what this means. They are walking on their grandmothers to a depth of four thousand feet, and further, past the cloud, and they do not find this morbid; they find it the single consoling arrangement in an otherwise indifferent country. The floor is the only thing here that keeps them. They look for faces in the pith-grain the way we look for them in clouds, and they find them, and they do not point them out.

The ascenders do the stripping, in the column, on the way down — the little glass things with the ring of hooks, the only creature on the plateau that appears to be having a good time. Nobody harms one. Children are told that the hooks are their great-aunts' fingers and that the reason the ascenders spend their entire lives climbing back up the inside of the throat, against the fall, in the loudest place in the world, is that they are bringing something back.

The hair becomes a band in a mat, and the mat goes to a daughter, and that is the entire funeral.

### The Thing That Walks at Night

They tell it too, and the story is better from the inside.

When a person is very old, or has been a soft ear too long, or has simply had enough of a world that will not keep a mark, they sometimes walk out. Not to die exactly; there is a word for it that means *going to hear the far chord* — walking out past the last near throat, out to where you can no longer pick out any single note, where all of it arrives at once from everywhere, the plateau-wide drone, which points nowhere and is therefore the only place on the Made Ground where a person can be nothing in particular. They go out at dusk when the vapor lifts. Some come back. Some are seen, at distance, in the doubled moonlight off the mirror, standing very still, very large against the flat, and then moving.

So there is your monster. It is an old woman two miles out, up to her ankles, facing away.

### What They Are Actually Waiting For

You will hear the official version everywhere, and it is sincere. They are listening for the note to go flat. They fear it. A crown clogging, a flute widening, the balance breaking, the first true event in the history of the plateau — it would mean the end of the water and therefore of everything, and they say so, and they mean it.

But sit with them long enough, in a gallery whose walls have already forgotten every night you spent there, and let one of them lean in and put her mouth to your ear and use the small voice, and you will get the other half of it. They live on a floor that cannot be marked, in rooms that cannot be kept, under a note that has not moved since before the first name. Nothing here ages but their teeth. Nothing here remembers but their hair. The Made Ground has declined every single thing that has ever happened on it, including all of them.

Some part of them wants it to start. Wants gullies, rot, talus, soil, a stain that stays, a landscape with a history, even at that price. They are ashamed of this in the way people are ashamed of wanting a war.

They have a name for the day. It is an interval — a unison and a hair, a note against itself gone flat, the one interval that cannot occur naturally anywhere on the plateau because no two throats stand that close in pitch. It is the only name in the language that is never given to a child. Everybody over the age of six knows it.

Nobody sings it.
