## A Census That Keeps Failing

Begin with the largest resident, because it is also the architecture, and because it is one.

The fig is a single individual. Not a stand, not a colony — one seed, one germination, one continuous body that has spent two or three centuries learning the inside of a bell. Above, on the caliche, it keeps up a convincing performance of being a poor scrubby tree: fifty centimeters of gnarl, small hard waxed leaves, bark cracked white by the sun. That is a mask, or a mouth, or at most a lung. Its real body hangs in the dark — the braided curtains, the flattened wall-sheets that have fused across each other like poured wax, the eleven pillars. Down here its leaves are the size of a face and thin enough to see the hand through. Down here it grows all year, without seasons, because the room has no seasons, at a rate of perhaps a millimeter and a half of pillar diameter per year, which is how you may arrive at the age and how you may also arrive at the humility.

It does not know that it is holding up the ceiling. It is simply thigmotropic and greedy and very old, and those three facts have become a load-bearing structure.

It fruits twice a year on a clock that has nothing to do with the plain. Each fruit is an inverted garden containing several hundred flowers that will never see anything, and each requires a wasp two millimeters long to come down the slot — and they do come down, riding the scent up-column and then falling into the dark with it, female wasps with wings and antennae that will both be stripped off at the entry hole, because the entry hole is narrower than she is and she goes in anyway. She lays. She dies inside. Her daughters hatch in that closed room within the closed room, mate with their blind wingless brothers who are born without the equipment to leave, load themselves with pollen, chew out, and fly up the heat column toward a green slot of sky.

They are the only creatures here who have never been in the room.

---

**The frogs** number in the high hundreds and come in three kinds, and all three have kept the throat and lost the use of it.

The big pale-throated ones sit in fern axils, one to an axil, in the little cistern of water each frond-fork holds. They are fat the way things get fat when food is constant and flight is rare. A male courts by pressing the ball of one hind foot against the frond and releasing it — a soft, deliberate beat that travels down the petiole into the root and out along the root into wherever the root goes. A receptive female answers on the same instrument. They will do this for two hours, a duet conducted entirely through plant tissue, with pauses, with something that looks from outside like patience and is probably just the round-trip time of a signal moving through wet fig-wood.

The second kind lives in the liverwort shingles on the wet wall, and is the size of a thumbnail, and completes an entire life — egg, larva, adult, death — inside thirty milliliters of ceiling-water held in a single overlapping leaf.

The third kind is nearly black and belongs to the vents. It lays in the warm films at thirty-eight degrees, where the eggs develop in eleven days instead of forty, and its tadpoles are what crop the blue-black moss so close and so even. That mown look on the warm stone is their work. They are grazing animals, briefly, before they stop being.

All three still have vocal slits. Males under real duress will sometimes inflate — the whole apparatus rises, the throat balloons, the body goes rigid with an effort a hundred thousand years out of date — and nothing comes out. The gesture has outlasted the sound.

Once in a great while something goes wrong and one of them produces a noise: a single flat croak, half-formed. It comes back off the dome four times, from four directions, and for one long moment every animal in the room stops moving. Geckos flatten. Snails pull. The moth-haze eight meters up shreds and re-forms. Nothing arrives, nothing happens, nothing has happened here in a very long time — and after a few seconds the ticking reasserts itself and the room resumes. The terror is older than every individual holding it.

---

**The geckos** are the population you cannot get a number for. Six or seven to a square meter in the good places, meaning the warm wall, the pillar bases, the underside of the larger root-sheets where the drip doesn't land. Their eyes have gone enormous and gold-flecked and lidless, with a pupil that in full dark opens to the whole iris. They have fringed lamellae on the toes that muffle the tick of the feet, which is not nothing; a gecko from the plain above would be, by the standards of this room, a man in boots.

Their whole politics is display. Tail-flagging — slow, wide, deliberate arcs, nothing like the snap a surface gecko makes — plus the open mouth and the violet tongue, which is the closest thing to shouting that exists here. Fights are rare and brief, because fighting is loud. Hierarchy is instead settled over the forty minutes of the sunwedge, which is property: the oldest and heaviest hold the center of it, the young hold the burning margin, and the rest watch from arm's length with their heads angled to catch the reflection off the wet stone. When it goes, they disperse without argument.

**The bats** are two hundred, in the highest pocket of the dome, and they do not sing. Somewhere back along their line the larynx stopped being used and then stopped being built. They fly on vision in a dimness that gives them almost nothing, and on memory, which gives them everything: the room is learned to the centimeter, the way a blind man learns a house. A pup rides its mother for three weeks and after that navigates by having flown here before. When a slab comes down off the dome and changes one curve of the flight-path, a few of them die that year, hitting the new shape at speed. The colony learns it within a season. Mothers find their own young among two hundred by scent and by a rubbing of the muzzle along the shoulder, patiently, checking.

They take figs and carry them to chewing perches, and the spat pellets of fiber drop into the pool and feed the blind shrimp, who are therefore, at three removes, eating the ceiling.

**The crickets** have wings with no file on them, nothing to rasp against anything. A male stands on a frond and shivers — a fine, sustained tremble, whole-body, nothing you would call a movement unless you were watching the frond edge against the light. The frond carries it. So does the next frond that touches it, and the root that carries both. Rivals within reach do the obvious thing: they tremble out of phase, and the signal goes to noise, and two males will stand a handspan apart jamming each other for most of a night while a female somewhere below waits for a clean line.

**The snails** are the size of plums and pour over the wet stone in files of thirty or forty, rasping the algal skin into wide pale grazing-scars. Put your ear to the wall and you will hear them, a sound like distant sanding; it is the only voice in the room and it is not one.

**The whip scorpion** — there are perhaps a dozen — walks with its first pair of legs held out and vibrating, reading the world as a blind man reads a corridor, and takes crickets by feel in total darkness.

**The ants** run three castes up the largest pillar in a column you can follow with your eye for a hundred meters of switchback. They harvest fallen fig, moth-wing, dead gecko, each other. After a flood year the highway is somewhere else entirely and takes about nine weeks to become inevitable again.

**The moths** live in the ceiling. They hatch below, climb the heat column once, and mostly never come down; the warm stratum under the dome holds the night's scent in suspension and they hang in it, hundreds at a time, reading. Several kinds have no mouthparts at all. They have three days and one job and they spend it drifting in a brown haze eight meters over your head, and when they die they fall, slowly, all night, like the fine ash of something.

**The rat snakes** — twenty, perhaps twenty-five, pale bluish and very long — are the enforcement. They are effectively deaf to air but exquisite to the substrate; they feel the frog drumming through the fern stem and come up the stem. Twice a night, the two-second thrash. The frogs three fronds away go on with their tattoos, because the alternative is to stop forever.

---

Then there are the ones who do not live here but are entered in the ledger anyway.

The civet-thing comes down the chimney at fig-fall, twice a year, and is otherwise never seen. It arrives at the volume of a page turning and the room still empties ahead of it like water ahead of a hull. It eats in the crown for two nights and goes back up. It belongs to the plain. It has the manners of a guest who has not understood that it is one.

And the people, who were, ecologically speaking, a large slow seasonal bat: they came down for water in the dry months, six cut steps, a cord in a polished groove, a lamp in a niche, a hand on the pillar going past. They took nothing the room noticed. They left four sherds and a hollow worn into fig bark at shoulder height, and nothing else at all — no mark, no image, no burial, not one scratched line on all that available stone. A people who made images everywhere else, presumably, and made none here.

They learned the rule in under a minute, the way everyone does, and then they kept it for however many hundreds of years. There is a fact buried in that habit about what they did and did not carry down with them — about infants, about the sick, about whoever could not be relied upon — and nobody wrote it down, and it does not seem kind, and it is not the room's fault.

---

Above the bat pocket, in a shelf of the dome you would need a rope to reach, there is a floor of compacted pellets a meter deep.

Take it apart and you get the room's only archive: forty thousand years of sorted bone, ninety percent of it frog and gecko and cricket-head, layered like silt. And down near the bottom, in the oldest and hardest of it, the bones of species that are no longer in this room, and among them, four times, the skull of a bird.

It is not a big bird. It has enormous ear openings, and they are set asymmetrically — one high, one low — which is the architecture an animal builds when it intends to locate prey in three dimensions in absolute darkness by sound alone.

That is the guess, and it is only a guess, and it explains everything. For some long span of time, this perfectly domed whispering gallery contained something that heard four copies of every noise and triangulated the original. Anything with a voice was found. Anything without one inherited the room.

It has been gone a long time. Nobody down there now has ever met it, or met anything that met it, or carries any image of it whatsoever. They carry only the rule. The silence is a law still being kept, in full, by nine hundred citizens, on behalf of a state that fell in the Pleistocene — the way a door gets closed in an empty house.

---

Last, and oldest, under the ledge at the north end where the pool stops being measurable:

the water goes back perhaps eleven meters into a flooded sump, and in that sump there are salamanders.

Unpigmented, the color of the inside of a wrist, sixty centimeters long, with feathered external gills they never lose and eyes that formed and then were grown over by skin before hatching. There may be nine of them. There may be thirty. They eat something on the order of six times a year — shrimp, a drowned frog, a fig pellet gone soft — and breed once a decade or so, and it is entirely possible that individuals down there are twice the age of the fig, which would make them the senior residents by a wide margin, and would mean that some of them were already adults when the last person came down the steps with a cord and a clay jar.

They have never been in the room. They have never been in light. They surface in the black under the ledge, take a breath of air that came down the slot that afternoon, and go back, and the fine broken ring they leave is one of the dozens constantly interrupting the iridescent skin of the pool.

For them, the room is not a place. It is a climate — a pressure, a temperature, a faint rain of food from somewhere above. The whole cathedral of ferns and figs and geckos and moths, the wedge of sun, the ticking dome, the worn place on the pillar where six hundred generations of hands went past: all of it is, to the oldest things living here, merely the weather.

And when the ceiling finally comes down and the floor finally comes up and the two of them meet and close the room into a black seam of soil in white rock — the sump will still be full. It is below. It will hold water long after there is anything to drink it.

They will go on breathing in the dark, occasionally, slowly, and not being found.
