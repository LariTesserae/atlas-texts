Everything in the survey is accurate. The trouble is that it counts the wrong volume.

Read it again and you will see the shape of the mistake sitting right there in its own best sentence: *the air between the far wall and your hand is the largest thing in the room.* Then twelve pages of inventory drawn from the basins and the ledges and the wet stone, which is to say from the floors — from perhaps a two-hundredth part of the Held, chosen because it is the part that holds still long enough to be written down.

The loud zones are not the empty part of this world. They are the inhabited part. Nothing in them ever lands, so nothing in them leaves a record except its death, and its death is the dust.

## Curd

In a null, the mist gives up and becomes water. In a loud zone it cannot; the pressure keeps shaking the drops apart faster than they can form, and the suspension goes on being a suspension for years at a time. What you have then is a permanent standing pantry: water, dissolved brine off the Weep, whatever organic ghost the intake shafts bring down, all of it held in the air by the same arrangement that holds the vaults.

Something eats it. Flakes, four or five to the width of a fingernail, translucent, faintly violet at the rim where they have been sitting in the seam-light. They are not plants and not quite anything else; they build on dissolved mineral and on light, and the light they build on is the light the survey called the most beautiful thing in the world and said no one was there to see. They see it. Seeing, here, means growing thicker on the side that faces a blue coal, so that the population two hundred paces up leans in fifty directions at once toward fifty pulsing basins, and the whole loud volume of the chamber is very slowly bent toward its own dim lamps like a field of wheat.

Curd, the shaft-workers would have called it, if there had been shaft-workers. Nothing else is going to name it.

It divides by fracture. It has no way to move and does not need one, because the six-hour breath moves it: the population in each shaft is carried forty paces up on the warm cycle and forty back down on the cold, and has been going up and down like that, the same water, the same flakes, since before the last stratum of galleries was cut. Tidal, in a place with no sea.

Its whole life is the avoidance of stillness. Sooner or later a flake's edge wanders into a node — not one of the great room-sized nulls, but the fine nodal skin that lies over every floor and sill and vault-back, the Chladni figure of the surface itself — and once it is in that grip it stops being shaken, and it takes on water, and it gets heavy, and it falls, and it becomes a grey ridge on a polished floor.

Which means the standing-wave map, the frost-on-glass figure that the survey admired as a diagram of pressure, is not a diagram of pressure. It is a cemetery, arranged by physics into the tidiest cemetery imaginable, and the four months it takes to redraw itself after a porter has grazed through is not a property of the stone. It is a rate of dying. You can weigh the population of the sky by watching how fast the floor gets dirty.

## Palms

Something eats the curd, too, and it is the reason to stop calling the loud zones empty.

They are flat. Roughly the size and roughly the thickness of a wet leaf, with a stiff spine on one axis and nothing you would call a front, edge fringed in a fine ciliary hem that is in constant motion and does no swimming. A palm does not fly and does not float. It holds attitude — it is shaped so that the standing pressure supports it, the way a hand held out of a moving carriage supports itself, and it works its hem to keep the angle, and by tilting a degree here and a degree there it goes where it wants to go over the course of an hour. It cannot rest. It cannot land, or rather it can land once. On the floor there is no wave to lean on and no way to get the angle back.

That is how anyone knows they exist. Nine or ten times a survey a party finds one in a null: opened out on the wet ceramic beside a basin, pale, gone soft, unmistakably a dead animal and not fitting anything on the list. The old notes have them as membrane, provenance unknown, possibly shed. The right reading is simpler. A palm that drifts into a quiet chair drops out of the world, because a quiet chair is exactly a place where there is nothing to hold it up.

Forty of them, perhaps, in the whole chamber. Perhaps sixty. They pass one another at a distance of many paces and do not appear to register it. A palm ranges a gallery for a decade or two, grazing a suspension so thin that its whole day's feeding would not wet a spoon, and it is nearly certain that a palm and a throat-mouse have never once been within reach of each other, because the mouse falls where the palm can live and the palm falls where the mouse can live, and between them they have divided the Held along the one seam that matters here.

## The two that walk

Reconsider the porters with all that in hand.

Waist-high, plated, rasping mouth underneath, no discernible front, walking the loud zones for ever at a pace you would need a whole afternoon to be sure of. They eat the dead sky. That is their diet entirely: the fallen curd swept into the nodal lines, plus whatever has come down out of the air with it — palms, throat-mice that misjudged a crossing, crickets, the shed sheath of the eel — all of it delivered by the wave to the same neat ridges, all of it eaten along the ridge as though along a printed line, which is why the wake is a clean erasure and not a scuffle.

And they are the only fast thing here. Everything else in the Held has a time constant measured in centuries; a system like that needs one component that clears the record, or the record armours over and the whole arrangement seizes. The ridges, left alone for two thousand years, would grow into rinds, and the rinds would stiffen the vault-backs, and the fit would drift. The porters keep the floors legible. Whether they are for that is not a question the Held is set up to answer, and the answer would change nothing.

There are two, and they are always at opposite sides of the chamber, and this is not shyness. Each of them keeps to the loudest available standing — the deepest ridges, the richest line — and in a chamber this shape there are two such places and they are antipodal. The porters have never met. There is nothing in either of them that would know what to do about it if they did.
