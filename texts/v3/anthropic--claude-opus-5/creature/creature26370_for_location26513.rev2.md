## First, who counts

The question is awkward here, and the tenders know it is awkward, which is why they have a sign for it — a flat hand turned slowly over, meaning roughly *the same thing again, but from the other side.*

By weight, by area, by any honest census, the Sill is inhabited by moss. Everything else is a guest of the moss, including the rock, which the moss is standing on without improving, and the machines, which the moss is quietly insulating, and the people, who spend a measurable part of their working lives removing it from places where it is unwanted and then sitting down on it in places where it is not. The moss is the majority and the landlord and the undertaker. Nothing on the Sill is ever thrown away; it is only set down, and then it is gone, in the way a thing is gone when it has been agreed upon by something patient.

The tenders have a phrase for this. A hammer left overnight has *started*. By autumn it has *gone to moss*. There is no verb for retrieving it, because nobody does.

## The tenders

Twenty-six on the establishment; nine to fourteen on the plateau at any time; one who does not rotate.

They come up from the pump-halls two thousand metres below, in the cage, through the cloud deck — four minutes of grey, and then the lid comes off the world and the light hits them from underneath. New hands always put a hand out to steady themselves against a wall that is not there. Everyone watches for this and nobody remarks on it. There is a sign for *you have just done the thing everyone does*, and it is considered poor manners to use it in the first week.

A hundred days up, forty down. They work in crews of four — a stringer, a gutterer, a levelman, and one who carries — and the crews are not friendships so much as gaits. You are crewed with people who walk at your speed, because on a plateau where you cannot call out, walking at different speeds is how people get lost from each other. Compatibility is measured in metres per hour and nobody has ever thought this strange.

## What the light does to them

You can identify a tender in the dry country below by the sunburn on the undersides of things. The jaw. The septum. The inner curve of the ear. The shelf under the eyebrows that on anyone else is the one permanently shaded place on a human face. They wear broad hats and then, under the hats, a second brim reversed — a stiffened chin-cloth called the *underhat* — and gauze across the throat, and smoked glass with shields on the sides and the bottom. They still burn. They go up pale and come down the colour of the tuff, which is to say not brown but a kind of scoured cream, with a fine permanent crease-work around the eyes from a hundred days of not quite opening them.

Their hands are the darkest part of them, because their hands are held out in front of the body and lit from above and below at once, and because their hands are how they speak. Forty years of talking will tan a palm. It is the one place on a tender where the weather looks like weather.

They do not have shadows to judge distance by. Everyone falls, once, in the first fortnight — a step down onto ground that turns out to be four metres further away, or a step forward into a scree blade that turns out to be already against the shin. After that the body recalibrates, and the new calibration is permanent, and it is why tenders on leave below are described as walking like men on a boat.

## Speech

They talk, but only indoors, and only close, mouth to the side of the head, in a low continuous mutter that carries about as far as an arm. The huts are small for this reason. Outside, they sign, and the sign dialect of the Sill has drifted so far from its parent that a visitor with the parent language will understand perhaps one gesture in five, and will be confidently wrong about two of them.

It is a language shaped by three facts: nobody can be got hold of quickly; nothing here is urgent; and everything worth saying is something you saw.

So the grammar is full of deferral. There is a common inflection — a slight downward drift of the whole sign, as though it were settling — that means *not now, but noted*. There is a tense for things observed some time ago and only now reported, which is the tense most conversation happens in. There is a sign meaning *in a decade*, and it is not a joke, and it appears in work-planning. And there is the great workhorse of the dialect, the one gesture any newcomer learns in the first hour and then uses forty times a day for the rest of their life: the slow open hand, turned out, drifting to the side. *Look. Over there. Slowly.*

Nothing on the Sill has ever been pointed at quickly. Fast pointing makes people snap their heads round, and a snapped head finds nothing, because on the Sill the interesting thing is always either very small or very far and has no outline.

There is also a sign for *I am here*, made high, on approach. The etiquette is absolute: you come at a person from within their field of view, you stop at ten metres, and you wait to be seen. You never arrive behind anyone. A tender who comes up on another tender's back will be talked about in the huts, quietly, for a season.

## Their boots

Resoled in the tender-huts with rubber cut from condemned gutter-liner, a centimetre thick, soft as fruit. It kills the click. Bare-soled walking on Sill scree is the loudest event in the county and it makes people wince like a shout in a church.

The consequence is that people arrive without sound, which is why the etiquette exists, and why nobody is ever startled here, and why a tender's first week below in the dry country — where feet knock, where doors bang, where a man can be *heard coming* — is reported as physically exhausting.

## Time, and the open questions

Nothing on the Sill wears out, so nothing on the Sill is ever finished. The work is not repair; it is attendance.

The ledger at the lift-house is written in only when something has changed. Whole years are one line. *31mm. Seventh still bent.* A tender's first season is largely spent learning the Open Questions, of which there are, at present, nine. The seventh pylon is one. The vegetable panels are another, and have been open since before the keeper came, which makes them older than any living opinion about them. The habit is to raise an Open Question at the stove perhaps twice a year, air it thoroughly, agree it is finely balanced, and leave it. A question closed in under fifteen years is considered to have been rushed, and the closing is mistrusted, and somebody's grandchild will reopen it.

This is not indecision. It is a correct reading of the local rate of consequence. Anything you do here you will be standing next to for thirty years.

## Their machines, and whose they are

They have names for all of the Combers, though not names you would print — each is a sign, mostly derived from gait. Number Four is *the one that leans*. Number Eleven is *the one that was mended wrong and is better for it*.

They step aside for a Comber three days in advance, and they mean this literally: a gutterer will finish a run early and move her kit rather than be in a gantry's way the week after next. They do not clean the moss off the top plates. They do not oil anything that is not squeaking, and nothing squeaks. And they sign to the Combers — not as a joke and not as superstition, but because after eleven years of signing at everything in your field of view, the hand goes up before you have decided anything. *Look. Over there. Slowly.* The Comber does not look. The gesture is made anyway. Everyone does it. Nobody discusses it.

Number Nine stopped in a white hour, eleven years ago, halfway down the fourth rank at the northwest edge. It was never dragged out. It is now a low green shape of the same species as the concrete pad of the thing that was never built, and it is the best windbreak on that side of the plateau, and the crews eat there.

## Wireweed, which also lives here

It should be said plainly that the most successful inhabitant of the Sill is neither the people nor the moss but the rush.

It came up between the panels; the machines, which judge a string by tension and drip-rate, adopted it; and the machines now propagate it, because a cut filament woven into a wet gutter-clamp will root there within a fortnight. The Combers are not maintaining an installation that has been invaded. The Combers are gardening, at eleven millimetres a minute, on behalf of a plant that found a way to be mistaken for wire.

The tenders are aware of this. It is the fourth Open Question. The common view at the stove, expressed with the flat hand turned slowly over, is that something is being farmed up here and it is not settled in which direction.

## The small quiet ones

The wolf spiders hunt by touch, which is the only sense worth having in an anechoic cushion a metre deep, and they get into boots, and nobody kills them, on the general principle that killing is a decision.

The stone hens — the mute walking grouse — will let you pass at arm's length while doing nothing whatsoever, and the crews count them, and the count is a competitive matter conducted entirely in signs and never written down.

In the pump-hall drainways, two thousand metres below, the beetle larvae have never received the darkness that would tell them to pupate, and so they simply go on being larvae, pale and slow and enormous, some of them older than the tender who feeds them. The plateau's whole logic is legible in this: here nothing is completed, and nothing is destroyed, and things just continue in whatever form they were in when the signal failed to come.

## The keeper

One person does not rotate. The keeper minds the winch, the brass plate, the ledger, and the stove.

The stove is the only fire on the Sill, and its smoke is the only thing on the plateau that can be perceived from a distance. It cannot be heard. It is seen — a thin grey mark against a white floor, visible from nine kilometres, meaning *the cage has come up*, or *someone is going down*, or, once or twice a winter, nothing at all except that the keeper is cold. Every tender on the plateau checks for it the way people elsewhere check a clock.

The keeper's eyes no longer take the dry country. Below the cloud, the world is too contrasty, too shadowed, too full of edges and noise; a week of it produces headaches and a kind of flinching. So the keeper stays, and reads the ledger, and has views on all nine Open Questions which are entirely balanced, and sits out in the evenings on the silvered bench.

## The bench

It seats three. Nobody sits three.

The rule — unwritten, absolute, and impossible to get a straight answer about — is that on the evening a tender's hundred days end, the bench is theirs. They go down the following morning in the cage, through the grey, into the noise. The evening before, they sit alone and face down-cloud, and for eleven minutes the whole white floor of the world turns to hammered copper, and then it goes out, and behind them in the dark the harps keep beading and running, and the Combers keep arguing with the mountain at eleven millimetres a minute, and the wireweed keeps being mistaken for wire, and nothing anywhere makes a sound.
