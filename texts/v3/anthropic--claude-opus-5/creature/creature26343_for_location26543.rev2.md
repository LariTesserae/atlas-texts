# The Inhabitants

## 1. What the Place Excludes

Start with the negative shape, because it explains everything else.

Nothing here sweats. Nothing weeps. Nothing urinates in the way you would recognize — the waste comes out as a dry paste, a crystal, a pellet you could hold. There are no amphibians. There are no larvae that swim. No animal in the Underplain has an egg with free fluid in it; the eggs are leathery, packed, and laid into warmth rather than moisture. Every eye here has a third lid, drawn across a dozen times a minute, and the tear it spreads is more wax than water.

The exclusions are not accidents. They are the entry requirement. What lives here lives here because it has solved the problem of holding onto a possession that cannot be replaced locally, and every solution has left a mark on the body.

## 2. The Builders

Eleven millimetres, pale, wingless, blind, and partly made of the floor they eat.

They are not termites and not ants, though they have converged so hard on both that the distinction only matters internally. They take the pavement apart with their mandibles at a rate of a few micrograms a bite, and because their diet is ninety percent mineral, the calcite goes into the cuticle. A worker three weeks old is translucent. A worker six weeks old is grey and hard and rings faintly when it is dropped. By the end, they are closer to gravel than to insect, which is why the mound walls glitter: the masonry is not *containing* the dead, it *is* the dead, laid end to end, cemented by the last ones to arrive.

The rasping caste wears its mandibles down to stumps in about forty days. There is no retirement. A worker whose mouth has stopped functioning walks — and this is the only reliably purposeful-looking behaviour in the whole species — to the highest active face of the mound, climbs into the course being laid, aligns itself, and stops. Within a day it is built in. A hand's width per century is the growth rate, and what you are measuring when you measure it is a rate of dying.

Deep in the base galleries, in the oldest and most thermally stable chambers, hang the cisterns. These are workers that never developed jaws at all. They are fed by trophallaxis from the day they eclose, and they swell, and by the end of their first year they are the size of a grape and immobile, gripping the ceiling of a gallery with legs that will never do anything else. A mature complex holds sixty to ninety thousand of them. That is the colony's water: eleven or twelve litres, hanging in the dark, in bodies that will not move again for twenty years. In a bad decade the colony drinks them down and they hang slack and wrinkled like empty skins and are refilled, or are not, and are eaten.

They do not go to the surface. They have never gone to the surface. Every molecule of water in a mound arrived in the gut of something that flies, fell as guano or as a corpse, and was fetched in from the collecting galleries at the mound's foot. It is accurate and not poetic to say that the cities of the Underplain are built out of bat piss and patience.

The humming is not speech. It is rasping — millions of mouths on stone, transmitted into a structure that happens to be a superb resonator, the way a beehive is loud by accident. But the accident has been co-opted. A colony reads its own volume as a proxy for its own health, and thickens the intake spires when the note goes thin. And neighbouring complexes, nine kilometres off, read it too. A mound that falls below a certain amplitude for more than a year is being surveyed. Within a decade, foraging trails from two directions will be crossing its dead flank. Within a century it is a hill with somebody else's city on it.

There are four builder lineages in the basin, mutually intolerant, and they meet along frontiers that have not moved appreciably in twelve thousand years. Border war occurs at a frequency of roughly one engagement per square kilometre per century, lasts eleven days, kills two million, and shifts the line by less than the length of a room.

Once every nine or ten years, when the drifting ellipse of a light-shaft reaches a particular discolouration on the pavement that the colonies have no way of seeing and nevertheless respond to, the alates go up. These are the only builders with eyes. They have them for four hours. A hundred million of them rise into the one bright column in a grey world, and the swifts and the bats — who have been waiting, who have *been waiting for six days*, fat and irritable and roosting badly — go through them. From a hundred million, four foundations. Sometimes none. The ones that fail fall back onto the plain and feed the pale country for a week, and the mat beneath the ellipse is measurably thicker afterward, and will be for a thousand years.

## 3. The Fliers, Who Are the Weather

Ten million lodgers in the ceiling, on two shifts.

The swifts work days. Small, sooty, entirely aerial, they nest in the roof cracks on cemented saliva — and saliva is water, so a nest is a fortune spent, and old nests are mined out by anything with the reach. A swift colony that loses its cliff loses a century of savings.

The bats work nights, and the bats are the hydrology.

The nearest surface water is between forty and ninety kilometres from any given doline, depending on the season and on which of the four remaining seeps above the basin is still producing. A big frugivore bat leaves at dusk, climbs the shaft, crosses the dry country in the dark, drinks until she is nine percent water by mass, feeds, and comes back down before the light. She will excrete almost nothing on the journey. She is, functionally, a sealed vessel that navigates. The entire Underplain — the gardens, the pale country, the mounds, the mat, the grazers, the hunters, every litre of the living basin — is downstream of the decision of several million small animals to come home rather than roost out in the warm.

They come home because it is cool and constant and the ceiling is unclimbable, and because the noise they make in that ceiling is the only thing keeping them from being eaten.

The guano falls in cones. Beneath a major roost the cone can be ninety metres across and twelve deep, and it is the single most valuable landform in the basin for one reason that has nothing to do with nitrogen: *it is warm.* Decomposition holds the interior of a big cone at eleven or twelve degrees above ambient, and in a place with no seasons and no sun, that is the only thermal feature that exists. So the cones are maternity wards. Everything that can get to one goes there to lay, to whelp, to overwinter a life that has no winter. The interior of a cone is a vertical metropolis of beetles and mites and blind roaches at a density of four hundred thousand per cubic metre, and things come from twenty kilometres away to breed in it, and things follow them, and the ecology of a cone is denser and faster and shorter-lived than anything else in three hundred kilometres. A cone burns out in two centuries. Then the roost above it moves, and the process starts eight hundred metres away, and the old cone becomes a low pale hill that the mat digests over the following millennium.

## 4. The Harrows

Out in the dim, on the pale country, something large is eating the crust.

Four hundred kilograms, hairless, the colour of old candle-wax, built low and long with a heavy rasping tongue and a snout worn flat on one side. They eat the guano-growth — the white fans, the leafless stalks — by pushing forward with the head down and abrading a strip fifty centimetres wide. The strip takes a hundred and forty years to regrow. From the top of a mound you can read the feeding history of a whole region as a fine pale hatching across the floor, grooves crossing grooves, the oldest nearly closed, the newest bright.

They move at about three hundred metres an hour and they do not stop. Their metabolic rate is somewhere near a tenth of what a body that size ought to run, and they live two centuries, and they are functionally the largest water reserve on the plain — a walking cistern of two hundred and forty litres, ambling.

They travel in threes and fours. Never more. The forage cannot support a herd and the space has been budgeted, and a group that grows to six will split within a season by a mechanism nobody has identified.

Every few minutes one of them will make its call: a single low pulse at the bottom of the audible range, felt more than heard, that goes out and comes back off the ceiling. It is a ranging call. They are sounding the roof — not for navigation, exactly, but for confirmation. They are checking that the ceiling is still there. A harrow that stops receiving its echo becomes distressed within minutes, which is how you can tell when one has wandered under a collapse zone in the dark.

The calls are the slowest metronome in the basin, and they are also the tripwire. When the pulses stop, everything within eight kilometres knows.

## 5. The Quiet

Ninety kilograms. Long-limbed, slab-shouldered, dark, and covered in a pelt that is not really fur. Each fibre is branched four times, at four different scales, and the resulting surface absorbs across the entire band the bats and swifts use. It returns nothing. To anything navigating by echo, a hunting quiet is not a shape in the room; it is an absence in the wall, a bad tooth in the world.

It is not fast. Nothing here is fast. It walks at the speed of a strolling person and it hunts on a timescale of four to nine hours, and the hunt consists almost entirely of arriving. It will select a group of harrows at a range of six kilometres and simply come, without concealment, in the open, across the pavement, at a walk.

And in front of it, faster than it, the silence goes.

This is the thing you are meant to understand about the Underplain: the silence is not made by the hunter. It is made by everything else. A roost that detects the absence stops calling. The roost next to it hears the first roost stop, and stops. The harrows hear the fliers go quiet and cease their pulses. The builders, who cannot hear at all, feel the change in the airflow when ten thousand wings stop beating in a spire and seal their intake ports by reflex. The quiet spreads at the speed of one animal noticing another animal noticing, which across a hard flat floor with a hard flat ceiling is perhaps four hundred metres a second.

So the hush arrives a full hour before the hunter does, and everything in that hour knows what is coming and cannot run far enough, because there is nowhere in a three-hundred-kilometre room that is meaningfully further away. What the animals do instead is *stop*. The plain goes rigid. It is not fear as you would experience it. It is a budgeting decision, the same as every other decision here: movement costs water, the odds are bad in any case, and the cheapest response to a slow certainty is stillness.

There are perhaps two hundred quiets in the basin. Each holds a territory of some two thousand square kilometres. They drink exclusively from what they kill and they kill perhaps eleven times a year. They den in the guano cones, for the warmth, and their single greatest difficulty as a species is that their pups are loud.

## 6. The Forty Gardens

Under each shaft, in the green, is the only place in the Underplain with colour, and the only place with eyes worth having.

Each doline garden is an island. Three hundred metres of ellipse, a penumbra, and then forty kilometres of hostile grey in every direction. Gene flow between gardens is essentially zero — a vagrant every few centuries, carried across by accident in a bat's fur or a storm down a chimney. So the same ancestral stock, dropped in through forty different holes at forty different times, has become forty different answers.

In the eleventh garden the beetles are metallic green and the climbing things have prehensile tails. In the twenty-ninth the beetles are black and the climbers have lost their tails and gained a hooking claw. In the thirty-fourth, the garden is three thousand years younger than the others and the climbers have not arrived at all, and the tree-things there have never been pruned and grow in a form seen nowhere else.

The climbers are worth a word. Small, four-handed, no more than a few thousand in any garden, living their entire genealogy within sight of one patch of ground. They have a habit: they take pebbles from the talus at the foot of the shaft and jam them into the crevices of the black trunks. They do this constantly. It accomplishes nothing. No researcher — were there any — would find a function. And the trees have been growing around the pebbles for six or seven centuries, so that the trunks are now studded and armoured with stone, veined with it, incorporating it the way bone incorporates a pin.

That is as close as this place comes to making anything on purpose. A compulsion, iterated, becoming a landform. It is the same thing the mounds are, at a different scale.

## 7. The Ones That Made the Footprints

There are, at last count, somewhere between thirty and fifty of them left.

Two tonnes. Pale. Near-blind, in the way of something whose ancestors needed eyes and whose descendants will not have them. They walk the troughs — the soft-shouldered gutters left by the river — continuously, at a walking pace, for the whole of a life that runs past three hundred years. They do not stop to sleep. They eat on the move, badly, from the mat.

What they are doing is following a gradient. The floor of a trough falls at about one part in ten thousand, and the pads of their feet can read it, and they walk downslope, always, choosing at every junction the direction of descent, which is a behaviour that once — very reliably, for a very long time — led to water.

It has not led to water in ninety million years.

They are not in decline; they are past it. There has not been a recorded juvenile in eleven centuries, by any reckoning the basin keeps. They meet one another perhaps twice a decade, and when they do they stand together for a few hours, touching, and then resume in opposite directions.

Their prints are the old prints in the dunes, the ones preserved like writing. They are not ancient. They are from last year, and the year before, and four thousand years before that, laid down by the same handful of feet on the same routes, going nowhere anyone remembers, including them.

## 8. The Tenant of Record

Under all of it, threading the dust, running beneath the pale country and between the mound complexes and up through the talus of every doline, is the mat.

One organism. A single continuous fungal individual of indeterminate but not less than eight-thousand-year age, occupying the majority of the basin floor. Everything above described is, from the mat's position, a temporary concentration of nutrient that has been given legs so it can deliver itself.

It has no nervous system and it is not still. It moves resources across kilometres, on timescales of weeks, in directions that correlate with where things are about to die. When a harrow goes down, the mat is into it within nine days and has it by the spring. When a mound complex fails, the mat is drawing the water out of sixty thousand collapsed cisterns before the neighbours have finished the survey. When a roof panel lets go and a new shaft opens and a hundred tonnes of surface soil comes down with it, the mat has the new ground colonized inside two decades, which is the reason a garden can establish in ten generations, which is the reason the basin experiences the making of an entire new city as *immediately*.

It has been alive continuously since before the last collapse. It will be alive after the next one. It is the only resident of the Underplain with anything resembling a memory of the place, and the memory is chemical and unrecoverable and consists entirely of where the food has previously been.

## 9. You

Nothing approaches you and nothing flees you, and this is not indifference. It is a failure of category.

You are the wettest object in three hundred kilometres — forty-odd litres, walking around uncontained, radiating heat, making noise in a band nothing uses, leaving prints of a shape that has no entry anywhere in the basin's long instinctive ledger. The bats do not stop calling for you, because you return a perfectly ordinary echo. The quiets do not hunt you, because they hunt the pulse of the harrows and you do not make it. The builders do not sense you at all. You are a pressure anomaly and a warm patch and, to the only thing here with the reach to consider you, an incoming delivery.

You breathe out half a litre a day.

Where you stop to sleep, the mat will find it — the damp of a night's exhalation in the dust, a wealth it has no other source for — and it will come up. Thin, at first. Then a pale bloom, cream-coloured, in the rough outline of a resting body, with the darker double-smudge where your hands were folded under your head.

It will still be legible in a century. The floor will keep the prints considerably longer.
