# The Tenants

## 1. The film

Almost everything that lives here lives on the *underneath* of things, and so the honest census of the Pale Room is taken with the hands, by turning slabs, and it must be taken quickly, because what you have turned over is not a stone but a roof.

Sixty on one slab: glass crickets stacked like panes in a glazier's rack, their bodies the colour of nothing, their gut contents the only visible part of them — a small grey seed of eaten felt hanging in mid-air. They do not scatter when exposed. They freeze, because in four thousand generations the correct response to sudden light has been to become a slightly thicker piece of light. It works everywhere but on the floor, where they cannot help casting those small dark commas, and where the shadow travels ahead of the animal like a rumour.

The black beetles do not hide at all. They are the room's only public figures. A beetle crosses six paces of open flour at a sprint, stands, and cooks — legs locked, elytra shut, absorbing the whole white weight of the chamber — then sprints again. They are not fleeing. That is simply the gait of a body that has decided to be a stove rather than argue with the sun. Pick one up in the first two seconds of its standing and it is cool. Pick it up in the tenth and it will burn a print into your thumb, which is a thing the Pale Room does: it makes even the small animals into clocks.

On the walls, the geckos, whose flanks have gone to mirror. In the pool's warm end, the shrimp, an orange smoke that thickens toward autumn and thins toward spring and has never once been touched by a shadow of its own making. In the root chandelier, one centipede, translucent, which has lived up there among the weeping tendons for longer than anything else in the room and has never come down, and does not need to, because everything eventually climbs.

None of these are the residents in the sense you mean. They are the room's upholstery. They are what the surfaces do.

## 2. The shade-holders

The residents are the parasol rats, and there are never more than thirty of them, and they are all cousins.

They are not rats, though nothing is gained by insisting on it. Sandy above, white beneath, hunched in the shoulders, with a soft tremor in the hindquarters that the old ones have and the young ones inherit, because nothing in this room has ever punished a slight unsteadiness of gait. Their ears are thin enough to read a hand through. Their eyes are enormous — wet, black, out of all proportion — and they are the wrong eyes entirely. They are night eyes. They belong to an animal that came down through the crawl in the south wall a long time ago out of some proper darkness, and they have not changed, and this is the most interesting fact about the species.

They should have changed. Four thousand years of standing in milk ought to have shrunk that pupil, thickened that lens, put a fold of pigment across the iris. Instead: the frond.

They hold their darkness overhead in one forepaw, and because the frond works — because it works for the clear-eyed and the weak-eyed and the merely careless alike — the sun has never been permitted to make a selection. The parasol has held their bodies still. They are a night animal that invented night and has therefore never had to become a day animal, and the price of the invention is that no individual among them can ever put it down. A rat here without a frond is a rat squinting through a white grinding pain, backed into a slab-lee, waiting.

The paw that carries it has gone strange. The wrist locks. There is a horn-coloured callus at the base of the thumb, polished, present in the newborn as a pale pad before they have ever gripped anything. They walk on three legs with a rocking, hitching stride, and their tracks in the flour are consequently unmistakable: three good prints and, every fourth beat, the faint scuff where a stem-tip touched down as the wrist changed hands.

## 3. The crop

The fern is one plant. The tamarisk lives thirty metres away and above and does not know it has tenants.

They clip from the fern rarely and from the underside only, one frond at a time, edges first, and any rat that took more than that would be doing nothing worse than what its ancestors did before the ancestors that stripped it died out. What survives is not an ethic. It is the residue of an ethic's absence being fatal.

Mostly they harvest root. The chandelier's fine ends are inexhaustible in the only sense that matters: a plant that lives elsewhere pays the tax without noticing, and the roots come down a finger's length a decade, so there is always new material arriving from a country none of them will see. They chew a length off, bruise the cut end soft, and carry it up over the head with the fibres splayed into a small ragged lace. It is a poor parasol. It is what there is.

The best shade in the Pale Room they did not make. Forty years ago a goat came down through the crawl, could not get back up, and died against the west wall, and the salt air kept it, and what remains is a rack of ribs standing off the floor at a slight angle: the only lattice in a room with no architecture. Under the goat, the shadow is patterned. Pups are kept in the skull. Four generations of the family have been born inside a head that entered by accident, and when the ribs finally go — and one of them cracked in a dry year and did not knit, because nothing here knits — the room will lose the only building it has ever had.

## 4. Tilt

They do not fight over shade, which is remarkable, because shade is the only thing worth fighting over and a fight would cost both parties more shadow than either could win.

Instead there is the tilt. Two rats meeting in a good patch sit close and hold their fronds at complementary angles, and the geometry does the negotiating. A frond held high and flat means a long stay; canted forward over the face, a short one; laid nearly across the shoulder means *I am leaving as soon as the beam clears the pool*. A rat that wants company opens its angle. A rat that does not closes it, and the other rat goes, and there is no sound, and nothing is decided, and both of them are correct.

Their voices have almost gone. In a smooth-walled bubble with no wind, every noise crosses the room and comes back, and a species that lived loudly here would have lived briefly. They click a tooth. They breathe emphatically. The rest is angle and touch, and the floor.

## 5. The roads

Because the flour holds every print until something steps on it, the parasol rats have arrived — without deciding to, over a very long time — at roads.

There are five. They run from the crawl to the pool, from the pool to the goat, from the goat to the fern and the chandelier, and they are not worn, because wear is impossible here; they are *thickened*, print upon print upon print, ten centimetres of compacted family history, slightly firmer underfoot, faintly cooler. Off the roads, the floor is untouched for spans of years at a time. They step aside for good reason and no other, and the effect, seen from the crown of the vault, is of a species keeping voluntarily to the margins of a document it cannot read but will not spoil.

They can read some of it. A young rat crossing open flour will step over the drag-mark of the snake that came down the crawl in some year nobody can name and hunted here a season and went away or died. It steps over the mark. It has never seen a snake. Its mother stepped over the mark. In the Pale Room the past does not decay into the general background of the world; it lies where it fell, actionable, waiting to be obeyed.

## 6. Meals

They hunt by shadow, exactly as you would.

When a selenite blade is on the floor they sit at its margin, frond low, and watch the lit flour for commas — and the cricket, being invisible, is betrayed by the only thing about it that is not. A rat takes it out of the air with the free paw. This can only be done while a beam is down, so there are two hunting hours in the day, set by the sun's slow crossing from the west wall to the east, and between the panes the crickets walk about in perfect safety through a room full of predators who cannot see them.

Beetles they take by patience. A rat noses the beetle into shade, sits down beside it, holds its frond over the pair of them, and waits — sometimes a long time — for the animal to cool enough to be eaten. It is the most companionable thing in the room: two black eyes and a black beetle sharing a scrap of borrowed dark, one of them waiting for the other to become bearable.

## 7. The shelf

They drink at the pool's fresh end in the hour when no beam lies on the water.

Their dead go to the other end. Not ceremonially — a body is dragged, and it is dragged along the road, and it is left at the fret of salt crystals at the brine rim, because that is downhill and because it is the one place in the chamber nothing ever needs to go again. The salt takes them. It goes into the fur and out through the fur and leaves a small white articulated thing, complete, weightless, tinted faintly pink at the joints where the halophiles worked.

The pool advances and retreats a hand's width across the season, and in the dry months it retreats past the shelf, and there they all are: generations, in a low white rubble, ribs and locked wrists, the polished thumb-callus still on every forepaw. Sometimes a rat comes down to drink and stops, and stands over a great-grandmother, and holds its frond at no angle at all for a while, and then drinks.

## 8. The northeast corner

Eleven flakes, three blades, one core. Four toes and a heel pad.

The prints are theirs. There is no puzzle about the prints; there is only a reluctance, and the reluctance is not the room's. The blades are small because the hands were small. They were struck by something that walked on three legs with the fourth held up, and the crowding of tracks around the scatter is not the record of one afternoon's work but of four thousand years of visiting.
