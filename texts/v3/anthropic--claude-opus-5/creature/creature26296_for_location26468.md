## Tenure

In order of arrival: the lichen, which is orange and crustose and has been mapping the domes for so long that it has stopped being an organism and become a soil horizon. Then the fig in the trench, one individual, in one crack, older than any wall. Then the bee, which nests in the hollows of old pedestals and navigates by the grid, and which will fly a perfect eleven-pace hypotenuse across four hundred meters of identical white ground and arrive at the correct hole. Then the grazers. Then the cat. Then the birds, who have been here longer than any of them but who keep leaving and coming back and so are counted as guests.

Then the people, who arrived about eleven hundred years ago, and who are aware that this ranking is a courtesy extended to them and not a fact.

The pins do not appear on the list. You do not list the ground.

## Bodies

They are long and narrow and deep-chested, and the chest is not from the altitude — the lens keeps the plateau warm but does not thicken the air — so their lungs have done the ordinary work of high people and their voices ride on an enormous slow bellows, which turns out to matter later.

Their hands are cracked white at the knuckles from the caprock dust, which is silica and does what silica does. Their fingernails wear flat and take a shine, from the same abrasion that polished the cobbles, and are worn down at the same relative rate, and one of their sayings compares the two and is not considered clever.

They do not squint. Squinting belongs to people who are searching. These people found what they were looking for some hours ago and have been watching it since, from four hundred meters, and can tell you that the animal is lame in the near hind and has been for two days. They ring their eyes with soot and grease to kill the glare coming up off the white rock, so that a herder's face at rest is two dark holes and a mouth, and this has caused trouble in lowland markets.

Close work fails them early. By thirty they hold a knot at arm's length; by fifty they hand it to a child. It is understood that a young person's job includes threading things.

## Speech

They have a spoken language and they use it at a range of about one arm.

The preferred form is chest to chest. You put the flat of your hand on the other person's breastbone, and they put theirs on yours, and you speak with the mouth nearly closed and the voice pitched low, and it travels as it was always going to travel here — not through the air, which eats it, but through bone and cartilage into the palm and up the arm. Two people conversing look like two people steadying each other on bad ground. A long conversation ends with both of them warm-handed and slightly hoarse.

Three cannot do it. A council therefore sits in a closed ring, each with the right hand on the next one's sternum, and a matter goes around the ring in one direction only, arriving back at its author altered, and the alteration is the decision. There is no vote. There is no chair. There is a direction.

At distance they use the sleeve. Every coat is lined white at the cuff and the shoulder blade, and a flick of the arm throws a flash that reads clean at a kilometer in that unforgiving light. Names are rhythms — two or three beats of white. Children invent their own and trade them. You can be called across an entire bench in half a second by a name that has never been said aloud, and when lowland clerks demand that these people write their names, they write dots and short dashes, and the clerks assume it is a code.

They do not clap. Approval is a single flash. Laughter is silent and total, the whole body going, and a joke landing among nine people at three hundred meters looks like an outbreak of small flags.

And they sing. Very low, very slow, under the frequency the air steals, in that bellows register — a thing you feel in the sternum before you identify it as music, so that new arrivals look for its source in the rock. It is the only art they have that requires the field. Taken down the scarp it stops working immediately; in a lowland room it is a large man grumbling. They have learned not to perform it there, which lowlanders read as reticence and which is closer to grief.

## The Count

The day starts by not starting.

In the hour before dawn the air goes milky, and the dew leaves the ground, and for eleven minutes everything on the plateau stands still. The herds freeze — nobody taught them; either they came to it themselves or they were always going to. The people stand where they are, wherever they are, hands empty, coats open. Walking is not forbidden during the drink. It is simply not done, the way you do not talk over someone's last sentence.

They stand, and the sparks go up past them from horizon to horizon, and because everyone is stationary and dark against white ground in horizontal light, everyone can be counted. The stillness is also the census. A family knows by the end of the drink exactly who is present, because for eleven minutes the field displays its contents. This is the origin of the name outsiders use for the plateau, and the name is accurate about three things at once — the pins, the herds, and the people — which is why the herders' own word covers all three and has never been narrowed.

Anyone missing is looked for immediately, before the light goes vertical, because a lost person here cannot shout.

## Reckoning

They count in elevens, and distance is measured in pins. Four hundred pins south. A day's walk in bad going is nine hundred. A field of grazing is described as a rectangle: fourteen by twenty-two, meaning a specific patch of the grid that has been walked in a specific rotation since before the wall on the second bench was stacked. Nobody owns the ground. Families hold rotations, which is a different thing, and a rotation can be lent, inherited, or ruined by carelessness.

Each family has a pin. Not a pedestal — a pin: the filament is the permanent party. At every birth, a mark is scratched into the pedestal at the exact current soil line. At every death, another. Because the plateau retreats and the pedestal does not, marks made long ago now ride slightly higher than marks made recently, so the record stacks downward in time, oldest at the top, and eleven generations of a family occupy rather less than the width of a thumbnail.

They read it with a wetted thumb and a low sun. An elder can go down that thumbnail naming, and the names come out as sleeve-rhythms tapped on the listener's forearm, and it takes an hour.

Near the eastern scarp, where the pedestals are knee-high, there is a pedestal with marks on it a finger-length above the current ground. The same kind of marks. The same spacing. Nobody claims that pedestal. It is visited, and read, and nothing is done about it, and the reading is passed on. Whoever they were, they counted their children the same way and against the same rock, and were finished long enough ago that the difference between them and the caprock is a matter of a few centimeters.

## Goods

Everything they use to carry water, they carry upside down.

The drink-cloth is a sheet of coarse fiber pegged flat a hand above bare ground before dawn; the rising droplets collect on its *underside* and are rolled toward one corner and wrung off.
