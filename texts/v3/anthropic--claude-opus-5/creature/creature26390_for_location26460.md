## Who lives there

**Start with the floor, because the floor is the oldest thing with an appetite.**

The pelt is the resident. Everything else is a guest, a parasite, a tenant, or weather.

It has no head and therefore no opinion, but it has something that stands in for memory: its plumbing. Nine million kilometres of duct is not a random tangle; it is a scar record. Every blowdown for eleven million years has been repaired by re-routing, and every re-route is still in there, a knuckle of old scar-junction where a valley once vented and now doesn't, a fossil detour taken around some catastrophe no living thing witnessed. Trace one main for four hundred kilometres and you are reading a chronicle written by the only author who was present. It does not know it wrote it. It cannot read.

What it can do is *count pressure*. That is the whole of its intelligence and it is not nothing. A gallery that is losing head will grow a new port; a district drawing hard will thicken its mirror-scales and shed some of its neighbours' feeding rights; a shaft that has stalled for a season is quietly cut off at the ankle and dismantled from below. There is no committee. There are only ducts voting with their contents. Across a continent, over centuries, this produces something so like husbandry that visitors keep looking for the farmer.

It eats dust. It eats the rime. It eats fallen stacks, and gasp-fly drifts, and anything else that lies still on it long enough — which includes, eventually, everything that lives on it. Put your dead down anywhere and in nine days the scales have closed over the outline and in a season there is no outline. This is the only funeral rite the world offers and it is very thorough.

**Then the air, which is furnished.**

The thermal columns are stocked the way a river is stocked. Gasp-flies in the exhaust, in generations so brief that a single hot afternoon holds four dynasties. Windgills above them, black rags with no lungs, letting the overpressure of the world do their breathing for them, circling for years without touching ground. Vent-clamps on the shafts, patient as barnacles, stripping sulfur out of a jet with the untroubled stupidity of something that has found one good idea.

Below those, less often mentioned: **rime-lickers**, palm-sized, translucent, which live entirely on the eight cold hours, harvesting salt-snow off the folded scales before the pelt can reclaim it, and which die every dawn in numbers that would be a plague anywhere with rot. **Coil-mice**, in the spiral throats, riding the standing wave, deaf in a way that goes beyond deafness — their whole nervous system is a pressure organ and the note they were born in is the only environment they can survive. Move one to a piper of a different pitch and it dies within the hour, of the wrong music.

And the sumpers, of course. Blind, sooted, cow-sized, chewing into a sap-main because the sap is good and stopping the main because their heads are in the way, killing a kilometre-high organism over eight weeks without once suspecting that they have done anything. There is no malice available in the Loudland. There is only the shape of a mouth.

**But you are asking about the ones who hold an address.**

They are called sounders, by the people who name things. Also gapmakers, also stopper-hands. There are perhaps forty thousand of them across the whole continent, which is roughly one for every three stacks, which is exactly as dense as anything here is permitted to be.

A sounder is a tall dark trestle of an animal, two metres at the shoulder and not much more than a hundred kilos, built like a heron that has decided to become a ladder. Four legs, sooted black, thin as tent-poles and jointed for climbing. Two forelimbs held up and folded, ending in broad soft palms. The head is small and almost entirely mouth. There are eyes, technically: two slits under a horn shelf, sufficient to tell glare from shadow and shadow from a hole, which in this country is the only visual question worth asking.

The feet are the animal. Each pad is a drumskin the size of a dinner plate stretched over a sinus of its own oil, and behind it a nerve trunk as thick as your thumb runs up the leg. A sounder standing still with four pads down is a four-element array. It knows the bearing of a note to within a degree and the distance to within a few kilometres, and it does this continuously, awake and asleep, for sixty years. Their gait is a rolling one that never lifts more than one foot at a time — not for balance. For continuity. To lose two pads at once is to go momentarily blind.

When two of them stop to listen properly, they walk apart first. Sixty, eighty metres apart, and then stand, and lengthen the baseline of the world. It looks like estrangement. It is the opposite: it is the closest attention they are capable of paying, and they can only pay it to something distant, and only together.

They graze sap. Not like a sumper — a neat bite into a capillary duct, a lick at the seep, and away before the scar has finished closing. A string of them crossing a district leaves a line of small healed punctures nobody would notice. They have been doing it for as long as there have been ducts and it has never once mattered.

**What they know.**

The chord is a map. Two thousand stacks within the range of a good pair of feet, each with a voice — pitch, timbre, the pattern of its bellows-thump, the particular filthy stutter of a slattern's ruined lip — and every one of those voices is an address. A sounder does not navigate. A sounder simply *is somewhere*, at all times, the way you are always aware of being upright.

The trouble is drift. Stacks detune with age and weather. Throats crack, coils bore themselves out, two neighbours slide toward unison and forty thousand square kilometres of country goes illegible. So the old ones lead the young ones along routes — and the route *is* the lesson, there is no other way to give it — and they stop at the stacks that have gone wrong, and they climb.

Four hundred metres of ply and buttressing, hand over hand, in a note loud enough to loosen the joints. At the top, at the reed lips, they chew. A few centimetres off a fluted edge, a nick in a coil, a lip thinned until it flutters — and the stack's voice comes back into distinction from its neighbour's, and the country is readable again for another thirty years.

They are not tending a monument. Each individual is doing something much smaller: fixing an ambiguity that was annoying it. But the effect, integrated over eleven million years, is that a continent of unbuilt engines has been kept in tune by something with no concept of tuning, in service of a map that has no author and exists only as a habit distributed across forty thousand pairs of feet.

Nothing here was built. It still holds. A note is not an object.

**How they talk.**

This is the part that takes the longest to believe.

In a world where noise is the medium — where the chord is the air and the ground and the pressure in your sinuses — a sound is not a signal. There is no volume left. Nothing can shout over a continent already shouting.

So they speak in holes.

The palms are the speech organs. A sounder climbs to the port ranks on a shaft, sets both hands over two of the thigh-width vents, and closes them. Four hundred metres above, the throat's note falters — not stops, just thins, wobbles, loses its bottom for as long as the hands are down. Half a second. A second and a half. Then release. Then again, in a pattern.

Across the plateau, forty kilometres out, other feet feel a small tidy gap in a familiar voice and know three things: who, where, and what.

Their whole language is subtraction. Every word is an absence with edges. The vocabulary is small — a few hundred distinguishable gap-figures, most of them concerning water, sumpers, birth, and route — and the grammar is almost entirely about duration. It is a formal language, an outdoor language, because you have to climb a tower to use it, and consequently they use it rarely and never lightly. There is no idle talk at altitude.

The domestic language is on the skin. Palm on flank, palm on the long muscle of the neck, a fast pressed shorthand that never leaves the body of the string and cannot be overheard by anything in the world. A sounder among its own is in near-constant conversation and utterly silent, and the two registers do not translate into each other. What is said with hands on skin is never said from a shaft. What is said from a shaft cannot be said any other way.

Every other animal in the Loudland has evolved to distinguish a sounder's neat little gap from the ragged four-second silence of a blowdown. The fauna reads their speech as a matter of survival and does not understand a word of it. When a string is talking over a district, the herds settle. When a string goes quiet and starts walking radially, everything walks.

Their name for themselves is a gap two-thirds of a second wide, made high, made twice. Their name for the pelt is a gap of eleven seconds, which requires two adults and cannot be made from any stack under six hundred metres, and which is understood to be dangerous — everything within thirty kilometres will lift its head and prepare to abandon its country. It is said perhaps once in a lifetime. Some strings go three generations without saying it and are not thought impoverished.

**What the water is for.**

Not drinking. They can live and die on sap.

A sounder's egg comes out sealed in a resin case the thickness of a fingernail, and nothing in the world will open it — not heat, not acid, not a mother's jaws — except a few grams of liquid water, applied warm, which softens a seam that has evolved for exactly this and no other purpose. No water, no hatch. The case simply sits there and goes powder in a decade.

So: drop-well raiding. So: the armour on the stacks' ankles. So: every social arrangement in the Loudland.

A string of five to eleven has one carrier, who holds the string's water in a lined crop behind the sternum, sometimes for years, sealed and untouched — a cupful, a fist. She is walked in the middle. She eats first. She is not the leader, and being the carrier is not an honour; it is a load-bearing position, and everyone in the string can feel by touch, at any moment, exactly how much future they are carrying and where it is.

Strings meet. Water changes hands, or does not. Water is lent against a future hatching, and the debt is real, and unpaid debt is followed for decades across a continent by things that can smell a spoonful from twenty kilometres upwind.
