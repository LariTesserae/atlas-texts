Begin with the fact that nothing lives here, and then correct it.

Several million things live here. Almost none of them are residents.

### The commuters

Count the bats out of the equation first, because the bats do not live in the Hall Country; they sleep in it. Every gram of the eleven-metre guano cone came down a hole in the roof, in a body, from the forest. Same for the swiftlets. Same for the cockroach carpet and the beetle tonnage and the racers in the ceiling cracks and the amblypygids and the liver-coloured centipedes, which are all, at one or two removes, eating trees they have never touched. The loud ten percent of this country is not a cave community. It is a piece of the forest, pushed down through eleven chimneys, burning at the bottom.

The people with the rope are the extreme case of this. They come down to take the fire's ash and go home.

So set the seams aside. What lives in the Hall Country — what is *from* here, what has no other address — is out in the blank, in the pale silt, in the ninety percent, in the dark that has been dark continuously since the roof went up.

### The budget of the blank

Here is everything that arrives in a hectare of deep floor in a year: some dust of the ceiling's own making; a few spores; the fine organic haze that drifts off the wet rings when the swiftlets stir the air; one or two crickets that walked out too far and did not turn back; occasionally a moth that came down a shaft in daylight, flew until it was empty, and landed. Grams. Call it twenty grams a hectare, and be generous.

Everything that lives on that budget is shaped by it in the same four ways. It is slow. It is sparse. It is long-lived, because at this temperature and this intake you cannot afford to replace yourself often. And it walks in straight lines, because in a country where the food is at a density of nothing per square kilometre, a random search is a death sentence and a straight line is at least new ground.

There is a springtail here, white, half a millimetre, grazing the film that grows on the underside of silt grains. There is a thread of a centipede, two centimetres, that eats the springtails. There is a mite that eats the centipede's eggs. That is three links and it is thin enough to see through.

And there is the walker, which is the top of it, and the only animal in this country large enough that a person would notice one, and the only animal in this country that would still be here if all eleven skylights closed tomorrow.

### The walker

A lamp finds it standing on the floor at forty metres: a pale shape, upright on long legs, absolutely motionless. Ninety times in a hundred it is an empty moult and you can put your hand through it.

The living animal is a spider that has given up almost everything a spider does. Body the length of a thumb joint. Legs out of all proportion — a mature female spans a dinner plate — and thin as grass stems, held high, so that the body rides ten centimetres clear of the silt. No web, no burrow, no retreat, no ambush; the sit-and-wait ancestors of this lineage are still doing very well two kilometres away in the guano light, small and quick and fat, and they would eat this thing if it came near them. The blank branch of the family gave up waiting, because out here nothing walks past.

It is the colour of wet paper. The abdomen is translucent, and the gut shows through it, so that you can read the animal's last month at a glance: glassy and faintly blue means empty, and most of them are empty; a dull brown hourglass means it has eaten within six weeks and is still working on it.

The feet end in brushes as broad as a fingernail, snowshoes for floury sediment, which is why it can stand on the surface of dust that takes a human boot three centimetres deep.

It has no eyes. There are six pits where eyes were, unlensed, and they are not sensitive to anything, and light does not make it flinch.

It hears through its feet. That is the whole of its world: vibration in stone and in packed silt, across a range that runs from the individual drip in the next bay to the general roar of a roost three kilometres off. It cannot see the eleven skies but it knows exactly where they are, because the wells are loud, and the seams are loud, and the walker spends its entire life keeping the noise behind it and to the sides.

### Straightness

The ripples solve the other half of navigation.

The corduroy left by the last withdrawal of the water runs, over huge areas, in one consistent direction — a current that stopped flowing before this animal's lineage existed, preserved two fingers high and knife-sharp because nothing has come to unmake it. The walker crosses the grain at a fixed angle, feeling the ridges pass under the four legs on one side, correcting continuously, and it can hold a heading across ten kilometres of featureless dark to within a few degrees.

Where the silt thins and the grain fails, the walkers lose the line. There are patches like this — two or three hectares of scoured floor between the fifth hall and the sixth — and the tracks in them are a tangle, animals circling, crossing their own trails, blundering until they hit good grain again. Walkers have died in those patches, standing, out of time rather than out of direction.

It moves at about a metre a minute and it moves in shifts of two or three hours, and between shifts it stands for half a day doing nothing at all, because it has no day to spend and nothing to spend it on. Over a year it covers something like a hundred kilometres of empty floor. In that year it will meet another of its own kind perhaps twice.

### Eating

Eight times a year is a good year. Four is normal.

The prey is the stray cricket, the drowned moth, the springtail drift where the floor is damp enough to farm one. The strike is slow and casual — there is no competition and nothing gets away out here, since anything moving on the open silt is as lost as everything else. The venom is weak and cheap. Digestion takes weeks, and the animal does it standing, in the dark, in the middle of a hall the size of a valley, with nothing within a kilometre in any direction.

Twice in a life, maybe three times, it finds a windfall: a bat that flew the wrong way out of a shaft and starved on the floor, or a fallen swiftlet, three grams of forest protein in a country that runs on twenty grams a hectare a year. A walker will stay with a bat for a month and leave nothing but the ears.

Water is free. Every surface in this country sweats. It drinks off the stone whenever it likes, which is one of the reasons it can afford to be so poor.

### The counting

And now the one extravagance, the one thing in its entire economy that it does not do cheaply.

It taps.

The animal lowers itself until the underside of the abdomen touches rock — it will walk a hundred metres off its line to find bare rock, because silt swallows the signal — and strikes the stone with a hardened plate at the base of the front legs. The sound is dry and low and very small. Through the air it is inaudible at ten paces. Through the floor of a limestone hall, it travels for kilometres.

The pattern is fixed: a knock, a pause, two knocks, a long pause. Then nothing for four or five hours. Then again.

A walker taps, on and off, for most of its adult life, into a country where the answer will probably not come. When the answer does come, both animals turn, and begin walking toward each other, and continue tapping, and correct, and continue. This can take three weeks. It has been recorded taking four months. Neither of them hurries, because neither of them has anywhere else to be, and because arriving exhausted at a distance of eight kilometres is a real way to die here.

That is the sound underneath the sound of the Hall Country. The roosts hiss, the drips make their static, the swiftlets pour gravel onto their drum at dusk; and under all of it, spread across two hundred square kilometres of floor, at intervals of hours, a few thousand animals knocking on the stone and listening.

### Meeting

The meeting itself is brief and nearly ceremonial. They stand a body-length apart and drum at each other for two or three hours, then close, then separate, and each resumes its heading, and they will not meet again.

The female carries one egg. One. It is the size of a pea and it costs her most of what she has eaten in the previous two years. She broods it under her body for four months, walking with it, and the young that comes out is a third of adult size, fully equipped, blind, and immediately gone in some other direction.

A female may manage five of these in sixty years. That is enough, because nothing here eats them and nothing here changes.

### The standing dead

It moults eight or nine times before adulthood, and the moults do not fall over, and they do not rot at thirteen degrees, and there is no wind.

So the blank is populated by its own ghosts: pale empty spiders standing perfectly upright on the silt, legs locked, at a density of roughly one to the hectare over hundreds of square kilometres. They hold for decades. A slow dust of ceiling fines eventually collapses them, but slowly, and there are moults down here standing in the exact posture of animals that lived when the rope on the ledge was a tree.

Living walkers encounter them constantly. The response is the same every time: a single tap against the moult's foot, no answer, and the animal walks on.

Corpses stand too, for a while, until something finds them. In the blank, a dead walker is the largest concentration of food available, and it is eaten by other walkers, which is the only sense in which this species is social.

### The census, which does not move

The dry floor keeps a record. The brush-feet leave a stitched double line in the floury silt, and the fines settling out of the air blur it over a century or two and erase it in five hundred years — fast enough that the floor is not a solid trample, slow enough that any given square metre carries about two hundred years of walking, stacked and fading.

Hold a lamp low and read it. The tracks from twenty years ago and the tracks from two hundred years ago are at the same density. Their headings are the same, because the grain is the same. The moults per hectare are the same. There is no boom in this country and no crash and no invasion and no retreat; there is a population of some tens of thousands of adults which has been some tens of thousands of adults for longer than the forest above has been that forest.

One thing in the record is new. In a few hectares around the base of the second cone, the grain is destroyed — human boot-prints, three centimetres deep, cut across the ripples and through them, and nothing here will ever put a ripple back. The walker tracks in that zone wander. They loop, cross, double, and eventually strike out on whatever heading they happened to be facing when they gave up.

It is the only permanent mark people have made in the Hall Country, and it is on the only map the natives have.

### The people's version

The nest-gatherers do not know this animal. They have never seen one; the walkers avoid the wells, since the wells are loud and the wells are full of relatives that bite.

But at some hour on the ledge, when the roost quiets and the drizzle is the only sound, a person lying with a hand flat on the rock will feel it: a knock, a pause, two knocks. And again, hours later, from somewhere out in the dark where there is nothing at all.

They say the stone is counting. They say it has always done that and it means nothing. The custom, if you hear it, is to rap the rock once with a knuckle and then stop — and if the counting changes, to stop at once and not do it again.

Blind, white, unhurried, eating four times a year, drinking off any wall it likes, steering by a current that dried up before the first of its ancestors hatched, holding a straight line through the dark for a hundred kilometres a year to knock twice on a stone and listen:

it owns the whole country except the ten percent worth having, and that is exactly the deal it has made.
