# What Lives in the Nine-Week Rock

## Four Hundred and Six

Start with the beetles, because they are the largest thing here and the most badly mistaken.

They are flat, bronze-black, the length of a grain of rice and about as thick as a fingernail clipping, and they have travelled between two and nine kilometres uphill to arrive at a place that cannot possibly do them any good. Behind their middle legs, in two small pits, they carry an instrument of extraordinary refinement: a cluster of fluid-filled spheres that expand when infrared strikes them and press on a nerve, giving these animals the ability to detect a burning forest from across a valley. It is one of the finest pieces of sensory engineering in the insect world, and it evolved for a single purpose, which is to find wood that has just finished burning, because scorched phloem is the only substance in which the larvae will develop and because the trees are undefended for perhaps two days after the fire passes.

There is no wood in this pocket. There is no wood within a vertical kilometre. What there is, is heat — a great deal of heat, an unrefusable amount, a signal so loud and so continuous that the instrument reads it as the largest fire that has ever existed. They came in through the dome's fractures during the second night, crawling in the dark up the gradient, and they arrived at the margin and stopped, because the gradient stopped, and because nothing in their behavioural repertoire tells them what to do when the fire has no edge.

At the peak, in the third week, there were something over six hundred. There are now four hundred and six. The difference lies on the floor and has largely been dealt with by other residents. Nothing has hatched and nothing will; every beetle in this room is an original arrival, and the population is a subtraction problem being worked slowly toward zero. They are packed carapace to carapace, three and four deep, in the slits and behind the collapsed blocks, legs interlocked in a mass that redistributes itself constantly — not restless, exactly, but never at rest, each individual making minute adjustments toward its preferred temperature and thereby displacing its neighbours, so that the whole heap shifts and rasps and clicks without ever going anywhere. This is the continuous dry crackle. It sounds like a fire. It is four hundred and six animals attempting to arrive at a fire they are already inside.

One of them, in the eastern taper, has been sitting for weeks within a centimetre of the whistling slot. Its elytra have gone dull and straw-coloured on the exposed side, cooked. It has not moved because by its own measurements it is in exactly the right place.

## The Air Itself

The flies are not a population, they are a medium. In the margin, at the densities now obtaining, the air has body: it resists a movement, it registers as fabric. Somewhere in the low hundreds of thousands of small dark platypezid and empidid flies, the smoke-swarmers, the ones that assemble in the plumes of burning brush and are almost never seen anywhere else, occupy the survivable shell as a suspension rather than as a crowd.

They came for smoke and this is smoke. Some of them feed on the microbial film at the cool cracks; most of them appear to feed on each other's dead, and on the beetles' dead, and on whatever the gas leaves on the walls. The hum they produce is not loud. It is only unceasing, and it occupies a band the roar does not, so that in the four minutes of the cracking phase, when the fundamental thins out and the glass is going off like coins, the flies are audible underneath — a soft insistent seam of sound, the pocket's actual voice, if it has one.

## Felt

Turn over any block on the floor and the underside is not rock. It is orange, and it moves the way a liquid surface moves, and it is composed of thermophilic mites at a density where individual animals cannot be resolved. They graze the film. They breed. They are, unlike the beetles, entirely successful here: generations are turning over in this room, mites are being born eleven metres inside a mountain to mothers who were also born there, and there is now a lineage in this pocket that has no experience of any world but this one.

Two species of pseudoscorpion work the felt, walking through it like harvesters, forelimbs open. They are the top predators of the cavity — three millimetres of armoured patience.

And here the place's own account of itself must be corrected on a small point. Rule Three says nothing is built, no artifact, not one. This is very nearly true and it is false by exactly one item: in a hairline crack on the northern wall, a female pseudoscorpion has spun a silk chamber the size of a grain of rice, walls opaque, entrance sealed with a plug of her own making, and inside it she is moulting. It is the only made thing in the world under discussion. It is the only structure here that exists because something intended it. It will last perhaps a month.

Nearby, on the floor rubble, the thumbnail flake of glass — the entire erosional history of this place — has cut something. There is a scored track on the underside of a beetle's abdomen and a smear of dried haemolymph on one fracture face. The single geological event of nine weeks and the single wound of nine weeks are the same event.

## The Ones Everything Else Is Made Of

At the outermost cracks, where the gas has fallen to sixty degrees, the grey-pink stain does the only real work in the pocket. There is no light and therefore no plant, no chlorophyll, no photosynthesis at any point in this food chain. Everything alive in this room is ultimately built out of sulphur, hydrogen, carbon dioxide and heat — out of the mountain's exhalation, chemically dismantled by archaea and bacteria that consider three hundred and forty degrees of vapour to be a delivery of groceries.

The film is perhaps four cells thick. It is the base and the whole base. The mites eat it, the nematodes in the ash between grains eat it, the flies eat it, the pseudoscorpions eat the mites, and the beetles eat almost nothing and are eaten when they stop. A complete ecosystem, saturated, with a producer layer you could wipe away with a thumb.

## What It Is Like In Here

The place's account says the crystal gardens have never been lit and never will be. From inside a beetle this is not quite right.

The pocket is not dark to the things that live in it. It is bright, in the only sense available: the floor seams blaze, the ceiling drips hang overhead as a warm even field, the west wall is dimmer, the cold fractures are black windows, and the sublimate gardens register — crudely, three or four pixels wide, unfocused, but they register — as a patch of slightly cooler nothing off to one side. The room is legible. It is a room with a fire on the floor and a draught from the north, and every animal in it is oriented, at every moment, to that arrangement.

And it has a tide. Each rise of the magma column drives hot gas up through the floor fractures, and the survivable shell is shoved a few millimetres outward; each fall draws it back. Twice an hour, on a period of thirty-eight minutes, four hundred and six beetles and some hundreds of thousands of flies and an unbroken felt of mites slide out and in again, following a boundary they cannot perceive as a boundary, only as comfort and its absence. The population breathes with the mountain. Nothing in it knows this.

## The Resident

Which is the honest answer to the question, and it is not a species.

What lives here is a shell. A curved band three to eight centimetres thick, hugging the outer wall, closed on itself like the rind of a fruit, made of beetle and fly and mite and nematode and film and one silk chamber, with a temperature at its inner face of about eighty degrees and at its outer face of about twenty, packed solid, breathing twice an hour, sounding continuously, and moving inward through solid rock at a centimetre a month. It is roughly the shape of the cavity and roughly the volume of two loaves of bread. It has been alive for nine weeks. It has never been seen, and it has no name, because a thing has to be noticed before it can be given one, and no instrument of any kind has ever been within eleven metres of it.

It will die in about thirty years, from the inside out, as the engine below loses its stroke and the shell thins and closes and there is no longer anywhere in this room that is neither too hot nor too cold, only cold. The beetles will go out through the fractures. The flies will drift into the corners. The mites will spore down.

And in the coldest fissures, dried to tuns, a scatter of tardigrades will not die, because dying is not what they do about conditions they cannot afford. They are the only residents whose patience is the same order as the rock's. They will still be there, glassy and folded and legally alive, when the landslide comes in eleven thousand years and opens the pocket to the sky for about ninety seconds.

Some of them will be facing the opening. One or two might be lifted by the air moving out. The odds are terrible.

They are not zero, and that is the entire plan.
