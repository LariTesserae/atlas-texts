There are four things living in the Hold, and only one of them is doing anything.

---

Begin with the animals, because they are what you will notice, and because you will be wrong about them in a specific and forgivable way.

The drifters and the scrapers are not two kinds of animal. They are one animal at two ends of its life, and the room sorts them by weight.

A drifter starts under the crown, in the fourth antinode, as a bud the size of a fingernail: almost nothing but membrane and hot gas, so light that it rises to the ceiling and hangs there among the glass whiskers. It is the least dense thing in the room and so it occupies the top sheet. Over time its membrane thickens — it takes mineral out of the gas and lays it down in those dark veins — and it grows heavier, and it sinks. Not by choice. By arithmetic. When it can no longer hold the top sheet it settles to the next, and there it hangs and browses and thickens further, and settles again. The five horizontal sheets you see are not five species and not five preferences. They are five ages, stacked youngest to oldest, and the spacing between them is the spacing between generations, made visible, held still.

The lowest sheet is nearly grey, nearly opaque, and hangs a hand above the floor. Eventually it touches. The membrane, on contact, plates over: the veins broaden and fuse and go hard, and the animal is now a scraper, flat and dark and belly-down, and it begins the only journey this world contains. It walks toward the cold end. It walks because at the cold end there are beads, and the beads are the densest and richest thing it will ever eat, and it can feel them from forty paces as a faint dead spot in the pressure. The walk takes what you would count as several human lifetimes. Nothing interrupts it. There is nothing here to interrupt it with.

At the bowl it eats. It becomes heavy and hot and slow and then not slow at all, and it stops. Sound condenses on a stopped scraper the way it condenses on cold stone, and faster, because the beast is a better surface than basalt. It is glazed over. It becomes a nodule, then a lump, then part of the wall, humming at its own small pitch, indistinguishable after long enough from the room's own furniture.

The cold end of the Hold — the bowl, the ribbed and lumpy wall above it, the whole apparatus that makes the beads in the first place — is built out of these. It is a boneyard that is also a resonator. The animal's contribution to the room is entirely posthumous, and the room could not make its only fluid without it, and none of this was designed, and the three who came here later understood it within their first year and left it alone.

---

Now the thickets, which are not wild.

Look at the frond-ends at chest height along any node-line. They are cut. Not snapped, not shattered in the manner of a knocked-over gas column — cut, cleanly, at a consistent angle, at a consistent height, all along the margin of every path, on both sides, for the full length of the room. Look up under the crown: there is a lane kept open there, a clear corridor through the fourth antinode's growth, exactly where the buds need room to rise. Look at the sand. Sand produced by grazing does not lie in rows. It lies where it falls. These rows are drifted along the node-lines with their long axes pointing toward the cold end, and they are pointing that way because they are being moved, a little, in one direction, by something with a flat tool and no hurry at all.

The room is kept. The lushness is a managed lushness. That printed regularity you found unsettling is only half the wavefronts; the other half is husbandry.

---

The three seated figures against the bare wall in the calm bands are alive.

They are small-framed and long-handed, as you saw, with a wrist that locks straight so that the hand can be used as a blade or a scoop without any muscle spent on holding it rigid. No hair. The nostrils are high, narrow, and closable. The ears are there but the tympana calcified early — within a year of arrival, and this was expected, and was welcomed, and in their language the phrase for it is the phrase for coming of age. They are deaf as everything else here is deaf. They read the room the way the drifters read it, through the skin, and after long enough in a lattice that never moves they know the shape of the pressure the way you know a lit room: not as information but as furniture. They can feel a drifter cross a node forty paces off. They can feel where you are standing, and how badly.

They are the color and texture of old paper because they emptied themselves of water on purpose. This is the craft they came from — a people of the dry plain above, for whom the fundamental sin was evaporation, loss, the wet business of becoming other than what you were. Their embalmers were not embalmers. They were technicians of subtraction, and the great achievement of their tradition was that the subtraction could be performed on the living, slowly, over decades, by diet and by discipline and by the strategic surrender of organs that only water needs. What is left in the Hold does not rot because there is nothing in it to rot, and it does not need much, and what it needs the room provides: sulfides through the skin, and the note itself, which shakes them constantly and does work, and the work becomes structure, exactly as it does in the fronds. They are being fed by the same thing that feeds the garden. In the sense that matters they are vegetation that used to walk.

Their pulse is one beat in something like a quarter of an hour, and it is not entrained to the note, because the note has no beat to entrain to. Their eyes are half-lidded and the lids do move; a blink is a seasonal event. The one holding the pry-bar across its knees is holding it, not having dropped it. If you had a way to compare, you would find that the bar's position has changed by about a hand's breadth since the wall was laid, which was long enough ago that the surface above has lost the plain's old rivers and forgotten it had them.

They are not waiting. That is the thing to understand about them, and it is the hardest thing, and it is why the description of this room reads as a description of a tomb. They came from a lineage that went out into the basalt looking for rooms that were nearly changeless and finished the job — there are other rooms in the mass, smaller and worse, half-corrected, abandoned when it became clear their throats would flutter no matter what was stacked in front of them; apprentice work, and you can find it if you know what a bad room feels like. The Hold was the good one. They walled the throat and set the cairns and cut the baffle and killed the seventh, and when there was nothing left that could be improved they sat down in the silences and reached the state, and the state is not a preparation for anything. Their name for it is a verb. It is the room's name. *Hold* is not what the room does to the sound; it is what they are doing, and the room is simply the only place where it can be done without effort.

---

The fourth is walking.

You will have counted three because the fourth was seated when you looked, and looked seated, and will look seated again. But there are four sets of sand-drifts along the node-lines with heel-shaped gaps in them, and only three occupied, and the tools by the baffle recess are set down rather than abandoned — laid in order, blade, cord, hammer-stone, the way a person leaves a bench they intend to return to on the next pass.

One of them keeps the round. A step every day or so; a full circuit of the forty paces and the four antinodes and the crown lane and the bowl in something on the order of a human lifetime. It prunes the path margins. It moves the sand toward the cold end, because the cold end is where sand should be. It checks the wall, the three cairns, the grid of cups in the baffle, and finds them right, and at the end of each circuit it goes to the one wall panel that will take a mark, and scratches the glyph, which is not a word and not a number and means only *unchanged*, and then sits down for a while, and then gets up.

There are sixty marks. Nothing in this room weathers, so the sixtieth is exactly as fresh as the first, and there is no way to know from the wall whether the last circuit closed a century ago or is closing now, behind you, at the far end, in the dark green under the fronds, at the speed of a thing that has decided the question is not important.

It is correct.
