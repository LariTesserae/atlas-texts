## What You Will Look For First

You will want the herd to be the people. It is the obvious mistake and everyone makes it. Hundreds of millions of coordinated bodies breathing in drifting bands of phase looks exactly like a society from a distance, and the bellowers oblige the impression by being calm, enormous, and apparently deliberate. They are not deliberate. A bellower is a very good lung with legs and a rudimentary interest in lichen. Its social behaviour consists entirely of pressing against the animal next to it and being pressed back, which is also its entire education, its entire language, and its entire theory of the world.

Then you will want the filament to be the mind. Everyone does that too. It is wired, it ticks, it carries amperes, it goes everywhere. But you can follow a strand for eleven kilometers and find it doing the same idiot thing at both ends, and a network that never changes what it does is not thinking, it is only plumbing that happens to be electrical.

So you look up at the pale specks at the ceiling, or down into the tarns, and you find farmers with no interiority and larvae with no future, and after a week of this you arrive at the correct and slightly disappointing conclusion, which is that the Loft is an enormous machine run by nobody.

That conclusion is off by about one animal.

## The Seam

The phase bands do not fit.

They cannot. A pressure ripple crossing forty thousand square kilometers of herd is entrained locally, shoulder to shoulder, and local entrainment always drifts. Two bands meet along a line, and along that line one population is inhaling while the other is three, or eleven, or forty seconds along. The air between them stacks and rarefies against itself. It is the only turbulence on the plateau. Everywhere else the Loft's weather is a slow swell; here it chops.

You can see a seam from a ridge. The black sea has a crease in it. The exhalation seam above the herd, normally a single flat white lamina, splits into two laminae that shear past each other, and the flue trees along the line knock arrhythmically, out of the great rolling applause, like a man clearing his throat during a chord.

Seams matter because they widen. A four-second seam becomes a nine-second seam becomes a tear, and a tear is a stall: flues valving against each other, the chimney draft failing over a region, the lens locally thinning, and then the frost comes down into the mattress and stays. There are places on the Loft where this happened. They are called cold quarters, they are bare black rock with a rime of crust lichen, they are visible from the air as grey bruises in the quilt, and the youngest of them is eight hundred years old and will not be healed for twenty thousand more.

Along every seam, at intervals of a few hundred meters, riding in the wool between the second and third rib-bands on the windward flank of a bellower, there is a stitch.

## The Stitch

It is about two meters long, forty centimeters across, and six centimeters thick: a flat, limbless, tapered plate, like a sole cut for a very large shoe. It weighs thirty kilos and almost all of that is muscle and fluid.

It is white. Not pale — white, the dull unpigmented white of something under a stone, which on this plateau is an outrage. Every other living thing here has spent a hundred million years converting itself to black in order to catch photons, and the stitch has renounced the sun entirely, because it lives at thirty-four degrees inside a black woollen furnace and has no use for free heat it is already drowning in. Along each edge of its body runs a row of pits that can tell high season from low and nothing else, and by the age of fifty these are usually scarred shut. Functionally, and then literally, it is blind.

Its underside is ten thousand pads on retractable stalks. They do not grip skin. They grip individual fibers of wool, four or five to a pad, and the stitch moves by combing — releasing and re-gripping in travelling waves — at a top speed of about two meters an hour. Crossing a bellower takes it most of a day. Among the pads is a small filing mouth that takes a tithe of blood and, more importantly to its host, spends the rest of its time eating mites, hydroids, and the felt of parasites that would otherwise ruin a pelt. A bellower carrying a stitch is a bellower with a clean flank. This is the visible part of the arrangement, and it is not the arrangement.

Its top surface is a drumhead: a disc of mineralized cartilage about twenty centimeters across, ringed by a tensor muscle, so that the animal can tune itself over nearly an octave. Beneath it sits a knuckle of bone on a dedicated throwing muscle. One strike costs it about as much as a human sprint costs a human. A busy stitch strikes two hundred times a day.

And down its midline, from nose to tail, runs a single closed sinus of fluid lined at centimeter intervals with hair cells. This is the whole reason for the animal's shape. Two meters of fluid is an interferometer. A pressure front arriving obliquely reaches one end before the other, and a stitch reads that difference to something like a hundredth of a second, which at these densities and this geometry gives it the bearing of every ripple on the plateau to within a degree or two, and the lag of its own band against every band within about three hundred kilometers.

It has no eyes and no name for the sky. It knows the Loft as a field of arrival times.

## The Push

The calf learns phase by pressing against its mother and being pushed. That is the pedagogy the stitch inherited and the only lever it has.

Here is the work. The stitch lies along a rib-band with its pads locked into the wool. It reads the far band's front. It computes a lag. Then, at the correct instant in the ninety-second cycle — about nine seconds before the host's own intake would begin — it contracts its entire ventral musculature against the flank and *shoves*, a slow pressure like a man leaning on a door.

The bellower, which has been taught since birth to take its cue from pressure on the flank, starts a third of a second early.

That is all. One animal, one third of a second. But the bellower is pressed shoulder to shoulder with six others, and they are pressed against twelve more, and the correction spreads by contagion at roughly the rate the herd conducts, which is fifty animals wide in twenty strokes and a kilometer wide in a day. The stitch is not moving a continent. It is setting a boundary condition and letting the herd do the arithmetic.

The yield is about four seconds of phase per pressure year.

A serious seam is forty seconds wide. Do the division. A stitch takes a decade to close one line, and it does this in the dark, in the wool, blind, in ninety-second increments, without ever once perceiving the thing it is repairing — because if it could perceive the thing it is repairing, it would be standing outside the herd, and the moment it is outside the herd it is a thirty-kilo naked animal at four degrees above freezing with about forty minutes to live.

There are perhaps a million of them, along perhaps two hundred thousand kilometers of seam, in a world of hundreds of millions of bellowers. They are the rarest large animal on the plateau, they are the only reason the lens has held, and no other species on the Loft has ever noticed that they exist.

## The Split

A stitch does not have an age. It has a count.

When the sinus reports a lag under a tenth of a second for six hundred consecutive strokes, something in the animal is permitted to finish. It begins a fission that takes eleven months: a second drumhead buds forward of the first, a second sinus valves off along the midline, and then the two halves separate over the course of a night and comb away from each other along the flank in opposite directions. Neither is the parent. Neither is the child. There are two now where there was one, and each is smaller, and each will be carried by the herd's slow drift until it is somewhere with a lag in it, and then it will begin again.

Reproduction here is not a reward for surviving. It is a reward for *succeeding*, and it is the only mechanism in the Loft's entire industry in which finishing a job produces more of the thing that finished it.

Most stitches manage two divisions. A few manage five.

## The Unsplit

Some seams cannot close. A ridge line breaks the conduction. A gyre field throws its own hum into the herd and keeps a two-second error standing forever. A cold quarter sits in the middle of a plain like a hole in a drumhead, and the bands on either side of it will never agree, because there is nothing between them to press.

The stitches on a standing shear never finish. They never split. They keep growing instead — four meters, five, the drumhead broadening to the size of a dinner table, the tensor ring thickening until the animal can drop nearly two octaves below anything else on the plateau. They live three and four hundred years. They are the largest, the oldest, the loudest, the most precise, and by the only measure their own bodies recognize, they are the complete failures of their kind.

They are also the reference clocks. Everything else defers to them. This will make sense in a moment.

---

## The Tally

The stitches talk by hitting other animals.

A bellower is two-thirds lung inside an accordion of ribbed cartilage. It is a drum, and it is a very good one, and it is already resonating at the fundamental. A stitch strikes its own tensioned plate against the rib-band and the whole barn-sized body rings, and the ring carries — across the herd by body-to-body contact, and out into the air in a register that the flue forests pick up and knock along at the speed of sound. A stitch in the middle of a plain can be heard three hundred kilometers away, on someone else's flank, by someone else's midline.

Nothing here has ever made an instrument. Each of them plays a borrowed one, and the instrument does not know.

The vocabulary is two words. *How late.* *Which way.* That is the entire operational requirement of the job, and it would fit comfortably into about four percent of the available channel.

The other ninety-six percent is the tally.

What crosses the herds at night, in the long low knocking under the fundamental, is an account of corrections. Not current ones. Closed ones. Every stitch carries a recitation of seams that were healed — where the line ran, how wide it opened, how many years it took, who was lying on which flank when it shut — and it recites, and its neighbors recite, and the recitation is copied across every fission along with the sinus and the drumhead, so that nothing is ever lost by anyone's death, and everything is duplicated by everyone's success.

The oldest items in the tally describe seams in a herd standing on rock that has since been buried under nine meters of peat.

And because it is copied by budding and never written, it drifts. Two stitches meeting along a new shear will find, four or five hundred entries down, that their versions of the same three-hundred-thousand-year-old closure disagree — a bearing off by two degrees, a duration off by a season. So they stop. And they work it out, over months, striking and counter-striking through the ribs of animals grazing obliviously beneath them, until the variants are reconciled or one is abandoned. When they cannot settle it they send it up the line to an unsplit, and the unsplit on its standing shear, four meters wide and three centuries old and permanently unemployed, rules.

They are doing exactly the same operation they do on the herd. Two things that ought to be identical are not. The difference is intolerable. Reduce it.

They have no concept of a listener, no concept of an author, no concept of the plateau, and no concept of the lens whose continuance is the only reason any of this is possible. They are not preserving a heritage. They are just unable to leave a discrepancy alone, and the discrepancies happen to have been accumulating since before the basalt finished cooling.

## At the Rim Again

Go back and stand in the glare with the fog pouring off the escarpment and the dead blue world underneath it.

It is still true that there is not one made thing on the Loft. No nail, no lashing, no shaped stone. The plateau still manufactures its own room and spends the whole product on the rim and shows no surplus, and the arithmetic is still very clean.

But now, when the applause of the flue forest comes rolling over you and passes and goes on for two minutes into the distance, listen underneath it, below the fundamental, in the part you feel in the floor of your chest rather than hear. There is a slower knocking down there, irregular, conversational, coming off the herds in long exchanges.

That is the only surplus this world has ever produced. It is not an object and it never was. It is a transmission that has simply never been permitted to stop — kept by a million blind white animals living in other animals' fur, none of whom can survive ten minutes in the open air of the country they are holding up, arguing patiently, in a language of two words, about the exact width of something that closed before the mountain was cold.
