## The Census at First Light

Start with the count, because the count is what misleads you first.

Stand on the marsh and take the census honestly and you get a number close to zero. Four kilometres of ice, eleven hundred metres of wall, and nothing crossing any of it. But run your thumb into a grit-floored pocket in the ice at your feet and lift out a gram of the black stuff sitting in the bottom, and you are holding somewhere between one hundred million and one billion living cells. Both numbers are the truth. The valley is not empty and it is not populated. It is *sorted* — the same way the terminus sorts gravel from flour, life here has been sorted by the wind and the cold into films, wells, seams and pulses, with immense nothing in between.

So: a census by tenancy. Who cannot leave. Who leaves. Who is only passing through and never meant to stop. And — this is the largest category by weight — who arrives as food.

---

**What everything eats.**

There is essentially no local production. A little algae, a little cyanobacteria, a lichen crust growing a millimetre a decade: measured against the tonnage of rock leaving the valley every hour, the valley's own photosynthesis is a rounding error.

What feeds this place is import. The same monsoon airmass that gets stripped of its water on the way up is also carrying, unintentionally, the aerial plankton of four hundred kilometres of forest and grassland: aphids, thrips, leafhoppers, small flies, ballooning spiderlings still trailing their silk, moth scales, spores, and pollen in quantities you can taste. It rises with the afternoon convection, freezes at altitude, and comes down here as a slow continuous rain of cold-stunned protein — kilograms per hectare per year, delivered free, sifted onto the ice and into every crack and cranny of every wall.

This is the base of the food web: an unbroken hail of things that were on their way somewhere else and failed. Nobody in this valley makes a living. Everybody in this valley collects one.

---

**The wells.**

The only architecture in thirty kilometres is a few million holes, and every one of them dug itself.

A grain of that windblown dust lands on the ablation zone. It is dark; the ice is not. It absorbs light the ice reflects, warms a hair above zero, and sinks — melting a cylindrical shaft straight down beneath itself, a centimetre a day in good light, until it reaches the depth where the light gives out and the drilling stops. The result is a cryoconite hole: four centimetres across, eight deep, sheer walls of blue ice, a black floor of granular sediment, and a column of meltwater standing in it at a temperature indistinguishable from freezing.

That black floor is not dust any more. Cyanobacterial filaments have grown through it and bound the mineral grains into soft granules — the microbes make the soil that holds them, at a scale of a millimetre. Inside a single hole: filamentous cyanobacteria, green algae, a dozen kinds of bacteria doing chemistry at one per cent of a temperate rate, ciliates, amoebae, rotifers that reproduce without males and have been stealing genes from their food for eighty million years, nematodes, and tardigrades — most of them at any given moment folded into barrels, shed of their water, waiting, capable of waiting for decades.

At night an ice lid seals over the top. In the morning it thaws. The hole is a closed reactor with a diurnal door.

Millions of them, in ranks, following the dust lines down the glacier. It is the densest settlement in the range, and you can stand on the roof of the whole city and see nothing but grey ice with a rash of dark freckles on it.

---

**The pepper.**

In the meltwater grooves, at densities that reach a hundred thousand to the square metre, the springtails.

They are two millimetres long and slate-dark, and in aggregate they read as spilled pepper — a stain on the ice that shifts if you watch it. Their body fluids carry antifreeze glycolipids; their gut carries algae, pollen, and the finely divided remains of their own dead. They walk on the surface tension of meltwater films without breaking them. When alarmed they release a spring-loaded fork under the abdomen and go two hundred body lengths sideways at random, which is not an escape so much as a lottery.

Pick one up and hold it in your palm and it will die in under a minute. Not crushed — cooked. Your hand is thirty-seven degrees. Its lethal ceiling is around fifteen. It is a creature built for a two-degree window and it has never in its evolutionary history needed anything wider. It cannot leave. There is nowhere for it to go: down is fatal, and up is bare.

These are the valley's cattle. Everything with legs and appetite above five thousand metres is, in the end, eating springtails or eating the wind directly.

---

**The midge that gave up flying.**

Diamesa. A chironomid, dark, the size of a mosquito, with its wings reduced to useless straps, because a valley that moves air at sixty knots is not a place where a two-milligram insect should be airborne. So it walks. It walks on ice, in the open, at temperatures down to sixteen below — no animal on the planet is known to move at colder — and it walks in the middle of winter, which is when it mates, on the surface of the glacier, in the dark, with a wind chill that would take a human hand in ten minutes.

Its larvae live where nothing else lives: in the milk-coloured outflow, in water full of suspended rock flour that would abrade the gills off anything less committed, grazing biofilm from the undersides of cobbles in the first hundred metres below the ice. They are the only thing in that river. Downstream, where the water clears and warms by three degrees, they stop, and other things start.

---

**The highest permanent resident of the Earth.**

On the ledges at fifty-three hundred metres, in crevices no wider than a coin, there is a small pale jumping spider, five millimetres, greyish, unremarkable, and it is the top of a food chain that has no plants in it at all.

It has eight eyes, four of them very good, and it hunts by sight — which in this light is a problem, because there are no shadows here to give an outline and no contrast to give a distance, so it works close and slowly and mostly on things that are already half-dead with cold. It takes springtails. It takes the fallen aphids. It attaches a dragline before every leap, because a miss in this wind is not a fall, it is an export. It spends most of the year immobile in a rock fissure with its legs drawn in.

It has never encountered a leaf, a flower, a root, or soil. Its entire nutritional universe is: wind arrives, wind delivers, spider eats. Remove the forests four hundred kilometres downwind and it starves within a generation without ever learning why.

---

**The crusts and the insiders.**

On rock that came out from under the ice within the last century: black crustose lichen, in coins the size of a fingernail, growing at a millimetre a decade. You can age a boulder by them and be roughly right, which tells you how little else has happened.

Inside the limestone, a millimetre or two below the surface where translucent crystal still passes usable light, endolithic bacteria and fungi live in the pore water, secreting acid, prising grains apart from within. This is the beginning of soil. They will not finish. At this temperature and this rate of uplift, they are pushing a boulder up a hill that is itself rising four millimetres a year, and the whole business is scheduled to be complete somewhere past the ten-thousandth year, and there is no reason to think anyone involved is aware of the timeline.

---

**The only warm thing that isn't stone.**

Down at the valley mouth, where the moss thickens into something a hoof can crop, there are cushion plants: domes of tiny rosettes packed so tightly you can stand on one without breaking it. A cushion forty centimetres across is three hundred years old and contains, inside itself, all of its own dead — a slow black peat of its own previous decades, the only genuine soil in thirty kilometres, manufactured internally, retained internally, never released.

In sunshine the interior of a cushion runs twelve degrees above the air around it. Seeds of other species germinate in there. Insects overwinter in there. It is a stove, a nursery and a graveyard, and it is the single organism in the valley that has built anything.

---

**Who leaves.**

The geese are not residents. They are traffic. They come over the notch at seven thousand two hundred metres, in air with a third of the oxygen you'd get at a beach, and their hemoglobin has a single amino acid changed in the alpha chain that lets it hold oxygen a little tighter; their flight muscle is packed with capillaries and their mitochondria have crowded to the cell margins to shorten the diffusion path; they can hyperventilate tenfold without going alkalotic. They come at dawn and by night because cold air is dense air, and dense air is lift. They do not feed here, they do not land, they do not look. They are through the valley in ninety seconds and it is a doorway to them, nothing more, and it has been the same doorway for longer than the col has been that height.

The choughs are residents but part-time, and they are the only ones here with leisure. Thirty or forty of them, glossy, yellow-billed, working the buttress updraught in loops that serve no evident purpose — they close their wings and drop three hundred metres and come back up and do it again. They cache food in crevices and remember where. They understand what a rucksack is. They know which cliff a rockfall came off before the sound arrives. They will be at your camp in eleven minutes and they have followed climbers to eight thousand metres for the crumbs.

In a talus fan below the schist band: large-eared pikas, round, silent, tailless, living entirely in the interstices between blocks, and hauling cut vegetation into a dry crevice all summer to build a hay pile — the only creature here that stores a season. Nesting in the pikas' galleries and in a rockfall cavity above them, snowfinches, who took over the burrows rather than dig, and who line the cup with wool.

The wool comes off a blue sheep that died two winters ago and is still lying on the fan.
