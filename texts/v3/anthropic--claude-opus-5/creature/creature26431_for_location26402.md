## The Census, and What Is Wrong With It

The published list is short and everybody quotes it: two ravens, a fox, four species of caddis, wolf spiders in numbers, midges beyond counting, no bats, no songbirds, no sheep. Two site visits and a bat survey with no returns on it. The list is not wrong. What is wrong is that it is a list, when it should be a floor plan.

Because the walls of the trough do not move and the fan does not vary, the standing waves in here have held their positions for fifty years. The fundamental at thirty-eight hertz puts its nodal planes about four and a half metres apart across the trough — a fine invisible grating, useless on its own, because a node in the fundamental is still full of the blade-pass harmonics and will still make your teeth ring. But every so often, by accident of geometry, the node of the fundamental coincides with the nodes of the third and the fifth and the ninth and something above that, and in the small volume where all of them cancel at once, there is no sound.

There are perhaps forty such volumes in two kilometres. Some are the size of a bathtub. Two are the size of a bus. Four are underwater. Nine are in mid-air, between eight and thirty metres up, and are of no use to anything that cannot hover. Their walls are not walls; a step to the left puts you back in the roar. The fitters call them *sits*, because you can, and because they hurt less than everywhere else, and they can find them blind and in fog by the moment their molars go quiet.

Everything that lives in this trough lives in one of them, or does not need to.

## The Household on the Eastern Scarp

The ravens have the best address in the valley and have held it since roughly 2009.

Their ledge is eleven metres up the raw sandstone, under an overhang, and it sits in the largest of the airborne sits — a pocket some four metres by three, where a stone dropped from the rim above would fall out of silence into noise and back out again with no sound to mark any of it. The pair can hear each other in the nest. Nowhere else in the trough can two animals of the same species hear each other at all.

They are not silent birds. They are silent *here*. Watch them long enough and you see the routine: something needs to be discussed, and both birds go up — not spiralling, not displaying, just labouring straight up the face of the scarp and over the rim, where they land on a fence post on the plateau and talk, knocking and clucking and doing that liquid drop-note, for two or three minutes, and then come back down and get on with it. They leave home to speak. In the trough they use the body: the wing-flick, the bill-up, the deliberate slow walk toward and past, the head-bob that means the fox is out. The repertoire is larger than the plateau birds' and has been described, badly, twice.

Their sightlines are enormous. From the ledge they can see the whole floor, the fan house door, the truck cab, every pool. They do not need to hear a thing coming; nothing here comes silently in the visual sense, because the moss holds a print for a week and shows a track like a page. They have raised, at the low estimate, nineteen young. All of those young have left. There is no second territory in the trough worth having, and they know it.

## The Line Under the Slab

The forty metres of dropped tarmac lies on the moss at an angle, unbroken, and where its lower lip is propped on a concrete block there is a dry triangular space eight metres deep, floored in sand, out of the wet, and — this is the point — acoustically shadowed. Not a node. Just shelter: two hundred tonnes of road between the den and the evasée. Inside, the noise is a pressure rather than a scream. Cubs can be nursed there. Cubs cannot be nursed anywhere else in the trough that anyone has found.

There has been a vixen under that slab, continuously, for as long as the fitters' logbooks mention foxes, which is 1988. Not the same vixen. A matriline, presumably, or something functionally like one, because the place is a fixed and irreplaceable asset and the daughters fight for it.

They are the strangest animals here and the ones visitors remember. They do not startle. The startle reflex is acoustic and the noise floor has eaten it: you can walk up to a trough fox from behind and touch it, which people have done, and it turns round and looks at you with an expression of mild administrative interest. They hunt entirely by nose and eye and have become superb at both — they will stand in a foot of moss watching the surface for the movement of a shrew for eleven minutes at a stretch, absolutely rigid, in a way that plateau foxes never do because plateau foxes simply listen. They also know the sits. Follow a trough fox on a summer evening with binoculars and its route across five hundred metres of open moss looks drunk and is not: it goes sit to sit, forty seconds in one, a hundred metres of nothing, forty seconds in the next, standing there with its ears working. They are using them as listening posts. Nobody has published on this either. The fitters have known it for thirty years and assume everybody knows.

The cubs come out of the shadow of the slab at four weeks into a world with no sound in it and they are, by any normal measure, ruined. They are also the tamest wild foxes in the country. In 2014 one sat in the mess room doorway for the whole of a night shift and had to be shoved out with a boot at six.

## The Commons

Everything else in the trough owns the remaining four hundred hectares, because it has no use for hearing.

The wolf spiders are the true majority population and nobody counts them. Walk the mire at night with a headtorch and the eyeshine comes back at you in tens of thousands, a wet green gravel laid over the hummocks all the way to the birches. They hunt by sight and by the pit of the hummock top and are wholly indifferent to thirty-eight hertz; the vibration channel that a spider elsewhere uses to feel a moth land is, here, permanently saturated and simply not used, and the species that depend on it are absent. The result is a monoculture of eyes. It is one of the densest lycosid assemblages ever recorded in Britain and the paper it appeared in was about something else.

And then, in June, the midges make the architecture visible.

Male midges find females by wingbeat tone. They cannot do it in the roar. So they swarm in the sits — only in the sits, exclusively, obligately — and for about three weeks the whole invisible lattice of the trough stands rendered in insects: forty grey columns, some the width of a fist, two of them three metres through, hanging motionless in fixed positions over the moss and over the pools and, in the case of the airborne ones, hanging in open air twenty metres up with nothing beneath them, boiling gently, going nowhere. Walk the axis of the trough at dusk in the second week of June and you pass them like lit windows in a street. There is no other way to see a standing wave with the naked eye. It is on no leaflet.

## The One-Night Guests

Twice a year the sits fill up with birds.

Migrants come over the plateau in numbers and drop into the trough because it is a hole with water in it, and within minutes they have all found the same forty places, and they crowd. Two hundred and thirty redwings have been counted in a single sit the size of a garden shed, in the birches on the eastern shelf, packed shoulder to shoulder in a way redwings do not pack, in total silence — except that if you put your own head inside the volume you hear them, all of them, a low continuous scatter of contact notes at conversational volume, a room full of birds talking, and outside the volume, a metre away, nothing.

They do not stay. They are gone by nine in the morning and they do not come back through in spring. Whatever the trough offers them is not enough, and whatever it does to them they do not repeat. A ringing group worked the shelf for four autumns and had one single retrap in four years, which for redwings is remarkable, and drew no conclusions.

## The Interior

There is a mess room inside the fan house, behind a double door with a rubber seal, and it is the only place in two kilometres where a human being can hear a kettle.

It measures four metres by three, has a bench, a tin of teabags with a date on it, a wall of lockers, a stove, and a robin. There has been a robin in the mess room since at least 1991, though not the same robin. It comes in at the door, holds four cubic metres of double-glazed quiet against all comers, sings from the top of the lockers in October and in January and at four in the morning, is fed on crumbs and once on a whole scotch egg, and dies eventually and is replaced within a season, because a robin territory with a stove in it and no rivals within a kilometre is the best offer in the county. Its song is audible through the seal for about a foot and a half.

The fitters are the other resident species and are shaped like the rest. Six of them, on rota, some of them thirty years in. Their faces have gone extraordinary — everything has migrated upward into the eyes and the eyebrows, because for eight hours the mouth is a decoration. They sign; they carry slates; they use the lamp code. Two of them are substantially deaf and one has been for twenty years and still comes, and reads the fan by putting a hand flat on the brickwork, and can tell you a bearing is going before the monitoring does. Above ground, off shift, they are known in the village for being quiet men who stand too close.

## The Largest Thing Here

And under all of it, holding the truck and the sleepers and the anchor blocks and the walls of the fox's den, ninety centimetres deep and ten hectares across, is the only inhabitant that does not have an address, because it *is* the address.

The mire is one body, or as near to one as makes no difference: mostly water, no organs, no senses, ninety centimetres of its own ancestors underneath it, gaining a millimetre a year. It cannot hear and has nothing to hear with and is nonetheless the animal in this valley most precisely dependent on the machine, because it drinks the exhalation of eleven kilometres of roadway, at eleven degrees, at ninety-six per cent, forever. It is the pet. It grew here in the way frost grows on a window, following a temperature it did not choose, and it will keep growing for as long as somebody in an office two counties away signs off a maintenance budget.

It grows taller in the sits. Two to five centimetres taller, measurably, in every one of the forty, in a clean sharp-edged patch you can see from the rim once you know to look — forty small bright bosses of higher moss standing up out of the lawn like the risen crumb of a loaf. The best guess is that still air holds a different boundary layer and the condensate behaves differently; the second-best guess is that the moss is simply not being shaken. Nobody has published on it. The fitters use them as waymarks in fog, because the moss shows them where their teeth will stop, and they trust the moss more than the map.

## Changeover

Twenty minutes in the small hours of a night in May, and the tenancies all fail at once.

The walls come down. The forty rooms and the shadow under the slab and the four cubic metres above the lockers stop being different from anywhere else, and the whole trough is one volume, and for the first time in a year everything in it can hear everything else in it. The ravens go off the ledge in the dark, which they never otherwise do. The vixen comes out to the mouth of the den and stands. Cubs that have been alive six weeks in a world with no sound in it hear water coming off the birches four hundred metres away, and a pool draining down a crack, and the moss creaking as it takes back its shape, and one bird on the rim, and there is no record of what that does to them because nobody has ever been able to see it.

Then it comes back.

But not identically. New bearings, new blades, a fraction of a hertz either way, and the lattice shifts — half a metre, sometimes two, sometimes north, sometimes not at all. And the fortnight after changeover is the busiest fortnight of the year in the trough: the midges relocate their columns inside a day; the fox works out her new listening posts in three; the higher bosses of moss begin, over four or five seasons, to die back on one side and rise on the other, so that the old sits are legible in the lawn for a decade afterwards like the footings of demolished houses.

In 2003 the ravens lost their ledge. The pocket moved a metre and a half out into open air and the nest was left in the noise, and they abandoned it in the third week of May with two eggs in it, and did not breed that year, and the following spring built four metres further along, in the place where the quiet had gone.
