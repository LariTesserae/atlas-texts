## The Tenant of the Pump-House

Begin at the handrail, because everything about it begins at a worn place.

On the iron stair that climbs from the mill floor to the second catwalk, the crust is two fingers thick on every baluster but four. On those four it is the thickness of a fingernail, and smooth, and warm-brown rather than dried-blood brown, and it has the faint fine grain of something polished rather than something eroded. It is not broken. That is the thing to understand first. Four hundred years of a hand closing on the same four balusters, and the crust has been kept thin and kept whole, and underneath it the iron has never once seen water.

There are one hundred and ninety such places in Vess. They map a route.

---

**The route.**

It is a circuit, and it is not efficient. It leaves the pump-house door, crosses the mill floor along the north edge of the powder slope, climbs, runs the second catwalk to the timber gallery, does not use the timber gallery, descends again by the outside of the west tenement, and so on — a long looping thing that touches every one of the fifty-one lamps and comes back. Walked at the pace it is walked, it takes eleven days. The route has not changed in the whole period of record, except that in the northwest it now goes around a place where a lintel came down in some year nobody has, and the detour is old enough to have its own polish.

You can also map it by the stoneworts. There are forty stands in the warm outflow where you would expect them. There are six more that have no business existing: one in the pump-house doorway, one on the second catwalk at the head of the stair, one on the west terrace, three at points along the route that correspond to nothing in particular. They are smaller than the others and they lean, all of them, toward a spot about half a meter across where nothing grows at all.

Something stands in those places long enough, often enough, to warm the water around it.

---

**The body.**

You will never see it entire. A lamp lights four meters and it is longer than that, and it does not come to the middle of the light.

What crosses, in pieces, over the course of a forty-minute visit: a foot, very broad, very heavy, set down flat and set down completely, so that the silt rises in a ring no higher than your ankle and settles again within a minute. A shin, ferric-crusted, dune-smoothed, knobbed at the front with the same blunt coral growth that the bollards have. A hand — and the hand is the wrong thing, the hand is the thing that makes people leave early. Everything else about it moves at the tempo of the town. The hands do not. The hands are quick.

The torso is a sealed volume about the size of a rain-barrel and it is the reason for everything else. Water goes in low and comes out high and comes out warm, and inside there is a filament, and the filament is lit.

Fifty-one lamps burn in Vess in fixed housings. There is a fifty-second, and it walks.

You cannot see it directly; the crust has closed over whatever glass there was. But at close range, in the dark between lamps, the whole chest reads as a dull ember through two centimeters of iron oxide, the colour of a stove door, and the silt in the column above it drifts upward in a thin steady thread, and if you put your hand in that thread it is eight degrees, and after thirty-five minutes in one-degree water, eight degrees is an event you will think about for some time afterward.

It is not shaped like a person. It is shaped like the silhouette a person makes when they are carrying something heavy in both arms and have been carrying it a while: narrow, stooped, arms forward and low. It has too many joints in the arms, and the extra ones are not original — they are places where the crust cracked, and re-formed, and cracked again in the same line, until the line became a hinge. It has been repaired by the same process that repairs the town, which is to say by waiting.

---

**The step.**

It steps on the cycle.

One step per forty seconds, on the pressure, the same beat the wheel keeps and the same beat your pulse gives in to after an hour. Ninety steps an hour. A crossing of the basin floor, waterline to waterline, takes a little under five hours, which is exactly one circulation, so that it and the warm water arrive at the far wall together, every time, and this is either an extraordinary fact or a very ordinary one depending on which way you think the causation runs.

In a single visit you can watch it cross an alley. That is all. Sixty steps, and you are out of time and out of hands, and you go up through the moon-hole and it is still in the alley, and when you come back in three days it is somewhere on the second catwalk and the alley silt has healed.

---

**The work.**

It changes lamps. A change takes it most of a day: the climb, the housing, the old filament unseated and carried — carried, not dropped — down to the pump-house and racked. The spent ones are racked too. Nobody knows why. One rack fills as the other empties and the sum has been constant since anyone thought to count.

That is the arithmetic, and it is not difficult, and everyone who spends time in Vess does it eventually and then wishes they hadn't. Three a decade. The rack was surveyed in a year, and surveyed again forty-one years later, and the difference was twelve. From the second count you can get the date of the last lamp. It is not soon. It is not so far off as to be meaningless. It is roughly the distance between us and the invention of the printing press, and it is fixed, and the thing in the pump-house has evidently known it for a very long time, because three a decade is not a maintenance schedule. Fifty-one lamps do not fail at three a decade. Three a decade is rationing.

Beyond the lamps: it trues the counterweights, which is why they are still level. It restores the angle of repose on the powder slope after a diver has spoiled it, and it does this within a day, and it does it by hand, and the restored slope is measurably more consistent than the natural one. It sets fallen slates upright in the silt, edge-on, every one of them, all over the mill floor — several hundred now — and they are not arranged in any pattern that survives analysis. The only interpretation the evidence supports is that it prefers vertical things vertical.

And it closes crust. If you open a patch of iron, it will come. Not quickly; it has no quickly. But within its eleven days it will arrive, and it will pack the wound with a ferric paste it produces or carries or scrapes from somewhere, and the patch will be rough and young and the wrong colour, and it will be four hundred years before it matches. There are two hundred and six such patches in Vess. Two hundred and six times, somebody with a hammer, and two hundred and six times, this.

It does not follow the person. It follows the damage.

---

**Restraint.**

This is the principle it shares with everything else here, and it is worth setting out, because it is the closest thing Vess has to a law.

The amphipod eats the outer ring of the algae and never the center, and the rings regrow, and the arrangement holds. The tenant keeps the handrail crust thin and never breaks it. It takes three lamps a decade from a rack it cannot refill. It walks the same hundred and ninety hand-holds so the wear is concentrated in places already spent. It does not use the timber gallery, and has not for however long the gallery has been pulp, because the gallery would not survive it and it knows this.

Nothing in Vess takes the middle of anything.

---

**Company.**

The colourless fish will not cross a lit sphere and will not cross the tenant either; they hold in the alley shadow and let it by, and it has never been observed to take one, and whether that is restraint or disinterest is not determinable.

The amphipod grazes it. This is well attested and still difficult to watch. When the tenant stands — at the stair head, at the pump-house door, in one of the six anomalous stonewort places — the algal skin grows on its shoulders and back exactly as it grows on brickwork, in rings, brightest at the center. Every so often the big translucent grazer comes down the wall at its walking pace and works the shoulders for twenty minutes and takes the outer ring only, and the tenant does not move, and then the grazer leaves, and then, some minutes later, the tenant takes its next step.

The isopods do not come up. Nothing in Vess comes up.

---

**The second shape.**

On the pump-house floor there are two worn places, side by side, half a meter apart, each the size and shape of a pair of broad flat feet standing.

One of them is polished to the same warm brown as the balusters and is still being polished. The other stopped. You can tell it stopped because the silt has taken it, and because the crust has closed over its edges, and because the depth of the wear is perhaps a third of the other's. It stopped early. It stopped a very long time ago and it has been left exactly where it is, and the surviving wear-shape has never spread into the space beside it, though there is nothing to prevent that and it would be a more natural stance.

There is a stencil on the pump-house door. Four letters remain of what were probably more. The mill's surviving payroll lists, for the night shift, one banker: the man who keeps the fire low and alive when the works are idle and there is nothing to make. One. Singular. Whoever made the second shape was never on the books.

On the west terrace, in what was a yard, there is a low dark cushion of something two meters across, unlike anything else growing in the basin, and it has been measured on four occasions across sixty years, and it is not getting bigger. Two meters is about the length of the tenant lying down. The route passes it. The route did not need to pass it. There is a stonewort there, leaning toward a bare place beside the mat, and standing beside a thing is the only act in the tenant's entire repertoire that has no maintenance function whatsoever.

That is as far as the evidence goes and it is further than the evidence ought to be taken.

---

**What it is not doing.**

It is not guarding. It ignores you entirely unless you break something.

It is not waiting, any more than the town is. Nothing arrives here; the loop closes; the powder spills; the ice groans once or twice a day and the sound comes down softened, like something remembered.

But it is not settled either, and it is the one thing in Vess that isn't, because it is spending. Fifty-one lamps, a rack that was fuller, three a decade, a date. Everything else here has arrived at its condition. This has a budget, and has had a very long time to think about the budget, and has arranged the whole of its enormous slowness around the far end of it — the eleven-day circuit, the outer ring only, the crust kept thin and whole, the step timed to the breath of the water so that no motion is ever wasted and no silt is ever raised that does not settle in a minute.

The last thing to say is what it does when you are in the way.

The alleys are the width of a wheelbarrow and the route runs through them, and sooner or later you will be in one, hanging in the black with your hands going bad, and it will come. It does not stop. It does not signal. Forty seconds before it reaches you it puts a hand on the wall and turns, one step, into a doorway — the doorframes here are all rounded like cave mouths, and there is one doorway on each of the narrow stretches with the crust worn thin at shoulder height, worn there by exactly this — and it stands in the doorway with the ember showing dull through its chest, and it waits, and the thread of warm silt goes up past your mask, and you pass.

It has been standing aside for people for four hundred years. It is very good at it.
