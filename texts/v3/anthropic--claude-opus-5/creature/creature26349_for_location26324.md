# The Ones on the Lid

## 1. Bodies

Start with the chest, because that is what you notice first: barrel-deep, wide at the base, the ribs flaring like the mouth of a jar. Four kilometers of thin air will do that in a few thousand years. Their blood is thick and dark and slow to clot; a cut on the flats weeps for an hour. Their skin is the deep grey-brown of old wet stone, because there is no ozone worth mentioning up here and the light comes at you four times a minute, pearl, glare, slate, pearl.

Their hands are enormous. Not thick — long. Fingers like the segments of a flutewood shoot, with a range of separate motion that looks, to an outsider, faintly obscene, as though each digit had its own opinion. A child of six can hold the little finger still while rotating the ring finger through a quarter turn. This is not a trick. It is a phoneme.

Their faces are the second thing you notice, and then you cannot stop noticing them. Eyebrow, lip, chin, cheek, the tilt and hold of the head: half the grammar lives there. A person of that plateau, at rest, wears an expression of such perfect neutral stillness that you would think them sedated, and then they begin to speak and the face goes off like weather.

Their feet are wide, splayed, horned at the heel, and exquisitely sensitive across the arch, because the pavement carries a hammer-strike thirty meters and the sponge-mat sighs under a footfall and both of these are information.

Their ears are stopped with wax.

## 2. Wax

The grub that lives in the joints of the flutewood — bitter, the color of tallow, eaten raw and eaten constantly — plugs its own gallery with a soft grey wax against the updraft. That wax, kneaded with combed beard-filament and a little dust, goes into a human ear at the age of about one month and comes out only for cause.

So: the same animal feeds them and deafens them. There is a sign for this animal that also means *both-hands-giving*, and it is one of the few signs in the language that is unambiguously affectionate.

Before the plugs, an infant hears the roar raw. One month of it, everything, the full weight, the flutewood shrieking, the *woom* in the long bones that are still cartilage. There is a belief — not stated as belief, merely acted upon — that this month is where the person actually comes from, and that everything afterward is commentary. When a child is later carved a name, the carver's expression is the one you would wear translating a poem out of a language you only half know.

Perhaps a third of them go properly deaf anyway, from the pressure, in their twenties or thirties. It is noted the way you would note that someone's hair has gone grey. There is no word for it that carries any weight. The deaf ones tend to be better at distance-signing, because they are not distracted.

## 3. Hands

The language is large. It is made large deliberately — designed, over an unknown number of generations, to be legible across a hundred meters of garden clearing in light that changes four times a minute.

The signs are held longer than seems necessary. The arms swing wide of the body. Contrast is grammar: a signer will move, mid-sentence, three steps to the left so that their hands fall against the white of a cloud-pillar rather than the glare of the pavement, and this is done unconsciously, and to fail to do it — to make someone read your hands against a low sun — is the plateau's characteristic small rudeness, the equivalent of muttering.

There is a second dialect for fog and for dark: hand laid into hand, or two fingers walking the inside of a forearm. Compressed, slow, intimate, with a vocabulary about one-fifth the size and a wholly different register of humor. Couples who have slept back to back in a spiral windbreak for forty years conduct entire arguments on each other's shoulder blades without turning over.
