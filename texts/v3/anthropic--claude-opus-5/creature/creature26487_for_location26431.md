**What the bowl holds**

Begin with the budget, because the budget decides everyone.

The seep delivers a fixed and unremarkable amount of chemistry per year. The mat converts it at the efficiency available to organisms working without light, which is poor. Everything downstream of that — every frog, every moth, the one large animal — is spending the same small allowance. If you gathered all the animal flesh in the bowl into a heap, it would not fill a rucksack. Two hundred paces across, sixty deep, ten thousand years of stable operation, and the whole assembled fauna weighs less than the person walking through it.

So expect nothing crowded, nothing quick, nothing young. Everyone here is old, thin, slow, and extremely good at one thing.

---

**The floor**

The mat is the resident. Everything else is a guest of the mat, including the sound.

Whether it is one organism is not a question the bowl will answer. It has no seams. Cut a plug from the peat two hundred paces apart and the tissue matches; the root-braid is continuous under both; the fluted columns rise out of the braid and the wall-curtains hang down into it and they meet, and nothing about the join suggests two parties. Call it a body, then, sixty metres across and two hands deep, which has been the same body since before the ridges came down.

It is a body with no urgency and total coverage. It feels the entire floor at once. Kneel and it registers your knee — not as alarm, it has no speed for alarm, but as a change in pressure and a change in warmth that it will still be adjusting to when you are back down the mountain. The rosettes are the fast part, and even they are only fast by local standards: touch one and it folds over about a second and a half, with a small dry *tock*, like tissue snapping over a cavity.

The tock is the mat's only utterance and it is probably an accident of the mechanism. It has nonetheless become a public announcement. A rosette closes on a moth; the tock says *a moth was here*; and everything that eats moths turns toward it. The mat feeds its own pollinators to its own predators and has never adjusted this. Ten thousand years is long enough to fix a thing like that and it has not been fixed, which suggests it isn't broken.

**The moths**

Thumbnail to hand-span, furred, no eyes at all — the front of the head carries instead a shallow fan of pits, wet-looking, exquisitely thermal. They are reading warmth at a resolution you would call sight if you were being fair.

They are the only working eyes in the bowl and what they see is a shopfront district. Flowering columns broadcast: a warm ring, a warm spot offset in the ring, a slow pulse at four or five seconds. The grammar is old and stable and the moths have it by inheritance. Your hand, at thirty-seven degrees, is a garbled advertisement of extraordinary brightness, which is why they land on your knuckles and stay there, working the skin, finding nothing.

They mostly walk. Flight is expensive and the air is empty; a moth may spend its whole adult life within six metres of where it eclosed, crossing the mat on foot, climbing the curtains. They cannot leave. Two metres above the rim the temperature ends them, and even if it didn't, the plateau is illegible: outside there is only freezing rock and, sometimes, a sun, and no sentences anywhere between. The bowl is an island the way a deep-sea vent is an island. Nothing here has relatives.

Their larvae eat the mat. The mat feeds the children of its pollinators, which is a bad deal, stably held.

**The frogs**

Forty-odd, at a guess. Palm-sized, skin nearly clear, viscera visible if there were any light to see them by. Each frog's head runs a degree or two warmer than its body, in a pattern of blotches unique to the individual — a face, in the local sense, and the only way frogs identify one another when close.

They do not identify one another when far. That is done through the reservoir.

A frog calls a few hundred times a year, always aimed at the wall, never at another animal. The note goes into circulation and stays. What a female listens to, over weeks, is not a chorus but a bank: a smear in which the same signature recurs at every degradation, five years deep, forty years deep, and she is reading persistence. A male who has been here through two seep-cycles is written into the walls thousands of times. A young male is a whisper laid over him.

The flaw is obvious and unfixable. The bank does not clear its dead. She will choose a signature, follow it — and there is no direction in a smear, so following means covering ground, testing warmth, for a season or more — and arrive at a patch of mat where the singer has been gone for sixty years. She waits there. This is not rare; it is the ordinary outcome. The bowl's courtship is conducted almost entirely with the deceased, and the population is stable anyway, because the few pairings that do happen are enough at this metabolic rate.

Eggs go into the post-sockets. Twelve were cut; four hold standing posts, four hold broken stubs, four are open stone cups lined with the manufactured skin. Water collects in them, and water in a socket is a hole in the world: no warmth comes off it, nothing reads it, it is not there. A clutch laid in a lined socket is invisible to everything in the bowl that hunts. The tadpoles graze the film on the stone rim above the lining — nothing grows on the skin itself — and most starve, and a few do not, and every three or four years the bowl goes from forty-one frogs to forty-three.

Somebody, an extremely long time ago, built a light-swallowing instrument on a mountaintop. Its last function is that of a nursery, on the grounds of being blank.

**The listeners**

The clicks belong to something long-legged and soft. No shell worth the calcium; a pale segmented body the size of two fingers, held up on eight limbs of thread, each joint drum-tight with membrane. They stand still for days.

They do not listen for sound. Sound here is a constant, a bath, arriving from every direction with no information in it whatsoever — the reservoir is the local equivalent of a uniformly grey sky. What a listener attends to is subtraction. A body passing between it and the wall removes a sliver of that constancy, and the notch of silence has an edge, a size, a speed. They hunt shadows in an unvarying noise.

And they improve their conditions. A listener clicks, steadily, into its own patch — not at rivals, not at mates; it is filling its ten square metres with a denser, sharper deposit than the ambient, so that anything crossing casts a crisper shadow. It is making the only lighting in the bowl, out of nothing but noise and patience, and refreshing it daily, and dying inside its small bright room.

Two listeners with overlapping rooms produce a beat pattern that both find intolerable and both retreat from. Since the rooms persist for years after their builders die, a listener may spend an entire life avoiding a neighbour that does not exist.

**The one that goes round**

There is one. There has been one for as long as there has been a bowl, and it is never two except for a short while in the middle.

Two metres from nose to tail-root, low-slung, four short heavy limbs, hairless except for a wet nap that carries its own lawn of the mat's founding bacteria; it is, in strict terms, a piece of the floor that has learned to travel. Where eyes were, skin. The whole face is a fan of thermal pits and it holds that face against the peat when it feeds, shearing the top centimetre with a flat plate of teeth. You feel it before you hear it, as a slow compression travelling through the mat, and if you put your palm flat you can feel it chewing.

It moves perhaps a hundred and fifty metres a year, always the same way round the inner wall. A circuit takes it thirty to forty years, which is exactly how long the mat needs to come back to grazing depth. It is not clever about this. It does not need to be. It navigates by the reservoir, and the way it does that is the finest thing in the bowl.

Its whistle — the long descending note you heard, and decided correctly belonged to something large — drops in pitch across its life. High and thin in the calf; a groan at the end. It calls every few hours, and every call stays in the walls.
