## The Unit of Measure

Go back to the terraces and look at the lips again. *Two hands high.* Everyone who writes about this place uses that measure and nobody asks whose hands, because the answer arrives so easily — yours, the reader's, a human span, a convenient rule of thumb. It isn't. The figure is older than that and it was arrived at honestly. Two hands high is the height at which a rim of sinter, set on a slope of this angle in water of this chemistry, stops leaking when a set of palms is laid along it and held there while the mineral does its work. Above that height it slumps. Below it, it overtops. Two hands is simply the correct answer, and it was found the way all the answers here were found, by everything else being tried first.

The hands are not yours. They are wider than yours, six-fingered, with a hard flanged edge running from the little finger to the wrist like the edge of a trowel, and their owner is standing about forty meters away from you right now, motionless, in vapor, with its back to you, doing nothing that you would recognize as anything.

## What You See First

Not the animal. What you see first is a discarded rind: a pale hollow shell lying in the shallows of a warm basin, curved, cracked open along one side, weighing almost nothing, so thin the light comes through it orange. It looks like the mold of something rather than the thing itself. It is. There are thousands of them scattered down the staircase and worked into the terrace walls, and the walls are partly made of them, the way a limestone cliff is partly made of shells.

Then you see the animal, and you have probably already looked at three of them without registering it, because at rest they are the same color and the same posture as a wet slump of sinter and they do not fidget. Nothing here fidgets. Fidgeting is a way of shedding heat and up here that problem is already solved.

Waist-high, if it stood, which it rarely bothers to. Longer than tall. A barrel of a chest — vast, the whole front third of the body given over to lungs, because four thousand meters is four thousand meters no matter how warm it is — tapering back to short, dense, powerful hindquarters made for gripping wet glass. The head is small and low and almost all nostril, two valved slits that close entirely underwater, and the eyes sit forward and deep, clouded by middle age and not much missed. The ears are gone. Where ears would be there are two smooth plates of bone that lie against the skull and conduct.

And it walks on the heels of its hands, fingers curled up and held clear of the grit, the way a scrubbed surgeon walks from the sink to the table.

## The Rind

They are naked, and the skin beneath the rind is thin enough to read blood through. In a landscape where every surface is warm, an animal's whole thermal problem runs backward: nothing needs holding in, everything needs getting rid of. So they carry radiators. A double membrane runs from the angle of the jaw down each flank, folded flat in the cool of the upper terraces and opened in the heat below like a book laid face-down, veined, translucent, held out into whatever draft of vapor is going. An overheated one lies in the steam with its fans spread and its head turned into the wind and looks, from the rim, like something that has been broken open.

The water plates them. That is simply what this water does to anything that holds still in it — it plated the terraces, it plated the chimney, and it plates them, laying down a matte rind of silica a molecule at a time, thickest on the back and shoulders, cracking at every joint. They shed it in pieces, about twice a year, and they cannot do it alone; the seams along the spine are out of reach. So the fundamental social act of this species is two of them sitting in warm shallows for an hour, one picking the cracked plating off the other's back with the flanged edge of a hand, slowly, with a faint sound like someone breaking thin ice in a cup. That is their affection. That is nearly all of it. They have no grooming beyond it and no embrace.

The rind takes color from wherever they spend their time. Bacterial films stain it. A young one who works the middle stair is olive down the back; an old one who has spent forty years in the seventy-degree pools is stained a hard chemical orange from crown to hip; the very old go pale again. You can read an individual's entire working life off its shoulders at a glance, and they do, and it means to them roughly what an accent means.

## The Work

They do not build the terraces. It is important to be exact here, because the temptation is enormous and it is wrong. The terraces build themselves; the geometry is physics and remains physics. What these animals do is smaller and stranger than construction.

They stop leaks.

A sinter lip fails at a point, and the water begins to go through rather than over, and where water goes through a narrow gap under pressure it makes a sound — a thin, continuous, tuneless hiss, the specific frequency depending on the width of the breach. That sound is unbearable to them. Not dangerous, not alarming: *unbearable*, the way a dripping tap at two in the morning is unbearable, an itch located outside the body. And so one of them goes and puts its hands on the place and holds them there, usually for twenty minutes or so, while its own dissolved load and the water's precipitate together into a seal, and the hiss climbs and thins and stops.

There is no plan. Nobody surveys. There is only a population of several hundred animals distributed across three kilometers of staircase, each of them permanently annoyed by a different set of small noises, each of them going to the nearest one. Multiply that by two hundred generations and you get a hydraulic system in excellent repair, maintained to a tolerance no engineer specified, by a workforce that has never once considered the system as a whole and would not know what you meant.

This is why the caldera is quiet. Not the thin air. Not the moss. There is a crew.

## Talking Through the Floor

Sound goes nowhere in this atmosphere. Shout and it dies inside a hundred meters, flat and close. So they gave up on air a very long time ago, and every adult carries a stone.

One stone. A river-smoothed nodule of dense old basalt from the swallowed ridge, about the size of a plum, carried in a loose fold of skin under the jaw and taken out perhaps two hundred times a day. They strike the sinter with it. The staircase conducts — through the glassy rims, through the linked columns of water, through the saturated floor — and a rap delivered at the top basin arrives at the marsh three and a half seconds later, softened, with its high frequencies stripped.

The code is percussive and not large: figures for *come*, *don't*, *here*, *hot*, *broken*, *the child*, *the bird*, *enough*. What makes it remarkable is not the vocabulary. It is that the message cannot be separated from its medium. Every figure they tap arrives at every listener having passed through the entire plumbing system, colored by every channel and blockage and thinning wall on the way, so that a single word spoken at the head of the stair is also, and simultaneously, a complete diagnostic report on four hundred basins. They do not have a word for the difference between *saying something* and *checking the pipes*. There is no difference. They have been talking to each other for fifteen thousand years and the conversation has been, in its entirety, a continuous read-out of the state of the mountain, and it is genuinely unclear — to me, standing on the rim with binoculars, and I suspect to them — whether they are using the plumbing to speak or using speech to listen to the plumbing.

Each adult owns exactly one thing. It is the stone.

## The Nursery Stair

They are born at forty-one degrees and they cannot survive a change of more than about two degrees a week.

So the young are moved. A mother, or as often some unrelated adult who simply took the job, carries an infant one basin at a time up the staircase over a period of four or five months, waiting out the acclimatization at each step, sitting in the shallows doing nothing for six or eight days at a stretch while a small blind thing adjusts its blood to the next increment of heat. The route is not fixed but the pace is, and at any given time there are twenty or thirty of these slow ascending pairs distributed up the northern arc like beads on a wire, weeks apart, in no hurry, nodding in the steam.

They do it in silence, mostly. The stone stays under the jaw. A carrier taps only *here* and *enough* and, if the infant is failing, nothing at all.

## Eleven Minutes

Four and a half hours, and then the big vent goes.

They do not flee it and they do not worship it. What they do is stop. Every one of them, wherever it is, three kilometers of staircase and the marsh below and the sulfur beds east — they put the stone down, open their fans, and hold still for the whole eleven minutes, facing whatever direction they happened to be facing. It is the one sound in this world that they have never been able to stop and cannot stand to hear and will not leave. They sit in it like people sitting out a siren.

Then it ends, and the terraces flush hot for a quarter of an hour, and the flush changes the note of every leak in the system at once, and when the water settles the tapping starts up again all down the stair — dozens of them at once, checking, comparing, each report arriving at every ear, the whole apparatus sounding itself out after the shock. This is the busiest they ever get. It happens five times a day and has happened, at that interval, since before the plain below had grass.

## The White Basins

At the head of the staircase the water leaves the ground at eighty-six degrees and boils immediately, and the basins there are dead white, scoured, lethal, lined with nothing but glass.

That is where they go.

An old one — half its rind fused solid, fans stiff, most of a working life spent in the orange — stops answering, and climbs, against its own comfort, past the last temperature it can tolerate, and lies down in the glare. The plating starts within days. Within a season there is a shape under the white, and within a decade there is no shape, only the same blinding correct geometry as everywhere else, two hands high, growing at a couple of centimeters a year. The purest and emptiest part of the whole apparatus, the part that looks scrubbed of life, is the part that is made of them.

The living do not go up there. But at the edge of the white there is a flat pan of sinter perhaps thirty meters across, and it is covered — thousands upon thousands, in drifts, some so old they have been plated over and are visible only as knuckles under the glaze — with small smooth basalt stones. Nothing about the place indicates ceremony. They walk up, put the stone down, and walk off. The soarers know the pan. They circle lower over it. They have learned, in the way a bird learns, the specific sound of a striking stone set down and not picked up again.

## The Rim

Once, every one of them climbs to the rim.

Not for a view — they can barely see. Not to leave; nothing leaves. They go up through the shrub margin and the shear-stunted trees into the cold, where the vapor is going out in its slow white river, and they stand there for a few minutes with their rinds contracting and their fans locked flat and the air suddenly too thin to drink, and then they come back down.

I used to think this was a rite, and then I watched one do it and understood, and it is worse than a rite. At the rim, the ground stops conducting. The staircase is behind you and below you and its whole long braided signal — the taps, the flushes, the four hundred basins reporting themselves continuously into the bone plates at the side of your skull, the background murmur you have been inside of since you were carried up the stair at two degrees a week — simply ends. They spend their entire lives enforcing quiet, patching every stray hiss, tuning twelve kilometers of mountain down toward a legible hush. And the thing at the top of the mountain, the thing they go up once to touch and never again, is what that hush would sound like if it ever finished.

They come back down within the hour. They do not go further. They have never gone further.

---

Stand on the rim at midafternoon, when the birds are all singing at once and the geyser is building and the vapor is going out through the western notch, and you will have the strong impression that somewhere below you a factory is running its shift.

There is no factory. There is only water, going around.

But there is a shift. There has always been a shift. Several hundred of them, stained orange and olive by the temperatures they prefer, walking on the heels of their hands so as not to spoil the fingers, each carrying one stone, none of them aware that there is a mountain — only that there is a hiss at the fourth basin, and that it has gone on long enough, and that someone ought to go and put a hand on it.
