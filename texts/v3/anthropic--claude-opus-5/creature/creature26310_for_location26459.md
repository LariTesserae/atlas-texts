# What Lives in the Sweating Notch

## The honest answer, before the interesting one

Nothing lives here the way things live in a valley — nothing with a den, a nest, a returning-to. There is no burrow because there is no soil to burrow into, only rock with a wet green pelt on it. There is no hollow tree because the tallest tree is knee-high and its trunk is a fibrous stalk you could cut with a thumbnail. The one bed-shaped thing in the notch is a stack of galvanised plate stock going green under moss, and nobody has ever slept on it, though several have lain down on it and looked up into the cloud being made and said afterwards that they wished they hadn't.

So the resident population divides, cleanly and strangely, into two migrations that arrived from opposite directions and met in the last half-metre of the world.

## The ones who came down

Everything green here fell out of the sky.

The mountain is too young to have inherited anything. When it came up out of the warm ocean it came up sterile, glassy, salted, and every living thing now in the notch is descended from something that arrived after — a spore, a seed no larger than dust, a fragment of liverwort torn off a valley wall and lifted nine kilometres by the same updraught that feeds the condenser. The notch is the mountain's windward pocket, and what the updraught delivers, the notch keeps. It has been receiving mail for a few hundred thousand years and has answered none of it.

That is the first thing to understand about the inhabitants: none of them are from here. There are no natives. The bricks and the mosses are immigrants of different centuries, and both were carried up.

The lichens took the first tenancy — the black crust that is not a coating but a rasp, biting into glass-skinned rock a micron a decade, and above it the rust-orange one that the crews call *scab* and that they are technically forbidden to scrape off the survey pins, though they do. Then the liverworts, flat overlapping palms with the sheen of something recently swallowed. Then the moss, which is not a plant here so much as a landform.

## The mattress, and its majority

The moss is the largest living thing in the notch and it has no edges. A hand deep, everywhere, warm to the wrist, so charged with water that pressing it is like pressing a sponge in a sink. The crews call it the mattress. It advances on the wind-scoured path at the rate of a thumb's width a season and the wind pushes it back, and the negotiation has been running longer than the machinery.

Inside the mattress lives the actual majority of the notch's population, and it has never been counted properly.

Rotifers by the million, spinning their little wheels in the film on every leaf. Nematodes. Mites so small that you scoop a thousand of them up with each bootprint and set them down again in the next. And water bears — tardigrades, four or five kinds — which is where the notch gets funny, because tardigrades are famous for what they do when their world dries: they fold up, they expel their water, they become dust and wait. The notch's water bears do not do this. They have not needed to for so long that the lineage appears to have mislaid the trick. Dried in a dish, they simply die, like anything else. In the wettest place above the weather there lives a family of animals that has forgotten how to be dry — and they are the closest thing the notch has to a citizen, because they cannot leave, cannot travel as dust, cannot go anywhere at all except a thumb's width a season inside the moss that carries them.

## The knuckles

Out of the mattress rise the cushion plants, and the crews call them knuckles, and each one is an individual with a history.

They are tight as brains, dense enough to stand on, and each is a self-contained bog: living green skin over a metre-deep column of its own dead. Drill one and you take out a core of its own biography, brown and sour and older at the bottom. The largest knuckle in the notch is beside the eastern pylon, chest-high, roughly the size of a crouching man, and it has been dated. It germinated within a decade or two of the year the first turbine pad was poured.

Nobody planned that. The knuckle and the machinery are the same age because both wanted the same thing — a place in the notch out of the scouring lane, where the water runs and the wind is broken. There are only a few such places. Everything that wants to stay ends up standing next to everything else that wanted to stay.

## The tenants of the ironwork

Out of the knuckles come the dwarf tree-ferns, no taller than a kneeling child, croziers uncurling at a pace you measure with a matchstick and a week. They crowd the concrete pads because concrete holds the day's warmth and the machines leak heat, and a fern will lean toward waste heat as reliably as toward light.

On the fern stems: epiphytes, strap-leaved, forgettable.

And on the housing of the western turbine, bolted-fast in a seam of rust and grease, one orchid. Pale green, unremarkable in every particular, flowering whenever it likes without reference to season, and smelling — anyone will tell you, everyone tells you — of hot iron. There is a running argument in the valley station about whether it smells that way because of what it grows on or whether it simply always smelled that way and found, at last, a place where the smell made sense.

It has never been found anywhere else on the mountain. It has been found on both turbines. It arrived, it lives on machinery, and if the machinery were removed and taken down the cable, the honest thing to say is that the orchid would go with it.

## The ones who came up

The second migration came from below, and it is still coming.

Deep in the crevices where the rock is warmer than blood — the places you can only reach with a knife blade and a torch — there are films. Cream, salmon, a pink like the inside of a lip. They are not photosynthetic. They do not care about the sun, have no use for it, and would be killed by it. They live on what the water brings up: sulphur, iron, the mountain's own chemistry dissolved and rising. They came out of the fractured interior with the vapour, and the notch is where they stopped, not because they wanted to be here but because this is where the rock finally cools and the water finally becomes liquid and there is nowhere further up to go.

So the notch is a border town. On the outside of the rock, the descendants of things blown up from a valley; on the inside, the descendants of things pushed up from a depth nobody has bottomed. The film and the lichen meet at a seam a millimetre wide, and the lichen eats the film, and this — a black crust digesting a pink smear inside a crack — is the notch's entire geopolitics.

## The fauna, which is a rumour

Four beetles, small, iridescent, all four of them mattress-dwellers, all four of them found nowhere below. They graze the fungal threads. Two are blind and one has beautiful useless eyes.

One long-legged fly, which the crews call the smoke, because for the whole of the summer it hangs in a shifting grey column over the condenser gutter, a thousand at a time, mating in the only reliably still air in the notch. Its larvae live in the gutter silt. Its adults live perhaps two days. It has no idea it is a guest of a water utility.

And beneath the pump house, in the warm wet dark under the brick, forty frogs.

Forty in the world. Pale, with translucent flanks — you can see the heart working, the yolk in the young, the eggs in a gravid female, the dark meal moving in the gut. They have no lungs worth mentioning. What they have instead is a pleated skirt of skin along each flank that they fan slowly in the seep, and in air a third as thick as sea-level air they take their oxygen through it like a fish taking it through a gill, which is why they never dry, never bask, never leave the drip-line under the brick.

They also never use water. This is the joke of them. In the wettest place on the mountain there is no pond, no pool, nothing deeper than a bootprint, and so they have dispensed with the tadpole entirely: eggs laid in the moss in a clutch of nine or eleven, direct development, and out comes a froglet the size of a match head, fully made, into a world that is already the inside of a mouth.

They are the apex predator of the Sweating Notch. Everything with a nerve here is eaten by them or is them. The whole food chain, base to summit, is a hand deep and lives under a brick cube that pumps drinking water to a town.

## The ones who never land

The swifts are not residents. They should be mentioned anyway, because the notch would be bare rock without them.

A dozen, maybe twenty, dark and hoarse, riding the updraught, and close enough sometimes that you hear the *fp* of a body going through air. They do not land. Nobody has found where they land, and the standing suspicion at the valley station is that some of them don't — that they are up for months, sleeping in pieces on the wing, and that the notch is not a place to them but a corner they turn.

What they leave is nitrogen. The updraught brings the spores and the swifts bring the fertiliser, and the mattress is a hand deep because for a very long time birds that have never once touched this rock have been flying over it and letting go. The green owes everything to tenants who have never signed anything.

## The ones who are fed

Two turbines, old, riveted, turning at the one speed the notch permits, minutely out of phase so that the sound beats against itself: *waaah — waaah — waaah.* The crews call them the sisters and can tell you which is which with their eyes shut, because the eastern one groans a half-tone higher and always has. Neither has changed speed in living memory. Both are greased by hand, on a schedule, by people who climb up to them with a gun and a rag and touch them the way you touch a large animal you respect: firmly, on the shoulder, letting it know where you are.

The pump strikes six times every eleven minutes and has struck around two million times. Every fern on the pad shivers at each stroke. So do the frogs; there is film of it.

And the notch is warping under all of them, four to six centimetres a year, patiently, so that twice now the pads have been re-shimmed. The graph is in a filing cabinet at the valley station, a straight line sixty years long, and everyone laughs at it once.

## The ones who come up on the cable

There is a human population of the Sweating Notch and it is roughly nine people, and none of them have ever been here at night.

They are valley-born, barrel-chested, with blood like syrup; they cannot donate it, and they sleep badly at sea level. Their fingertips are white and ridged at the end of every shift. They all have the fungal thing between the toes that the station doctor writes down as *notch foot* and has stopped trying to cure. They speak with their hands — thirty-odd signs, most of them about valves, but there is a sign for the orchid, which is a small flare of the nostrils, and a sign for the frogs, which is two fingers walking on an open palm. They are quiet down below, and their families have opinions about this.

And every one of them, by their fifties, has a notch in their hearing: a narrow band of deafness carved out at exactly the frequency of the sisters. The machine erases itself from them. The longest-serving of them — thirty-one years, four thousand-odd ascents, more waking hours in this saddle than in her own kitchen — cannot hear the turbines at all now, and claims that in the silence after the sixth pump stroke, lying on her stomach beside the brick with her ear in the moss, she can hear the frogs calling.

Nobody has been able to confirm it. The recordings show something in the third second, a smear, possibly a valve.

She says it sounds like a finger drawn round the wet rim of a glass, and that they only do it after the hammer, in the gap the hammer makes, as though the machine had asked them something.

## Who has the night

That is the whole census, except for the part nobody has seen.

Twice a day the bucket-car goes down and by the last light the notch is empty of everybody who can leave. The cloud continues to be manufactured overhead. The rock continues to weep.
