## The Tenants

Begin with this: none of them are from here. Not one. There is no native species in the hollow, no lineage that was present when the spine let go eleven years ago, nothing that survived the socket being made. Everything green in that place arrived after, from outside, by air, as a single cell.

So the first and largest fact about the inhabitants is that they are all immigrants, and most of them are dead on arrival.

The spore rain over the lip is constant and enormous. On a still afternoon — as still as it gets, with the plume going straight up and the crosswind slack — the air coming over the rim carries something on the order of tens of thousands of viable propagules per cubic meter: moss spores, fern spores, liverwort gemmae, fungal conidia, algal cells, bacteria riding on grit. This is not special to the hollow. This rain falls on everything in the range. What is special is that the hollow is one of the very few places at 4,300 meters where the rain lands somewhere warm and wet, and so the germination rate is grotesque, and so is the mortality. Perhaps one in ten thousand of the spores that touch down produces a green thread. Perhaps one in a hundred of those threads produces a body large enough to see. Perhaps one in ten of those bodies lasts a year.

The residents are the survivors of that filter. There are not many kinds of them.

---

**The founders.**

Take a lamp and put it flat against a boulder's warm flank and look sideways, so the light rakes rather than floods. You will see, on rock that was bare rock last season, a haze. Not a growth — a discoloration, a faint green bloom like breath on cold glass. Wipe it with a thumb and it comes away and your thumb is stained.

That is protonema: the first, filamentous, undifferentiated stage of a moss, the thing a spore becomes before it decides to become a plant. It is the most abundant living tissue in the hollow by area and the least visible by far. Every warm surface has it. Every square centimetre of vent-heated stone that has been undisturbed for three weeks is furred with these threads, branching, groping, dividing every few hours in the wet heat, growing at rates that would be indecent anywhere else — and being cooked off in sheets when a vent breathes wrong, and coming back within a fortnight, and being cooked off again.

Where the protonema is allowed to persist — three months, four — buds form, and the buds become shoots, and the shoots become a cushion. Nine cushion-forming mosses have been recorded in the hollow. Two of them account for nearly everything: a *Campylopus*, coarse-leaved, hair-pointed, rust-backed, the standard opportunist of volcanic ground worldwide; and a *Philonotis*, softer, blunter, brilliant sap-green, a specialist in warm seeping water. Both are among the fastest colonisers on the planet. Both are, in most of the world, considered weeds of disturbed ground.

Here they are the establishment.

And here is the thing about them that is hard to hold in the mind: each cushion is almost always one individual. A single spore, landing alone, growing outward in all directions for as long as it is permitted. There is rarely a partner within reach — mosses need a film of water for the sperm to swim, a few centimetres at best, and the neighbours are usually the wrong sex or the wrong species or simply too far — and so most of these plants never reproduce sexually at all in their lives. They grow. They fragment. Bits of them break off in a rockfall and roll into a warm crack and start again, identical. A cushion a metre and a half across is one organism, clonally, a hermit that has spread out until it looks like a crowd.

You are not standing in a meadow. You are standing among perhaps four hundred separate lonely individuals, most of them incapable of ever meeting.

The oldest of them is a *Campylopus* on the north lip, in the lee of a wedged block, where the vent beneath has been steady for a remarkably long time. It is nine years old and about two metres across and it is the elder statesman of the hollow, the closest thing here to an ancient tree, and it will be gone the season the block moves. In the range outside, nine years is nothing; there are cushions on the ridges above that are three centuries old and the size of a dinner plate, growing a millimetre a year in the cold. This one grew a hundred times faster and will die a hundred times sooner, and the exchange is not a bad one, and it did not choose it.

---

**The tiers.**

Above the moss, in the steam lanes, the filmy ferns: *Hymenophyllum*, leaves one cell thick, no cuticle, no ability to control their own water at all. They are not really land plants. They are aquatic plants that have found an atmosphere thick enough to swim in. They cannot survive a dry hour and so they live only where the machine runs continuously, and this makes them the most reliable instrument in the hollow: where the ferns are, the vent has not skipped a beat in eighteen months. Where the ferns are brown lace, it skipped.

On the undersides of slabs, the liverworts — *Marchantia*, thallose, varnished, forked, throwing out gemmae cups full of clonal buds that get splashed a hand's-breadth by dripping condensate. This is the entire dispersal ambition of a liverwort: to arrive somewhere by accident, then move fifteen centimetres over the course of a year, deliberately.

And below all of it, in the places the moss cannot go — the scalded ground, the hot margins, the throats of the smaller vents where the rock is fifty degrees and acid — a green that does not look real. A film, slick, luminous, the green of an aquarium light. That is *Cyanidium*: a single-celled alga that likes sulphuric acid and darkness and fifty-five degrees, that grows inside fumarole crusts and in caves where no other photosynthetic thing can go, that requires almost no light at all and gets it, here, from a dusk that would not let you read. It thrives exactly where the mosses die. When a vent shifts and cooks a cushion white, this is what moves in on the corpse within a week, and it is the reason so many bleached cushions have that faint jade sheen when you turn the lamp on them.

---

**The natives, after all.**

There is one exception to everything above, and it lives in the fissure.

Down the throat, past where any surveyor's probe has gone, in water at 90-something degrees under pressure, in the acid, in the dark, in the sulphide: mats. Films of pink and grey and ochre on the walls of the plumbing, slick as mucus, centimetres thick in the pockets. Chemolithotrophs — archaea and bacteria that eat hydrogen sulphide and hydrogen and iron and need no light and no oxygen and have no interest whatsoever in the surface.

These are the actual old residents. They did not arrive on the wind eleven years ago. They came up with the water from wherever the water has been circulating, and their lineage in this mountain is probably as old as the mountain's hydrothermal system, which is to say tens or hundreds of thousands of years, which is to say that the collapse of the spine and the making of the socket and the whole eleven-year drama of moss and fern and scald is, to them, weather. A change in the ceiling. They lost a roof and gained a chimney. Their world was never the hollow; their world is the plumbing, and the plumbing is intact.

Everything visible in the hollow is a tenant of eleven years. Everything invisible is a landlord of a hundred thousand.

---

**The empty houses.**

You already know there are no animals. What is worth adding is that the absence goes further in than you would think.

A moss cushion, anywhere else in the world, is a city. Pull one apart in a temperate wood and you get tardigrades, rotifers, nematodes, mites, springtails, enchytraeid worms, a whole fauna that exists nowhere but inside moss, hundreds of individuals in a handful. Pull one apart here and you get moss, and hot water, and nothing. Not depleted — empty. The interior of a cushion in this hollow is flooded with the ground's own exhalation, carbon dioxide and sulphide pooling in the peat at its base, anoxic, warm, sterile of anything that breathes. The house is built and furnished and vacant. It is the only place I know where you can hold a saturated moss cushion in two hands and be certain that nothing in it is looking at you.

And no lichens. This deserves saying plainly, because lichens are the signature of stone that has been left alone, and there is not one in the hollow, not a crust, not a smear of map lichen, nothing. A crustose lichen needs decades to be visible. Every surface here is younger than its patience. Their absence is how the hollow's age is written on its walls.

What does arrive, and does work, are the fungi — moulds, sooty threads, tiny black stalked things — coming in on the same wind, colonising the imported dead. The moth folded into the wet green does not simply lie there. Something is at it, invisibly, from the inside, and turning it into peat within a season. That is the hollow's only food web, and it has two links, and neither of them is a mouth.

Consequence: nothing here is ever eaten. Not one leaf in this hollow has ever been bitten. In the entire range, across every slope and screefield and cushion-heath below, every plant is chewed, mined, galled, grazed, sucked; here alone the vegetation grows without a single predator, and the only editor is heat. That is why it looks the way it looks — the absurd unbitten emerald of it, the immaculacy of new growth. It is not thriving. It is simply unbrowsed and doomed.

---

**What actually lives there.**

Push on the question and the individuals start to fail as an answer. The cushions are too short-lived, the algae too fungible, the archaea too indifferent, and none of them is the thing you are aware of when you stand in the noise with your palms out feeling for warm ground.

What lives there is a *rate*.

The hollow's resident is the population — not any of its bodies, but the process that keeps replacing them, which is older and more continuous than anything it is made of. It has been running for eleven years without interruption. It does not depend on the survival of a single organism; it depends only on the spore rain continuing to fall, which it will, and on the ground continuing to be warm and wet somewhere, which it is, though never in the same place twice. You could sterilise the floor tomorrow, cook every cushion white, and in five months the green would be back at eighty percent, from outside, from strangers, none of them descended from the dead. Nothing survives here and yet the living cover has never once been broken. That is the trick, and it is the only inhabitant worth calling by a name.

Look at it from above, from the rim, if the steam thins: the floor is a wound eleven years old and what is growing on it is granulation tissue. That is exactly the biology of it. Fast, vascular-less, over-abundant, ugly-lush, provisional — the frantic cheap flesh a body throws into a hole in the expectation of being replaced by something better organised. Everywhere else on the mountain, that phase ends. The willow comes, the lichen crusts, the peat deepens, the scar sets, and the fast things are shaded out and evicted by their own success.

Here the wound keeps reopening. Every few weeks a vent moves and the healing is set back to the beginning, and so it never graduates, and so it goes on being young, for as long as the mountain keeps breathing through that particular hole.

Which will not be forever. When the vents finally cool and steady, in some decade, the hollow will fill with something slow: cushion plants, prostrate willow, a grey rind of lichen on the walls, a bird or two, the ordinary quiet biology of high stone. And on that day the inhabitant of this place — the immigrant, the hermit, the unbitten green, the thing that lives by not persisting — will be gone entirely, killed not by heat but by peace.
