# The Several

## What they call themselves

Their word for *person* and their word for *difference* are the same word. The plural of it is the name of the people; the verb form of it means *to work*.

You will hear this explained to you within an hour of arriving, usually by a child, usually in the tone children use for facts so obvious they are almost funny. The plateau wears out distinctions. Everything here that has stayed anything has either given up being more than one thing, or has put four hundred metres of bare wet stone between itself and anything similar. The Several have done neither. They live nine hundred to a mound, shoulder to shoulder, in houses of the same stone, doing the same work in the same weather — and they stay apart from one another by hand, daily, the way you would keep a fire lit in a wind.

It is not a metaphor for them. It is the largest item in the household budget of a life.

## Bodies

Deep in the chest, broad through the back, short-legged, wide-footed. The hands are the first thing you notice: thick through the palm, fingerpads flattened and horn-hard from stone and cord, with a shine on them from the grease they dress themselves in against the perpetual wet. The skin is dark and permanently damp and holds a high blood colour in the cheeks and the ears. They go mostly bareheaded and crop-haired, because hair holds fog and fog holds cold.

They lose heat unreasonably slowly. This is the local physics doing them a kindness — the gradient laws are lazy here, and a body and a cold thing in contact will stew in their disagreement for hours rather than settle it. A woman can stand ankle-deep in the Sheet from dawn to noon with her feet in ice-film and not suffer for it. The same slackness is a horror in illness: a fever does not break here, it *sits*, at the same height, for twenty days, and either the person outlasts it or does not. A bruise keeps its colour for a season. A burn argues with you until midwinter.

Their eyes are set deep under heavy brows and they blacken the skin beneath them with lamp-black, every morning, everyone, from the year they can walk. The sky is dim but the ground is a mirror, and the light comes up at you all day. A face without its black is an unfinished face; you would no more go out without it than without trousers.

They walk flat. The plateau gait is a low shuffling glide, the sole never leaving the water, reading the ground by foot because the eye cannot be trusted with it. Visitors lift their knees and stumble for a month. You can identify a plateau-born person anywhere by the wake: a continuous low hiss, no splash.

## Keeping

A person's *keeping* is the set of things they do that nobody else does.

It is assembled over a lifetime and it is mostly small. A particular knot used for nothing in particular. The habit of stirring wrong-handed. One substituted word in a common song. A scar deliberately made and deliberately maintained. A way of setting down a cup so that it clicks twice. The keeping is private — not secret, exactly, but not discussed, and never demonstrated to a stranger — and its purpose is structural. It is the ballast that keeps a life from drifting into the shape of the life next door.

Greeting, therefore, is the naming of a difference. *Left thumb short.* *I sleep on my face.* *I am the one who burnt the east store in the year of the long fog.* The other person answers with theirs. It is not boasting; it is closer to two ships showing lights. The surveyors' greeting — stating the distance to the nearest cairn, being corrected, thanking the corrector — is a specialist's version of the same courtesy, and the Several find it a little grand.

Names are issued at a table, in the Common Room, by the tellers-apart, and must be unique across the entire plateau. This is checked against the wall. A name is never a lineage and never a virtue; it is a description of a deviation. *Reaches with the wrong arm.* *Cries at the Rehearsal.* *Born in rain.* Names lengthen through life as a person accumulates distinctions, so that the very old have names that are effectively paragraphs, and nobody says the whole thing except at the laying-out.

To copy another person's keeping, deliberately, is the great crime. It is understood not as theft but as an attempt at murder by other means — and, because the process is symmetrical, as an attempt at suicide at the same time. The punishment is not exile. The offender is given a hard, visible, permanent difference by the tellers, and is required to maintain it, publicly, for the rest of their life. The plateau does not remove people. It thickens them.

## Twins

A twin birth stops a town.

The two are separated before the second day and sent to towns at least six hundred cords apart, raised on different water, given opposite trades, cropped differently, taught different songs, and never, under any circumstance, brought into the same room. The parting is conducted with great tenderness and without argument, and it is one of only two occasions on which the Several are known to weep in public.

It works about four times in five. When it does not work, what happens is gradual and well documented: one twin begins to wake with the other's grit in her boots; both develop the same small limp; a cut opens on a hand nine hundred kilometres from the knife. For a while the towns find this useful, and are ashamed of finding it useful, and use it anyway — a living telegraph is a serious asset. Then the thinning goes past the point of return, and there is one person, and she is in two places, and she is too large for either, and she cannot answer a question without answering it twice out of step with herself. Then there is one person in one place. Then there is a socket of dry air, about the size of a kneeling body, which does not fill, and which the surveyors come and measure and write down.

## Speech, and cord

The voice carries forty paces and then hits the curtain. Their speech has arranged itself for two metres: low, fast, very heavily consonanted, spoken with a hand resting on the listener's forearm, the fingers tapping the stresses. Much of the grammar has migrated into the fingers. Two people in conversation look, from a distance, like two people holding each other up.

Beyond arm's reach there is cord. Every house is strung through the walls with greased line on wooden eyes, and a household's cords are its nervous system: four tugs for the store-room, a long pull and two short for *come now*, a slow repeated stroke that means nothing in particular and is what people do instead of humming. Children learn the cord alphabet before they learn to talk, and infants are put to sleep against the main line so they can feel the house being awake around them.

Crowds are silent and are conducted by placard. A market is hundreds of people making no sound but feet in water and boards being turned over.

And because the slack in sequence runs both ways, conversations here are frequently begun by the answer. A man says *yes, at the third cairn* and then waits, comfortably, for his sister to ask him where he will meet her. Nobody remarks on it. To remark on it is provincial. The only rule is that you may not use it to win an argument, because an argument that has already been conceded cannot be had, and the Several consider a foreclosed argument a small act of violence.

The corresponding sorrow has a name and a procedure. If you find yourself grieving with no cause — abruptly, in the middle of work, without a death to attach it to — you go to the Common Room and sit under the chalk and wait for the news to arrive. Usually it comes in a day or two. Sometimes it takes a year. Sometimes it does not come at all, and then you have to decide, alone, what it was that you lost, and go on without ever being told.

## The far one

You marry at maximum distance, and generally you marry a voice.

The reason is structural and everyone knows it: two people who share a room, a routine, a diet, a bed and a trade will begin, over decades, to thin towards one another. Love is understood here to be exactly as dangerous as it feels. So a marriage is contracted in the Common Room between two people four hundred or nine hundred kilometres apart, standing in the same nine walls, unable to see each other, able — if the room is very quiet and both parties press their cheek to the stone — to hear each other faintly, as if through a door at the end of a corridor.

That is the shape of the thing, then: a lifelong conversation conducted in chalk on a shared wall, in murmur through nine matched surfaces, with three or four physical meetings in a life, each one planned like a sea-crossing, with drift accounted for and a willingness to be somewhere else. Their word for a spouse means *the far one*. Their love poetry is almost entirely about accuracy of memory and the difficulty of arrival, and it is very good.

The household is a different institution altogether: a *hearthing* of six or eight unrelated adults, deliberately assembled from unlike trades, unlike towns, unlike habits, who raise whatever children the hearthing produces in common. You are chosen for a hearthing on the grounds that you are unlikely to become anyone in it. A hearthing that has grown too harmonious is broken up, gently, by the tellers, and everyone involved understands and nobody forgives it for about ten years.

## Listeners

Every Common Room, in every town, has a listener on shift at all hours, sitting on the floor in the centre of the bare nine walls with their eyes closed and a slate on their knees.

What they hear is everything. Every town's room is this room. A listener hears chalk being scraped in a settlement she will never walk to, a marriage being made, an argument about draw-schedules, someone singing to a child, a man three provinces away saying his own name into the corner because he is frightened and wants to be somewhere. It all arrives thin and overlapping and unaddressed, and the listener's job is to catch what carries a town-mark and write it down.

Shifts are four hours and no one serves more than nine years. Longer than that and you go room-lost: you begin to be uncertain which floor you are standing on, then which mound you were born on, and finally the question stops seeming important, which is the dangerous part. The room-lost are led out by the hand, fed food cooked only in their own town's manner, and walked slowly around the outside of their own house with their fingers on the courses until they find the stone they scratched as a child. Most come back. Those who do not are called the wide ones. They are fed and housed and consulted, because they are frequently right about things, and they are not permitted to give anyone advice about their own life, because they have stopped being able to see the point of a life having edges.

## The green one, eaten

They live on the cushion, and the cushion is one thing, and they have no plural for it.

It is cut in blocks with a copper edge, pressed for its water, dried, ground, soaked, fermented in clay, and eaten at every meal in perhaps two hundred forms, none of which taste as different from one another as everyone politely maintains. The tree-wards keep the fog-trees' bright rings, which are the only place anything grows that is not the cushion — a few sharp-tasting fine things that are used the way other peoples use salt.

Eating one enormous individual for eleven thousand years has left a mark on their manners. You do not say that you killed anything; the cushion cannot be killed, only annoyed, and it closes over the cut in a season. But you do not say you took nothing, either. The convention is to name, before eating, the exact place your portion was cut from. It is the closest thing they have to grace, and it is really a surveyor's habit: a statement of position, offered to be corrected.

## The two quarrels

**The surveyors** are the priesthood, and their doctrine is that a thing is where it is measured to be, and that identity is a species of location. This is why they will not record the fetches, and will not record the upright ones that have begun to appear at the west walls in the last two generations, walking on two legs, at human height, with the fog-habit of stopping and turning back. A fetch has no position. A fetch is weather with a memory in it. It cannot be surveyed and therefore cannot be discussed, and the standing instruction is to keep walking.

**The potters** say otherwise. A potter's mark is not a signature; it is a prophylactic, a deliberate flaw put into every vessel to keep it from becoming any other vessel, and a potter therefore spends her life with her hands inside the actual question. Their doctrine is that identity is not a place but a practice — that a thing is itself only so long as something keeps doing it — and it follows, for them, that a habit performed nightly in the fog by the air is not the *ghost* of an animal. It is the animal. It has simply lost the parts that were optional.

The quarrel is polite, ancient, and load-bearing. It is why the towns can hold both a telegraph and a funeral.

## Going level

There is no burial. The dead are washed, named in full — the whole paragraph, all of it, every distinction they ever earned — and carried out onto the Sheet, four thousand cords from any town, and laid down in the three millimetres of moving water with their black wiped off.

What happens then is slow and is the point. Lying still, doing nothing, keeping nothing, a body stops working at being different from the stone. The plateau is patient and the plateau is thorough. After some decades there is a shape; after some centuries there is a slight smoothness; then there is ground.

To be alive here is to be several, and it is *work*, hourly, and everyone is tired. To die is to be permitted to stop. Their word for it means *evened*, and it is not said with fear. The fear is reserved for going level early — for the hearthing that grew too harmonious, for the ninth year on the listening floor, for the twin who was not carried far enough on the second day.

## The well

There is one well. Every town has it. It is the same well, and it is going down a finger a decade, everywhere, at once.

They tried, four hundred years ago, to dig a second. Different site, different tools, different diameter, sworn different in every particular. Within three generations the flattening had found the likeness — a hole is a hole and water is water — and there was one well again, a finger lower.

The current proposal, chalked and argued across every wall on the plateau for the last eleven years, is the kept well: a new shaft, and a warden whose entire life's work would be to make that well unlike any other well — hourly, deliberately, by a thousand arbitrary acts, for sixty years, so that it never once holds still long enough to be recognised. It is an appalling vocation. It is also, everyone agrees, the only thing the Several have ever been good at, performed on a hole in the ground instead of on a self.

Someone has volunteered. She is nineteen. Her name, so far, is short.

## At first fog

Go out before light and you will see them: figures standing singly on a plain of black glass, each under their own tree, each an enormous distance from the next, blacking their eyes, checking a knot nobody else ties, waiting for the *tk, tk, tk-tk* to come walking across the plateau towards them.

The cushion solved this place by becoming one thing everywhere. The trees solved it by standing four hundred metres from anything like themselves. The Several are the only life on the plateau that chose the third answer, which is to crowd together in the wet and hold the difference open by hand, every day, forever, knowing exactly how it ends.

They do not think of this as tragic. They think of it as the work — and if you ask one of them why they bother, she will look at you for a moment with the plateau's own patience, and then tell you her name, and wait to be told yours.
