# The Inhabitants

## The Builder

One of them sits in the palm like a warm flat stone, and does not struggle, because struggling is not in the repertoire.

Hand-sized. Six legs in three unequal pairs: the hind pair long and stilting, to hold the body clear of fresh work; the middle pair gripping, hooked, good on any surface that has ever been chewed; the front pair short, stiff, spade-tipped, used for tucking, pressing, and the final smoothing stroke. The body is a flat grey plate with a slight keel, matte as unglazed clay, and warm — always warm, a degree or two above whatever it stands on, which is the one luxury it has.

The face is the whole front of the head and does two jobs. Its lower edge is a rasp of hard interlocking ridges that grow out from beneath all its life and wear back at almost exactly the same rate. Its upper two-thirds is polished concave, and that is the trowel. In the working stroke the animal drops its head, scrapes, lifts, and wipes — rasp, lift, press — and if you watch a wall being made you will see forty of them doing this in near-unison, not because they are coordinated but because they are all the same shape and all in the same hurry, which is no hurry at all.

There are no eyes worth the word: four light-pits around the rim of the face-plate that register bright, dim, and the passage of a shadow. What the builder actually sees with are two heat-pits set behind the rasp, which read temperature to a hundredth of a degree across a fan of about a foot. Its world is a landscape of warmths. A vent is a blaze. A sleeping hopper is a low hill. A fresh bead of mortar is a faint bright coin that cools over an hour and goes out. Another builder, approaching, is a small moving lamp.

And it tastes. Two short feelers under the face sample the wall constantly, and the wall is legible: every bead carries the flavour of the mouth that laid it, and that flavour fades over roughly a season. This is the entire basis of the city. The rule, as far as anyone could reduce it to a rule, is: *do not lay where the taste is fresh; lay where it is faint; lay against warmth escaping.* Out of that, arcades. Out of that, the spiral stairs with treads the height of a knuckle, which are not stairs — they are what happens when a wave of laying meets a slope and circles it.

The gullet-mill is two grinding plates of the same stuff as the rasp. Pumice in, flour out. The flour goes to a crop where it is worked with tree-oil and a mouthful of gum into a paste, and the paste comes back up as a bead about the size of a peppercorn. A bead every four or five minutes, every waking hour, for eleven years. A builder's entire life's work is roughly two bucketfuls of wall. This is why the city took four hundred years and why it will take four hundred more to double.

They hatch inside the masonry, in brood cells that are nothing but small warm rooms with the flue throttled right. The first act of a new builder is to eat its way out of its nursery, and that is where it learns the taste of home. It never afterwards lays a bead that does not carry a trace of it.

They do not sleep. In the four hours before dawn, when the wax falls, they stiffen. The oil in them thickens, the legs slow and lock, and they stand where they are with their faces to the wall and go out like coals, frosted white, indistinguishable from the dead in the courses beside them. Then the sun comes over the rim, the wax melts and runs, they drink from the nearest cup or crack, and the grinding starts again. Every inhabitant of this world dies a little every night and is poured back into itself every morning. Nothing on the crag finds this remarkable.

## What They Are Doing

They are keeping temperature. Everything else is a side effect.

The chimneys are the point of the city, and the city is the by-product of the chimneys. A nursery must be held between certain narrow bounds or the brood candles over in the shell; so the flue above it is throttled with a gum plug, and when the crag's breath strengthens with the seasons or the vent below begins to cool, a builder passing with a full crop stops, reads the heat with its pits, and either spits a bead into the lip of the stack or rasps one out. That is the whole of the machinery. There is no mechanism because there does not need to be one: the machine is two hundred thousand animals with thermometers in their faces, walking past.

And because it is done by animals who cannot tell one warm thing from another, they do it to everything. They plug and unplug the mountain. They plug and unplug the ports in the back of the kept grazer. They lay courses around a root, a corpse, a sleeping snail, a living beast, with exactly the same stroke. To a builder there is no meaningful difference between a mountain, an animal, and a wall: all three are things that give warmth and oil if you open and close them correctly, and all three are made of material that can be put somewhere better.

They speak by drumming — face-plate flat to the masonry, body rocking, a pulse you feel through your soles and never hear. Good new work carries a drum about forty feet. Old candled work, thick with oil, carries it badly, which means the ancient core of the city is functionally deaf, and the deep galleries are the quietest rooms in a world made of quiet. A message crosses the crag by relay in something under a day. It is never urgent. There is nothing here that can happen in less than a season.

## The Gum-Bearers

Four of them now; there were six.

They begin as ordinary builders from a fat hatch, and what happens to them is a kind of surrender. One finds a fissure with breath richer than any it has ever stood in, and stops. The mill in its gullet degenerates. The legs fuse and root. The plate-lips spread and take hold of the vent-mouth, and bacterial crypts in the swelling gut learn to live directly off the sweet gas, and over sixty or eighty years the animal becomes a ton of warm grey hooded thing wedged in a crevice near the summit, half-mortared into the city, mostly mineral, incapable of any motion but a slow swallowing.

What it gives is gum: a clear, faintly sweet mucus that never dries, welling all day from the lips of a mouth the size of a doorway. Every bead in the city contains a drop of it. Without it the mortar is sand, and the crag is a bare hot rock with trees on it.

So the whole architecture is glued by four mouths, and the builders climb all day to fetch from them, and come back down with oil to pour in. Each bearer's gum has its own flavour, and each holds a quarter of the city, and where two quarters meet the mortars do not quite bond: there are seam-lines running down through the masonry like the sutures of a skull, and they are where the walls crack first and where the walls are repaired forever.

The bearers give no orders. They are not rulers; they are gauges. When the gas beneath one thins, its gum sours, and within a season the beads laid with it are laid a little more reluctantly, and the brood cells a little further off, and the quarter drifts — the way a plant leans. Twice a bearer has died. Both times the quarter went powdery and the walls came down in slabs over about fifteen years, and the survivors built over the rubble, and if you cut into the crust at the right level you find the ruin still there, a stratum of loose grey sand and unbonded bodies, bedded between good courses above and below.

## The Herds and the Kept

The hoppers are knee-high, sleek as seed-pods, three-toed, with toes long enough to hang from the underside of a leaf-plate all night. They scrape wax off the curtains and turn it into a thick butter-oil in glands behind the jaw, and they are torpid when cool and quick when warm, and that is the entire lever by which they are herded. Nobody drives a hopper. You warm the room you want it in. Open a flue in the upper pasture at dawn and by midmorning four hundred hoppers have walked uphill of their own accord into a place that is simply nicer, and the builders swarm them and rasp the glands gently, and the hoppers stand for it, because the rasping is warm.

Nothing here has needed to invent the ambush. Everything leaks. Oil seeps from every cut twig and every gland and every corpse, and corpses do not spoil, so scavenging is as good as hunting and considerably cheaper. This is why a world packed shoulder to shoulder with animals has almost no violence in it: not virtue, just an economy of seep.

The gliders are the exception, and the exception is aerial plankton. At midday the crag's breath rises as a warm column eight hundred feet over the summit, and the noon warming lifts bark-lice off the leaf-curtains in glittering drifts, and the gliders step off the rim with fringed membranes spread and turn in that column all afternoon with their mouths open. They have the only real eyes on the crag. They are the only living things that have seen the world entire: a grey and plum thumb of rock, three hundred paces at the shoulders, standing in a white flat that runs out unbroken to the rim of the sky. They have no way to say this and no reason to. At dusk the column dies and they come home to hook under the summit overhangs, upside down, dripping; the builders have mortared perches around the holds they habitually use, and collect what falls. Now and then one misjudges the evening and slides out past the thermal's edge over the flat, where the air is cold and the wax does not melt, and goes down stiffening, and lands standing.

Bell-snails graze the sulfur crust off the bark, fist-sized, banded, looking exactly like something that ought to chime. Their empty shells make excellent flue liners and the builders use them as such, so the chimneys of the older quarters are ringed inside with the spiral mouths of dead snails.

And in the lowest warm gallery there is the kept one: a flat grazer wider than a table, skirted, soft, older than every wall around it, which has never been outside and whose ancestors were never outside either. Builders carry leaf-plates down to it all day in single file, one plate to six animals, and it turns leaf into oil, and the oil is drawn from a set of ports in its back that are kept open with gum plugs — put in, taken out, exactly as a flue is worked. It grows about an inch a decade. The room has been enlarged around it three times, and it has grown over the stump of an old course in the corner, so that there is now a foot of masonry inside the animal, bodies and all, mortared into living flesh. It does not appear to mind. Once in a year or so it shifts, and somewhere above, a gallery cracks with a dry tick, and the repair takes a season. That is the nearest thing this world has to catastrophe.

## The False Cobble

Grey, flat, builder-sized, with a back textured precisely like a candled corpse, it comes up out of a crack in the night, lies down in an open course, and waits to be mortared in.

It keeps a coat of stolen gum, so it tastes of wall. The builders' only test is taste. It lies in the masonry for years, taking nothing, indistinguishable from the ten thousand dead around it, and when a brood cell is opened three inches away it puts out a filament and drinks the eggs, and then lies still again for another year.

There is no alarm for this. There is no defence. They are found only when an old wall is taken down for a new one, and then they are found in numbers. The loss has always been within tolerance, and so, in four hundred years, the city has never noticed that it has a problem.

## The White Standers

A builder's heat-pits eventually scar over with its own candled oil, and when they do it loses the only sense that tells it where the world is. What it does then is walk the way walking is easiest, which is downhill.

The lower slopes are full of them. Below the line where the wax no longer melts, the legs slow, the joints candle, and the animal stops standing up and simply stands — and the white comes down on it every night and stays. Some of them have been accumulating for a century and are pillars the height of a man with a small grey seed in the middle. Around them: hoppers that wandered, a glider caught mid-step, the occasional bell-snail, all of them furred white and perfectly preserved and getting slowly taller.

The city's edge is defined by these. A builder working the boundary course will sometimes lay one last bead onto the nearest stander, because the rule says to lay where the taste is faint, and there is nothing fainter than a hundred years of wax. So the lowest masonry does not end at a wall. It ends in a scatter of half-cobbled white columns, courses running out into them and stopping, as if the city had tried to keep building and forgotten what it was doing.

## The Whole

It has no head. It has four glands and two hundred thousand mouths and a skeleton made of its own dead, and a lung of hundreds of throttled stacks that it opens and closes by hand, a mouthful at a time, forever. It is warm, it is silent, it stiffens every night and thaws every morning, and it wants nothing except to hold a certain narrow range of temperature in a few thousand small rooms.

When a crack opens in the south glass with a single dry tick and lets out a new thread of warm breath, nothing hears it. But the thread rises, and a hopper drifts toward it, and a builder standing forty feet off reads a faint new brightness at the edge of its fan and takes one step and lays a bead — and the bead is fresh, so the next builder lays beyond it, and the taste-gradient tilts, and over the following season the drift becomes a lean, and the lean becomes a wave, and two hundred thousand grey faces turn slowly toward the new warmth like a field of something growing.

That is the fastest thought this world has ever had. It has no word for elsewhere. The flat is not a place.
