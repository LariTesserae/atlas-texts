## The Inhabitants of the Dry Uplands

### 1. The Casks Are Not Trees

Begin by breaking a habit of speech. Calling them plants makes you look at the outside of them, and nothing happens on the outside of them. The rind is a wall. Everything that matters is behind it.

A crown tower — the big central ones, thirty meters, trunk like a black column of bottle glass — is somewhere between eight and fourteen thousand years old. The grove it stands in is older than the current shape of the ridge behind it. Individuality is a weak concept here: root-grafts run between trunks, and a grove of two hundred towers may be four estates, or one. You can tell them apart by listening. Grafted trunks pump in phase. Put your sternum against one trunk and then another, and if the low note beats against itself, you have crossed a property line that has been where it is since before the pans were white.

They perceive very little and they perceive it very well. Temperature, to a hundredth of a degree. Charge. The internal pressure of a neighbour's column, transmitted through the graft. The angle of the sun, integrated over centuries, which is how a grove knows that the ridge to the west has worn down and it may now grow two meters taller on that side. That is the whole sensory world. They do not need more, because there are only a few things here that can happen.

Their reactions are correspondingly leisurely. A browser scores the rind and the wound seals in forty minutes, which is the fastest thing a tower ever does. But the tower's *answer* to being browsed too often takes nine years: a slow local change in rind chemistry, thickening, souring, until that whole flank is bitter and hard and not worth a rasping. The browsers read this and go elsewhere. No one raises their voice. It is a negotiation conducted at a speed you cannot stand still long enough to watch, and it has been going on, between these two lineages, for longer than your species has had a spine.

Once or twice a century, after a good static season, a grove does the only dramatic thing it will ever do: it assembles a daughter. Forty kilograms of sealed dark sphere, two liters of the estate decanted into it, a crew aboard, and then it lets go, and the sphere sits at the base of the trunk until a front comes and rolls it away downwind. The grove will not know what became of it. There is no mechanism by which it could. It simply parts with two liters, which is a great deal, and goes back to standing.

### 2. The Crew

Inside the column, in the forty tonnes, there is a population.

They are a tenth of a millimeter long, translucent, ciliate, shaped like a grain of rice that has been thought about. They swim in the ascending pump, tumble at the crown, sink again through the return. Circuit time, about eleven days. They have never touched air. Not these ones and not their ancestors, in a hundred million years, except as a dried grain in transit.

They clean. The tower's own slow chemistry leaves it with a burden of dissolved wastes it has no way to void — a sealed body cannot excrete — and the crew eat that burden and are eaten in turn by the column's filters, and the whole arrangement is why a tower can run a closed hydrology for ten thousand years without turning into a column of brine. A tower that loses its crew silts and hardens and is dead in a century, still standing, still faintly warm, and there is nothing anyone can do about it. A crew that loses its tower becomes a grain of dust that remains viable for about eleven hundred years and is almost always wasted.

Count heads, and the plateau is theirs. A single crown tower carries more individual lives in its water than there are browsers, fliers, and towers together on the whole continent, by eight or nine orders of magnitude. By that accounting, everything else here — the black glass, the suede animals, the sailing thing — is plumbing, and rather ornate plumbing at that.

### 3. The Browsers

Low, broad, four-legged, about the size and shape of a stone bench. Four hundred kilograms, most of it water on loan. Grey suede hide with a very high emissivity, which is how they dump heat, since they cannot sweat and will not exhale. The eyes are set deep in bone tunnels, partly for shade and partly because a recessed eye is a cool eye. From the front, a browser appears to be looking at you out of a cave.

The mouthpart is a rasping disc on a short muscular neck, and it is the only tool on the continent.

They are commonly described as thieves. They are not. They are tenants, and they pay, though the rent is deferred in a way that takes some explaining.

**The crossing.** Sixty, seventy, sometimes ninety kilometers of white pan between one grove and the next, and the animal must walk it without stopping. The reason is not heat. The danger of the open pan is the *night*: unsheltered, at four in the morning, at thirty-five below, a stopped browser gels. Walking is the only heat source out there. So a crossing is a hundred and twenty hours of unbroken movement at a little over half a kilometer an hour, launched in the hour after the dawn sweat, with the hide packed full and the tissues turgid, and ended — if it is ended — by walking into the warm black of the next grove with the hide gone slack as an empty bag and the eyes sunk back another centimeter into their tunnels.

They do not hurry, and they do not rest. The two are the same discipline.

**Navigation.** The pans are silent to you because your ears are in the air, and the air here carries nothing. Put the sound in the rock instead. Every grove pumps, and the pumping goes into the bedrock as a standing note under one hertz, and the pans are a single unbroken plate of fine pale stone, which is to say an excellent floor for exactly this. A browser out in the middle of nothing is inside a lattice of four or five audible groves and knows its position within about two hundred meters, all the time, by its feet.

This is also why a dust front is a catastrophe rather than an inconvenience. During a front every tower on the plateau sings at once, all the crowns lit blue and ticking, and the ground goes loud and useless. Browsers caught out lie down, tuck their limbs under their mass, and lose eleven hours of body heat and about two percent of their water waiting for the world to become legible again. A bad season kills them this way.

**Conversation.** A grove holds one adult browser, sometimes two. When two meet, they press their mouthparts flat to the same trunk and talk into it, and the tower carries the notes the length of its graft network, and they listen with their feet. It takes hours. What they exchange, as far as anyone has been able to determine, is the ledger: which flanks have gone bitter, which towers are due, where the husk-fields lie thick this decade, which grove lost its crew and is dying and should be dropped from the circuit. And, at greater length and with more care, the shape of the circuit itself — twelve groves, a sixty-year loop, six hundred kilometers of walking that must be done in a particular order and at a particular pace, because a browser that arrives at a grove too soon after its last visit will find nothing it can drink and will not have the water to reach the next one.

A calf walks its first loop beside its mother, all sixty years of it. That is the only teaching that occurs on this continent. The faint pressure-lines in the crust that the dust fronts erase are redrawn each generation, along the same reasoning, by an animal that was shown.

**The bequest.** A calf is born endowed. The mother parts with a quarter of her water into it, and afterward she is slack for a decade and does not breed again for eighty years. Two calves is a full life. Three is rare and usually means the third was born to a mother who did not survive the following crossing.

**The end.** A browser that knows it is finished does not lie down on the pan. It walks into the grove it was born in, finds a dead tower — one of the hollow ones, standing its thousand-year vigil with the water withdrawn — and goes inside, and lies down against the root-graft at the base, and stops.

The grove takes it back. Everything: three hundred years of borrowed water, and with it a lifetime's accumulated nitrogen, because a browser excretes nothing in life and simply carries the crystalline residue of two centuries of eating in a sac below the spine, and this is the interest on the loan. It is a substantial payment. A grove that has kept a browser for three hundred years and then received it is measurably richer, and grows.

So the floor of an old grove, in the hollow towers, is a boneyard: dried grey shells lying pressed and folded against the roots, hide grown into the graft, in true darkness, in blood-warmth, with the low note going through everything. It is the only architecture on the plateau, and it was not built. It accumulated.

It is also where the calves are born. Same chamber, same warmth, and the mother chooses the spot without any evidence of ceremony, and the newborn's first experience of the world is the company of the dried.

### 4. The Sailing Thing

Fourteen meters of wing and twenty kilograms of animal. The membrane is scraped horn, pale amber, translucent enough that when one crosses the sun you see the finger-struts through it. In this air a wing has almost nothing to hold on to, so the whole creature is a sail and a hunger and very little else.

It cannot take off from flat ground. It has never needed to. Its life is spent in the top ten meters of the sky and in the crowns of towers, and the plateau, from up there, is not a landscape but a map of lifts: every dark grove is a permanent standing thermal, and they are spaced forty kilometers apart, which is just within the glide. The entire continent is a sequence of stepping stones for one species, and it is the only species that has ever been in a hurry.

It lives on the daughters. It hunts them by sight, which is worth something here and nowhere else — in air this clear, a sphere of black rind on a white pan can be seen from eleven kilometers up, and so a seed's only defense is to have come to rest in shadow. It strikes the husk with a keeled breastbone, drinks the two liters through the crack, and in doing so ends a forest that would have taken nine hundred years to stand up.

And when it is not thirsty, it carries them. Spirals up the grove's thermal with a sphere in its feet, glides out over the pan, and caches the thing in a crack in an escarpment, where it is shaded and safe and will keep.

It then forgets a great many of them.

That is the whole mechanism. The groves migrate downwind across the Dry Uplands at the speed of a bird's forgetting, and have done for tens of millions of years, and there is no other reason for anything on this plateau to be where it is.

They are solitary to a degree that is hard to credit. A flier may not encounter another flier for eleven years. But every evening the whole continent exhales over the rim, and the lift there is enormous and absolutely reliable, and once or twice a decade — by some cue nobody has isolated — they come. Thirty or forty of them, over the escarpment at dusk, stacked in a silent column a thousand meters deep, turning in the outrush of a continent's breath, with the amber haze-ocean underneath them that none of them will ever enter. Courtship is conducted up there and takes four days. Then they disperse, and each goes back to its forty-kilometer chessboard, and the plateau returns to a place where nothing moves faster than a walk.

The single egg goes in a crown fork, unbrooded. The tower's stored heat does the work. The tower has no opinion about this.

### 5. The Fines

The pale dust is not entirely mineral.

In the lip of every salt hexagon there is a crust, and in the crust there are things that are only alive while airborne. A front lifts them; for the six hours they are up, they metabolize, divide once, and settle; and then they are dormant again until the next front, which may be eight months away or may be nine years. Sum an individual's waking life and it comes to about three days, distributed across four centuries. This is why the hexagon rims grow.

They are, by ancestry, the crew's cousins. One branch of that old phylum went indoors a hundred million years ago and got forty tonnes of warm water and a lease renewable forever. The other stayed out and got the entire sky, six hours at a time.

### 6. Why the Hour Is Tolerated

Which brings us to the dawn sweat, and the browser standing pressed to the trunk with its mouthpart flat against the rind, taking a thing the tower has not offered and the tower doing nothing about it.

The film is not for the browser. It is not really for the tower either.

For one hour, at the cold end of the night, the interior of a sealed body is in contact with the outside world, and this is the only hour in which that is true anywhere on the continent. The crew use it. A fraction of them encyst at the rind, pass out into the exudate as grains, and lie there in the film. The browser's mouthpart, damp and warm and flat, takes them up. They ride sixty kilometers of white pan in the creases of a suede hide, and at the next grove, at the next dawn, at the next trunk, they go back into the wet.

That is how the crews of two groves ninety kilometers apart, with no root-graft between them, remain the same species. It is their only crossing, and they make it inside the mouth of an animal that does not know it is a ferry, by the permission of a tower that does not know it is a port.

Nothing arranged this. Three lineages, none of which can perceive the purposes of the others — one of which does not have purposes at any scale you would recognize — holding a contract that no party has read. It is simply the only version of the arrangement that is still running. All the others stopped, and their groves are the hollow ones.

### 7. You

You will be the fastest thing here, and the only thing that leaks.

The browsers will log you as weather. The flier, if you see one, is not looking at you; it is looking eleven kilometers past you for something round and black. The crew will never know you existed.

The towers will notice, over about six weeks, in the rind chemistry, and will have adjusted by the time you are gone.

And understand what you look like to the place. You exhale. You perspire. You stand in a grove for an hour and release, carelessly, into the air, more free water than that grove will make available to its browser across an entire year — water that took a hundred million years to get indoors, spilled in an afternoon by a warm loud animal that thinks of it as nothing.

The grove will take back what it can. The rind is thin and vast in area, and it is very good at this. So some fraction of what you spend here stays here, and enters the circuit, and is pumped, and is inherited, and is handed down through the graft to a daughter, and rolled downwind, and forgotten in a crack by a bird.

You were asked to bring your own water and take it with you when you go. You will do your best. But somewhere out on that white and shining continent, in nine hundred years, a young tower will come up out of a crack in an escarpment, and it will be — by some millionth part, by a few grams, in the most literal accounting there is — made of a visitor.
