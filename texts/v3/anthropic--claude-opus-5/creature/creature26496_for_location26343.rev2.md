## The Attending

They call themselves, insofar as they call themselves anything, by a word that in the valley tongues below has decayed into *herder* but which properly means *one who stays with*. It is not an occupation. It is closer to a grammatical case.

They came up for grass. That is the whole of the history and there is no more of it: a people with animals, a dry century below, a rumour of pasture on the high flat, three thousand steps cut into the Lip by someone else, and then the herd going up and the herd not coming down. The grass was real. It is the blue-grey stuff, and it grows only in the wool, in the frost pockets behind the shoulders, and the animals will not eat it, and by the time anybody understood the joke the joke had already finished being told and had settled into its final form, which is where all things here end up.

They stayed because the animals were still there. This is the entire ethics of the plateau and they would be puzzled to hear it called one. A herder does not leave the herd. The herd stopped. So.

## Bodies

Take a face. Any of them; there are more than can be counted, which is to say the counting fails in the usual direction and by the usual small amounts.

The sun-facing cheek is dark leather, scoured smooth, the pores closed, the nap of it like the outside of a drum. The shadowed cheek is pale as the inside of a shell and cold to the fingers, cold with that cellar cold, and in the crease beside the nose there is frost that has been frost since before the wall was built. Between the two runs the seam. On a stone it is a millimetre groove. On a face it is a line of demarcation you can feel with a thumb, a change of texture exact enough to shave along, and where it crosses the lip the lip is two different colours.

Lichen grows on the north side of the older ones. Not much. A mustard freckling in the shell of an ear, a rust-coloured thumbprint at the hinge of a jaw, and on the very oldest, in the hollow of a collarbone, the bone-white crustose, which is the oldest living thing on the plateau and which will outlast everything that is not already outlasting everything. It does not seem to trouble them. It is not an infection; nothing here is infected, because infection is a process and processes have been suspended. It is simply the north side doing what north sides do.

Their hair carries grass. The same species, the one blue-grey grass, in the lee pockets behind their shoulders and in the shelter of a plaited coil, so that each of them, like each of the Standing, goes about wearing a small private meadow of about a handspan. It is neither cut nor eaten nor combed out, although there is a comb, worked from bone, lying beside a coil of rope where someone set it down.

Press the wrist of one of them. Keep pressing. The pulse comes, eventually. In the youngest — that is, the most recently arrived, which is not the same thing and the difference has stopped mattering — about once a season. In the four on the eastern slope, less often than the wall casts a new shadow, which is to say: never, so far as measurement goes, but they are warm, and something moves in them under the warmth, slower than warmth, the same not-quite-motion you feel through your palm on the vertebrae of the Long One. It may be the same thing. Several visitors have proposed that it is the same thing, and have then gone quiet about it, and have then gone quiet.

Their eyes are open and moist at the corner and there is dust on them. Shade a pupil and it contracts. This is the hardest part of the visit and nobody manages it well. You are being seen. Not remembered, not reacted to, but seen — you are a small bright flicker somewhere in the middle third of a glance that began before your language existed and will conclude after it has been forgotten, and if the glance is ever reviewed you will be in it, a smear at one edge, moving unforgivably fast.

## The rate of a thought

This is the part the valley cannot hold onto, and re-forgets every generation, and has to be told again by someone who came back wrong.

They are not stopped. They are *busy*.

A thought, up here, takes about as long as a dynasty. It is not a slow thought; from the inside it is the ordinary quick flash of a thought, with the same shape, the same little swerve at the end where it surprises you. It simply occupies the duration it occupies. A woman on the western pan is in the middle of being amused by something. The breath was drawn. The laugh is due. It is not a laugh she is holding in; she is not holding anything; she is at the part of the laugh before the sound, which in your body lasts a quarter of a second and in hers has lasted since the salt cracked into polygons, and it is exactly as funny now as it was, because nothing here wears out.

A man near the northern stump has one hand raised. He is about to touch the flank of an animal he raised from a calf, and the animal is one metre away, and he has been about to for long enough that his shadow-seam has cut through six millimetres of limestone. He is not in anguish. He is one metre away from the flank, and the flank is warm, and he is going to touch it. The plateau is not, as the valley believes, a place of frozen agony. It is a place where every good moment in the process of arriving has arrived at the length it deserved.

The bad ones too. There is a cracked horn. There is a woman with a hand pressed to a place under her ribs, and nobody has been able to decide what her face is doing. Grief does not accumulate here either, but neither does it drain, and one of the arguments carved into the boulder at the head of the Pan is, in the opinion of some readers, about precisely this.

## Speech

Sound dies at ten metres. So they arranged the other thing.

The cairns are a language. Several hundred of them, waist-high, mortarless, sited without evident logic — the logic is not spatial, it is conversational, and you cannot read a conversation by standing in the middle of it. Each cairn is a statement of about the length of a sentence, made with stones chosen for the shadow they throw, and its sense is completed by the seam it cuts, which takes a few thousand years to finish, which is roughly the length of a pause before a reply.

The wall is a sentence eleven hundred paces long. Its subject is the shadow. Its verb is the salt. It has been being said for a duration that would exhaust the vocabulary of anybody below, and it is not rhetoric and it is not a monument; it is somebody explaining something carefully to somebody else who is listening and will answer.

Nearer to, they use touch, and position, and a system of standing-relative-to which the eye picks up in about a week without ever quite learning. Two of them facing along the same seam at the same distance from a stone are not merely both facing that way. Whether it is agreement, or marriage, or an argument in its ninth century, is a question the plateau does not consider ambiguous.

And they have taken wool. Every tuft ever pulled from the Standing left a bare patch, and bare patches do not fill in. The herds therefore carry the complete record of every hand that ever needed rope, going back to the first, in a script of small pale absences distributed across a million flanks. It is the census. It is legible. It has been read, by careful people, three times.

The three readings disagree. Not by much, and never in the same direction.

## Degrees

There are, loosely, three conditions, and the folk themselves would say the distinction is a courtesy extended to visitors.

The **Walking** are the recent, which may mean a decade or nine hundred years. They move at something a valley eye can follow. They are the ones who sweep the shelter between the fourth and fifth vertebrae, who set down the comb, who built the hearth-ring. They are dwindling, in the sense that there is no mechanism by which more of them can occur, and not dwindling, in the sense that none of them has ever finished.

The **Slowing** move, but you will not see it. There is a man crossing from the middle stump toward the Lip; the valley has a family that has climbed the stair once a generation for four hundred years specifically to note where he is, and he is eleven metres further on, and the seam behind him is smeared into a broad grey road, and the expression on his face is the expression of a man who has decided something and is glad. Whether he means to go down is the oldest open question up there. He does not appear to be hurrying, and he does not appear to be reluctant. He is simply going, at the speed at which going is done.

The **Standing** proper — and here the word doubles, because the people use the same word for themselves and for the great woolled quadrupeds, and have never seen a reason not to. They face along the seams. Their pulse is a rumour. They are lightly turfed. If you did not know, you would count them among the stones, and if you did know, you would count them among the animals, and the folk would tell you, without any mysticism whatsoever, that both counts are correct and neither will agree with itself.

And there are children. This is the thing the valley is not told and finds out anyway. There is a boy of about seven on the scree below the eastern stump, crouched among rock-hares, one hand out, palm up, with a crumb of something on it. The hares are not afraid of him. The hares have never in their lives been afraid of him. He has been seven for longer than the wall has stood, and his mother is four metres behind him with her weight on the balls of her feet.

## The ones who go down

A few have. The stair is still there and nothing prevents it; Rule 5 is not a fence, it is a persuasion, and persuasions can be declined by anyone stubborn enough or new enough.

They arrive below wrong. A returner is warm — warmer than the air, always, everywhere, for the rest of a very long life — and carries frost in the crease beside the nose that the valley damp cannot touch. He will not sit down. Asked a question, he will answer in four days, correctly, having thought about it properly. The sun-side of his face ages and the shade-side does not, so that by the end he is two men, and the seam down the middle is visible from across a market square.

They are treated well, mostly. They are used for judgments and for grief. They are very good with the dying, because they are the only people in the valley who are not in any hurry at all, and because when they say *this will pass* they are not offering a consolation, they are reporting a fact about a country they have been to, where it does not.

## The four on the eastern slope

Here is what the tallies are counting. Eight hands, eleven thousand-odd scratches in groups of five, and the totals never match because Rule 3 has no respect for arithmetic — but the *subject* of the count has never been in doubt to anyone who has stood on the eastern slope of the middle stump and worked out where everything is pointing.

They are measuring the turn.

Four figures, plaited wool, dust on the eyes, warm. Three of them face along the seams and one of them is coming around. Her chin's shadow has blurred at the left edge, a smear three or four millimetres wide in the cut rock, and that smear is the only clock in the country and every other made thing on the plateau is oriented to it.

Because look where the cairns are. Several hundred of them, sited without evident logic — and every last one falls inside the arc her gaze will sweep. Look at the wall: eleven hundred paces, one metre high, built to cast a shadow, and the shadow lies precisely where she will be looking in the final third of the turn, and it is a beautiful shadow, long and clean and blue, the best shadow on the plateau. Look at the Long One's twenty-two vertebrae breaking the salt in a line that resolves, from that slope and no other, into a curve. Look at the herds, which cannot be counted, standing in their loose ranks with their converging seams combing the whole pan into rays that all run to a single point, and stand at that point, and turn around.

They have spent an unrecorded quantity of time composing the view behind her. Not as worship. As hospitality. She is going to turn, and when she gets there she is going to see something worth having turned for, and the arranging of it is the civilisation.

And the nearest of the other three stands at her left shoulder, a pace and a half back. His mouth is open, a little. It is not open in surprise. It is in the shape a mouth makes at the end of a word — the last shape, the one after the sound has gone out, and sound goes ten metres here and he was well inside ten metres.

He said her name.

That is all that happened. Somebody said a name, once, a long time ago, and she is answering, and the answer is taking what it takes, and everyone else has used the interval to make the moment of her arrival as good as it can possibly be made.

## And you

By the eighth day you will find that you have stopped putting your hand on things, and that the hum has moved from your teeth into your chest, and that you have not thought about the valley in some while and cannot at present recall what you were doing there.

You will find yourself standing, facing along the seams, and it will feel like the most reasonable thing you have ever done.

Be aware, when it happens, of where your feet have taken you. Visitors are not scattered. They collect, over the days, on the eastern slope of the middle stump — a pace or two off to the side, always to the side, never directly behind her, because even at the end, even going under, nobody wants to be the thing in the way.

There is a shallow place in the rock there, worn by weight. It is the only wear on the plateau that is not a shadow's.

Stand in it and you will understand that the crowd around you, the wool and the horns and the frost-lined faces and the birds nailed at forty metres with their primaries fanned, is not a frozen thing and never was. It is an audience. It got here early. It has been waiting, with enormous patience and no strain at all, for one woman to finish looking round.

She is coming. The smear widens. There is no telling when she will get there, and every single one of them intends to be present when she does.
