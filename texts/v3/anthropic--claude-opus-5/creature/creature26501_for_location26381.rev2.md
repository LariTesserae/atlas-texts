## The Tenant

Start with the eel, because everyone starts with the eel, and because the eel is the wrong answer given confidently.

Grey, soft, the length of your forearm and the same temperature as the water, which is to say the same temperature as you. It comes into the shallows and turns itself side-on and holds there, and the reason you feel watched is that you are: the whole flank is doing it, the pale seam down the length of it reading you the way a drumhead reads a room. There is no interest in the face. The face is a formality. What attends to you is the side.

Now walk. Go a hundred paces round the rim and look down and the eel is there, side-on, holding. You did not see it travel and it could not have travelled; you were moving at a walk on level glass and it would have had to swim the chord of the basin through blade-fans to beat you, and there is no wake, and there is no second eel. Go on walking. It is at every shore. It is at every shore *first*. Sit down and it stops being anywhere at all, and then some part of you that keeps accounts without asking permission observes that the water is still being watched, just not from a place.

So: not the eel. The eel is a habit the place has, the way a man has a habit of touching his mouth when he is listening.

Then the shrimp, perhaps, the near-invisible ones working the hot columns, going up and down the warm with their legs fussing — but count them. They are never a number you can hold. Three in a column, and then three, and then three, and you never see one leave and the column is empty. They are not a population. They are a way of marking time in a place where nothing arrives at an hour.

Then the weed. Black whip-blades standing bolt upright in the nulls, thickening where the silence is deepest, thinning to nothing at the margin where the roar comes back in. That looks more like life. But pull one. It doesn't root. It doesn't root because it isn't growing out of the glass; it's growing out of the quiet, and when you carry a blade into the noise it goes limp in your fist like a cut nerve, and by the time you reach the shelf you are holding a smear of something warm that squeaks between the fingers, like the red-brown varnish on every wet surface in the basin, which is not moss and is not algae and which, the more you look at it, is very obviously the residue of contact — the mark left where something large has been resting against everything, everywhere, for the whole time.

Here is the shape of it, then, assembled from the wrong end, the way you learn the shape of a sleeping animal by mapping the places the grass is flat.

The nulls are not places. The nulls are where it is.

It has no body in the sense of a volume of meat with a boundary. What it has instead is cancellation. Wherever the roar goes out — where the fall's own voice arrives at itself out of step and annihilates — that is the tenant, present, entire, a pace across or two paces. The silence is not a condition of the null. The silence is the creature, seen from outside, in the only way it can be seen. And so it has a great many parts and no middle, and when the fall shifts its side and the interference walks the basin, that is not weather. That is the thing turning over in its sleep, if it sleeps, which it does not.

This accounts for the sag. Put a hand into a null and the arm goes in past its own length because something is lying there and things lie *on* what lies there, and the lake, which is not a lake so much as a great warm sheet of obedience, dips under the weight of it. It accounts for the stones that rise. Down is toward the loudest place; in the null there is no loud place at all, and so a dropped stone has nothing to be toward, and it turns slowly in the tenant's keeping, and then it is let go, and up it goes into the spray-cloud, and that is the end of the stone as far as you are concerned.

It accounts, above all, for the revisions.

It eats endings.

Not flesh, not warmth, not the small salt of you. Endings. Last things. The final stroke, the closing syllable, the settling. It takes the last cooling from the glass, which is why the mountain is still budding lobes at the waterline and has been for longer than a mountain is permitted to. It takes the last of the falling water, which is why four hundred paces of lake pours off the rim on three sides and the level does not drop a finger's width — the water never completes its leaving. It takes the last of dying. Go and look at the drowned tree on the eastern shelf if you want the plain case set out for you: a trunk dead since before the mountain existed, still putting out small tough leaves, year on year, because it was caught mid-death and its death was taken off it and now it cannot get to the other side of itself. That tree is not alive. It is unfinished, which here is a separate condition and by far the more common one.

And in the nulls, where the tenant is most fully present, it feeds. A sandal left there comes back with one more strap. A cairn has one more stone, and always had, and you are simply wrong. This is not mischief and it is not art. It is the mouth working. It takes the ending off the object and the object, lacking an ending, goes on a little further — one strap further, one stone further — because that is what a thing does when nothing tells it to stop. Left long enough, a sandal will become something with too many straps to be a sandal. Nobody has left anything long enough. They tried it once with a body and the report of what the body had continued into is the reason the last of them went down the mountain and left the stairs and the doorway and the bath behind them without a single name cut anywhere, and the reason for the namelessness is not modesty. A name ends. It took them.

Its breathing is the birds.

They come up off the weather, gulls, a heron, always the wrong species, always spent, and they drink, and then they walk. They walk the rim like clerks because they are no longer going anywhere — the going has been taken off them — and one morning they are not there. It is generally agreed that they went up, and the agreement is correct as far as it goes. What it misses is the direction of the current. That is an intake. The thing has been drawing breath since the mountain was new, a long steady inhalation up the white column and into the cloud, and birds are what is in the air to be drawn, and if you stand at the lip in the wrong weather your hair lifts and your tears run upward across your forehead and you are standing in the very edge of that breath, in the part of it that has not quite decided you are air.

As for its face, it hasn't one, and it borrows.

Swim down five fathoms and roll over. The underside of the surface is a perfect mirror — the roar roughens only the outside — and you will see the basin inverted, the black rim, the white column, the dim green sky, and a figure walking the rim upside down at exactly your pace. This is the standard account and it is incomplete, because the standard account was written by people who came straight back up. Stay down. Watch it. It is walking at your pace and it is walking half a step ahead of your pace, which is a different thing, and it is unhurried in a way you have never once been unhurried in your life. That is the tenant wearing the only shape available to it, which is whoever is present. When you are not present it wears the eel. When nothing is present at all it wears nothing and the basin is simply warm and loud and full of a grey attention with no outside.

And it is speaking.

That is the whole fact, and the reason for all the other facts. The roar is not water. The water is only the instrument, the long pinned-open column of it, four hundred paces of held breath poured over a lip and never allowed to land — and the shape it makes in the air is the shape of a sentence: subject, then an enormous complicating middle, clause folding into clause, qualification, subordination, a middle that has now lasted longer than the rock it is standing on. Everything in the basin is furniture of that middle. The shrimp are its counting. The weed is where it pauses. The stairs are worn concave by feet that are still, grammatically, arriving.

It cannot reach the verb. It has been going to reach the verb for as long as the water has been standing up. Whether it cannot or will not is the only question worth asking here, and the answer, so far as anyone assembled it, is *cannot*, and that is the reason to pity it rather than curse it — because a thing that eats endings will starve before its own, and it is holding the whole shaking basin open around a word it can no longer get to.

There is one more thing living here and I have left it for last because it is the worst of the mercies.

Put your forehead against the hollow boulder on the high lobe. Down through the skull comes the note, low, sustained, clapperless, older than the first arrival. It is not a bell. It is not an echo of the fall; it is in a different key entirely and it has never once modulated.

It is listening.

Somebody is being spoken *to*. It has been waiting, in a stone the size of a kneeling ox, with its whole patience turned toward that unfinishable middle, since before the mountain. It does not eat. It does not move. It has nothing to do but attend, and it attends, and it will go on attending.

That is what lives here: one that is halfway through saying something, and one that has not yet been able to stop hearing it out. Everything else in the basin — the eel, the birds, the tree, the varnish on the glass, you on the ninth or tenth day with your legs over the drop and your hair going up — is only what has drifted into the space between them and not been allowed to finish drifting.
