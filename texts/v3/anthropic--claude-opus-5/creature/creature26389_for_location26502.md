## The Seven

You can tell the lived-in doors of the dormitory row by the boot-scrapers. Seven have them, worn to a bright bevel; four have the four bolt-holes where a scraper was going to go. Nobody has ever moved a scraper from an empty door to a full one, though it would take a minute and a spanner, and the spanner is forty metres away in the machine shop.

They are seven and they are, in the main, unremarkable to look at, except that they are all of them glossy. Nothing dries in Kettle. Sweat stands on the skin in a film and stays there, so everyone shines like something just landed, and everyone smells faintly of brine and coal and the inside of a kettle, which is where the name comes from, and not the shape of the lagoon, whatever the tally-man says.

**Sarn** fires the boilers. He is big and unhurried and works four hours out of whatever a day is, and the coal heap under the crane holds its shape the way a dune holds a shape — you can see him take from it and you cannot see it go down. He knows the pressure in all three boilers by the chord from the relief valves, particularly the flat one, the fifth voice, which is his boiler and which he loves. His forearms have been burned so many times that the burns have joined up into a single smooth glaze, pink-brown, and no new burn has taken on it in years. He says this is skill. It is not skill.

**Thessa** walks the berms. Hydrometer in a glass jar, jar on a string, string over her shoulder; twenty-two pans, nine of them alive, and she reads all twenty-two because thirteen dry pans have a density too and it is zero and zero is worth writing down. She is white to the eyebrows with salt. Her ledger is the only object in Kettle that gets fuller: three columns, a range in the second decimal, page after page after page in a good square hand. It is also the only clock. Everyone has privately estimated how many blank pages are left, and everyone has a different number, and everyone is wrong, because Kell counted them once and wrote the count in the back, and counted them again a long time later, and got the same count, and did not tell anyone for a while.

**Anselm** keeps the shop, which smells of hot oil and always will. Nothing in Kettle breaks. So he makes spares. There are shelves of spares for parts that have never failed — glands, keys, eccentric straps, a complete second duplex pump in pieces across two benches, assembled in his head and never in his hands, because the first pump has not stopped and there is no reason for a second. In the bottom drawer, wrapped in oiled rag, is a clock he made in his first year. It keeps perfect time. You can hear it if you put your ear near the drawer. Nobody takes it out and nobody throws it in the lagoon, and both of those failures are the same failure.

**Ivett** is the chandler and the weighbridge and the accounts. His work is dust. That is the joke and it is not a joke: nothing settles in Kettle, and yet the shelves are clean, and the reason the shelves are clean is that Ivett goes along them with a rag, aisle by aisle, room by room, and when he reaches the end he starts again at the beginning, and this is the only labour in the settlement that is against something rather than merely for it. He balances the salt out against the salt shipped and it balances to the sack. He orders exactly what has left. The orders are filled exactly. He has never once had to write a letter of complaint and he has drafts of several.

**Ma Ounn** cooks. Rice, fish from the margin, greens from the lighter, chilli, tamarind, an astonishing quantity of it, and nobody in Kettle gets any fatter or any thinner. She lays out eight plates. Seven get eaten. The eighth she scrapes into the lagoon off the end of the bathhouse walk, and if you ask, she says it is for the crabs. The crabs do not eat it. They go down when she comes, and the plain blinks, and they come up again around the food and stand in a ring at a polite distance with their one red claw each, and wait, and do not close.

**Coy** oils the pumps. One circuit of the line is fifty minutes with the long-spouted can and he does it and does it, and he is deaf in the left ear, which is the pump side, and he talks in the two-stroke — thud, gap, thud-thud — so that his sentences arrive offbeat and you learn to wait for them. He is the only one of the seven who sleeps properly. The others lie down and go somewhere shallow and come back.

**Kell** surveys. He has a plane table, a level, a chain, and a notebook, and he is the youngest-looking and always will be. He says he was left by the party whose prints go west; nobody else remembers a party. He measures the lean of the stack and gets four degrees.
