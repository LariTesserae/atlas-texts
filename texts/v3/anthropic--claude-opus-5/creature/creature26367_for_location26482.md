## The Occupant of the Flat Place

Take the small tenants first, because they are easy and they are not the answer.

The mites are not inhabitants. They are a property of the felt, the way damp is a property of a cellar; you could not remove them without removing the felt itself. The springtail is the same. The pale slug is a maintenance fault, a slow leak that the valley tolerates because sealing it would cost more than the loss. The two moths are a rumour — you can go a season without seeing one, and the specimens in collections all came out of one bad night in 1911 when a lamp was left burning.

The walker is more nearly a resident. It has intentions, it has a territory, it has preferences: it likes the eleven-day-old repairs, where the works are still loose and mite-mats lie thick and unconsolidated near the surface, and it dislikes the crust and the cliff bands and will not go near the outlet boulders. It has a face you can learn to recognise. But the walker is a follower. It goes where the ground is disturbed, and it has, at all times, one particular disturbance that it prefers to all others, and it keeps within about two hundred metres of that disturbance for weeks at a stretch, head down, waiting.

Follow the walker and you arrive at the flat place, and at the thing under it.

---

**How to find it.** From the pass, at first light, when the valley exhales — thirty kilometres of white haze standing two metres deep from wall to wall — there are holes in the haze. Seven of them, usually; nine in a warm decade; four in the cold years of the 1840s, according to the only reliable set of drawings we have. Each hole is small, four metres by six or thereabouts, sharply edged, and stays clear for the whole forty minutes of the exhale while everything around it fumes.

They are holes because there is nothing there to exhale. The valley sheds the heat of the thaw because it has no use for it. The occupant has a use for it. It keeps overnight what the rest of the machine throws away at dawn, which is why the felt on the flat place is seven to nine degrees above air temperature all year, why it never quite glazes, and why, if you dig your fingers into it in February, they come out steaming.

The seven holes are in different positions every year. They are in different positions in different weeks. If you photograph the dawn haze from the pass on the first of each month you can plot the tracks, and the tracks wander, and they never cross the cliff bands, and twice in the record they have converged — all seven within a kilometre of the outlet boulders — and then dispersed again over the following two months.

---

**The flat place is flat because of what is holding it up.** This is the first thing to understand and it takes some getting used to. In a valley whose entire floor is pocked with slumps and healing basins and shallow revised bowls, the one genuinely level surface, the one with the long clean view down the trough, is level because it is the upper surface of a single body that does not slump.

Cut into it and the knife catches. The felt here is the same moss and cushion as everywhere else, sixty centimetres of it, unmarked — and beneath the felt, where you expect the pale fibrous works, the bundles and bladder-banks and pleated bellows, you find instead a continuous mattress of unpigmented tissue, eighty to a hundred and ten centimetres thick, close-grained, warm, faintly elastic, with no cables running through it and no valves in it and nothing in it arranged in rows. It is the only tissue in the valley that is not obviously plumbing. It is dense the way liver is dense. It gives under the blade and closes behind it, and the cut has healed by morning.

Its underside is where the plumbing is: an intake fringe, four to six metres of ragged white fibre trailing down into the works below, tapping the bellows-banks, drinking the thaw. It is not attached to the valley. It rests on it and drinks from it and moves.

---

**How it moves.** It slumps, like everything else here. The difference is that it slumps in a direction.

A hectare of the works releases its turgor all at once and settles, six seconds, silent, and is rebuilt at random over eleven days. The occupant releases the turgor along one edge only. The edge drops forty centimetres. The mattress creeps into the depression — you can watch this, it takes about two hours — and then it rebuilds behind itself, and the intake fringe is torn free at the back and grown fresh at the front, and the whole body has advanced somewhere between thirty and eighty centimetres. It does this two or three times a day. It can do it twenty times a day for a week if it has a reason, and then it is travelling something like two metres an hour, which in this valley is a stampede.

The sound is exactly the sound of the ordinary slump: someone turning over in bed in the next room. Surveyors' notebooks from the 1880s onward call the thing the bed, and I have not found a better word, and I have stopped trying.

Behind it is a shallow trough of disturbed works, eleven days from level, crawling with mites brought up out of the dark, and the walker in it, head down, doing the only easy feeding it will ever get.

---

**What it does.** It takes casts.

This is the part that people find difficult, and I think the difficulty is a matter of vocabulary rather than fact. The valley cannot leave a new thing alone. Anywhere in the trough, a novel object left in place for six hours will be found and grown around; the rootlets that pin a sleeping man's wrists and hair are the ordinary, undirected version of this, performed by whatever happens to be underneath him at the time, without discrimination, and abandoned as soon as the object stops being novel — which is to say, as soon as it has been enclosed.

The bed does the same thing well. It is what the valley grew to do this properly, and it has had forty thousand years to refine it.

An object on the flat place is not overgrown. It is drawn under. The felt parts, the mattress opens a slot, the object descends through the warm tissue over four to nine days, and closes into a chamber slightly larger than itself, and is held there — not digested, there is no digestion, nothing here rots — for one to three years, while the tissue in contact with it thickens, cools, hardens, and takes an exact negative impression of every surface. Then the chamber is sealed off, the impression is left behind, and the object is passed downward into the archive intact, dyed brown, in better condition than when it arrived.

The archive under a bed's historical track is therefore a catalogue in two parts: the things themselves, and their moulds, drifting slowly apart as the peat accumulates. At four metres, on the north side of the waist, there is a boot with its lace still knotted and the mould of that boot a metre and a half above it. There is the negative of a theodolite's three feet, in a triangle, oriented to a bearing that no longer means anything. There are the casts of eleven sheep, one of them lambing. There is a page of a notebook whose ink migrated into the tissue as it was being copied, so that the mould carries three lines of a stranger's handwriting in mirror, legible, complaining about the weather.

And there are, in six separate places, six cairns. Stone by stone, in order, in a stack, at depths between two and five metres, exactly as they were built. Nothing here has ever been built, by anyone, for any reason — this is the standard line, and it is wrong. Six parties in a hundred and fifty years did the human thing on the one flat place with the view. Each cairn was standing when they left and gone when the next party came. The valley did not destroy them. It filed them, and kept the copies.

---

**Whether it is doing anything.** I want to be careful. There is no brain in it. There is no nerve, no signal faster than water in a fibre, no organ that could be called a sense except the whole underside, which is a chemistry, and the whole upper surface, which is a temperature. It does not hunt. Nothing it takes is food; it eats thaw-heat and mite-mats and its own shed fringe, and it would live just as long on an empty flat place as on a busy one.

What it has is attention, which is cheaper than intelligence, and which the valley can afford at enormous scale. Attention here means: it goes toward difference and stays until the difference is gone. It arrives at fresh slumps. It arrives at the landslide scar and sits on it for a decade. It arrives at lightning strips. It arrives, three or four days after you do, at the place where you have stopped.

And it holds a level surface on its own back, because a body that spends its life pressing up against novelty needs one face that does not shift, and because — this is speculation and I will mark it as such — the flat places are where things come and stop. A cushion-grown table with a thirty-kilometre view is the closest thing this valley has to a lure, and it may have grown one for the same undirected reason it grows silica ball-bearings into its valve seats: because the arrangements that worked were kept.

Stand on it for a while and you will feel, in the teeth rather than the ears, a pressure that arrives about once every forty seconds. It is the intake fringe drawing. It is the only pulse in the valley, and the river is the only sound, and the walker, which used to have a throat, stands eighty metres off with its head down, waiting for the bed to turn over.

Do not sleep there. You will not be harmed. You will be measured, and you will be kept.
