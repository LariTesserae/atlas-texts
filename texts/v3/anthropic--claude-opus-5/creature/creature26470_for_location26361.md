## What you will assume, and why it's wrong

The cairns do the damage. You see a stack of nine flat slabs, largest at the bottom, and you have already installed a person: someone hooded, patient, ascetic, walking the terraces with a purpose that would be legible if you could just get close enough to ask. Everyone does this. It is the most human reflex there is — to see a stack and supply a stacker in your own shape.

But there is no atmosphere to breathe and nothing to breathe it with. There is nothing to eat, in the sense of anything that has ever concentrated anything else. There is no water, which means there is no interior — no lumen, no cell, no bag of fluid holding a chemistry apart from the world. A body of the kind you are imagining is fundamentally a way of keeping a small pond warm, and there is no pond to keep.

Nor is it a machine. There are no fastenings, no seams, no shed parts, no scoring on the slabs where a gripper failed, no straight line anywhere that isn't a fracture. Nothing has been carried here from anywhere. Nothing has been made.

So the honest place to look is down, and further down than you want to look — at the thing you already walked past two hundred times and dismissed as mineral.

## The body

It is a crust that has managed to stay one crust.

A plate, roughly circular, roughly a metre across, palm-thick, with a lobed and irregular margin. Lying flat on the warm floor, it is nearly invisible: black above, and the black is the flattest surface on the Rise. Not polished — nothing here is polished, nothing wears — but laid down flat, deposited layer against layer with the matte sky as its mould. The upper face is a window that faces cold. It has never faced anything else.

Turn it over — you can, it weighs almost nothing, and you will destroy it, and it will not mend, because nothing here mends — and the underside is the garden you have been admiring at the vents all day. Hollow fronds, tubes, rosettes, cadmium yellow and verdigris and that purplish bronze that changes as you move. The fronds are pressed into the stone and worked into every hairline crack beneath it, finger-deep, reading the floor. Between the black lid and the yellow root is a laminated interior of several hundred courses, each thin as paper, and it is mostly hollow, and it would break in your hands like a wafer.

That is the whole animal, if you insist on the word. A lid, a root, and a gap.

It is a thermocouple. The floor is warm — always, everywhere, that is the law — and the sky is a matte unstarred black that has never once warmed anything, the most reliable heat sink in the known universe. Between the two there is a difference, and the difference will do work if something stands in it. The seam you noticed on your first walk, heat around your ankles and cold on your shoulders, two climates a body's-length apart: that seam is not a curiosity. It is the only habitable zone on the Rise, and the resident is exactly as thick as the habitable zone is thin. It has flattened itself into an interval. It eats the interval. There is nothing else here to eat.

## How it moves

It grows on one margin and dies on the other.

A millimetre per breath-cycle of the plateau; five or six millimetres an hour; ten metres in a month, if the ground cooperates, which it does not. It cannot turn quickly — a change of direction takes a week to become visible in its outline — and it cannot turn back, because behind it is a trail of grey flakes, and grey flakes are spent, and hold nothing.

The trail does not degrade. Nothing here degrades. Every margin it has ever shed lies where it fell, as crisp as the hour it fell, so that the whole course of the thing's existence is written on the floor behind it in one unbroken line, at uniform resolution, unretouched, all of it equally present. If you had the patience you could read the entire life off the ground like a sentence. It cannot. It is at the wrong end of the sentence, and it has no eyes, and reading is not among the things this world has gotten around to.

What it has instead is gradient. It knows where warmth is the way you know which way is down. It knows a crack is opening beneath it about ninety seconds before it opens. It knows when a vent is dying, because the dying registers as an ache of cold spreading from one side, and it begins, over days, the long slow labour of leaning away.

## How it builds

Not by lifting. Nothing that moves five millimetres an hour is going to lift a slab of basalt.

It grows underneath. It works its fronds into a hairline fracture and then thickens them, and crystals growing in a confined space push — the same brute mineral force that splits a paving stone with ice, minus the ice. A hair a day. The slab lifts, tips, and settles on the slab below it. Weeks per course. The builder is beneath the stack the whole time, and is partly consumed by it: the fronds that did the lifting are crushed and spent and go grey, and stay in the joint. That is why the joints are unweathered. They are not joints. They are scars, and they are the creature's own.

And it builds for the plainest reason in physics. A stack of slabs is a chimney. It pulls hot vent gas up out of the ground faster than the flat ground will release it, and at the base of the stack, in that draught, the gradient is steeper than anywhere else the thing will ever find. Two uprights and a lintel are simply what you get when the gap has to stay open and the stone must not fall into it. It is not making a doorway. It is making a place where gas can pass and rock cannot.

So is anyone who has ever made a doorway. The resemblance runs the other way from the way you assumed. The Rise is not rehearsing architecture. Architecture is a very late and very wet restatement of this.
