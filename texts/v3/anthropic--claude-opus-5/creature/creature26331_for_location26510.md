## The census

If the question is who lives on the Shoulder, the honest answer is the fly.

*Ephydra* in the brine, four instars of it, a standing population somewhere in the low hundreds of millions across six pools, with a turnover of about eleven days in summer. Weigh the mountain's living tissue and better than nine parts in ten of it is fly: fly larvae in the warm anoxic muck of the inflow terraces, fly pupae cemented in bands to the salt shelves, adult flies in a black felt three deep on the last two metres of dry terrace, walking on each other, mating on each other, taking off in sheets the size of tablecloths when a shadow crosses and coming down again behind it without a sound anyone can hear.

Everything else on the Shoulder is a subsidiary of the fly. The spiders in the corridors are a fly business. The lizards are a spider business, which is a fly business at one remove. The three toads are in flies. The six hundred flamingos are a fly cartel, standing all day in scalding water with their heads underwater, pumping larvae out of the muck through the lamellae of their bills at a rate that would empty the pools in a fortnight if the pools did not resupply themselves. The mouse-eared thing takes flies when it can and everything else when it cannot. The eagles and the caracaras are a fly business four steps up and pretending otherwise.

And then there are seven men, which on this mountain is a rounding error, and they know it.

## The seven

The payroll at the rail spur says six.

Rufino Ayaviri, sixty-one, brakeman, which means he owns the top station: the drum, the band brake, the little bolted-on generator, the count. Everyone calls him the clock. He has worked the Shoulder for thirty-four years and has never once been down the tower path in under seven hours or up it in under eleven and a half, which he considers a fact about the path and not about himself.

The Chura brothers, Nicanor and Baltasar, thirty-eight and thirty-six, jackleg drillers, the fastest crew the mountain has had and the least conversational; they work a bench in a rhythm that has no talk in it at all, only three taps of a wrench on the steel meaning *back off* and two meaning *again*.

Emeterio Puma, forty-four, compressor man, who apologizes for the diesel out loud, every morning, in a low voice, and gets it going.

Justo, sixteen, water and food and candles, nephew to somebody, up here for a season that has now lasted two years.

Ilario Colque, age not certainly known even by Ilario, who does no physical work and is not asked to. He keeps the tool wall in order and he keeps the ledger, and these are understood to be the same job.

And Sabino, who is the seventh and is not on the payroll. Sabino has not gone below four thousand metres since 1998. His face is the colour of a plum skin, his fingertips are clubbed, his hematocrit was measured once at seventy-six and has not been measured since, and he sleeps sitting upright on a box with his forearms on his knees because lying down drowns him. The cure for what Sabino has is to go down and stay down. He knows this. Everyone knows this. He is fed out of the common pot and nobody discusses it, and when the buckets are running he sits at the top station beside Rufino and counts under his breath, and his count is always right.

## What the place has made of them

Barrel-chested, all of them, even the boy, who has grown into it in two years. Resting pulses in the nineties. Blood thick as gravy. Hands cracked into a grid, like the surface of an old glaze, with the deeper splits packed by grease and sulfur into black lines that will not wash out and no longer try to. Fingernails yellow. Beards yellow at the edges. Hair, clothes, sacking, straw, the inside of a mug of tea — all of it faintly sulfurous, all of it tasting of a struck match, which they stopped noticing years ago and which returns to them violently on the second day of any trip down, when food tastes of food.

Their eyes are the thing a visitor notices last and remembers longest. Thirty years of ultraviolet coming off cream-coloured rock at 5,900 metres builds a wedge of grey tissue in from the inner corner of each eye, and Rufino's have grown far enough that when he looks straight at you he is looking around two small opaque triangles. They squint permanently. They do not have crow's feet; they have a single deep pleat.

A cut on the Shoulder does not go bad. It also does not close. There is not enough oxygen in their blood for granulation tissue, and there are not enough bacteria in the air to make an infection, so a split thumb simply stays a split thumb, clean and open, for eight or nine weeks, and then one morning is a scar. Nothing on this mountain rots and nothing on this mountain heals, and the men are inside that rule with the mule and the dogs and the four hundred years of vicuña.

They all walk the same way. Long stride, flat foot, no pause, arms loose. Standing still in a corridor cooks you; sitting down in shade is how people die here; so they walk in shadow and rest upright in the sun with their shoulders to a pillar and their soles lifted alternately, one and then the other, an unconscious tick that every man on the crew has and none has noticed. Their boot soles are truck-tyre rubber over llama hide, sewn, never nailed. Hobnails conduct. There is a man in the basin town below who makes nothing but these boots and has made nothing else for forty years.

## How they talk

Barely, and almost never at volume.

A shout at this altitude costs more breath than it delivers meaning; two hundred metres away a man is silent by physics, not by choice. So the crew have converted speech into other currencies. Wrench taps on drill steel. A two-note whistle that carries better than a voice and means *come*, and a three-note descending one that means *don't*. Hand signs at the brake drum, six of them, all made low and close to the body. A slate at the workshop bench where the day's needs are written in chalk and rubbed out at dusk: *bit 32. lamp oil. Baltasar knee.*

The radio works. There is a schedule, the fourteenth of the month, and on the fourteenth Rufino reads numerals into it — tonnages, bucket count, oil, the word *nada* — and switches off. It has never been used for anything else. Twice in living memory the loading station has called up out of schedule and both times every man on the mountain stopped working, because a voice arriving unasked out of a box is a thing this place is not shaped for.

## The four-hour window

They live in it, exactly as the moss does.

It does not rain and they cannot carry water up nine kilometres and two thousand three hundred metres of ladder path, so their water is condensation, which means their water is the mountain's breath cooled on rock. Around the camp, forty-odd pillars have been fitted with aprons: sheets of flattened tin bent to the flutes and lapped down into canvas gutters that run to four galvanized tanks. From about three in the morning until seven the aprons tick and stream. By nine they are dry and warm and every pillar in the field has gone back to smelling of matchheads, and the tanks hold what the crew will drink and wash in and put in the drills.

So the whole camp is nocturnal at one end. Justo is up at two, going the round with a lamp through corridors that are dripping and steaming and glistening in the light, at body temperature, tapping each apron to shed its salt crust. It is the only hour on the Shoulder that could be called pleasant, and he has never said so to anybody.

The water is faintly acid and tastes of sulfur, and it is boiled at 82 degrees because that is where water boils here, which is not hot enough to sterilize anything, which does not matter, because there is nothing in it to kill.

## Food, and the oven

The brick oven has not been lit since Doña Sebastiana died in 1991, and it has not been lit not because it is broken but because lighting it would mean the crew had appointed a cook, and appointing a cook would mean deciding who, and nobody is going to do that. So they eat in turns and badly: chuño, tinned sardine, rice cooked for an hour and a half and still chalky at the core because of the pressure, llama jerky, tinned milk, sugar, and tea, enormous quantities of tea, and coca in the cheek from waking to sleeping, which is the only reason any of the rest of it is bearable.

They eat standing, in the sun, in the wind. Six men in a row against the bunkhouse wall with their soles lifting alternately, chewing, not speaking, facing west at the white basin sixty kilometres off with its own weather sitting on it.

## Sleep

Six sleep; Sabino sits.

At this altitude nobody breathes smoothly asleep. The breath goes deep, deeper, then shallow, then stops for fifteen or twenty seconds, then restarts on a gasp. Every man does it. Nobody has ever observed it, because to observe it you would have to be awake, and the man awake is out on the aprons.

But the bunkhouse, four hours after dark, does this: the six periodic breathers drift into phase, the way pendulums on a shared beam will, and then for fifteen seconds at a stretch the room contains six sleeping men and no sound at all, and then all six gasp within a second of each other. It happens perhaps twenty times a night. There is no record of it. It is not in the ledger. It is the single most exact echo of anything on the mountain, and the men it happens to will go to their graves not knowing they were doing it.

## The dead, as furniture

They navigate by them.

The pinnacle field has no landmarks that are not other pillars, so the corridors are indexed by what stayed in them. *Left at the Sergeant* — a vicuña wedged upright between two pillars with its neck arched back, hide over bone, in place for something like two hundred years. *Down past the Widow.* *The dogs*, plural, three of them, 1920s, in a slot near the second vent cluster, and the crew's live dog will not go down that slot and has never been made to. *The Pack* is the mule, still lashed, and it is the turning for the western benches, and men have been giving directions by it since before Ilario's father worked here.

The crew's own dead do not stay. There is no digging: the ground is rock and it is sixty degrees. They go down in the buckets, one man to a bucket, roped in, nine kilometres and two thousand three hundred metres at walking pace, and the weight goes into the ledger because Rufino will not falsify a count, entered in the margin with a name instead of a grade. Doña Sebastiana went down that way and is buried at the rail spur under a slab with a fly on it, which is a joke, and which everyone on this mountain understands.

The ones who were not found stayed. There are two.

## The ledger

Ilario keeps it and Ilario is, more than any of them, what lives here.

It is the sixth volume. The columns were ruled and headed by a man named Vargas in 1954 and every book since has been ruled the same way out of no motive but continuity: date, buckets down, grade, oil, weather, *arriba lleno*. That last column — *ascended full* — has an entry roughly once a month going back seventy years, and it is Ilario who writes them, and he writes them exactly as he writes the oil.

He has one observation about it, and he will give it to you if you ask him twice. The sulfur in the ascending buckets is hand-graded, and it is graded to a screen standard that the plant below stopped using in 1961. Somebody is sorting rock to a specification that no longer exists, and doing it well, and putting it in an empty bucket somewhere on the nine-kilometre line, and there is a level place under tower fourteen with a fan of screen tailings on it that a survey party found in 1974 and that nobody has been back to look at, because looking at it is not anybody's job, and the tonnage goes into the bins and out of the accounts and this suits every party concerned.

Ilario also keeps the tool wall: fifty-one tools hung in order, each worn thin in the specific places hands wore it, several of them older than the ropeway. He rehangs the wall each evening. It takes four minutes. This is the only ritual on the mountain that is performed with any regularity, the candles in the chapel being, as everyone agrees, sufficient unlit.

## The tenant

But if you stand on a pillar top and ask the question properly — who lives here, what is the resident and what is the lodger — the men are not the answer and neither are the flies.

Every eleven minutes, to the second, for longer than anyone has kept time, the tunnels and the vents exhale together. Warm white steam fills nine kilometres of corridor to an arm's length of visibility, and the flies go up, and the men keep walking because they know the corridor by touch, and forty seconds later it clears and the whole forest of pillars is beaded and dripping and the green stripe is wet at its lower edge, level to within a few centimetres, all the way to the horizon.

The moss lives on that breath. The flies live on what the breath carries into the pools. The flamingos and the toads and the spiders and the eagles are that breath rearranged. The crew's drinking water is that breath, cooled on tin at three in the morning; their light is the mountain's own weight falling; the sound they set their nights by keeps going at its nine-second interval
