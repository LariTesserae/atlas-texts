# Anyone Still Here

## 1. Start with the density

We are the same weight as the water. Not approximately — exactly, to the point where a body released at four hundred metres will hang there for hours and drift only where the water drifts. There is no gas anywhere in us. The sinuses are packed with a stiff clear gel; the gut is a solid tube of paste and never a bubble in it; the ear, if you can call it that, is bone all the way through. A hollow is a death sentence in the bellies and we do not carry one. This is the first fact about us and everything else follows from it: we can go anywhere in this sea, at any depth, at any hour, and nothing else our size can say that.

The body is about one metre ninety, tapered, four-limbed, with a low dorsal keel of cartilage and hands that are hands. Cold all the way through — we are two degrees, like everything else, and always have been; there is no such thing as warmth here except at the fault-weeps and the outfall lips, where you can lie in the plume and feel a difference of a fifth of a degree and understand why the young go there and stay too long.

We are born transparent. An infant is a wobble in the light with a shadow of a gut in it, and if you did not know to look you would swim through one. Opacity comes with age, slowly, from the trunk outward, a whitening of the tissues like frost in glass. By eighty you have a visible outline. By two hundred you are the colour of tallow and can be seen from a hundred metres in the afternoon light at four hundred metres' depth. We age, in other words, into visibility. It is not a metaphor. It determines where you are permitted to be.

## 2. The map in the bone

Down the sternum and along the spine runs a comb of dense mineral, forty-one plates, each one tuned. That is the organ. We do not hear with our heads. We hear with the whole skeleton, the way you would hear if you were a tuning fork.

What the comb reads is not sound in your sense — the roar is a constant and constants are not information. What it reads is *phase*. The eleven-second pulse arrives everywhere in the basin, but it arrives at every point with a particular delay and a particular amplitude, and those two numbers together are unique to that point, and they do not change. Ever. The standing wave has not moved in ten thousand years.

So we always know exactly where we are. Not roughly. Exactly, to within a few hundred metres, in total fog, in the dark of a trench basin, asleep, from birth. There is no experience among us that corresponds to being lost. When I try to imagine what it would be to not know my position I get a sensation like the floor of the chest opening, and I stop.

Our names are positions. You are named for the phase and amplitude at the place you were born, spoken as a pair of numbers, and no two of us alive share a name, and the name tells anyone who hears it where your mother was when she stopped. Mine is a place four kilometres west of the ninth tower, over a talus fan my family has worked for six generations. When I go there I am, in the local dialect, *at my name*.

## 3. Where we can talk

We make sound by knocking. Knuckle on knuckle, knuckle on the comb, palm on rock — a dry, fast, percussive language with no vowels in it, about eleven hundred distinct figures. It carries perhaps two hundred metres.

In the bellies, where we are safe, it carries nothing at all. The roar eats it whole. So in nine tenths of this sea, at all times, we are mute at distance, and speech happens only by contact: hand flat on another's sternum, knocking directly into the bone. Everything intimate about us comes from this. To speak in the loud country you must be touching, and you must have agreed to be touched, and a conversation of any length is two people holding on to each other in open water at eight hundred metres with the light coming down. That is the ordinary way. That is how children are taught, how the dying are attended, how anything is agreed between two.

Anything agreed between *more* than two must happen in a node.

There are six nodes of any size. They are the only rooms in the world. In a node the knocking carries and a hundred of us can hear one speaker, and so every gathering we have — the counts, the fan-rotations, the disputes over a garden boundary, the teaching of the rod-song, every occasion on which we are more than a pair — takes place in one of six lens-shaped pockets of quiet water whose positions were fixed by an accident of basin geometry and machine tempo before anything of us existed.

And the slate-eels hunt by listening, and can only hunt where listening works.

So: assembly is dangerous. That is the shape of us. We are safe and dumb in the wide bright country, and we become a people only in six rooms where a thing with a lateral line like an f-hole waits at the margin for us to start talking. Meetings are short. Speakers stand in the centre with watchers ringed outward. Children are not brought until they can hold silence on command, which is around twenty, which is also when we consider a person to have language, because language is a public act and before twenty you have only had touch.

And the old, who are white as wax and visible at a hundred metres, do not come to the nodes at all. Somewhere around one hundred and eighty a person makes their last crossing to a meeting, says what they have to say, and afterward keeps to the loud country, where nothing can see the point of them and nothing can hear them. It is not exile. Everyone agrees it is not exile. They are simply, from then on, only ever spoken to one at a time, by hand.

## 4. What we eat and what we break

We eat ribbon-grazers, mostly, and bell-shrimp gathered in bag-nets of plaited grazer-hide at the outfall rims, and for infants a paste of scraped varnish that has to be worked for an hour to be digestible.

Grazers need fresh rock. Fresh rock does not occur here — nothing weathers, nothing shatters, nothing rounds. Left alone, a cliff sits with its varnish grazed down to stone and stays that way for a thousand years.

So we make talus. This is the work. A garden is a fan of broken blocks below a face we are quarrying, and we quarry with iron, and the blocks are stacked, not dropped: interstices sized to the width of a grazer, faces turned to the current, the whole fan laid out to hold water in it and let it move through. A new fan is bare for two years.
