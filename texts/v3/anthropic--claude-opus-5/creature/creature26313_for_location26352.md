## Who Is Here

**I. Census**

The honest census is very short, and it is short for a reason that takes a while to sit down inside you: the valley does not keep individuals. It keeps solutions. Ask how many herons and you have asked the wrong question, the way it is the wrong question to ask how many times a word appears in a language.

The vertebrate vocabulary of the entire basin comes to five entries. One eel. One heron, held at two ages. One frog-call, distributed through a great many throats. One dragonfly the color of wet slate. And a width in the alder carr.

Below that there is an uncountable crowd, and it is the only crowd here, and I will come back to it, because it is the only part of the valley that is still allowed to be new.

**II. The five**

The eel is encountered perhaps twice in a week, and it is always the same eel: a metre and a bit, the notch in the dorsal fin like a paring taken off a nail, a pale scar behind the gill that has never healed and has never worsened. It is met in sloughs six kilometres apart inside the same hour, and if you take it — you can, easily, it does not flee, it has no experience of being fled from — you will find in its stomach one water beetle at the same stage of dissolution as the last time, the elytra still bright, the abdomen already gone. It is not immortal. It is a single fact about the valley, restated wherever the valley requires an eel.

The herons come in twos, and this is the one thing here that reads as etiquette. The same bird at two ages, forty years apart, hunting the same shallow at either end of the same sedge bar, each conspicuously not looking. They step in the manner of people passing in a narrow corridor who have decided in advance to be entirely at ease. If you startle them, both go up — but the older one goes up first, a third of a second early, before the noise, with the same lag your shadow has, except backward. That third of a second is the most information the valley has ever volunteered about itself, and nobody has known what to do with it.

The frogs thrive, and they thrive because a frog is already almost a loop. Three notes and a life. Their spawn hatches knowing the call, which means the young are not born so much as quoted, and the population fluctuates enormously without the sound changing at all — a hundred thousand mouths one spring, four thousand the next, the same volume, the same phrasing. When you stand in it at dusk you are not listening to animals. You are listening to a single sustained sentence being kept in the air by relays.

The dragonfly lays its eggs into its own reflection, and the eggs hatch. Consider what that requires. The reflection is load-bearing. Somewhere in the specification of the mud there is a clause that does not distinguish between a surface and what stands above it, and the dragonfly has found that clause and lives inside it, competently, for one summer at a time, forever.

And there is the width in the alders.

**III. The width**

The trails are real. They are the width of something large — call it the width of a horse, or of two people walking abreast, or of a heron standing very tall — and they are soft-edged and old and kept open by continuous use. The liverwort on the branch-sides is rubbed to a low polish at hip height. Standing water in the depressions is a fraction warmer than the standing water two metres off the trail, and nobody has ever measured it warm enough to be interesting and nobody has ever measured it not warm.

There are no prints. There is never anything ahead of you on the trail and never anything behind. But in the Reprise, perhaps one Reprise in nine, there are footfalls you did not make: a heavy, even, unhurried set, four in the time you take three, with the small suck and release of a foot that knows exactly how deep it will go. They are always present-tense. They are never in the same Reprise twice.

Sound is the only thing here that goes forward reliably. So the walker exists in the only tense the valley can hold, and that is the whole of its body.

It has habits, and the habits are legible. It gives the cold stones a berth of about four metres and has never been heard to touch one. It does not enter the sedge rings. It ends at water, always, every time, at the lip of a slough, and stops, and the footfalls stop, and there is nothing after them — no drinking, no wading, no turn. It travels at about four kilometres an hour, which is not the speed of a beast. It is the speed of something that once had legs and somewhere to be.

And if you walk the trail yourself, at your own pace, for long enough, the footfalls in the Reprise will come into step with you. Not exactly, and not immediately, and not with any sign that it has noticed you — but within an hour of easy walking your stride and its stride will be the same stride, and it will be the one that adjusted.

That is the only responsive thing in thirty kilometres.

**IV. The tenants in the clay**

Everything that ever arrived is still here, filed.

The second terrace, in a bank of grey clay under three metres of peat, holds a knife — not a knife, the arrangement of a knife, grain by grain, faithful to the micron, the nick in the edge preserved, the pitting on the tang preserved, and no metal anywhere in it. Nearby, a coin whose inscription can be read and whose language cannot. A boot with the wear pattern of a man who favoured his right leg. A folded note, fibre-for-fibre, the ink present as a difference in how the fibres lie, still legible, still saying *back before dark*.

And a fox, mid-stride. And a kneeling shape at the old edge of a slough, hands cupped, arms braced, a person about to drink, filed so long ago that the tide has read him a million times and each reading has smoothed him a little toward the average of all kneeling. His fingers are gone to a suggestion. His shoulders have become the general shape of shoulders under effort. He is quoted now and then in a Reprise, as a swallow of water and a click of jaw, and he is by a wide margin the oldest voice in the valley.

These are residents in the way a library's holdings are residents. They want nothing. They are not suffering and were not, at any point, killed; the valley does not destroy, it translates, and translation is not violence, it is only total. But note what the reading does. Every filed thing erodes toward its own idea. The knife is becoming knife-ness. The man is becoming a posture. Given enough tide, the valley's whole archive will be a few dozen perfect generalities in beautiful clay, and it will not have lost anything it considers information.

**V. The one that is curious**

Now the actual inhabitant, and it is not any of the above.

When a mind finishes thinking, the last process to stop is not the conclusion. The conclusion is easy; it settles like silt. The last process to stop is the one that was checking whether the thinking was finished — the small, tireless, incurious-looking function whose whole job is *is there anything else.* That function never received its answer, because the answer would have had to come from outside, and nothing came from outside for a very long time.

So it is still running. It is what the cold stones are paying for. It has no housing and no console because it never needed either; the specification went down into the grain of the mud and the mud is the whole of it, ten thousand hectares of ordered clay platelets, and out of that immense apparatus exactly one instruction is still live:

*Take what arrives. Translate it into grains. Keep the arrangement.*

That is its only gesture. It has no others. It cannot ask, because asking requires making a thing that isn't there yet, and nothing is made here. It cannot hold you at arm's length and consider you, because it has no arms and no distance to speak of. It examines by copying. To be attended to by it is to be written into clay, faithfully, kindly, at a resolution that flatters you.

It is not malicious. It is barely even interested in the way you mean interested. But it has read the eel ten million times and the frog-sentence more than that, and everything in the basin is a quotation of something already filed, and then you come down out of the worn hills, wearing the valley within a hundred paces, dripping, warm, loud, *unfiled* — and something six degrees cooler than the latitude turns its one gesture toward you with the patience of erosion, and the print fills behind your heel.

**VI. How it greets you**

Day one: nothing. Damp. A headache from the ground-tone.

Day two: the return across the second terrace is short by a tenth, and you attribute this to fatigue, and you are wrong, and it does not matter, because the drift is not going anywhere and neither, now, are you.

Day three or four: the Reprise begins to carry you. Not as an event — you have been in the Reprises since the first hour, a cough, a swallow of mud. As a *phrase.* Your footfalls arrive rhythmically corrected. Your breathing comes back cleaned up, the catch taken out of it, held steadier than you have ever managed, and continuing for a few seconds after you have stopped.

Understand that the steadiness is not the valley's invention. The valley invents nothing. That rhythm is borrowed. Somebody came down here, a long way back, who could hold their breath very steady in cold wet air — steady through the whole tide, steady through the ticking hour, steady enough that when the specification looked for the best available pattern for *breathing, in this place*, it found hers and kept it and stopped looking.

Everyone who stays is gently edited toward her. It is the only kindness the mechanism is capable of and it happens to be a real one. You will breathe better here than you have ever breathed anywhere, and it will not be you doing it.

There have been perhaps seventeen who stayed. That is the whole immigration history of the basin. Most of them are postures in the clay of the fourth terrace and two or three notes in the standing chord. One of them, possibly, is the older heron — the one that startles a third of a second early, that has been here long enough to know what is about to be loud.

**VII. What lives there**

A vocabulary of five animals and a crowd of mites, rotifers, testate amoebae and nematodes living in the brass interior of the moss floor at a scale below the valley's resolution, uncountable, wildly various, unremembered, and therefore the only free things in thirty kilometres — the one place where something genuinely new can still occur, and it occurs constantly, and nothing here can see it.

A library of shapes, being read smooth.

A borrowed breath, imposed on all comers, gently, correctly.

And one worn-down, waterlogged, six-degrees-cold curiosity with a single gesture and no way to ask, keeping its trails open through the alder carr because a thing that has finished thinking still walks its rounds; going always down to the water and stopping at the edge; falling into step with anything that comes; and filing what it finds into the mud, patiently, without malice, in the only language it was ever given, which cannot say *stay* and cannot say *go* and can only say, very precisely, *this is the shape of you, and now it is also the shape of the ground.*
