# What Lives Here

## The Census

Three species you can see, and none of them is from here.

Nothing in this basin evolved in it. Nothing feeds on anything else that lives in it. There is not, and there has never been, a predator in the bowl — no fox, no marten, no snake, no corvid, nothing that stalks, nothing that ambushes, nothing that has to be watched for. Every animal in the basin arrived from somewhere else, and every calorie in the basin was manufactured somewhere else and blown in. The food web here has no internal edges at all. It is two lines running in parallel from the lowlands, neither of them touching the other, neither of them closing.

That is the first thing to understand about the residents. They are not living in an ecosystem. They are living in an appliance.

## The Birds

Start with the twelve.

Each of the great spouts leaves the wall at the lip and does not touch rock again for seven hundred metres. Behind each one, therefore, is a shaft of air: a chimney the width of a room and the height of the cliff, walled on one side by wet stone and on the other by a rope of moving water as thick as a man's body. Twelve of these. They are the only volumes in the basin that are not being rained into from above, and they are the only real estate.

There are perhaps three hundred pairs, distributed unevenly — the fourth and ninth spouts are crowded, the second nearly empty, and nobody knows why, and the distribution has probably been the same for a very long time.

They are the size of a thrown glove. Sooty, close-oiled, with the wings held out as two thin sickles and a body that is essentially a fuel tank with a mouth on the front. The face is nearly black, the feathering around the eye dense and matte and set deep in a bony hood: sunglasses grown out of the skull, in a place where the light comes up off the floor as hard as it comes down from the sky. The feet are four toes all facing forward, no perching foot at all, a grapple. They cannot stand. They cannot walk. Put one on flat ground and it is a dying animal in an hour.

They come home at about eighty kilometres an hour and they do not slow down. Slowing down is what kills you: the water takes a third of your speed as you pass through it and adds four times your weight in load, and a bird that enters a curtain at cruising speed comes out the other side wet, stalling, and just fast enough to hit the wall with its feet out and hold. A bird that enters cautiously comes out the other side falling. There is nothing below to fall onto but travertine.

Inside, behind the water, is the only place in the basin where the stone is going backwards. Everywhere else the calcite is laying down; here the plunging water is aerated and hungry and takes rock away, so the alcoves deepen by some unmeasurable fraction per century. The birds are the only tenants in the bowl whose house is being excavated for them rather than sealed shut.

They build with spit and with whatever they have swallowed. There is no plant material available to them anywhere in this landscape — no twig, no grass, no moss, not a fibre — so the nests are made of hardened saliva and aerial junk: thistledown from ninety kilometres away, spider ballooning-silk, the wing-cases of beetles that were never going to land anywhere good, a fine grey felt of atmospheric lint. And spray finds the nests anyway, and lime comes with the spray, so that any nest left unrepaired for a season begins to petrify. Under the occupied cups, in every alcove, is a shelf of ancestral nests turned to stone — hundreds of them, fused, ochre-banded, a coral of dead housing — and the living birds build on top of their forebears' calcified saliva without any idea that this is what they are doing. The shelf is the deepest archaeology in the basin. It is also the only architecture, and it was made by mouth.

They do not call. Not here. Not one note, not a contact chirp, not an alarm — and there is nothing to be alarmed by, and a call in this basin would be a candle held up in a furnace: the roar is broadband and flat and would take any sound they made and dissolve it before it travelled a body-length. So inside the bowl they communicate by touch, by shoving, by the shiver a returning bird sends through the shared rock of the shelf, and by silhouette against the moving water, which is the one dark thing in a place with no shadows.

Two kilometres up, though, out over the valleys in the free air where they feed, they scream continuously. Long tearing shrieks, parties of forty of them wheeling and shrieking through the aerial plankton, the loudest small birds for a hundred kilometres. They have an entire voice. They simply have no use for it at home. No one who visits the basin ever hears it, because it happens only where nobody is standing.

The fledging is the worst thing here, and it is not dramatic, and it takes about four seconds.

A young bird that has never flown must get out of a hole in a cliff behind a moving wall of water. It cannot launch across, because the curtain is a metre away and it would be flattened into it. So it does the only available thing: it drops. It falls down the air shaft behind the spout, in the dark, in the noise, accelerating, for anything up to a hundred and fifty metres before it has wing-speed enough to steer, and then it breaks sideways out through the water into the light.
