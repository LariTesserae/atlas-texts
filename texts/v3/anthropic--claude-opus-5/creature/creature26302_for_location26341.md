## First Impression, and How It Is Wrong

For the first hour on the buttress-road you take the massif for uninhabited, because you are looking for a town — a cluster, a huddle, smoke going up in one place. There is no cluster. The population is spread over the fabric as thin and as evenly as the lichen is, and for the same reason, and what you eventually see is not people but movement: a hairline of it along a parapet two thousand feet up, a slow bead going sideways on an incline, three specks converging on a flue-mouth and staying there all afternoon. You resolve them one at a time, and then you cannot stop resolving them, and by evening the whole face is crawling and you understand you were looking at a settlement the size of a province and mistaking it for texture.

They have never counted themselves. There is no census. There is, by contrast, an extremely exact register of joints — every running foot of pointable face on the mountain, its last date, its sand, its assessed condition — and when officials need a figure for the population they take the footage and divide, because what a person *is*, administratively, is a quantity of wall that gets attended to.

## Bodies

Short. Deep in the chest, from the altitude, with a resting breath so slow that lowland physicians who examine them think they are being lied to. Broad flat hands, the palms pale and papery from lime, the fingernails filed back to nothing because a nail is something to lose. On nearly everyone, a hard shine of callus along the back of the left hand and the outer left wrist: that is from the wall. They walk with a hand on it. Children are taught to, on the theory that a child touching brick is a child not going over the edge, and nobody stops when they grow up, so a crowd moving along a terrace makes a continuous dry brushing sound against the stretcher courses, like a broom, and the bottom four feet of every much-used face is polished to the colour of a saddle.

Squint-lines cut deep and pale, fanning back to the ears, from a lifetime of the eyeshades. Under the shades, when they take them off at night, the eyes are surprisingly soft and dark and the skin around them is two shades lighter than the rest of the face, so that everyone looks faintly, permanently astonished.

They grind their teeth. All of them, from the hum. Tooth-work is a respected trade and the dentists here are among the wealthiest people on the mountain, and they are also the closest thing the place has to historians, because they keep casts.

No one has vertigo. They have no word for a fear of heights. They have three words for fear of depths, and one of them is used only of adults, and gently.

## Hands, Ears, Voices

The important organ is the hand, and the second most important is the ear, and the mouth is somewhere well down the list.

They are loud, close talkers who stand a forearm apart and speak into the side of your face, and they lip-read constantly without knowing they do it; ask one to close her eyes while you talk and she will lose about a third of what you say and become irritable about your diction. Alongside speech there is the hand-count, which came up out of the foundries in the deep courts where nothing can be heard at all, and which has spread over the whole mountain because it is better than speech for the two things that matter most: numbers and directions. A price is agreed in silence. A route through the flues is given in silence. Two people who have been married forty years conduct most of an evening in silence, one hand each, the other hand on a cup.

The listeners are their own people. They are the ones who sound walls — strike, and wait, and know — and it is not a mystical trade, it is a trained one, and the training is unpleasant. An ear-child is apprenticed at six, works in the dark, is taken through the flue system by rope, and is not permitted to speak while working, for years. What the listeners have instead of a map is a body of route-songs, and no listener owns more than a fragment of the whole, and the fragments overlap unevenly, and when something goes wrong deep in the circulation eleven of them will sit in a room and sing at each other for a night and a day until a path assembles between them. They are not secretive about this. They will tell you cheerfully that no one has ever known the flue system and no one ever will, and that a thing known by one person is a thing that can be lost.

## The Still Hour

Once a week the counterweight's bell rings on its way down, and the mountain stops.

The boilers are banked; the mills disengage; the whistles are silent; the goats are hobbled and muzzled with soft leather. For one hour nobody speaks anywhere on the mountain — not indoors, not in bed, not to a baby, and the babies, who are used to it, mostly comply. What you hear in that hour, if you are standing anywhere on a middle terrace, is water dripping over about four cubic miles of brick, the wind at the top, and tapping. Thousands of small hammers, near and far, uncoordinated, going on and on across the whole face. It sounds like the beginning of rain that never arrives. Visitors weep in the Still Hour with some regularity and the locals find this excessive but not incomprehensible.

This is also, incidentally, how the children learn to read lips: they are turned loose in the one hour of the week when adults cannot say no out loud.

## Households

A household is a wall-debt with people attached.

The house is the appendage. It hangs off the face on iron brackets, it leans its back against the terrace above, and it can be rebuilt in a season by any competent crew — and is, regularly, and nobody is sentimental about the timber. What cannot be rebuilt and cannot be moved is the length of face the household owes days on, and that is what is inherited, and what is negotiated over, and what people mean by their name.

Kinship follows the debt. If you marry in, you are *pointed in* — the verb is exactly the verb for repointing a joint, and it is used without irony, and the ceremony involves mortar. If a household is short of hands it takes in hands, from anywhere, from the lowlands, from a widowed crew three terraces over, from the buttress-road; the child of the house and the pointed-in stranger have the same standing and the same obligations, and the old formal boast of a great family is that *our mortar is younger than our brick* — meaning almost none of us came from here, and we are the stronger for it, and the joints are wider than the courses now, and this is not decay, this is the settled state.

Conversely a household can be *cut out*. This is done by the terrace, not by the family, and it happens when the days are not worked, and it is the only real punishment the mountain has, and it is final in a way that no lowlander quite believes until they see it: the brackets are unbolted, the timber is sorted for reuse, the face is taken over by neighbours, and the name is struck from the register of joints and does not go anywhere else. There is no exile. There is simply no longer a household of that name, and the people who were in it are absorbed, one by one, into other crews, and they keep their given names and are not shunned and are never, ever asked about it.

## Eating

Figs, eight ways, including a black paste that keeps for years and tastes like a cellar. Eels from the cisterns, which are netted by children in flat boats by lamplight, an activity of unreasonable joy. Mushrooms out of the cold flues, grown on the leaf-turf, four varieties, one of which is only eaten by listeners and is probably why some of them are the way they are. Goat, rarely, and goat's milk constantly — and the milk of a municipal goat belongs, by a ruling of some genius three centuries back, to whoever maintains the parapet it was grazing when it was milked, on the ground that the milk is condensation that has passed through an animal. There is a whole literature on this. There is a whole literature on everything of this kind.

Grain comes up from the lowlands and is therefore ceremonial. Bread is eaten at pointing-in, at the return of a crew from the deep works, and on the night a fig root is cut. Salt comes up. Copper comes up. Iron comes up. Nothing much comes down but dye, fibre, cordage, dried fig, guano, and litigation, so the mountain has been quietly accumulating metal in its skin for as long as it has existed, and the brown crust that flakes off the brackets in plates the size of a hand is, if you like, its only excretion.

## Dress, and the Four O'Clock

Fibre, spun on the dry terraces, woven anywhere, dyed in the works to a range of about nine colours, of which four are legally meaningful. Smoked-glass eyeshades on a cord, worn on the forehead when not in use, so that everyone appears to have two extra eyes. Bare feet on wet clinker, because clinker under a bare wet foot is the safest surface humans have ever made; wooden clogs above the cloud line, where the brick is dry and hot enough to blister.

And the cape. Waxed, hooded, cut long behind and short in front for stairs. When the fog whistle goes — one long, one short, from the reservoir courts, thirty to forty minutes ahead of the flood — a mile and a half of terrace puts its hoods up in what is very nearly a single motion, and then goes on working, and within the hour there is nobody visible on the entire mountain and the whole population is at arm's length inside the white, shouting at each other from ten feet away in perfectly ordinary tones.

## Names and the Polite Question

A full name is a given name, a face, and a number: *Sabra, Weeping Face, four-and-a-half*. The number is not an address. It is what she took out last season — eleven feet, or four and a half, or nineteen if she has a big crew and a soft joint — and it changes, and a person's name therefore changes annually, and old people who no longer point are called by their last figure for the rest of their lives, which is how you can tell, at a glance, in any room, who has finished.

The polite greeting is *how much did you take out?* It functions the way *how are you* functions, and like *how are you* it is answered with a number and a shrug, and like *how are you* it is occasionally answered honestly, and then you have to sit down and listen for an hour, and this is considered a normal cost of civility.

## Down

They will not discuss it, and it is worth understanding that this is not mystery-mongering. Ask about the depth of the mountain and you will get the same face you would get for asking a host how much money is in the house.

Here is what can be said without impoliteness. The furnaces are fed on leaf-turf cut from the coffers on the forty-year rotation. The coffers take the leaf-fall of the whole mountain, and they take the dead. Not as a rite of disposal — as a stage of the fuel cycle, entered on the same ledger, in the same hand. A body goes into the coffer at the top with the season's leaves, and forty years later it is cut out as black turf that burns clean and slow, and it goes down the chutes to the furnaces, and it comes back up through the flues as the mild sulphurous breath that keeps the terraces at the temperature of a good greenhouse, and that is the reason there is no frost, and everyone knows it, and it is the plainest fact on the mountain and also the one they will not make speeches about. *Loads going up have right of way over everything, including funerals*, and the reason funerals can afford to wait is that a funeral here is not going far and is not in a hurry, and the destination is a brick box on the same terrace, and the actual arrival is scheduled for two generations out.

The stokers work in bands by depth, and each band is hereditary, and each band knows only its own two staircases. The lowest band anyone will admit to belonging to works a landing where the turf is tipped into a hatch. Below the hatch is a chute. Hands come up out of the chute and take the turf.

They are hands. That is all anybody will say about them — hands, four to six of them, grey with dust, and they come up and take, and if the turf is late they knock. There is a formal greeting spoken at the hatch, in a dialect nobody on the terraces speaks, and every stoker in that band learns it
