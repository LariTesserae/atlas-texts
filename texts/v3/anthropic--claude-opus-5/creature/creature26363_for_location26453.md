## The Kept

### Bodies

They are small, and they are heavy.

A trader from the reed country will notice the second thing before the first. You take a woman's forearm to help her up onto a boat-rail and it is like lifting a limb of wet oak. They are short — a tall man on the mound stands about five foot four — and narrow through the shoulders, because for eight hundred years the lanes have decided who could walk them comfortably, and broad-shouldered infants have grown into adults who bruise. But the water they drink and cook with and wash in is the same water that is building the town, and it builds them too, quietly, from the inside. Their bones are dense as ivory. Their teeth are excellent and worn flat by thirty from the grit that gets into everything. Their tendons thicken; the sheaths of their wrists go ropey and pale.

None of them can swim. Not one, ever, in living memory or dead memory. They live in a marsh and they sink like dropped tools, and so the marsh is crossed only where it is ankle-deep, and there is a word for water above the knee that is also the word used of a sealed room.

Their skin is the colour of the inside of a shell and permanently a little softened, a little wrinkled at the fingertips, the way yours goes after an hour in a bath. Feet are white and thick-soled and scarred in fine hatchings from the terrace rims, which cut children until the children learn to read a lobe of stone by its shine. Their pupils are large. They see remarkably well in the slotted half-dark of the roofed lanes and badly in open daylight, and on the dry-wind holidays, up on the roofs, they sit with rush visors over their eyes, squinting out at the silver flats like people watching a fire they don't quite trust.

Hair goes stiff and pale at the ends whether they like it or not. Some shave. Some lean into it: a young woman will dip the last hand-span of a braid into the overflow and leave it a fortnight and come away with a rigid white plait she can knock against a doorpost, and this is considered either very beautiful or very silly depending on who her mother is.

They smell of warm mineral, of wet chalk, of snail broth, and faintly — not unpleasantly — of eggs.

### Hands

Everything about them is in the hands.

Palms are soft from the water, but the pads of the fingers and the knuckles are hard, glossy, slightly flattened, like the heels of well-used tools. This is from tapping. They talk through the fabric of the town more than they talk through air, and a person's knuckles are their voice, and you can hear an old man's age in his knock the way you might hear it in a throat: a little dulled, a little slower on the rolls.

Their spoken language is a whisper-language. Voices die in twenty paces here, so nobody ever needed volume, and over generations the tongue has drifted toward sounds you can see — heavy on the lips, the teeth, the shaped breath. Their faces are extraordinarily mobile when they speak, mouths working with an exaggeration that outsiders find theatrical and mocking, and which is only the residue of a hundred generations who needed to be understood at a distance of six inches in a dripping tunnel. In the dark they speak by touch: two fingers laid along the speaker's jaw and throat, the other hand on the shoulder. Every child learns this before walking. It means that among them, intimacy is not a metaphor. To have something private to say to a person, you must be close enough to feel their pulse.

The tapped register, by contrast, is public — the floor carries it to everyone — and so it is formal, exact, unambiguous, and rather beautiful. It is the language of announcement, of law, of grief, of emergency. It cannot lie easily. There is a five-note figure for *the pulse has changed*, and there is a three-note figure that is an obscenity so carrying and so unretractable that a man who taps it in temper will be paying for it in scraping-labour for a season.

### Names, and what a person owns

A person here does not own a house. A person owns *openings*.

The register is a lattice of maintenance: this sill, that stair-tread, the four hand-spans of lane outside a door, the vent above a sleeping shelf, the third and fifth lobes of a particular terrace edge. These rights are the only property that matters, and they are the only thing that can be inherited, given, traded, gambled, or lost. A wealthy household is one whose obligations are contiguous and convenient. A poor household holds a scatter of scraping-rights on four different levels and spends its whole life climbing.

So names are surfaces. A man is *the low west sill under the willow*, and if he marries into a household on the north terraces his name changes with his rights, and old people are frequently called by names nobody living recognizes, out of affection, because they are still the names of doorways that closed a long time ago. There is a small dignified profession — the reckoner — whose entire life is the recitation of the register in walking order, an oral cadastre that takes most of a day to perform and cannot be drawn, because the town has no plan and never had one and could not have one. Nobody has ever seen the town. They have only ever recited it. It is the closest thing they have to scripture, and it is a work schedule.

Punishment follows from this with a horrible simplicity. They do not exile — forty days' walk in every direction does that for them. They do not imprison; there is no room. What they do to a person who has done something unforgivable is *stop scraping toward them*. Nobody scrapes the lane approaching that door. Nobody clears the vent above that roof. The stone is patient and the offender is one pair of arms, and the closing gains a thumb's width a year, then more, as the shape of the lane changes and the water finds new threads, and the offender scrapes and scrapes and loses ground. Sometimes it is reversed after a year, and the whole neighbourhood comes with paddles, and this is the town's happiest ceremony. Sometimes it is not.

### Children

There are perhaps sixty children on the mound at any time, and until a certain birthday they are the only people permitted to be loud.

They are also the workforce for the thin places. Every household has crawls too narrow for adults — the crevices between grown-together rooms, the wicking seams behind the sleeping walls, the places where the froth-stone puffs up unseen and would eventually shoulder a wall out of true. Children go in with a paddle and a rush lamp and lie on their sides for an hour in the wet dark, scraping, listening to the tapping of the town go past them through the rock, and this work is loved, resented, boasted about, and remembered forever.

It ends the day you no longer fit. There is a slot in a pillar near the crown, a hand's breadth of unscraped stone left deliberately unkept since nobody knows when, and it is the measure: you turn sideways, you breathe out, and if you cannot pass, you are an adult. Because the slot itself closes at the same rate as everything else, each generation is measured against a narrower gate than its parents, and so adulthood has been arriving earlier and earlier for centuries. This is the great constitutional argument of the mound. One faction has wanted to scrape the slot back to true for eleven generations. The other holds that the slot is the one honest thing in town, the only surface that shows the world what it is actually doing. Nobody has ever won. The slot narrows.

On the day you fail to pass, your mother's household holds the quieting: you are given sour water from the rosette to drink, and your voice is taken — meaning simply that from that evening you speak at the volume everyone else speaks at, and if you shout, people look at you the way one looks at someone who has knocked over a lamp.

### The throat-keepers and the counters

Somebody has to keep the vent open.

The spring is building the mound, and the mound is building over the spring, and if the throat ever paves itself the world ends — this is not a religious statement, it is arithmetic. So four times a year, at the slump of the pulse, a pair goes down into the vent on rush cords with wooden paddles, and works in blood-warm heavy water for the forty beats they have, and comes up when the swell comes, and goes down again. It is not scalding. It is worse than scalding, in the accounts they give: it is thick, and it is loving, and it wants to hold you.

They come out coated. The lime gets into the eyes. Everyone who does this work long enough goes blind, and they know it going in, and they go in.
