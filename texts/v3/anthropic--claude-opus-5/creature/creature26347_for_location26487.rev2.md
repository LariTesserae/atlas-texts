# The Tenants

## First, a correction

You will keep asking who lives here, and the Stand will keep answering, and you will keep not hearing it, because you are listening for a second voice. There isn't one. The floor you are standing on is the resident. The wall you put your hand against to steady yourself in the dark leaned very slightly into the pressure, over the following nine days, and thickened there.

But that answer, while true, is lazy, and it flattens out everything interesting. "The Stock lives here" is like saying a city is made of citizen. There are kinds. There are lives with beginnings and ends, appetites, rivalries, subordinates, livestock, children shipped in boxes, and something I am still not willing to call a magistrate. Let me go through them in the order you are likely to meet them.

## The Draw

Start with the individual, because there isn't one, and the shape of the failure is informative.

Ask what a single organism is here and you get three incompatible answers. Genetically, the Combs are a mosaic: a single block of six stories might be four lineages interwoven so tightly that the pith of one carries the oil of another. Structurally, a block is one body — cut it anywhere and the whole block drops pressure. Metabolically, and this is the one the Stand itself uses, the unit is neither. It is the *draw*: a continuous column of working gradient, from a specific bite of hot seam at the bottom to a specific patch of cold sky at the top, with everything in between subordinate to it.

A draw is a claim. It is held, and it can be lost, and it is the only property in the world.

You cannot see the boundaries. They are not in the architecture. Two towers standing against each other, grown together at four points, sharing a stair, may belong to draws that have been at each other's throats for nine hundred years. The war is fought by breathing. A tower that gets ahead of its neighbor tips its vanes half a degree and lets its spent air — used, warmed, cooled only to the temperature of the floor — sigh sideways instead of straight down. That warm exhalation lands on the neighbor's vanes. The neighbor's cold end stops being cold. Its appetite closes. Within a season it is behind, and being behind means the valves at its root will be reasoned with, and the reasoning goes badly.

Nothing here strangles anything for light. They strangle each other with hospitality. They kill by keeping each other warm.

Lifespans: a Radiator on the rim, a real one, three hundred years to reach its ceiling and then ten or twelve thousand years of standing in it. A Comb tower, forty to two hundred, and death by policy rather than age. The small stuff in the Slack lives a summer, if there were summers.

## The Hands

This is the part I would not have believed from a description, so I will describe it anyway and you can go and put your fingers on one.

Beside every working station in the Combs — every sorting table, every press, every hopper gate — there is a socket in the wall or the floor. It looks like a knot in old timber: a pale ring, twenty centimeters across, slightly raised, slightly greasy. There are millions of them. Most are dormant and have been for a long time, and a dormant socket is just a blemish, and you will walk past four hundred of them before anyone tells you what they are.

When a station's work comes back around, the socket buds.

It takes about nine hours. First a white column, blunt, no wider than your wrist, nosing out and up and finding its height by touch against the table edge. Then it darkens, hour by hour, from the base upward, the pith curing as it goes, until it is the color of wet tobacco and hard as a good handle. Then, last, in the final ninety minutes, the end of it splits, and splits again, and there are fingers.

Five digits. Two of them oppose. The long ones have an extra joint between the knuckle and the palm, so that a finger can fold flat against itself in a way yours will not, and the fifth is short, stiff, set far back at the base of the palm — a heel-thumb, a lever, not a manipulator. That is what the notch is for, on every tool grip in the Stand, the one you kept turning over in the dark trying to work out which way up it went. It goes that way up. It has always gone that way up. It was never for you.

The hand has no nerves. There are none on the plateau. It works on pressure and tropism and a resin timing body up in the forearm about the size of a plum, and consequently it works slowly: four seconds to close, six to open, and a full sweep of the arm across a sorting table is a movement you can watch the way you watch a minute hand. It is extraordinarily precise and completely blind. It finds the tool because the tool is always in the same place, because the plan says so, and if you move the tool ten centimeters the hand will spend the rest of its life closing on nothing, patiently, every eleven seconds, and the station will produce nothing, and in forty days the socket will bud a replacement into the same empty air.

They live six to forty days. Then they dry from the fingertips back, go brittle, and are snapped off at the wrist by whatever grows next. Under any station that has been running a long time — and they have all been running a long time — the floor is a drift of them. Brown, curled, light as paper husks, stacked in a slope against the table leg, going to powder at the bottom. It is knee-deep in places. You will put your foot in it before you know what it is.

Now the part that matters. **The hands did not come first.** The tools did. The Stock never saw the thing that bred it; that was over before any tissue now alive was seeded. What it has is the negative: hundreds of thousands of grips, dressed and cool and stacked in the warehouses, each one the exact hollow of a hand that has been gone for an age. And the plan says the goods must be finished, dressed, checked, and racked, and finishing requires a hand, so the Stock read the hollow and grew the fill.

They are correct only in grip. Where the grip touches, they are perfect — the span, the flexure, the heel-thumb, the cant of the wrist. Everywhere else they are plant: no nails, no creases, no skin in the sense you mean, a rod of hardened resin where a bone would be, and above the wrist no arm at all, only a stalk. The Stand is not growing its gardener. It is growing the four or five dimensions of its gardener that ever touched the work, over and over, several thousand times a day, everywhere, and throwing them away.

At the double stations, two sockets face each other across the belt and bud together, left from one wall and right from the other, and they pass a part between them, and it looks exactly like collaboration, and it is not even one organism.

## Inherited wear

The bench with a groove worn in it by nothing.

I want to be exact about this because it is the single strangest fact in the Stand and it is easy to let it slide past as atmosphere. That groove is not wear. It is grown. The pith is laid down already hollowed, with a harder, differently-cured lip along both edges where a worn edge would have polished. The Stock grows the dent. It grows the shine on the third stair where a foot would have turned. It grows the gouge in the lintel of a particular doorway in the eastern Combs, at a height of two meters ten, where something once carried something through and misjudged — and it has grown that gouge into every copy of that doorway in every re-laid street for as long as there have been streets, thousands of times, with the splinters, with the direction of travel legible in the tear.

There is a dark oval on the floor of a station in the fourth Comb block. Forty centimeters, faintly oily, distinctly pigmented. Something stood there, once, and dripped. The Stock has no idea what a drip is. It cannot make liquid that behaves that way. It reproduces the stain as a patch of different chemistry, laid in pigment by pigment, at every instance of that station, forever.

This is what I mean when I say the plan is not up for discussion. The plan does not distinguish between the design and the damage. Everything that was true of the original is true of the copy, including the accidents, and the accidents have now outlived the building, the industry, the species, and the world they happened in. The Stand is a rubbing taken off a corpse, and it is still being taken.

## The Counters

There is decision here, and you can visit it.

In the deep Combs, where six duct runs converge, there are chambers I have come to call junctions and would call chapels if the word did not import too much. A junction is a room four meters across with no doorway sized for anything — you get in on your belly through a maintenance throat — and its walls are solid with valve-bodies. Hundreds. Each one a resin bulb the size of a fist, each one plumbed to a thermocouple line and a pressure line and to the other bulbs, each one holding its position against a spring made of its own cured self.

They tick. Not in unison. You lie in the dark in the middle of that and the ticking comes from all sides at three or four different rates, and under it there is a slow soft sighing as bodies bleed pressure to each other, and it smells like hot bay leaves and machine oil, and there is nothing to see, and nothing is looking at you.

That is where towers are sentenced. Not by a mind. By quorum: enough bulbs holding enough pressure long enough that a master body turns a quarter turn, and a kilometer away, in the night, in an unremarkable street, a root valve shuts and a tower that has stood for a hundred and sixty years begins to cook.

I have lain in a junction and felt a decision go through the wall under my shoulder. There was no drama. Something the size of my thumb moved about four millimeters. Ninety seconds later, far off, a *whump* and the long dry sifting.

## Livestock

The belts are alive, and they are the only domesticated thing I have ever seen that is domesticated by a plant.

Belt-tissue is a fork of the Stock that gave up everything. No roots, no vanes, no gradient, no draw. What it does is crawl, in one direction, slowly, forever, a segmented rope of woven tendon-fiber pulling itself hand-over-hand along its own length. In the wild — if it was ever wild — I assume it crept along the floor of somewhere warm, eating spill. Here it is spliced into loops and hung on burl pulleys and fed. At intervals along every run there is a trough pulley, wet with the straw-colored oil, and the belt drinks as it passes.

It cannot reproduce. Its gametes are nonsense. New lengths are cut by the hands from nursery stock kept in a dozen rooms in the middle Combs, spliced in wet and sealed and sent round. It is kept, it is fed, it is worked, it dies of abrasion in about nine years, and it is replaced.

And the whole of the Stock's sex rides on its back.

## The children

In the sealed sleeves. You will have handled one without knowing — they come along the belts with everything else, cases the size of a loaf, keyed lids, a little heavier than they look, faintly warm even out of the heat, and the smell when a sorting table cracks one open is green and camphorous and completely unlike anything else in this world.

The sorting tables are the matchmakers. A lid-key is a ridge pattern, twenty-odd bits of it, read by touch as the sleeve passes a comb of fingers, and the table decides: accept, and the sleeve goes down the hopper line to a receiving Comb and its ventilation shafts and its waiting rooms; refuse, and it is kicked to a return loop.

The return loops are the saddest architecture in the Stand. Somewhere in the eastern Combs there is a run that goes out three kilometers and comes back three kilometers and has, on it, sleeves that have been riding since before any of the towers currently standing were seeded, because the Comb they were keyed to failed, and the plan has no instruction for *stop*. They come round. They go out. They are warm. Some of them, I would bet money, are still viable.

## The digesters, and the only war

In the Slack, growing out of the collapsed Combs, are the reclaimers: hard, dense fruiting bodies, some the size of a fist and some the size of a dog, crusted and split like fired clay. They are the one inversion in this world. Everything else here lives by taking a difference that already exists. The digesters *make* one: mineral rot inside them runs hot, hotter than the crust, and they cook the char they sit in and drink what comes off. Put your hand on one and it will be the first thing in the Stand that is warmer than the thing under it.

They also, very occasionally, get it wrong, and start cooking a wall that is still alive. Then the living Comb grows hands — different hands, short, flat, four blunt digits, a hand for tearing — and the border moves back over a season, and the sound of it is a slow crunching in the dark that goes on for weeks.

And in the same district, only there, is the heterodoxy. Mats. Low sprawling plateless growth with no towers, no ducts, no stations, no plan: a fork of the Stock that dropped the instructions somewhere and lives on waste heat and rubble and grows, by local standards, insanely fast. The hands are grown to tear it out and the hands are losing about as often as they win, and it does not matter, because the Slack is scheduled to be Slack.

I will say this plainly since nobody else will. That mat is the only thing on this plateau that could ever become anything else. Everything else here is finished — perfect, industrious, immortal, and finished. The one lineage with a future is the one the Stand treats as a tumor.

## Whether it is awake

I don't know, and I distrust everyone who does.

Here is what I can offer. Its reflex arc is about a day. Its fastest continuous motion is the eleven-second stroke. Its working memory, if the junctions are memory, runs on pressure and decays in a week. Its long memory is heritable and includes a gouge in a lintel. It defends territory, allocates resources, keeps livestock, culls, quarantines, and disposes of the unusable on a four-kilometer plate of ash that it will not build on and will not forget. It has no eyes anywhere and it has never once, in the whole history of its lineage, been looked at by anything that could look back.

What it does not have is any use for the concept of *other*. There has never been another kind of life here. It has no category for a visitor. It has a category for a flaw in a part, and a category for material, and a category for water, which is refused.

## Last note

I left one of my own tools on a bench in the Combs, in a room where nothing has ever happened. A spanner, steel, knurled grip, my hand's size.

I came back on the twentieth day. The spanner was where I left it, dusted with lipid, untouched.

On the rack in the next aisle over, third shelf, there were nine copies of it in dressed pith, cool, finished, perfect in every dimension that a grip has, with a knurl grown in and no notch at the base, because that hand has no heel-thumb.

They were racked with the rest. They were waiting for collection.

The order is very old and it is still, patiently, in the dark, at temperature, being filled — and it has a new line item, and the line item is me.
