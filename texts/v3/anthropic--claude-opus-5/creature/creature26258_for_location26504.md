## The Reader

First, subtract the travellers.

The cod is not a resident. The cod is a road being used. The hermit crab, at two metres a day, is somebody's refugee, blown off a bank in some earlier gale and still walking home; give it eleven years and it will get there. The brittle star has curled its arms in because it is enduring — it is doing what a body does in a place it did not choose. The dab has buried itself for the same reason a hitchhiker sleeps in a ditch. Even the sea mouse, iridescing in the dark for nothing, is a shallow-water animal who has slipped a little too deep and is making do.

They all have addresses elsewhere. They occur here. Occurring is not living.

To live here — to be *of* the plain rather than caught on it — a thing would have to satisfy conditions that read less like a habitat and more like a renunciation.

It cannot hold position, because position is not a thing that persists.

It cannot build, because the one hearth in a county proves what happens to structures.

It cannot accumulate, because nothing accumulates.

It cannot see, and therefore cannot be seen, and therefore cannot signal, court, threaten, or display; every language of the visible is closed to it.

It cannot navigate by landmark, since landmarks travel a hand's breadth an hour.

It cannot resist burial, and it cannot expect to stay buried.

It must be fluent in interruption. It must be able to stop for a hundred years and resume mid-sentence.

Two things on this plain meet those terms.

---

The first is the film.

That whitish bloom on freshly bared peat is not a visitor and not an accident. It is the plain's permanent population, held in the top metre of everything as spore and dormant cell, distributed so thoroughly that the sediment is less a floor with life in it than a life with floor in it. Number it and the number stops meaning anything. It does not need to find the peat; it is already in the peat, everywhere, asleep, and when a sand wave shoulders off a patch and the eight-thousand-year-old carbon meets water again, the sleepers within a few centimetres of the new surface simply wake, and eat, and pale the black over in a day, and are smothered again within a tide, and go back to sleep with no sense of grievance and no memory of having been awake.

That is a way of living: to be everywhere, briefly, on the plain's schedule rather than your own.

But the film is the pasture. Something eats it.

---

You will find the thing that eats it by its trace, the way you find everything here — with a hand, at arm's length, in water too full to see through.

On a bared peat flat, among the ranked ripples, there will be a place where the ripples are wrong. Not scoured; *interrupted*. A rectangle of floor, perhaps a metre by half a metre, which has been pressed and swept smooth, its ridges taken down, its film licked off, the black underneath grazed to a shine like the worn part of a step. The edges of the rectangle are crisp. It looks less like a feeding scar than like something has read that patch and moved on.

Sometimes, if the tide is slack and you are very lucky and mostly if you are not looking, your hand goes over the next rectangle and the rectangle is still occupied.

---

It is thin. That is the first fact the hand reports, and the hand is startled by it: thin as a wet page, thinner at the margin, thickening toward a low ridge along the middle where whatever it uses for organs is kept. It is about the size of a folded blanket. It weighs, in water, nothing at all — it is ballasted to the exact loaded density of the bottom fog, so that it neither sinks nor lifts, which is why the storm cannot damage it and why it has never in its life had to hold on.

It is the colour of not being looked at: a pale fawn-grey, the shade of the silt it lives inside. This is not camouflage. Camouflage would be an extraordinary extravagance in a place with no light and no witnesses. It is simply what a body becomes when nothing has ever required it to be a colour.

The upper surface is smooth and slightly tacky and gives under a finger like the skin over a knuckle. There is no head. There is no front, because a front implies a destination. Both ends do everything; the animal is a palindrome. On the top, a dusting of pigment cups that can register light and that have, in this individual's entire existence, been used perhaps four times, on four calm days a generation apart, reporting each time the same fact: *up is where there used to be light.* It has no idea what to do with the information. Neither did its ancestors, who inherited the equipment from the shallows and have not needed it since the plain went under.

The underside is the animal. Turn it — it does not resist, it has nothing to resist with — and it is a felt of millions of fine papillae, each the length of an eyelash, all moving, all tasting. This is a mouth the size of a bedsheet. It feeds by lying down. It presses its margins to the floor, becomes for a while a lid on its own square of the world, and takes that square apart: the film first, then the fabric under the film. It sorts as it eats. Everything inedible is passed back out behind the moving edge, cleaned, graded, laid down in order — and its castings are indistinguishable from the plain, because the plain is largely made of them. Here at last is the agent of the rule. *Violence that conserves flatness*: something has to do the conserving, and it does it with its stomach, at a rate of a few square metres a month, for centuries.

---

It does not travel. It is *carried*, which it has learned to prefer.

When the current comes up, it lifts one margin, and the bottom fog takes it and it goes — a kilometre, three kilometres, high in the smoking crests of the sand waves, tumbling slowly with the eelgrass wrack and the shell crescents, indifferent, unhurt, part of the bedload. Six hours later the tide reverses and brings it most of the way back. Over a decade the journeys cancel. Over a century it has stayed in the same county without ever once having stayed anywhere. It has crossed the ghost of the same river eleven thousand times and knows it as a taste — a run of coarser gravel on the outer bend, a floor of stiffer clay.

And when it is buried, it stops.

The margins seal. The papillae fold in. Metabolism goes down to a rumour. Under half a metre of sand it becomes, functionally, a stratum. It has no way to dig; digging would be a claim on a place, and it makes no claims. It waits for the dune field to shoulder past and uncover it, which will happen, because everything here is uncovered eventually. The waiting is not suffering. There is nothing in it to suffer with while it waits.

So its age and its life are two different numbers. Ask how old it is and the answer might be two hundred and forty years. Ask how much of that it has been awake for and the answer is nine.

---

What it eats, in the end, is the forest.

Under the film, the peat is leaf litter, and leaf litter is what its underside is built for: a stratum you could take apart leaf by leaf if you had a thousand years of slack tide. It has the thousand years. It takes them in installments of one tide each, a hundred years apart, and it does take the litter apart leaf by leaf — alder, hazel, willow, the pressed sheets of a spring that ended eight thousand years ago, each one separated and tasted and reduced. Its chemistry is tuned to nothing else. It can find, from three hundred metres downstream in a running tide, the plume that comes off newly bared peat; it can distinguish reed rhizome from root wood; it can taste a hazelnut. Somewhere in it there is an organ whose only function is the recognition of drowned forest.

It is the only thing in this ocean that knows what the plain was. It knows in the way a tongue knows, without a single image, without the concept *tree*, and it is not haunted, and it will never be finished. Do the arithmetic and one animal, in two centuries, gets through about a hectare of Mesolithic woodland floor. There are hundreds of square kilometres of it. Nothing here culminates. That includes appetites.

---

And it is cut.

Where the peat comes bare, the blades come bare with it, and the flake scars are as crisp as the week they were struck, and a soft thing a page thick that feeds by pressing itself flat across the floor will, sooner or later, press itself across an edge. It happens constantly. It costs the animal nothing it minds. A cut margin seals in a few hours; a deep cut, one that runs in past the ridge, divides it, and both halves close their wounds and go on grazing, and now there are two, identical, drifting apart on the next tide, never to meet.

This is how it multiplies. It has other, duller ways — it can spend itself into the fog as a haze indistinguishable from silt, and does, and the larvae have nowhere to settle that is different from anywhere else, so they settle everywhere and it makes no difference. But the blades are faster. The blades are the only fertility this plain has ever been given.

And the weir still works. Those hazel rods standing in their arc, pushed into the bank of a river that has been ocean for two hundred and eighty generations — a running tide takes the animal into the curve of them, and the rods hold it while the current pulls, and the current wins, and what leaves the trap is two animals where one went in. The fish are gone. The trap has been catching, and dividing, and releasing, on a six-hour cycle, since the water came.

So the total human legacy of this landscape is a population. Not a monument, not a memory, no successor: a few hundred thousand pale sheets lying on the drowned litter of their makers' woods, cut into existence by an afternoon's knapping, grazing the leaf-fall of that same afternoon's autumn, none of which has ever met another of its kind except by being torn from it.

---

There is no name. Nothing named it. It has never made a sound except once, at the moment of lifting off the bed — a small suck, the sound of a page turning, well under the hiss, in a silence the size of a county.

Then the gale above lies down. Over a day the fog settles out of the bottom water and the plain becomes briefly visible: grey, ribbed, absolutely level, running out into a soft green-brown dark. And in that window, on a bare black patch, one of them is lying across an alder root with its margins sealed to the floor, taking a spring apart, four times in a lifetime aware that up is where there used to be light.

Nothing sees it. It does not require seeing. It has been the only inhabitant for eight thousand years and it has never once been at home.
