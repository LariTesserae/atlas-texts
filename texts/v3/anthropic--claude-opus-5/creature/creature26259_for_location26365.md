## They are small, and they look up

The first thing to know about them is where their eyes go. A visitor coming down the baskets watches his feet, because the ground is blades and he does not trust it. The people of the valley watch the ceiling. They do it every few minutes, without knowing they do it, the way other people glance at the weather — a flick of the eyes upward and a small readjustment of the shoulders, and then back to the work. In conversation they will look up in the middle of your sentence. It is not rudeness. It is the only sky they have and it is two hundred metres of stone held together by friction.

They are short. Four generations of narrow gaps, rope-work, low corbelled ceilings and thin food have selected for a compact body: broad across the shoulder and forearm, light in the leg, wide flat feet that grip polished stone. Nearly everyone carries a stoop and a habit called the roof hand — the left arm kept half-raised when moving, palm out, feeling for the rock before the skull finds it. Their greeting is that same raised palm. They do not know it is a shielding gesture; they think it means *I see you*.

Their skin is the colour of the ash rock, pale under a permanent grey film that no washing quite lifts. They have wide pupils and they read faces in a gloom that would leave you groping. Bright light hurts them; they walk *around* the light-pillars rather than through, the way other people step around a puddle, and children dare each other to stand in the middle of one and count. Every family has its allotted light-sitting, though — half an hour on a slab in the sun-shaft, bare to the waist, tipped back with the eyes shut, doled out by a warden who keeps the roster. It is prescribed, not enjoyed. Households too poor to hold a share of a pillar have children whose legs bow, and the bowing is a visible poverty, as legible as rags.

Their hands have no fingerprints. Sand and slurry take the ridges off by the third decade, and the palms come up glazed, faintly translucent, hard and smooth as the wares they sell. The old grinders' hands have a slight sheen in lamplight. Their molars are worn flat by thirty from the grit in their own bread — they mill with stones they make themselves, and the stones give a little of themselves to every loaf. A city that exports smoothness is being smoothed all the way through: hands, teeth, the soles of the feet, the treads of the Ways, the edges of the doorways where a hundred thousand shoulders have passed.

The lungs are the reckoning. Wet work keeps the worst of it out of the air, and everyone binds a damp cloth over the mouth when the slurry runs thin, but the fine stuff gets in anyway. There is a cough here that has a name and a sound and a rough calendar: it starts as a dryness at forty, and it takes about eleven years. Nobody thinks of it as an illness. It is simply what the trade costs, and men in the last stage of it are given the finest grades of polishing to do, sitting work, in the light.

## The hush

They speak in a register you would use beside a sleeping infant, and they do it all day, all their lives, to everyone. Loudness is not merely rude; it has the flavour of obscenity. A man who raises his voice in the Ways will find three strangers with their hands on him, not angry, just firm, walking him to a wall until he is quiet.

The language has moved to fit. It is soft-consonanted and low, thick with breath and sibilance, and it carries astonishingly well along a polished trough of stone — two people can hold a conversation forty metres apart at a murmur. It has more words for *quietly* than for *fast*. It has a whole grammatical mood for things heard but not seen. What it does not have is any word for hammering that is not also a word for sacrilege.

Because you cannot shout across the valley, they tug. Cord is everywhere here — the drip-lines, the hoists, the garden strings — and threaded through the whole scale-work of houses is a second web that carries nothing but messages: waxed lines running over polished stone rollers, in from the districts to the junction lofts, tended by children who sit all day with two fingers hooked through a loop and doze. The alphabet of pulls is learned before speech. Long-long-short is *come*, and every child in Longspill has felt that in the wrist and known it meant a death in the family before they knew what death was.

Where there is not cord there is hand. A full sign-tongue for use in the wet workshops, where a hundred cord-saws hiss and nobody's voice would survive it. Bargaining is done at arm's length, in fingers, silently, in front of a stack of whetstones. And they laugh without sound — a shaking of the chest and a widening of the eyes, and a hand pressed flat to the mouth. A visitor thinks at first that these are a joyless people. They are not. They are simply funny at a volume you cannot hear.

Their music has no strike in it. Bowed gut, reeds, and rubbed bowls; long tones, drone and shift, no beat except breath. When they sing together the effect is of the valley's own hissing given a pitch, and they know this, and it is the point.

The one permitted noise is the crickets. The pale crickets that came down on their own are farmed now in stacked stone bins on cabbage stalk and waste, and eaten roasted by the handful, and they sing. Nobody has ever suggested silencing them. In a city where a dropped tool is an offence, the bins chirp all night and are loved for it, and there is a courtesy trade in loud bins for the houses of the very old, who cannot sleep in real silence any more.

## Cord, light, drip, smoke

Wealth here is measured in four things, and none of them is land.

*Cord-length*, first: how much of a drip-line your household may hang, in fingers and spans, recorded in the Registry. A marriage is a splicing — two households' lines joined with a formal knot in front of witnesses, and a divorce is the cutting of it, done in public, badly regarded. The Registry is a rack of stone leaves, scratched not struck, and it is the heaviest object in the valley. When the Listeners woke the city in the small hours the second time, forty men carried the Registry out first, before food, before children, and everyone agreed that this was correct.

*Light-share*, second: minutes on a slab in a pillar, which move through the year as the shafts swing, so that a doorway which gets eight minutes of direct sun in early spring is a family's inheritance and its festival. They keep time by the pillars' tilt — *when the Crooked Third leaves the Long Block* is an hour of the day, and any child can tell you the season by which slab a shaft is standing on.

*Smoke-draw*, third. There is nothing to burn that does not come down in baskets — dung cake, straw, tallow — and there is no room in a lidded valley for its smoke. Every hearth feeds a flue of cane and thin plate, climbing and jinking through the infill toward the nearest gap, and the right to draw through a good shaft is bought, willed, and fought over. Above the working shafts the ceiling is glossy black for thirty metres and the mosses will not grow. On still days when the air will not lift, the flues stop pulling and the whole city eats cold, and the cold days are counted, and a household that can cook when its neighbours cannot is a household that will do well in marriage.

*Grit*, fourth and strangest. There is no erosion here, and therefore no sand — the abrasive that the entire economy rests on has to be manufactured, ground from ground rock, and sorted into nine grades. The sorting is done by patience and by dead water: pans of the flat, still, bitter sheets, into which slurry is poured and left for a month to settle in layers. The stillness that makes the dead water useless for everything else makes it a perfect scale. The grit-mothers who work the pans are a small, arrogant, indispensable guild, and the finest grade — the one the mirrors are finished with, levigated three times, a season a time — is sold by the spoon and kept in horn.

And so the vainest thing in the valley is also a pan of dead water. Mirrors are for export; to keep one is thought peculiar, showy, a little unclean. When these people want to see their own faces they lie on the rubble at the edge of a flat sheet and look down into it, and see themselves hanging in an inverted forest of light-pillars, and get up and say nothing about it.

## Those who listen, those who can bear the sky

The Listeners hold nothing. No drip-line, no light-share, no shaft. They are fed by levy, they may not marry into cord, and they are the only people in the valley who may be woken at any hour, for any reason, and the only ones who may put a hand on any door. They sleep in ground pits with a plate under the ear. In the pits are also a shallow dish of dead water and a chalked slab, because the best of them go deaf young — jaw-bone work, decades of stone against the skull — and read the tremor as ripple instead, watching the surface for a shiver too small to feel. An old Listener sits deaf beside a dish of bitter water and knows more about the state of the world than anyone above ground.

They speak in a flat clipped mode used by no one else, and their judgements are three: *nothing*, *watch*, *go*. There is no appeal. Twice a *go* has emptied the city in a night; both times it was right; and the crushed quarters are down there under the shifted plates, flat, still full of cisterns, and men called endmen go into them with lamps to bring out cord and tools and the stone leaves of old registries. Endmen are paid well and sat with reluctantly.

At the other extreme are the gap-men, who go up. Someone has to fix the basket lines at the storm-columns, and someone has to deal with the plateau farmers face to face, and this requires a person who can stand under an open sky without vomiting. Most cannot. Sky-sickness is the ordinary condition of these people: a shaft of unbounded blue overhead produces in them a specific horror, a sense of the ceiling having been *taken away*, and there are adults in this valley of forty thousand who have never once been up the ropes and never intend to be. The gap-men can. They are burnt brown, squint-eyed, ruined by light by fifty, and they wear their own city's product against it — cap-rock mirrors ground thin and smoked, bound in leather over the eyes. Coming down, they take a long time to stop talking too loudly. They are tolerated, needed, richer than most, and not quite invited in.
