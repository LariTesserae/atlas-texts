## The Builders

Take one up. It will not struggle much, and it will not make a sound, because it has no organ for making one — no plate to stridulate, no chamber to force air through. What you feel in your palm is a dry ticking, the gullet-mill still turning over the last mouthful it took before you lifted it. That is the loudest noise it will ever make in its life, and it is not for anyone.

Hand-sized, flat as a spread palm, six legs held wide. The grey is not its colour. The grey is the work: pumice flour and gum-dust worked into every seam until the animal is the same shade as the wall it came off. Scrape a shoulder-plate with a nail and what comes up underneath is a deep oiled green-black with a faint spectral sheen on it, a beetle colour, a colour from somewhere else, wasted here where nobody looks at anybody.

The face is the whole animal, really. Two rasps hinged at the sides, a trowel edge below, and between them the intake of the mill. Behind the mill the body forks into two channels that never meet: one for stone and one for food. Pumice goes in, is ground, is wetted with oil and gum, and comes out at the trowel as paste. It is never digested. It never enters the animal. The builder is a short length of pipe with a mason's line running through it, and beside that line, a narrow private one for living on — vent-film, bark-lice honey, hopper-milk, oil from the basins.

Eyes are almost gone. Three pinhole ocelli on the crown that register bright and dim and nothing else. What the builder has instead, all over the front of its face and the undersides of its legs, are heat-pits: fine, dense, exquisite. It reads temperature the way you read light. To it, the crag is not a shape but a gradient — a warm bright core up in the fissures, a cooling shoulder, and far below, an edge where warmth simply ends. That edge is the horizon of the world, and it is visible from every terrace, all day, every day of the eleven years.

It has no direction-sense that is not thermal. In as far as anything in the walls means anything, *up* and *in* and *warm* are one idea, and *down* and *out* and *cold* are one idea, and there is no third.

## The Sound of Feet

They speak by striking the city. A builder standing still with all six feet down is not idle; it is listening with its legs. Comb-pads on the tarsi pick vibration out of the masonry, and the masonry is a good medium — dense, oiled, laid in continuous courses, four hundred years of it grown into one body. Old fat-jointed wall at the base carries long slow pulses a great distance. The fine new work up top carries short sharp ones and dies quickly. There are dead spots, seams, whole galleries that have gone acoustically silent because a vent cooled and the mortar there candled hard and stopped conducting.

News crosses the crag by relay: felt, re-struck, felt, re-struck, three hundred paces in something like a day. That is the speed of thought here. Nothing in this world can be surprised, and nothing can be told anything quickly.

There is a second language and it is chemical, and it is laid into the wall itself. Every mouthful of paste carries a mark of what it was placed for. A nursery smells of nursery. A ramp that leads only to a cold vent smells of that, faintly, for a century. And because they read by touch, the surfaces are marked too — those repetitive ornamental ridges and combings on the arcades are the rasp-strokes of a code that no one designed. It began as wear. It became legible because the animals that could read wear did better on the ramps. Now a builder crossing a bridge in absolute darkness knows from the tread-texture under its feet how far the drop is and which way lies warmth. The finest galleries are palimpsests, rasped and re-rasped by twenty generations, corrected, contradicted, thick with instruction.

They have no mark for *water*, or *rain*, or *sky*, or *far*. They have eleven or twelve distinguishable marks for grades of heat.

## The Day, and the Stiffening

At first light the city comes out to drink. This is the one hour when the terraces are crowded on their outer faces, tens of thousands of bodies on the leaf-curtains and the ramps and the lipped chimney-mouths, working over the white flocculence, taking it off the plates as it melts, riding the oil down plate to plate into the basins. The basins, mostly, are bell-snail shells: empty banded cups mortared into the walls at every level, mouths up, thousands of them, because nothing dissolves here and a dead snail leaves behind a perfect vessel that will still be a vessel in four hundred years. The city drinks out of its snails.

Then the work.
