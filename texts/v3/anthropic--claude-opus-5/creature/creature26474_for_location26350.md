Begin with the rivets, because the rivets are the only wrong thing in the room.

Everything else in the sump was set true. The groins meet where they were drawn to meet; the culvert mouth is level to within a few millimetres over two metres; the trough of the screw was bedded by men who had done nothing else for years and would do nothing else again. Even the decay is orderly. The bricks have rounded evenly, the iron has bloomed evenly, the sag of the arches is the sag the arithmetic always promised.

The repair plates on the buckets are crooked. Every one. Twenty-eight buckets, at least twenty-eight plates, and not a single one square to its bucket. They were set from below and from behind, in the dark, in cold water, at arm's reach above the head of whatever set them, with — you can read this off the peening — a loose stone held in the other hand as a dolly. Some of the rivets were driven cold. Some were driven twice because the first blow slipped. On bucket nine there is a plate riveted over a plate riveted over a plate, three generations of the same failing lip, and the outermost is the worst of the three, which tells you that the hand did not get better. It only kept going.

So: something lives here. Not merely something that exists here, which is thousands of things — the lice with their fur-nap crawl, the eels bricked into the wall like fixtures, the crayfish under its keystone with a furniture patience. Those inhabit. One thing dwells. There is a difference, and the difference is maintenance.

It is in the water now, in the slack behind the wheel, and it will be difficult to see, because it has done what the bricks did.

That is the first true thing about it. It has arrived at its final radius. Every place a body normally keeps a corner, it has given the corner up — the knuckles nursed down to low domes, the elbows and the heels gone smooth and gently swollen, the shoulders sloped into the neck without an interruption, so that the whole of it reads as a field of curves, a cobbled thing, a creature pretending to be a riverbed. The skin is thick, pale, faintly translucent at the margins, hairless, and it is the colour of the inside of a bottle of weak tea. It has no scars that are still shaped like wounds. Everything that could break has broken. Everything that could settle has settled. It will look exactly like this in a thousand years, assuming the wheel goes round, and it does not seriously entertain the alternative.

Size: bigger than the eels and smaller than the wheel. Two metres if it straightened, and it does not straighten. It moves through open water with the economy of something that has never once had to hurry, sculling, mostly, with hands.

The hands are the whole argument. They are too large, the way a mole's are too large, and the palms are worn glossy and slightly concave, and the nails are gone entirely from the working fingers and remain, thickened and useless, on the little fingers of both. The fingertips are ridged with old crush-marks. There is a pad of horn on the outside edge of each hand from bracing against brick. Whatever this creature is descended from, was made from, or fell in as — and the room preserves final shapes, not origins; the room is not interested in where anything came from — it has been holding stones against hot iron for a very long time.

The eyes are large and are not much good, and they are not for seeing. They are for the lamp. It can tell nine flickers a minute from eight and a half. That is the entire visual life: a slowly rotating disc of gold overhead, and its rate.

Because the sense that matters is hearing, and it is not a general hearing. This creature has one abstraction, one only, and it is the difference between a sound that is right and a sound that is going wrong. The sixty-cycle hum, the tearing of the column, the slop-and-gulp, the iron conversation with a stone or two in it, and the two-tone shriek of the bearings every six and a half seconds — that is not noise to it and never was. It is a body's worth of readings. When the shriek arrives at six and a half seconds it means nothing at all, in the way your own pulse means nothing. When it arrives late, the creature is already moving before it could be said to have decided anything.

It keeps the tides. On the bright, loud tide, when the column lengthens and the wheel finds its extra half-revolution and the lamp comes up, it works: the round of the axle housings, the packing of the trough, the paddles, the long crawl along the west wall with its cheek against the brick, feeling the gear train through the masonry rather than listening to it. On the dim tide it eats, and on the low slack of the dim tide it does the only thing it does that has no purpose. It goes out into the middle.

That is worth staying with. Ten metres of open water in every direction, nothing to hold, nothing to mend, three things visible and everything audible; and it hangs there, mid-depth, with its hands loose, for a period that is roughly consistent and not exactly consistent, and then it goes back to the wheel. Call it sleep if you like. It is the only interval in which it is touching nothing.

The grease is rat. The rat that crosses twice a tide, purposeful as a ferryman, is crossing above a tenant that has known the crossing for its whole life, and most crossings are permitted, and some are not. What comes of the ones that are not is a small quantity of soft fat worked between the palms until it is warm — this is the only warmth in the chamber and the creature does not notice it as warmth — and pressed into a bearing housing with the pad of a thumb. A season of that is what has kept a cast-iron axle turning past its design life by a hundred and thirty years. There is nothing tender in it. There is nothing cruel in it either. The eels take a share of the same drowned traffic. It takes perhaps one eel a year, which is few enough that no eel has ever lived long enough in fear to teach the others, which is why they hold station three metres from it with their heads out and their nerve intact, and why the description of them as the top of the food chain is one that they would give and the room would not.

Two more things, and they are the two that make it hard to leave alone.

The first is under the grating, in the one square where daylight lands on masonry. The moss is not tended; the moss can look after itself. But the hart's-tongue fern, pale and etiolated, two fronds and one of them broken, is above the highest waterline and has to be reached for, and it has been reached for. There is silt at its base that did not get there by falling. There is bone-grit worked in around the crown, white and clean, carried up from the ring on the floor a pinch at a time in a fist that had to be held out of water the whole way. The frond is broken because it was handled. This is not gardening. The creature has no concept of a plant and could not tell you what the fern is for, and the fern is for nothing, and it goes up the wall in the dim tide to put dead things around its foot, and it comes back down. That's all. Make of it what you want. The room has one square of daylight in it and one living thing that neither grazes nor is grazed, and the tenant has a habit about it.

The second is the pause.

Watch it work a full round on the axle and you will see it do the near side, and then cross under and do the far side, and then come back to the near side and stop, holding the housing, doing nothing, for about four seconds, and then go on. It does this every time. It is doing both halves of a job that two would divide, in sequence, including the interval in which the other one would have answered. There is nothing in the bone-ring on the floor that would confirm this, because the ring is a hundred and forty years deep and holds rat and eel and bullhead and, low down, some long articulated things with wide flat hands, and the creature visits the ring and does not distinguish. It has no word for how many of it there have been. It has no word, and no plural, and the four-second pause is the last surviving fragment of a grammar in which there was one.

It does not build. Nothing new is made here. It does not decay, or not quickly; nothing does, at eleven degrees. It does not leave — the midge column is the only thing that leaves, and it has watched the midges go up the shaft its entire life with the same attention it gives the bubbles, which is none.

It goes round. Nine times a minute the lamp dips and comes back, and every six and a half seconds the bearings say the thing they say, and the tenant of the sump lies in the slack with one hand flat on the housing, reading it, in a made place that outlived its purpose and kept its habits, worn down to its final smooth shape, and faithful, and not finished.
