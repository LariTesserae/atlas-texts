## The Bell

You hear it before the road turns — except that *hear* is the wrong verb, and everyone who lives up there will correct you for it. Four hours below the rim, at the switchback station where the mules are changed for the cable cars, the sound arrives as a pressure in the sternum, a low occupancy of the chest, as though something large were breathing in the same room. The station keeper hands you a tin of wax and a pair of clay plugs turned on a lathe, and a card printed in four languages that says: *Do not shout. Shouting is not possible. You will hurt your throat learning this.*

Then the road lifts over the lip and the basin opens, and the sound stops being a feeling and becomes weather.

The plateau is a shallow bowl about nine miles across, walled by soft rounded hills that were once sharp and are now the shape of old soap. Everything up here is the color of wet bone: pale tuff, welded ash, streaked with rust where iron leaches out of the seeps. It is warm — a heavy, held warmth that comes up out of the ground rather than down from the sky — and it is wet in the way a cave mouth is wet. Water is never more than a hand's depth beneath the crust. It stands in sheets in every depression, it beads on the undersides of stones, it walks slowly down the cliff faces in vertical smears, and it hangs in the air as a thin milk of vapor that lifts the sunlight and spreads it so evenly that the whole basin seems to be lit from every direction at once. Shadows here are weak and honest. Nothing glares.

At the center is the Throat.

The basin's groundwater collects in a broad brackish sheet, no deeper than your knee, and drains through a fissure roughly the width of a market square, and falls — no one knows how far. Steam comes back up. Water goes down warm and returns as a white column that leans with the wind and never stops. That column, and the falling water inside the rock, and the shaft resonating around them like the bore of an organ pipe, are the sound. It is not a roar exactly. Underneath there is a note, a fat low tone somewhere near the bottom of a man's singing range, and over it a white hiss, and inside both there is a slow beating pulse, five or six seconds long, which the plateau people call the *slack* and which they use instead of clocks.

It has been sounding for at least eleven thousand years. The pitch has dropped a little over a semitone in the eight hundred years since anyone began writing it down. The shaft is widening. This is the fastest thing that happens here.

---

The strangeness of the place is not the noise. It is what the noise has done to the ground.

Stand on the rim and look down and you will see that the floor of the basin is patterned — great smooth ridges and hollows of packed dust and pale gravel, curving in nested arcs and lobes, like the figures sand makes on a drumhead. It is exactly that, at a scale of miles. Eleven thousand years of a single sustained note have shaken the plateau's loose material out of the violent places and heaped it in the still ones. The bedrock underneath has been fretted to match: the ridges are floored with rock left rough, the hollows are polished. The pattern is the sound made visible, lying on the land like a fingerprint, and it is old enough that it counts as geology.

And in the hollows — the *nodes*, where the air scarcely moves and the note thins to something you could talk over — everything gathers. Dust gathers there, and so soil. Water pools there instead of trembling itself into mist. Seeds that land anywhere else are shaken loose before their root hairs can grip, so the only green in the basin is in the nodes: thin bristling meadows of salt grass, a low fleshy succulent with red edges, stands of scalded reed around the warm pools, and lichens in six colors that spread at about the rate of a coin's diameter per generation. From the rim, the vegetation draws the acoustic map for you in dull green on white. You are looking at a picture of a sound, painted by the sound, in plants.

Between the nodes: nothing much. Bare polished pans. Salt frost. The occasional beetle — dun, thumb-sized, no ears at all — sitting in the loudest places for reasons no one has satisfactorily explained, vibrating visibly. There are no birds. Birds pass overhead, high, and do not come down. There are pale eels in the warm seeps that navigate by touch and taste, and there is a breed of low-slung silent hound that lives its whole life within a single node and goes to pieces if carried across a ridge. That is most of the fauna. The land is not hostile so much as *loud*, and loudness turns out to be a kind of aridity.

---

The city is called Hush, after the thing it is built to collect.

It is a real city — forty thousand people, foundries, a cable railway, sodium lamps strung on cast-iron standards, a bonded warehouse, a hospital, a mint — but it is a city with holes in it, because it is not laid out for convenience. It is laid out on the standing-wave map. Every dwelling, school, sickroom, and bed sits in a node. Everything else — the streets, the yards, the long lamplit walks between quarters — crosses the loud ground, and you cross it fast, plugged and hooded, head down, and no one speaks on the way because no one can.

The houses are heavy: corbelled tuff, walls three feet thick, roofs weighted with lead-lined cisterns. And the walls are double, with a four-inch cavity between them, and the cavity is full of water. Water is the cheapest acoustic mass on the plateau and there is no end of it. Great slow pumps at the eastern seeps push it up through a mains network into the wall-cavities of every building in the city, where it circulates warm and eventually weeps out through the mortar, so that every house in Hush is faintly sweating all the time. The lower courses of stone are furred with green. Interiors are hung with felt and oiled hide; the air inside smells of mildew, hot iron, salt, and the tallow they burn in the shrines. You sleep under a damp blanket in a warm room and the note is still there, but reduced to something like the sea in another valley.

The nodes are the wealth. Quiet is real estate. The deep nodes, the ones where two hollows overlap and a person can stand in the open air and hear their own voice, are owned — by families, by guilds, by the city itself. The deepest is not built on at all. It holds the birthing house, a low white building with a garden, and every child born in Hush for six centuries has been born there, because a child born on loud ground never learns to hear, and the plateau, for all its noise, has decided it prefers its people able.

Outside the birthing house is the only lawn.

---

The industry lives in the antinodes, where no one can.

This is the sensible arrangement and it took them nine hundred years to arrive at it. In the loud lobes, where the air itself does the work, they built resonator halls — brick throats and bottle-chambers, tuned to the note and its partials, that convert the standing pressure into a rotating shaft the way a windmill converts a breeze. Add the geothermal side, boiler houses tapping the seeps, and the plateau has more power than it can use: enough for the pumps, the lamps, the cable railway down to the valley, the ore-crushers, and the great cold rooms where they keep food that will not otherwise keep in the wet warmth.

Machines don't mind. People do. So the antinode crews go out in *diving order*: articulated suits of leather and riveted plate, hooded, and filled with water to the collarbone. Water in contact with the body carries the pressure around you instead of into you. A crew going out to service a resonator hall moves at the speed of men wading, which is what they are, and they go in a line, and their lamps swing, and they are the most conspicuous and slowest-moving things in the whole basin. It takes them forty minutes to cross ground you could run in eight. They do not hurry. Nobody up here hurries; the plateau has trained it out of them.

Speech is for the nodes. Everywhere else there is the hand-language — fast, low, held close to the chest, with a vocabulary of about four thousand signs and a famous poetic register — and the slates, and the lamp codes on the towers, and, between intimates, *bone-talk*: forehead to forehead, jaw to jaw, a hand cupped over the join, the words going through the skull instead of the air. It is how business is done in a loud street and how the dying are spoken to. Visitors find it unbearably intimate. Locals find the visitors' habit of talking to the air unbearably wasteful.

---

And the whole thing is walking.

Because the pitch is dropping, the nodes are migrating — outward, mostly, and eastward, at something between a hand's breadth and a foot a year. This is the great slow fact of Hush, the fact that all its institutions exist to manage. A quarter that was quiet when your great-grandmother was married is loud now at its western end; her house is a shell, its wall-cavities drained, its stones being lifted one at a time and carted to the new ground. On the western edge of the city there are five hundred years of abandoned quarters, roofless, cistern-less, filling with salt grass and the red-edged succulent — because the nodes moved on and the plants had not yet been told. On the eastern edge there is new work, scaffolding, wet mortar, the pumps' mains being extended one length of pipe per season.

The Pitch Office keeps the measurements. Twice a month, at slack, a clerk carries a fork and a shallow bowl of water to the marked stone outside the birthing house and takes the note, and writes a number, and the numbers go into a ledger that is longer than the city's other records put together. Every forty years or so the Office redraws the map, and the map tells the masons where to move, and the masons move, and the city crawls across its patterned floor at the rate of a fingernail's growth, having gone about a mile and a half since it was founded, intact the entire time.

At night the lamps come on and the steam column goes copper-colored against the last of the light, and the beetles sit out on the polished pans doing whatever it is they do, and forty thousand people lie in warm wet stone rooms in the hollows of a shape they cannot see, listening — most of them without knowing they are listening — to the slack, the long five-second pulse, the plateau taking one breath after another, as it has, as it will, with no particular regard for anyone.
