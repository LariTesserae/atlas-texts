## The Lap

You come down into it from the north scarp, where the stone has been rounded so long it has stopped rounding. That is the first thing to understand about this place, and it takes a while to notice, because it looks like ordinary weathering: a low shoulder of grey-brown mudstone, soft as soap, scalloped by ten thousand years of drip. But put your hand in one of the scallops and you find its floor is polished and hard, sealed, done. The rain still falls into it. The rain takes nothing.

The basin below is maybe eleven hundred paces across. Call it the Lap. You could walk its rim in an hour if the noise let you think about walking.

---

## Terrain and Water

The Lap is shallow — sixty metres of drop from rim to floor, no more — and floored in a pale calcareous pan the colour of a wet cigarette. It holds a settlement, and the settlement holds a works, and the works occupy roughly a third of the ground and all of the sky.

Water arrives from above and from inside. There is no weather to speak of, only a condition: a low ceiling of self-made cloud at about a hundred and forty metres, fed continuously by the cooling flues, which returns as a fine cold drizzle over the eastern half of the basin and as beaded condensation everywhere else. Every iron surface in the Lap wears its own film of water. Every downpipe runs. The mist has an established boundary — you can stand with one shoulder wet and one shoulder dry, on the Long Riveter, at a place where the paving has been marked by nothing at all, and the boundary will not move while you stand there, or while you live.

A river crosses the floor from west to east in a stone canal: the Drab, four metres wide, olive, opaque, moving at a walking pace. It carries the same volume it has always carried. Its flood marks are two — a low one and a high one — both ancient, both dry, neither ever revisited. There are no braids, no cut banks, no bars. The Drab has finished being a river and now simply performs the office of one.

Nothing grows. Not a stem, not a spore-flush, not the green weep of algae you would expect on wet stone. The only thing that could be mistaken for vegetation is a hard grey crust on the north faces of the slag walls, in scabbed rosettes the size of coins, which is not alive and never was — a precipitate, mineral, laid down by the drip in exactly the shape a lichen would have chosen. It does not thicken. Scrape one off and the scar remains a scar.

## The Built

Low construction, dense in the middle, sparse at the edges: slag-brick, riveted plate, tarred board. Four- and five-storey blocks along the Riveter, blind-walled, with stairs on the outside. Sheds. A gasholder, half-risen, that has been half-risen forever, its guide-frame rusted to that particular deep chocolate rust that has reached equilibrium with the air and stopped. You can rub it and get colour on your fingers but the metal beneath is sound. It will be sound tomorrow.

Between the built things there is a great deal of nothing. Wide yards of empty pan. Streets three times wider than the traffic they carry. The Lap is built at the density of a town but arranged at the density of a moor, and the effect is that every structure stands alone with its own weather of noise around it.

The Cinder Works is the centre: eleven flues, two of them capped, a stamp-house, a melt-shed with a roof of louvred iron, a canal wharf, and the Returning Yard. It runs without tenders. It has run without tenders for a period no one in the Lap can name because no one in the Lap counts.

Its process, so far as it can be followed: the melt-shed takes in shell-matter from the sorting chutes and the crushed spoil, renders it, casts it into grey plates the size of a table-top; the plates travel by rail across the yard, are stacked to a height of exactly nine, and are then taken from the top of the stack by the same crane, in the same order, and fed back into the melt. Nothing leaves. Nothing arrives. There is no product. The works makes the works, and the mist, and the sweetwater, and the noise.

The furnaces glow. Stand at the melt-shed door and you can see white metal moving and you will feel no heat at all. The heat goes up, immediately and entirely, into the cloud-roof, which is why the roof is always there and why the basin is always cool — about eleven degrees, damp, the temperature of a cellar in spring. This is not a mystery locally. It is simply how furnaces work here.

---

## Sweetwater

At two hundred and thirty points around the works, warm condensate drips from bleed-cocks into stone cups worn into the paving. It is faintly sweet, faintly metallic. It is the base of everything.

## Fauna

And now the real inhabitants, and the reason your ears will ring for days.

The gutters are full. Not crowded — packed, solid, a moving mortar of plated bodies. The commonest are the **cuffs**: palm-sized, low-backed, eleven-legged, armoured in overlapping grey-green plates like a stack of thumbnails, with no eyes and a fringe of feeding-combs. They drink at the cups and they molt, constantly, a shell a day each, and the shells go down the drains to the sorting chutes to be melted into plates that will be melted into plates. Their sound is the ground-tone of the Lap — a dry continuous shirr, like rain on a tin roof heard from inside, over the whole basin, from every low place at once.

Above them, on the walls: **clappers**, flat as a hand, the size of a hand, clinging to wet plate by suction and clapping their two body-halves together — a hard wet slap, individually, but there are perhaps four hundred thousand of them, so what you hear is a rolling applause that swells and thins and never stops. They graze the mineral rosettes. They are the reason the rosettes do not thicken.

In the flues: the **gargles**, which are large. Two metres, sac-bodied, spiny, tolerant of the vertical, and possessed of a call in the octave below a bassoon. They call across the basin, one flue answering another, at intervals that are not random but are also not intelligible. You feel a gargle in your sternum before you hear it.

In the Drab: **sluice-eels**, thick as a wrist, blunt, in numbers that make the water look like moving muscle. They eat what the cuffs let fall. On the wharf, **stilts** — long-legged, plate-headed, wading in the shallows with a click at every step, taking eels, standing for hours.

And in the yards, working, or apparently working: **the drays**. Four of them. Waist-high, broad, six-legged, harnessed by nothing to nothing, walking a circuit of the Returning Yard that takes eleven minutes and has been polished into the pan two centimetres deep, and no deeper. They do not turn a wheel. They do not carry anything. Their circuit is not maintenance and it is not migration. It is what a dray does.

The fauna are overwhelming and yet the streets are largely empty, and this is not a contradiction: the animals occupy the wet edges, the undersides, the gutters, the flues, the channels. The middles of things are bare. You can stand in the centre of a yard alone, in flat grey light, in silence-shaped-like-thunder, and see nothing moving within thirty metres while every wall around you seethes.

## The Law of the Lap

Here is the rule, and it is the strange heart of the place.

Nothing here increases, and nothing decreases.

Kill a cuff — it is easy, they do not flinch — and within a few breaths, from a low chute in the north face of the melt-shed that the locals would call the Return if there were locals, a cuff emerges. Wet, adult, complete. Not that cuff. A cuff. The count is restored before you have finished feeling what you did.

There are no young in the Lap. No eggs, no larvae, no nests, no courtship, no growth. Bodies do not enlarge; they molt and stay the size they are. Death occurs, constantly, everywhere — you walk on shells and you walk on bodies — and it has no consequence whatsoever at the level of the whole. The Lap is not thriving. It is not dying. It is a chord held so long it has become the definition of the room.

Consequences of the Law that you will notice by the second day:

- The wear on every threshold has stopped at its current depth. Feet fall the same way. Stone gives up only so much.
- The rust has stopped. The mist boundary has stopped. The gasholder is half-full.
- There is no dawn and no dusk. The light is a flat, even, shadowless grey-white, the light of four in the afternoon under high overcast, and it comes from the cloud-roof, which is lit from below by the furnaces and from above by something no one has gone up to look at. You cannot tell time here by anything except the stamp.

The stamp: in the stamp-house, a mass of iron falls every four seconds. It has fallen every four seconds forever. It is the loudest thing, and after an hour it becomes the floor of your hearing, and after a day your heart finds a relationship with it that you will have to unlearn later, elsewhere, with difficulty.

## The Quiet Cell

At the east end of the melt-shed there is a small room, three metres by three, lined floor to ceiling in wedges of black slag-wool, with a heavy door on a counterweight. Nothing lives in it. The drays do not enter it. It is the only place in the Lap where the noise does not reach — not muffled, gone.

Standing inside it, you can hear the equilibrium. That is the local claim and it is accurate. What you hear is your own blood, and beneath that, at the edge of audibility, a very faint even hiss with no source, which does not change in pitch or volume, and which you will recognise, when you are back outside among the clappers and the gargles and the four-second fall of the iron, as the sound the whole basin is made of, played at its true size.

People do not stay in the Quiet Cell long. Not from fear. It is simply that after a few minutes there is nothing further to learn from it, and there never will be, and you can feel that with unusual clarity.
