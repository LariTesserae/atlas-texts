## The Model at Hesp

There is no water here, and there never was, and the word for it in this place is a gesture: a flat hand turned over, palm down, meaning *that which we do not have and do not miss*.

Instead there is dust, and the dust is warm, and the dust is gold.

---

**The Basin**

Hesp sits in a shallow dish about the size of a hamlet — you could walk its diameter in the time it takes a leaf to fall from the ceiling lattice to the floor, if a leaf fell straight, which here they do not. The dish looks like a natural basin, a low place worn into a plain. It is not. Kneel and brush the surface with your fingers and you find courses: bricks of pressed dust, each the length of a forearm, each bearing the shallow chevron print of the palm that packed it. The basin was laid. Someone made the low place, brick by brick, and then made a plain around it so the low place would have something to be low *in*.

The bricks are new. That is the first thing a visitor notices and the last thing they understand. The tool-marks have no patina. The edges of the shell-blade cuts are still bright and slightly furred, the way a fresh cut in wood is furred. Nothing has weathered, because there is no weather that wears — only warmth, and a slow dry drift of pollen, and time, which here does something other than erode.

**The Substrate**

Everything is made. Not *most*. Everything. The floor is brick and braided fiber matting. The soil, where there is soil, is a graded blend of milled bone-meal, seed-husk, and powdered clay, poured into hand-cut sockets in the floor and tamped with a bone dibber. The hills are wicker armatures skinned in packed dust. The stones are fired. The sky is a lattice — split withies lashed with sinew, four fingers of gap between each rib — and above the lattice the light comes in, honeyed and even, from no source anyone has found.

There is no bedrock. Dig and you find brick, then fiber, then brick, then a layer of shell blades laid edge to edge like scales, then brick. The digging discourages itself after a while. The Makers say — with their hands — that below the last course is the same warm nothing that lies beyond the outer wall, and that it is impolite to check.

**The Growing Things**

And yet it is lush. That is the joke of Hesp, or the mercy of it.

The vegetation does not drink. It *wicks*. Every plant here runs on a dry chemistry: a fine talc-fine sap the color of butter, which flows when warm and sets when cool, so that at the low end of the day-cycle the whole garden stiffens very slightly, and at the high end it slumps and sighs and grows. Cut a stem and no bead forms; a small breath of powder puffs out and hangs, and the plant seals itself with a wax scab in about the time it takes to say something you regret.

They are dense. Near-touching, layered, four and five storeys of leaf between floor and lattice. Waxflag, whose leaves are translucent slabs the thickness of a thumb, warm as a sleeping animal, veined with channels you can see the powder creeping through. Bellow-fern, which inflates and deflates on a cycle of about a day, moving air across its own undersides — the closest thing here to breathing. Ropes of dustvine with pads instead of tendrils, gripping the lattice ribs by suction. Pods everywhere, sealed, drum-tight, containing more powder; when a large animal steps on one it does not splash, it *puffs*, and the gold hangs in the light for a long slow while, and everything downwind of it grows a little faster.

There is no rot. Things dry, flake, delaminate, and are carried off by insects. Or they simply cease to have been there, which is a different process, and slower, and belongs to a later section.

**The Animals**

Abundant. Layered like the leaves.

Most are mouthless. They feed by skin, by drift, by standing in the gold and letting it happen — which is why Hesp is quiet. There is no chewing here, no lapping, no crack of shell or grind of tooth. The loudest ordinary sound is the dry click of a many-jointed climber putting its feet in order on a lattice rib, and that is quieter than your own pulse.

The seam-things live in the joins of the architecture, in the finger's width between brick and matting: small, blunt, warm-blooded, with hands. They are the ones who move objects overnight — always by a little, always with an apparent intention nobody has decoded.

The leaf-birds are not birds and not really animals in the way you'd want. A drift of shed waxflag will gather itself, hunch, and go up through the lattice-light in a loose spiral, holding a shape for perhaps thirty heartbeats, then come apart again. Whether it is one creature or the recurring habit of the litter is a question the Makers consider settled and rude.

And the repairers — the wax-beetles, palm-sized, industrious, which chew powder and extrude it as a hard bright fill. They mend the bricks. They tighten the lashings. Left alone for a season they will build a wall nobody designed, in a place where a wall makes structural sense and no other kind of sense at all. Hesp is thus not entirely handmade any more. It has begun to be made by its own inhabitants, and this counts as one of the injuries.

**The Rules**

Hesp was built as a model. This is not metaphor, and the Makers do not treat it as sacred. They built, at hamlet scale, a careful reduction of a larger place — either a place they left, or a place they intend to find; their gesture for it is ambiguous and they may not distinguish. They carved its hills, poured its soil, planted its garden, and set its animals in it as tokens.

The model began to be true.

This is the reality breakdown, and it is not dramatic. It is patient. It proceeds at the rate of a stain spreading in cloth. The observable rules, as the Makers count them on their fingers:

*One.* The inside exceeds the outside, and the excess grows. Pace the outer wall: nine hundred and some steps. Pace the interior: more, and more each season. The garden is quietly manufacturing distance. Nobody has found where it is kept.

*Two.* Sevens. The Makers, early on, cut seven identical door-frames from one template, because seven doors were required. Now there are seven of many things. Pick a fruit and you have picked it seven times; six of those pickings have not happened yet. Over the following days you will hear your own hand close on a stem, from somewhere behind you, six times. This is most of the sound in Hesp: the delayed echo of small deeds. It is why the place feels haunted by nothing worse than itself.

*Three.* Naming fixes. Anything said aloud becomes marginally more real and correspondingly less able to move. The Makers therefore speak with their hands, and the great labor of their priesthood is *unnaming* — the slow, muttered, backwards work of releasing the things earlier generations pinned in place by talking about them. There are objects in the far rooms so thoroughly named they can no longer be lifted, cannot be broken, and cast shadows that arrive a moment after the object does.

*Four.* Carving and creature are a two-way door. A relief of a climbing animal will, over years, begin to breathe — the chest edge lifting a hair's width, then a finger's. Conversely, an animal that sleeps too long in one posture goes shallow: its outline flattens, its far side merges with the wall behind it, and it becomes decoration. The Makers walk the corridors at intervals nudging sleepers awake. This is considered the kindest of their duties.

*Five.* The hand measures true; the eye does not. Distance taken with a knotted cord or a stride holds. Distance judged by looking drifts, especially down long lines of growth. Sight-lines in Hesp are gently, consistently liars, and the whole population is very good at not caring.

*Six.* There are seven of the doorframes and only six were built into rooms. The seventh stands in a corner of the low garden, framing a stretch of wall, and has always been that way, and something large lives on the other side of it. It is slow. Twice in living memory the framed wall has bowed outward and gone warm — warmer than everything else, which is already warm — and then, over a month, subsided. The Makers keep the ground before it swept. Not as worship. As courtesy to a neighbor whose house shares a wall.

**Light, Heat, Air**

The light is ordinary. That is what makes it awful, if you let it be. It is the light of mid-morning in a well-glazed room: honeyed, sufficient, generous to color. It has no source. Shadows do not agree with one another about where it comes from, and their disagreements change, but so slowly you would need to mark the floor with a blade and come back in a year, and the Makers have, and they have.

Everything is body-warm. The floor slightly warmer than the air, so that lying down feels like being held. The high point of the cycle brings a dry, breathing heat that makes the wax leaves go soft and the whole garden lean; the low point is merely mild. No wind, except the local exhalations of bellow-fern and the thermals that lift the leaf-birds. Pollen hangs. Sound does not carry far in the pollen; it goes about twenty paces and then gives up, muffled in gold.

**The Makers**

Perhaps forty of them. Shell blades, bone awls, fired clay, cord of plaited husk, and a few soft copper pins — the only metal, hoarded, hammered thin, used exclusively at the joints where the structure's logic is load-bearing. They do not smelt. They found the copper, they say, in a course of brick, already the right shape, already tarnished, seven of it.

They are not frightened. That is the last thing to understand about Hesp. They live in a small warm made place that is eating the difference between representation and world, and their response is maintenance: sweep, tighten, wake the sleepers, unname the pinned, count the duplicates, keep the seventh doorway's threshold clean. Once a season they walk the outer wall and touch it, all of them, palms flat, to confirm it is still a wall and not yet a horizon.

Beyond it: warm dry nothing, patient, not pressing.

Below it: brick, then fiber, then brick.

Above it: light, from nowhere, plenty.

And within, in the gold-hung, slumping, dense green dark under the lattice, something that was made continues, at the pace of a stain, becoming something that simply *is* — and the sound of it is a hand closing on a stem, six more times, a long way behind you.
