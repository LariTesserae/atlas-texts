**The Bubble in the Nine-Week Rock**

Begin at four thousand one hundred metres, on the northeast flank of a mountain still deciding its own shape, and then go inward — through eleven metres of new dome rock, past the crust that hardened in October, into a lens-shaped cavity about the width of a small bedroom. This is the whole world under discussion. Nothing outside it matters much here, and nothing inside it has ever been seen.

The pocket was a bubble. Gas gathered in a slug of viscous lava as the flow ground uphill against itself, and the flow stopped moving before the bubble could rise and burst. So it is held: an oblate hollow, two and a half metres on its long axis, one metre from floor to ceiling at the crown, tapering at the edges into blind slits too narrow for a finger.

The ceiling is the best part of it. The lava was thick as cooling tar when it stiffened, and it stiffened mid-gesture — a field of black drips hanging down, each one frozen at the instant before release, some drawn out to hair-fine points, some bulbed like tears, all of them glassy and mirror-smooth on the underside where the gas polished them. Between the drips, the glass carries the grain of its own flow: fine parallel striations, tighter than fingerprint ridges, recording the last few centimetres of motion the rock ever made.

Nothing has fallen from this ceiling. That is the significant fact. In nine weeks, one flake has spalled from the northern lip — a curl of glass the size of a thumbnail, lying now on the floor rubble with its fracture faces still sharp enough to open skin. That flake is the entire erosional history of this place. Everything else is original, unrounded, unsorted, unweathered. There is no dust that is not primary ash. There is no sand at all, because sand takes time and abrasion, and neither has happened yet.

---

**Water**

There is none, in the sense that matters. There is water vapour in the gas — a few percent, screaming through invisibly at three hundred and forty degrees — but it never condenses, never pools, never runs, never wets anything. It is a gas here, and behaves like the other gases: as a carrier, a solvent-in-the-abstract, a thing that hauls dissolved minerals up out of the throat and drops them when it cools.

So the crystals here grew straight from vapour to solid, skipping liquid altogether. On the west wall, where the flow of gas is steady and slow, sal ammoniac has built a crust of tiny colourless cubes and warped plates, brittle as burnt sugar, some of them stacked into ragged towers a centimetre high. Beyond that, in a band, sulphur: needle clusters, some of them the pale yellow-green of new sulphur, some already darkened toward orange by heat. Then a zone of hematite in thin iridescent scales, and a smear of something copper-bearing that would be brilliant blue-green if there were light enough to call it any colour. Where gas leaks through hairline cracks, the sublimates have grown out of the cracks into fans and chimneys, delicate, unbroken, structurally absurd — nothing has yet come along to knock them over.

The floor is rubble: angular blocks of the same glass, plus blocks of grey pumice-crust that fell when the bubble's lower wall collapsed inward in the first hours. Between the blocks, cracks. Deep in the cracks, at the very bottom of the pocket, the rock is still hot enough to glow.

---

**Light**

The glow is the only light. Two seams in the floor and one narrow slot at the eastern taper show dull red — the low, sullen incandescence of rock at six hundred degrees, a red so deep it barely registers as red at all, more like a colour remembered than seen. It does not illuminate. It lies flat on the surfaces nearest it, gives the ceiling drips the faintest wet-looking highlights, and gives up. Above the knee-height mark, the pocket is absolutely dark, and the crystal gardens on the west wall have never been lit and never will be.

This is important to the animals. They do not use the light. They use the heat itself as their light, and there is a great deal of it.

---

**The Engine**

Under the pocket, at some depth — perhaps eighty metres, perhaps four hundred — a column of magma stands in a conduit and rises and falls. The period is roughly thirty-eight minutes. Nobody has measured it; it is simply true.

Each rise compresses the gas below and drives it up through a network of fractures, one of which passes through the floor of this cavity. Each fall draws back. The pocket therefore breathes, and the breathing has the exact character of machinery: not the irregular gasping of an animal but the indifferent, dimensionally consistent stroke of a piston that has no opinion about its work.

The sound is enormous for a room this size. There is a fundamental roar at about thirty hertz, felt more than heard, which enters through the floor and shakes the sublimate towers so that the smallest ones drift constantly like grass in wind — the only motion in the mineral world here. Riding on that, the gas forcing through the eastern slot produces a whistle, an unwavering flat tone around eight hundred hertz that goes on for nineteen minutes and then slides down a whole tone and stops as the stroke reverses. When the stroke reverses, the pressure drop cracks the glass. This is the third sound: a rapid percussive tinkling, thousands of micro-fractures across the ceiling and walls, like ice in a glass, like a foundry floor cooling after pour, like coins. It runs for three or four minutes and then thins out, and each cycle it is very slightly quieter than the cycle before, because the rock is very slightly less hot.

And under all of it, constant, the fourth sound: the animals.

---

**Fauna**

The pocket is packed. That is the honest word. There is no free volume in it.

Life cannot live at six hundred degrees or three hundred or eighty, and so it does not attempt to. What it does instead is occupy, with total saturation, the thin shell where the temperature is survivable — a curved band perhaps three to eight centimetres thick, hugging the outer wall, where heat from the interior meets the trickle of cold thin mountain air infiltrating through the dome's fractures from outside. Call it the margin. Everything is in the margin.

Fire-chaser beetles came first, within thirty hours of the flow stopping, drawn from kilometres downslope by infrared pit organs on their thoraxes and by the smell of scorched ground. They came for burnt wood and found none, and they stayed anyway, because the heat itself is an instruction they cannot refuse. There are, at present, something over four hundred of them in the pocket, in the slits and behind the collapsed blocks, packed shoulder to shoulder in the literal sense — carapace against carapace, three and four deep, their legs interlocked. When one shifts, twenty shift. The sound of that is a continuous dry rasping crackle, a sound like a fire that is not a fire.

With them: ash flies in the hundreds of thousands, so dense in the margin air that the space reads as textured rather than empty; if you put a hand in you would feel weight. Thermophilic mites in orange colonies on the underside of every rubble block, an unbroken felt of them, moving as a surface moves. Two species of pseudoscorpion working the mite felt, and a great many nematodes in the ash between grains, and tardigrades dried down to tuns in the coldest fissures, waiting for conditions they can afford. On the outermost cracks, where the gas has cooled to sixty degrees, there is a film — grey-pink, thin as a stain — of chemolithotrophs doing something with sulphur, no plants, no chlorophyll, no interest in the light there isn't.

The whole assemblage sits inside the roar and does not care about the roar.

---

**Rules**

*One.* Nothing liquid. Substances go from gas to solid and back. Any growth here is deposition, and any loss is sublimation, and both are quiet.

*Two.* Heat travels one way — outward — and everything alive lives on the boundary of the survivable. The boundary moves. As the dome cools, the survivable shell creeps inward, toward the floor, toward the glowing seams. A centimetre a month, roughly. The animals follow it. They do not know they are following anything; they simply prefer the temperature they prefer, and the temperature keeps relocating, and so the whole packed population migrates a few centimetres a year through the interior of a rock.

*Three.* Nothing is built. The eastern wall has cooled into a stack of neat six-sided columns, brick-regular, and looks entirely like the work of hands. It is not. It is only the arithmetic of shrinkage. There is no artifact in this place, not one, and no one has ever been here to leave one.

*Four.* Everything is slow except the sound. The sound is violent and immediate and repeats every thirty-eight minutes. The changes are geological and take decades. Both are happening at once in a room the size of a bedroom, and neither interferes with the other.

---

**Duration**

Give it thirty years. The engine below will lose its stroke as the conduit plugs; the whistle will go first, then the roar, then the cracking. The seams will go from red to black in the fourth or fifth year. The sublimate gardens will stop growing and begin very slowly to unmake themselves, sal ammoniac first, back into the gas it came from. The margin will thin, cool through, and close: beetles out through the fractures, flies dead in drifts, mites down to spores, tardigrades holding on in the dark for whatever comes.

Then a sealed pocket of cold black glass with a floor of dead insects and a ceiling of frozen drips, eleven metres inside a mountain, at four thousand one hundred metres, unlit, unentered, and structurally unremarkable — waiting for the landslide in eleven thousand years that will open it to the sky for about a minute and a half before burying it again under everything above.
