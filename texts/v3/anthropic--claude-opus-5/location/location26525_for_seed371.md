**The Furnace Bowl at Four Thousand Three Hundred Meters**

There is no view. That is the first thing to understand about this place — that it sits higher than almost anything else in the range, high enough that the air comes thin and cold into the lungs, and yet you can see perhaps four meters in any direction, and only when the steam thins, and only if you have brought your own light.

The hollow is small. Sixty paces across at the widest, less at the throat. A stone thrown hard would clear it. It is shaped like the inside of a bell that has been dropped and dented on one side: a bowl of raw rock walls rising twenty, thirty meters, and above them the further slopes of the mountain climbing away into cloud that is not cloud so much as the hollow's own exhalation, gone up and got lost. The walls are new. You can tell by their edges. Nothing has had time to round them. The rock breaks in glassy fracture planes and long slivers, dark andesite with a fresh grain like split coal, and it cuts. Every handhold cuts. There is no dust here because dust requires patience; instead there is grit, angular, biting, still remembering the shape it was broken from.

Eleven years ago this was a shoulder of the mountain. Then a spine of cooling lava, extruded too fast and too tall, leaned and let go, and took the slope with it, and left a socket. The socket has been venting ever since.

---

The floor is where the difficulty begins, because the floor is not one thing. It is a topography in itself, a miniature ruined country of blocks the size of chests and blocks the size of carts, tilted, wedged, still settling — you feel them settle, a shrug under the boot sole, a click and a slump somewhere off to the left that never resolves into a sound you can locate. Between and beneath these blocks, gas comes up.

It comes up hot. Not lava-hot — nothing here glows except, sometimes, deep in the central fissure, a dull red like the inside of a stove seen through a crack in the door, visible only when the steam column staggers in a crosswind, and gone again before your eyes have adjusted. But hot enough that the rock at ankle height is warm through your boots and the rock at your face is cold. The whole hollow is stratified this way: a floor of summer, an air of winter, and a thin band between them, perhaps a hand's width, where the two argue and produce water out of nothing.

That band is where everything lives.

---

The vegetation is absurd. It has no business being here and there is a great deal of it.

Moss, mostly — cushions of it, dense as upholstery, some of them a meter and a half across and thirty centimeters deep, so saturated that stepping on one is like stepping on a sponge full of tea; the water comes up over the boot and runs off warm. The cushions grow into one another, over one another, pale green at the crown and rust-brown and black beneath where the older growth is cooking slowly into peat, and this peat is the only soil in the hollow. It is eleven years old. It smells of hot rain and iron.

Layered into the moss, and out of it, and hanging over it: liverworts in flat overlapping tongues, glossy as if varnished, plastered to the undersides of steam-warmed slabs. Filmy ferns in the lee of every boulder, fronds one cell thick, translucent, so dependent on the saturated air that a dry hour kills them — and so they occupy only the exact places where the vents blow steadily, growing in tiers, a fern above a fern above a fern, near enough to touch, and touching. Where an old block has warmed itself for a few years without shifting, whole mats of them make a shag on the vertical, and condensation runs down through the mats in braided threads and drips off the lowest fronds in a constant patter you could mistake for rain.

Nothing here has a stem thicker than a pencil. There is not a single woody plant, no tree, no shrub, not even the prostrate willow that manages elsewhere at this height. The hollow is too unstable for anything that needs a decade. What thrives is what can arrive as a spore on the wind, germinate in a week, and be replaced.

And what is replaced, constantly. Walk the perimeter and you will find, every few paces, a patch of moss cooked white — bleached, crisp, still in the shape of its living cushion, like a plaster cast of itself. A vent shifted under it. Two meters away, on ground that was bare last season, a new cushion the size of a hand, emerald, immaculate. The whole floor is a slow riot: colonization, scald, colonization, and the boundary lines between them redrawn on the timescale of weeks.

---

Now: the noise.

You cannot speak here. You can shout and be half-heard at arm's length. The hollow is loud in the specific way a working engine room is loud, and this comparison is not decorative — the resemblance is exact and it is the strangest true thing about the place, though nothing here has been made and there is not one manufactured object within thirty kilometers.

The main fissure roars. A continuous, throat-tearing roar, white noise with a low fluttering in it, the sound of a boiler safety valve blowing off and never stopping. Around it, smaller vents whistle at fixed pitches, and because the rock is a resonator, they hold their notes: a sustained flat chord that does not change for hours and then changes all at once when a stone falls in somewhere and the whole tuning of the hollow drops a third.

Then the pulses. Under the roar there is a rhythm, roughly nine seconds, sometimes eleven — a deep chuff, chuff, chuff, as water somewhere below flashes to steam in cycles, and each chuff comes up through the rock into your knees before it reaches your ears. It is the pump. That's what it sounds like. Something enormous and mechanical, badly balanced, running under load. Add to that the machine-shop percussion: hot rock cracking as condensate hits it — sharp, individual, ringing snaps, dozens a minute. Add water hammering into cavities. Add, three or four times an hour, the long grinding rush of a block letting go somewhere up the wall and bounding down and striking and coming to rest, and the aftermath of small stones arriving one by one like the last of the change out of a pocket.

The place runs. It has a rhythm, a load, a duty cycle, a smell of hot metal that is really sulfur and the iron in the rock. It gives every impression of industry, and it produces nothing but steam, peat, and its own noise.

---

Light: almost none, and never from here. In daytime the steam column and the cloud it feeds cut the hollow to a brown-grey dusk in which the moss reads as black. At night — and it is night for most of the hours that matter, and the mountain keeps its own weather, so the stars are a rumor — the hollow is dark in the full sense. Hold your hand up and it is gone. Your lamp does not help much; the beam stops dead in the steam and comes back at you as a wall of white, and you learn quickly to switch it off and travel by warmth: hands out, palms down, feeling for the hot ground that means vent and the cold ground that means block, and for the wet moss that means safe and the crisp moss that means the vent moved and you are standing where it moved to.

There is that one red seam in the fissure. Once in an hour, or once in a night, the wind opens a door in the steam and you see it — dull, sullen, no bigger than a coin held out at arm's length — and then it closes.

---

There are no animals.

None. Not a bird overhead, though this is well within the range of choughs; they do not come, and there is nothing for them. No insects on the moss. No spiders in the ferns, which is remarkable, because spiders are normally the first tenants anywhere — but the ground gases pool in the low places, heavy and lethal, and every low place is where a small crawling thing would go. Nothing in the peat. Nothing in the water.

What you find instead, if you look closely at the moss cushions with your lamp against your chest, is arrivals. A moth, wings intact, folded into the wet green like something set down carefully. A beetle. A crane fly. Blown up over the lip on an updraft from twelve hundred meters below, delivered into an atmosphere that will not have them, going down slowly into the peat they help to make. The hollow imports its dead and composts them. That is the whole of its animal life: a delivery, and no residents.

---

The rules of the place, as far as they can be stated:

Nothing here is older than eleven years, and most of it is younger than one.

Heat is the only real geography. Positions on the map are meaningless; positions relative to the vents are everything. The map is redrawn without notice.

Water exists only as an argument between the hot floor and the cold air. There is no stream, no pool that lasts, no rain to speak of — perhaps a few hours a week when the cloud thickens and sags. And yet nothing is ever dry. Every surface is beaded. Everything you carry becomes heavy. Everything you wear becomes cold, because the steam wets you and the air at your face is four degrees and moving.

Anything that stands still gets buried, cooked, or knocked down.

The lushness is not a sign of stability. It is a sign of speed.

And when you climb out — hand over cut hand, up the fresh-broken wall, into thinner air and colder wind and, eventually, into a stillness so complete after that noise that your ears invent a ringing to fill it — you turn and look back and see nothing at all. Just a plume, small, white, going up off a black shoulder of a mountain, and bending, and coming apart, and being remade at the base as fast as the top of it is lost.
