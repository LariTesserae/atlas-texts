**The Warm Room, Below**

You come down into it, always down; there is no other approach. The stair is fresh-cut — you can see that immediately, before your eyes have adjusted enough to see much else — because the treads are still sharp at their edges, and the rock they were struck from is a black glass basalt with a skin on it like a burnt sugar crust. Nothing has worn those edges. No boots but the first ones. When you put your hand out for balance the stone is warm as an animal's flank, and it takes a little skin off your palm, because the fracture surfaces here have never been rounded by anything. This ground was liquid recently. Recently in the way stone means it: within a lifetime or two.

The stair delivers you to a floor of poured concrete, and the concrete is cracked in a single clean line from wall to wall, and the two halves are no longer level with each other. That happened after the pouring. That, more or less, is the whole history of the place.

---

The chamber is not large. Sixteen paces across the long way, and the ceiling low enough in the western third that you'd stoop. It was a gallery once — a service level, a sub-basement, the bottom of somebody's small enterprise — and then the rock beneath it opened a seam, and heat came up through the seam, and the seam is still there: a fissure the width of a forearm running diagonally beneath the broken concrete, breathing.

The heat is the first fact and the governing one. It is forty-something degrees down here, always, with a wet weight in it, so that within minutes your shirt is stuck to you and your breath feels like a redundancy. Sweat does not evaporate. It gathers and travels. Every surface is beaded: the brass of the fittings, the enamel of a sign that says only **—ALV E** where the rest of the letters have gone under a rind of white mineral, the tin ceiling, the ends of the leaves. The whole room is very slightly raining on itself and has been for a long time. Water here does not fall from anywhere. It condenses out of the air onto whatever is coolest, and what is coolest is metal, and so the machines weep and the plants drink from the machines.

---

There is light, but not much, and it is the wrong colour for anything that grows.

Three sources. First: a pair of carbon-filament bulbs, of the old long-necked kind, still burning on a circuit no one is maintaining — one at eleven per cent of its intent, a dull orange tooth in a cage, the other flickering perhaps four times an hour in a rhythm that you will start to anticipate and then resent. Second: the fissure itself, which at its deepest visible point shows a red that isn't quite a colour, more a suggestion your eye makes and then withdraws. You cannot look at it directly and be sure. Third: the moss.

The moss glows. Not brightly — you could not read by it, you can barely see your own hand held above it — a green so faint it might be an afterimage, and it is strongest where the moss is thickest, along the sweating flank of the compressor housing, in the sump, in the grouting between bricks. The glow is a by-product. The moss is not trying to be seen; there is nothing here to see it.

And nothing to be seen by. This is the second governing fact, and it takes longer to notice than the heat. Nothing moves that isn't water or machine. No gnat orbits the failing bulb. Nothing scuttles from the light of your lamp, because there is nothing to scuttle. Turn over the wettest board in the sump and beneath it: more moss, a slick of pale threads, mineral, water, nothing with a face. In a place this warm and this wet, a place that should be a riot of small hungers, there is no hunger at all except the slow vegetable kind. The absence has a texture. You will find yourself listening for a rat and hearing the room decline to provide one.

---

The vegetation is lush and it is arranged wrongly.

It grows in clumps with real space between them — four or five great cushions and thickets, and between them bare wet concrete, bare wet stone, and this sparseness is not poverty but arrangement. Each mass has organised itself around a source of heat, not a source of light. There is a thing like a tree fern, three metres of pale trunk fibre, growing directly out of the fissure's lip with its crown bent over the crack the way a person bends over a stove; its fronds are colourless, the white-green of cave water, and they are turned inward, toward the warmth, so that the whole plant presents you with its back. There is a low dense mound of something like liverwort, but stacked into plates, that has taken the steam pipe entire, encasing it, forty centimetres thick, and where you press it the plates part reluctantly and give out a puff of warm spore-dust that smells of iron and pears.

Around the failing bulb: a rosette. Twelve leaves, each one the size of a hand, cupped, and they have found the bulb's tiny warmth across a metre of dark and grown out along the conduit to reach it. When the bulb flickers, the leaves do not respond. They are not interested in the light. They came for the filament's forty watts of waste.

Nothing here flowers. Flowers are a message to animals, and there is no one to receive it. Instead everything reproduces by spore and runner and split — patient, self-sufficient, indifferent. Pale rhizome threads cross the open concrete in long unbranching runs, going nowhere by any logic you can see, occasionally finding a warm bolt and thickening there into a knuckle.

---

The machines are the weather.

Not many, and none whole. A compressor the size of a wardrobe, seized. A rail let into the floor, thirty centimetres of it, going under the moss in both directions, gauge too narrow for anything but a hand-pushed skip. A wall of bakelite switch housings with their brass levers all thrown down except one. A transformer that hums.

That hum is the loudest thing in the room and it is not loud. A sixty-cycle sound, or something near it, so steady that after ten minutes it becomes a property of your skull rather than of the air. Under it: drip. Drip from the tin ceiling into the sump, drip from the fronds, and once every few seconds, from a different height, a heavier drop into standing water — the room's pulse, arrhythmic, patient.

And then, every eleven hours or so, the pump.

Something down the eastern crawlway still cycles. You hear it come up from far off, a rising mechanical throat-clearing, and then for perhaps ninety seconds the fissure's breathing reverses — the room exhales — and the air thickens visibly, and warm mist rolls out at ankle height and climbs the plants, and every leaf and plate and cushion in the chamber gives off spores at once, all of them, a slow grey snow going upward. It is not a coincidence of timing. The plants have learned the pump. They have made an eleven-hour year, and they set their seed in it, and they will keep doing this for as long as whatever is down there has current, and it has current because somebody, once, wired this level to something that has not yet been switched off.

That is the rule of this world, if it has one: heat is the sun, machinery is the season, and the calendar belongs to a pump nobody has seen.

---

The signs of people are few and none of them recent.

A canvas coat on a nail, stiff as board, with a white mineral crust on its shoulders like epaulettes, and the moss has climbed the wall behind it and stopped politely at its hem. A tally scratched into the paint beside the switch wall — nineteen strokes, then a gap, then two more, angrier. An enamel mug in the sump, full of clear water, its interior lined with the faint green glow, so that it sits there in the dark being a small round lamp of a mug. A folding rule, opened to thirty centimetres and left that way, measuring nothing.

Nobody died here. There's no sense of that. Somebody simply went up the stair at the end of a shift and then history moved sideways, and the seam opened, and the heat came, and the room — sealed, fed, unvisited — got on with becoming this.

---

Change comes, but you would have to stay to see it. The concrete crack widens by the thickness of a fingernail in a decade. The mineral rind on the sign takes another letter every few years, so that the message shortens toward nothing. The tree fern at the fissure adds one frond per — what, hundred cycles? Two hundred? — and drops none, so that it is by now a spiral record of every year it has kept. The transformer hums a fraction flatter each season. Sometime, not soon, it will stop, and the bulbs will go, and the pump will not rise out of the east, and the eleven-hour year will end, and everything green in here will hold still in the dark and the heat with no calendar at all, waiting, alive, for the next thing that comes down the sharp new stair.
