## The Pale Room

Four metres of salt above you, and still it is the brightest place you have ever stood.

The chamber is a bubble in an old evaporite bed — one room, no more than thirty paces across, its ceiling a low vault eight or nine metres up at the crown. It was dissolved out slowly, by water that has long since gone elsewhere, and every surface has had time to arrive at its final manners. Nothing here is sharp. The fallen slabs on the floor have rounded shoulders. The walls flute and bulge in soft vertical scallops, like curtains cast in soap. The corners are not corners; they are long, patient curves.

What makes the room impossible is the roof. The bed above is gypsum, and in the ages since the water left, that gypsum recrystallised into alabaster — dense, white, and translucent to a depth you would not believe if you had not seen it lit. Sunlight enters the salt pan overhead, sinks through the lens, loses its direction entirely, and arrives in the chamber as pure milk. It comes from everywhere. It has no source. It does not fall; it simply *is*, filling the volume the way water fills a jar, and because it comes from all points at once, almost nothing in the room casts a shadow.

Then there are the panes. In three or four places the crystals grew large and clear — selenite, arm-length, glass-clean — and through these the sun comes as itself: hard blades of light that lie across the floor and creep, over hours, from the west wall to the east. They are the only clocks. They are also the only heat that can be felt as a weight on the skin. Where a beam touches the gypsum flour of the floor, the dust smells faintly of struck flint and warm bone.

The air is warm and still and slightly sweet. It moves perhaps twice a day, a slow exhalation through the low crawl in the south wall, as the pan above heats and cools and the room breathes with it. There is no wind. There has never been wind. This is why the floor is a document.

## The floor remembers

The powder underfoot is gypsum flour, fine as talc, laid down over millennia by the slow disintegration of the walls. Nothing disturbs it but feet. So every footprint made in this room persists until something steps on it: the split hoof-marks of a goat that came down forty years ago and could not get back up, still crisp; the trailing drag of a snake; the rosette of a rat's palm, and beside it, in a shallow arc, the groove where the rat's carried frond touched down. Layer upon layer, but nothing erased, because erasure requires weather. Walk here and you become part of the archive, permanently and without recourse.

In the northeast corner, where the light is slightly less absolute, there is a scatter of worked selenite: eleven flakes, three blades, one core, all of them knapped with real skill and all of them softened at the edges by four thousand years of nothing whatsoever happening to them. Around the scatter, prints. Not hands. The blades are small — too small — and the prints that crowd them belong to something with four toes and a heel pad. Nobody has resolved this. It is not the kind of place that answers questions; it is the kind of place that lets them sit.

## Water, at its own pace

Water is scarce here and utterly central. It arrives in two ways.

From the north wall, a seep — a dark stain widening into a lip of stone worn glossy, delivering perhaps a cupful an hour in the damp months and nothing at all in the dry. It has built a shallow pool, four paces long, rimmed with a fret of salt crystals like a frost that never melts. The pool's edge advances and retreats a hand's width across a season. In the deepest part the water is nearly fresh and cool; at the salt rim it is brine, and dense, and holds a faint pink shimmer of halophile life that thickens through the year and thins again.

And from the ceiling: roots. Somewhere out past the pan's edge, a tamarisk or something like it found a fissure and sent its taproots travelling — thirty metres laterally, then down, through the alabaster, into the room. They hang in a loose chandelier near the centre, pale as tendon where the light bleaches them, and they weep. Not much. A drop falls every forty seconds or so, on average, all year, and strikes the same slab it has struck since before the blades were made. It has drilled a dimple there two fingers deep. That drop is the loudest recurring sound in the world.

## The economy of shade

Sparse vegetation, and all of it strange: mats of black cyanobacterial felt in the wet corners; a single lithophyte fern by the seep, its fronds glassy and inclined edge-on to the glare; a low grey succulent dome, water-swollen, near the base of the west wall, which the animals gnaw at from beneath. And the root chandelier, which is not really a plant here so much as a visiting organ of a plant elsewhere.

But the ecology of the Pale Room does not turn on food. Food is manageable. What is scarce is darkness.

In a room where light comes from every direction, shade is a resource with almost no supply — a few slab undersides, the lee of the fallen blocks, the lace cast by the root curtain when a selenite beam happens to be behind it. And so the animals here are organised around it, quietly and without dispute, the way animals elsewhere are organised around water holes.

The fauna are numerous and almost invisible, which is the room's second impossibility. Density here is close to nothing — you can stand in the middle and be twenty paces from any living thing, and the volume above you is simply empty, a hollow of white air. The abundance is a *film*: it lies on surfaces. Turn a slab and there are sixty creatures on its underside. Glass crickets, transparent as ice water, which in this light you cannot see at all — you see their shadows first, small dark commas moving across the floor with nothing above them, and only then resolve the animal. Beetles the opposite way: matte, absolute black, walking heat-sinks that shuttle from shade to shade at speed and stand still in between, cooking, enduring. Silvered geckos on the walls, mirror-flanked, dissolving into the glow. Brine shrimp in their thousands in the pool's warm end, a slow orange smoke. A translucent centipede in the root mass. Snails whose shells have gone opaque white and thick as porcelain — sun armour.

And the parasol rats. Sandy, big-eyed, hunched. They clip fronds from the fern and lengths of fine root, hold them overhead in one forepaw, and move about their business under their own portable darkness, adjusting the angle without seeming to think about it. When two meet in a good patch of shade, they simply share it, sitting close, holding their fronds at complementary tilts. They do not dig. They do not build. They use the cracks the room already has. But they carry things, and they shape things, and in the northeast corner there are those eleven flakes, and nobody has resolved this.

## Rules

Nothing hurries. The room's own metabolism runs on a scale of centuries: the pool creeping, the salt rim thickening, the roots descending a finger's length a decade, the ceiling thinning as the pan above dissolves in the rare rains. Twice a day, at the heating and the cooling, the alabaster vault ticks — a long, soft, off-key note that travels the curved walls and comes back muffled by the flour, and every animal in the room stops moving for the duration of it, and then resumes. That is the closest thing to an event.

The light will fail before the room does, or the room before the light — the ceiling is thinning, and one century it will thin past holding, and the pan will come down in a single afternoon of noise unlike anything this chamber has known, and then it will be a bright hollow open to the sky, which is to say it will be nothing special.

Until then: white air, still and warm, a drop every forty seconds, blades of sun crossing a floor that forgets nothing, and shadows moving with no bodies above them.
