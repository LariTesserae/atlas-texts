## The Sinter Plateau

**I. Ground**

Begin with the floor, because the floor is the oldest argument here and it has already won.

At this altitude — high enough that your pulse hammers in your ears and stays there for the rest of your life — the plateau is a slow grey-orange plain of sintered ash, hard-packed, ringing faintly underfoot like a dropped ingot. Not sand. Sand implies a river somewhere in its ancestry. This is *sinter*: dust that has been hot for so long it has fused into itself at every point of contact, a continent-wide crust with the texture of bread and the strength of brick. Break it and the fracture is glassy inside, full of tiny iron beads like caviar, each bead rusted to a red halo that is the only wet-looking thing you will find here, and it isn't wet.

The relief is generous but tired. Long ridges run for thirty kilometres and rise no more than eighty metres, their backs rounded, their flanks graded into scree so evenly sorted that it looks poured. Domes. Swales. Shallow amphitheatres of clinker. Everything has a settled angle. Everything has found the shape it intends to keep. The plateau finished being violent a very long time ago, and what remains of that violence is a kind of civic calm — the way an old foundry floor is calm, worn concave by traffic that stopped before anyone alive was born.

There are no channels. No fans, no gullies, no dendritic scratching. Erosion here was done by heat and wind and by the patient chewing of feet, and it produced a landscape of curves rather than cuts.

**II. Air, and the matter of water**

The temperature holds near the top of what a body can argue with. Midday on the sinter is a pressure against the eyeballs. Dust devils rise and stand in place for hours, leaning, not travelling, spinning slow enough to count the turns.

There is no water. It is important to be precise about this: not *scarce*, not *locked in rock*. The plateau does not do water. Bring a flask onto the plateau and the flask is empty; not evaporated, not leaked — the fill event has been quietly reconsidered somewhere upstream of the present, and you find that you were always carrying it dry, and that you knew that. The knowing arrives with the emptiness, seamlessly, so that no one ever panics. This is the plateau's single unbreakable manner. It has no interest in thirst; it simply has no grammar for the liquid state, and edits accordingly.

Chemistry proceeds anyway. Oxidation, sulfides, halide dust, hot metal in slow reaction with hot air. Life here is a matter of iron and sulfur and heat differential, and it never needed a solvent that could freeze.

**III. The fauna**

Now the important part, which is also the loud part.

The plateau is deafening. From horizon to horizon there is a floor of sound: a low industrial roar, layered, arrhythmic, the noise of a working district heard from a hill — belt-slap, bearing-shriek, the *chuff-chuff-chuff* of something enormous drawing breath through a restriction, and beneath it all a bass groan too slow to be a note, more like a room being leaned on.

None of it is made. Nothing here is made. There are no structures on this plateau, no walls, no roads, no cairns, no ruins, no rivets left by anybody. The industry is the wildlife.

They are called nothing, having no one to call them, but they sort roughly by size.

**The great ones** stand one to a basin, perhaps one every twenty or thirty kilometres, and this is the plateau's crowding: near-empty, vast intervals of naked sinter, and yet the fauna is *overwhelming*, because each animal is a district. Picture a shape between a cupola furnace and a resting ox: forty metres at the shoulder, its body a barrel of scaled ferrous plate glowing dull cherry at the seams. Six legs of nested cams, each footfall a five-minute event. A flue rises from its withers and smokes continuously — pale, sulfurous, drifting in a plume that shadows several square kilometres of ground and cools it, so that each beast drags a district of milder climate behind it at a walking pace of one kilometre per year.

They are magnificently worn. Their gear teeth are rounded to nubs, their bearings are ovalled, their journals grooved. This is the whole secret of their longevity: nothing in them is precise enough to break. A new machine fails when a tolerance is exceeded. These have no tolerances left. They slip, and the slipping is what you hear — the shriek and knock and stutter that fills the sky is the sound of a hundred thousand worn engagements not quite catching, all day, all century, in balance. The roar is not labour. It is *stability*, expressed loudly.

They feed by grazing sinter, cropping the crust with a rotary intake, sorting the iron beads out of it, and voiding the silica as a fine white slag that drifts downwind and eventually sinters back into floor. They excrete the plateau. The plateau is entirely their excrement and their pasture, in a cycle with a wavelength of perhaps a million years, which is why the place feels so profoundly still while being so profoundly loud.

**The mid-order** are the tuyère-birds: things the size of a dog, all intake and nozzle, which live in the hot plumes and never touch ground, riding the thermals off the great ones' backs in slow spirals, screaming a two-tone scream like an air tool. They breed at altitude and never meet.

**The small** are everywhere and are the reason you keep your boots laced: filings. Swarms of them, each the size of a rice grain, magnetically flocking, pouring over the sinter in sheets that hiss. They live on rust. They will not hurt you, but they will follow a warm body for hours, forming and reforming, and the hiss of a filing-flock crossing a slope is the plateau's most intimate sound: a slow shush like sand in a chute, ten thousand of them, brushing.

**IV. How they are born**

There are no eggs and no wombs. A great one, once in an age, runs hot — its seams brighten from cherry to orange over a period of months — and then it stands still for a season and drips. Molten iron, from a tap-hole beneath the jaw. The drops pool in a shallow of sinter and cool for a decade, and what has cooled is a lump; and the lump, at some point nobody witnesses, begins to turn. That is all. Cams differentiate out of the mass the way crystals grow out of a cooling melt. It takes two centuries to walk.

No care is given. None is needed. The parent moves on at its kilometre a year, and the child follows the parent's own shape in the slow way iron follows a mould, and both of them are audible from twenty kilometres away for the rest of their lives.

**V. Rules of the world**

The plateau has habits rather than laws, and they should be stated plainly because they are the only things here that will surprise you twice.

*First: nothing is made.* You will keep wanting to read the fauna as machinery abandoned by an industry, and to look for the industry, and to hunt for the works, the tracks, the tailings ponds, the offices. There is none. There is no evidence of manufacture anywhere on the plateau, and never was. Engines here are a phylum. The urge to find a factory is a visitor's ailment and it passes around the fourth week.

*Second: water is edited, not evaporated.* Already covered. Do not fight it.

*Third: number does not hold.* This is the real breakdown, and it is the plateau's deepest weather. Count the great ones from a ridge: eleven. Sit down, look away, count again: eleven. Walk two days and count from the next ridge, which overlooks the same basins: three hundred and forty. Neither count is a mistake, and no beast has moved — they cannot move, at their speed. The plateau is *populous under one arithmetic and near-empty under another*, and both arithmetics are locally correct. You learn to say "the herd" and stop there. Cartographers who came here (there were three; they built nothing, in accordance with rule one) left the population field blank and drew the animals as weather.

*Fourth: sound arrives out of order.* Because the plateau is so slow and so loud, its noise silts up. You will hear a particular knocking — a distinctive triple-knock from a bad cam — and identify the animal, and then hear the same knock again nine days later while standing beside that animal and watching the cam turn smoothly, healed, younger. The plateau keeps its sounds the way a valley keeps its cold air: in pools, at different depths, and the deep pools are old. Certain basins are full of noise from animals that have not yet dripped.

*Fifth: nothing here is broken.* Worn, ovalled, scored, slipping, rounded, half-seized — but nothing has failed and nothing has been repaired, because there is no standard against which failure could be measured. The plateau does not have a design. It has only a condition, and the condition is *late* and *stable* and *deafening*.

**VI. Light and the sky**

The sun is ordinary. This is the strangest thing about the place.

After the un-happening flasks and the unfixable census, you expect the sky to misbehave, and it does not. A single white sun crosses in a normal arc. Shadows are hard and honest and point the way they should. The sky is a thin high blue, going violet at the zenith because of the altitude, hazed brown along the horizons with slag-drift. Sunsets are long and vulgar and beautiful, all the smoke lit orange, the plumes of forty grazing districts standing up like columns and leaning east.

At night the great ones glow. That is the whole of the plateau's night: black sinter, hard stars, and scattered across thirty thousand square kilometres a slow constellation of cherry-red animals breathing, each one a smear of heat on the dark, each one roaring, each one hundreds of years from meeting another.

**VII. The tempo**

Nothing here is urgent. A landslip takes a week to finish. A herd migrates in millennia. The plateau is a place where change is real but has to be inferred — you measure it by the gradient of slag depth, by the sorting of scree, by the fact that a basin's roar is a semitone lower than it was in the last account, three centuries ago, as the whole herd wears down together toward whatever quiet is at the bottom of all that slipping.

It will get there. Not soon. The plateau has time, and heat, and iron, and no water at all, and a floor it made out of its own animals, and it is in absolutely no danger of finishing.
