## The Shelf

At this altitude the air is a thin blue solvent that will not carry sound and will not hold heat. Everything here is a consequence of those two facts.

Start with the ground, since the ground is the oldest thing and has stopped arguing. You are on a shelf about eleven kilometres wide and forty long, tilted perhaps four degrees, hung at roughly six thousand metres between two walls of rock whose tops you cannot see because there is nothing up there for light to happen to. The shelf is old. Not raw-old — not the fresh-cut, still-bleeding geology of young mountains, all knife-edge and rockfall. This range finished its shouting a long time ago. The scree slopes have found their angle and lie in it like sleeping dogs. The boulders have settled to the depth they will keep. The glacier that made this shelf has retreated to the high cirques and become a set of grey lenses that no longer move enough to grind, only enough to weep. What water leaves them arrives as seepage rather than torrent, and the channels it uses are already cut, already smoothed, already lined with a black mineral polish that took the length of a species to accumulate.

So: a settled place, in balance, breathing slowly.

Now the darkness. East of here the cordillera goes up another four thousand metres in a single unbroken wall, and the sun rises behind that wall and sets behind the western one, and between them it passes overhead for eleven days a year. The rest of the time the shelf lives in a light like the inside of a closed eye. There is glow — the sky at zenith is a deep dry indigo with stars burning in it hard and unblinking, no atmosphere left to soften them, and the snowfields take that starlight and give back just enough that you can see the shape of your own hands. Nothing has colour. Everything has edge.

And yet the shelf is green. Not visibly green — you learn it by touch and by smell, a wet ferrous vegetal smell like a cut stem in a bucket of iron filings — but green in the sense that it is thick with living tissue, layered, competitive, ancient, and complicatedly employed.

---

The plants here do not eat light. There isn't any. They eat the difference between things.

Every night — and it is almost always night — a cold mass slides down off the eastern wall and crosses the shelf at walking pace, and it is not dry. It has picked up moisture from the melt-lenses and the seeps, and it carries that moisture as fog so cold it is only just fog. The vegetation exists to take the water out of that fog, and to take the *motion* out of it, and to make the two of them work against each other.

The dominant form is what you would call, on first encounter, a tree, and would stop calling a tree within about ninety seconds. It has a trunk: a fluted column of laminated fibre and grown mineral, waist-thick, four to nine metres tall, hard as boxwood and slightly warm. It has no leaves. Instead, at the top, it carries a horizontal wheel of stiff barbed filaments, three metres across, and the wheel *turns*. The downslope wind pushes it. The barbs comb water out of the fog and the water runs inward along grooves to the hub, and the turning of the wheel drives a shaft down the centre of the trunk, and at the base that shaft drives a helical pump housed in a swollen woody casing, and the pump lifts groundwater out of the gravel and pushes it up through a second set of vessels to a bladder halfway up the trunk, where it is mixed with the combed fogwater, warmed by friction, and held.

They are engines. They were not built. They are, as far as anyone can tell by opening them, *grown*, cell by cell, with the tolerances of something that has had a hundred million years to get the bearings right. The bearings are the strangest part: a cartilage collar flooded with a waxy oil the plant secretes, replenished continuously, self-cleaning, silent. That is the crucial word. Silent. A hectare of them turning in a moving fog produces less noise than your own breathing.

There are others. Squat drum-plants that store the wind's torque in a spiral of tensioned fibre and release it in one slow contraction twice a night, kneading their own root-bed to keep the gravel loose. Vines that run for four hundred metres along the ground, taut as cable, transmitting rotation from a stand of wheel-trees down into a bog where something anaerobic and enormous is being stirred. Bladderwort mats that inflate and deflate with a pressure differential so precise that they function as valves in a hydraulic network nobody designed and nobody maintains, and which has not failed within recorded observation. Coils of rust-red tissue on the boulder faces that are, if you cut them, laid out like a transformer: alternating laminae of conductive and insulating growth, and if you press the ends of a wire to them you get half a volt, steady.

It is an industry. Fog in, water and torque and a trickle of current out. No smokestacks, no combustion, nothing forged, nothing quarried. Machinery in the sense a heart is machinery.

---

The density is the thing that will unsettle you most, and it takes a few days.

There is a wheel-tree, and then there is fifty or sixty metres of dark open gravel with nothing on it but frost-crust and a few flat lichens, and then another wheel-tree. The forest is a scatter. It has to be: each tree needs its own uninterrupted lane of wind, and the shelf's water budget is thin. So you walk through a lush ecosystem the way you would walk through an emptied cathedral — the sense of great volume, great height, great structural intention, and almost nothing occupying it. Your eyes keep reaching for a middle distance and finding only the next pale column, turning, four seconds per revolution, and then the next, and then the vast tilted floor going on into the dark until the two walls take it.

The animals follow the same rule. There are many of them and you will hardly ever see two at once.

They come to the trees, because the trees are the wells. The bladder halfway up each trunk is warm liquid water in a landscape at minus nineteen, and a whole guild has evolved around the theft and the toleration of that theft. Small six-limbed things with fur like wet felt and no eyes at all climb the flutes and drink through a needle and pay in nitrogen. A grazer the size of a horse but built like a folding chair, all knee and shoulder, comes down out of the western scree once every few nights and sets its long face against the trunk and drinks in silence for twenty minutes; its coat is a matte black that swallows starlight so completely that you perceive it as a hole. Something hunts the grazer. You know this because you find the leavings: a compact stack of cleaned bone arranged, always, at the base of a wheel-tree, hub-side, on the downslope face. Twenty-two of these in eleven kilometres. Always the same arrangement. No one has seen it done.

And there is the matter of how anything communicates here, in air too thin to carry a shout past forty metres. They use the vines. The taut transmission-lines that link the tree-stands are struck, plucked, gnawed rhythmically, and the vibration travels kilometres. Put your ear against the ground on a still night and you hear the shelf talking: irregular knocks and long slurring glissandi and patterns that repeat with variations, running under your cheek from somewhere up-shelf to somewhere down-shelf, animals speaking through the plants' nervous system because the plants happen to be strung the right way, and the plants presumably taking something from it too — nobody's sure what.

Above ground it stays quiet. The loudest sound in a normal hour is the tick of a barb shedding an ice bead onto stone. Then nothing. Then, maybe, hooves that make no noise at all.

---

Rules of the place, as far as they can be stated:

Nothing here is still, but nothing here is fast. Everything is on the pace of fog, root-growth, glacier-weep, the turning of a wheel at fifteen revolutions per minute. Speed exists only in the predators, and only briefly, and never where you're looking.

Water is currency and is always in transit. There is not much of it, and none of it is idle. Every drop is either in the fog, in a barb, in a bladder, in a gut, or on its way back down through gravel to be lifted again. The shelf leaks perhaps a hundredth of what it recirculates.

Heat is manufactured, not received. Friction in ten thousand bearings, the compression of bladder-pumps, the slow chemistry in the stirred bogs. The whole system runs a couple of degrees warmer than it has any right to, and the animals sleep pressed against trunks.

And once a year the sun clears the eastern wall for eleven days. Nobody who has seen it describes it well. The fog stops forming; the wheels stop turning; the entire industry stands down and the shelf fills with an unbearable flat white light and a silence even deeper than the usual one, because even the vines go slack and the knocking stops. Everything alive goes underground or under stone. The plants shed their oil. For eleven days the machine sits there, glittering, motionless, absolutely visible, looking exactly like an abandoned works — and then the shadow comes back over the wall like a lid closing, and the fog reassembles, and one by one, without a sound, the wheels take up the wind again.
