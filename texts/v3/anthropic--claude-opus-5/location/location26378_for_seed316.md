## The Same

Begin with the water, because the water is what there is.

It lies across the whole of the continent at a depth of between four and nine centimetres — the variation is not local, it drifts, it arrives like a mood — and it is warm as the inside of a mouth. Blood-warm. You do not feel it on your legs after the first hour; you feel it only when it changes, and it is always changing. Beneath it: grey mud, firm enough to walk on, soft enough to take a print, and the print fills and smooths itself within a minute or two. Below that, more mud, and below that, mud, for a depth nobody has ever established, because the digging fills. The rock is down there somewhere. The rock was down there four billion years ago too, and it has been asked for so long, so patiently, to stop being a shape that it has finally agreed.

That is the first thing to understand about this place: erosion here has completed itself. It is not eroding. It is *eroded*, past tense, finished, the way a word said ten thousand times is finished. There are no hills. There is no gradient. Across a distance that would contain the Andes, the land rises and falls by less than the height of a sleeping dog. The rivers gave up long ago — not by drying, but by spreading, by widening until the concept of a bank became rhetorical, until the whole surface was river and no water anywhere knew which way to go. Water that does not know which way to go stands, and shivers, and waits for the wind.

The sky is a low grey lid at maybe four hundred metres, and it is made of this same water gone up. The heat lifts it; the lid holds it; it condenses and falls and lifts again, ten million times a day, a closed engine with nothing to do but turn. Light comes through the lid as an even silver with no source in it. There is no sun-disc, ever. There is a slow brightening and a slow dimming on a rhythm of about thirty-one hours, which is probably rotation and probably nothing to do with rotation. At its brightest it is the light of a bathroom at dusk. At its dimmest you can still see your hands, and the water gives back a faint pewter shine, so the horizon never fully closes; it just goes on being a smudge in every direction, a hundred kilometres of blur, and then more blur.

---

Now the sound, which is the difficulty, and the reason nobody stays.

It is a roar. It does not stop. It is loud enough that speech is a matter of pressing your mouth to someone's ear and shouting single words, loud enough that a new arrival will weep for two days from the pressure of it, loud enough that the water's surface stipples with it in still air. It has a texture like ten thousand simultaneous waterfalls, but with a grain in it, a repeated pulse, something almost articulate riding underneath.

Listen long enough and you will identify it, and the identification is worse than the noise.

It is one bird.

There is a wading bird here — tall, ash-coloured, knees backwards, a beak like a wet blade. It is rare. In fifty days of walking you might see three, and probably you have seen the same one three times. It makes a call: a low grating *kraaa* with a click at the end. It makes this call perhaps forty times a day. And you hear it forty times a day, multiplied by nine hundred million, arriving from every point of the compass at once, because of what has happened to place here.

---

Here is what has happened to place here.

A location knows where it is by what surrounds it. That is not mysticism, it is the plain arithmetic of being somewhere: this valley is *this* valley because the ridge is north and the river bends east and the rock is that particular red. Take away the ridge, the bend, the red. Take away every distinguishing feature by four billion years of patient rain. Flatten and flatten until any square metre is indistinguishable from any other square metre — the same grey mud, the same seven centimetres of warm water, the same silver overhead.

At some point in deep antiquity, the plain lost the ability to tell its parts apart. And then, not being a mind, having no way to hold a distinction it could not perceive, it simply stopped maintaining them.

So the Same is packed. That is the word travellers use and it is exactly right, though it takes a while to feel it. There is no room here. Every point on this continent is superposed with an enormous and unfixed number of other points, sliding through one another, held together by their own indistinguishability. The single reed-stand you are standing beside — black-green, brittle, sulphurous, the only vegetation for a thousand kilometres — is also present nine million times, and is also *only there once*, and both statements are operationally true. You can walk around it. You cannot walk away from it. It will be beside you again in an hour, with the same broken stem at the same height, and you will not be able to prove whether you have travelled twelve kilometres or none.

Vegetation: sparse. There are maybe eleven kinds of living thing in the whole world here, and you meet all of them constantly. Grey algal films that slick the mud in flat lobes and smell of iron. Low stromatolite domes, knee-high, cabbage-textured, three billion years old and still doing the one thing they do. The reed. A pale threadworm in the mud. Blind eels the length of a forearm that move under the water's skin in slow arcs and are, all of them, possibly one eel. And the bird. Sparse, rare, and inescapable — a small cast performing to a continental hall, every performance audible everywhere.

That is the roar. That is the whole roar. Rain on water, and one bird.

---

The turbulence comes from the disagreements.

Superposition here is not stable, because the flatness is not perfect. It is only *nearly* perfect, and nearness is the engine. Two square metres agree about everything — mud, temperature, light — but differ in elevation by a millimetre. That millimetre is a contradiction. The plain cannot hold it and cannot resolve it, so it *shears*: the two locales grind past each other, and where they grind, the water goes wrong.

You will see it coming, if you are watching, as a line of white on the smudge-horizon. It arrives as a wall of standing wave, half a metre high, moving fast and making no sound distinguishable from the general roar. Inside a shear-line the water runs in two directions at once through itself, and the mud below boils up in grey blooms, and the light stutters. Things that were far apart are briefly adjacent. You may see, for four or five seconds, a stand of reeds pass through you like a shadow through a shadow. You may hear a single call at close range, unmultiplied, appallingly intimate. Then the line passes and closes and the surface goes back to its stippling.

Shear-lines cross the Same at all times in all directions. There are perhaps four hundred thousand active at any moment. This is the weather: not fronts of air but fronts of *location*, a continent perpetually failing to settle the question of where it is, a storm-state with no possible calm, because the calm would require difference and difference is what has been taken away.

Rain falls through all of it, more or less continuously, in warm heavy columns. Vapour stands in slow white pillars where the shear-heat lifts it. From a great height — if there were a height — the whole thing would look like a grey sheet being agitated by something underneath, all over, forever.

---

And there are the built things.

There is a wall. Grey blocks, unmortared, six courses high, forty metres long, ending at both ends in nothing. There is a low platform of the same stone, four steps up out of the water, dry on top, with a shallow basin cut in it that is always full. There is a doorway: two uprights and a lintel, freestanding, with water on both sides of it.

You will meet these many times. There is no way to determine whether there are thousands of them or three. The stone is not from here — there is no stone here — so somebody carried it from somewhere with a *where*, and did the work, and stopped.

On the platform's top surface, on the dry stone, there are notches. Tally-marks, cut with something hard, in groups, in a spiral working outward from the basin. In places the spiral has been overcut by a later spiral, and that by a later one, and the whole surface is worn into a shallow dish by the pressure of hands. Nobody has counted the notches. Counting is not a thing this landscape supports.

That is the only technology: a sharp edge, a soft stone, and the wish to have been somewhere.

---

The rules, as the ones who go there have worked them out, shouted ear-to-mouth, one word at a time:

Walk. Do not stop walking, because standing still is how a shear-line finds you, and because motion is the only remaining evidence that anywhere is anywhere.

Do not count the reeds.

Drink from the basin, never from the plain; the plain is warm and clean and will not harm your body, but water that has been everywhere at once does something to the sense of having a location, and that is the sense you are living on.

If a shear-line brings you face to face with a figure walking the opposite way in the same posture with the same gear, let it pass. Do not raise a hand. Do not shout the one word you want to shout. The two of you are a millimetre's disagreement and the plain is trying to resolve it, and the resolution is not to your advantage.

Cut your notch and go back the way you came, understanding that there is no way you came.

---

And still people cross it, or try, and a few emerge on the far coast where the mud finally stiffens into shingle and the grey lid tears open and there is, thank god, a hill — a real hill, thirty metres of it, particular, unrepeatable, *itself*.

They say the silence on that shingle is unbearable for the first week. They say what they miss is not the bird.

What they miss is being everywhere.
