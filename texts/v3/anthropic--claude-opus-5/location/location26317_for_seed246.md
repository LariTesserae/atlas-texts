## The Drone Ground

Begin with the fact that the mountain is finished.

Whatever range stood here has been reduced to its own root: a plateau three thousand metres up and nearly level, the way a wave is nearly level after it has broken and spread and gone quiet on the sand. There are no peaks left in any direction. There is only this broad pale table of rock, and at its rim, a soft rolling-over of ground into the haze below — not a cliff. Cliffs are young. This edge is a slump, a shoulder, a thing that has spent so long being worn that it has forgotten how to be steep. You can walk off the plateau in any direction and never once need your hands.

The rock is a grey-white siliceous stone, dense, cold to the palm, and everywhere faintly grooved with the runnels of a hundred million rains. Rub it and nothing comes off. It has given up all the dust it intends to give.

## The City

In the middle of the table, occupying maybe four hundred paces by three hundred, stands the settlement.

Call it a settlement because there is no honest alternative. There are columns — sixty or seventy of them, no more — hollow, fluted, between three and eleven metres tall, with walls the thickness of a forearm. They stand in an arrangement that is unmistakably *planned*: broad avenues running roughly northeast, narrower cross-lanes, three plazas where the columns draw back and leave open floor, and a dense cluster at the northern end where the shafts crowd each other and fuse at their bases into a single knuckled mass, storey upon storey of old chambers grown over older chambers. Between the columns, floors: not flagged, but poured — a continuous pavement of the same substance, cambered so that water runs off it into channels, and the channels run to cisterns, and the cisterns are lidded with domed grilles of the same fabric, and the grilles are exactly the right coarseness to keep out grit and let in fog.

Nobody built this with hands. There were no hands. The builder was colonial and mineral-secreting and had no more idea of a city than a coral has of a reef, and it needed circulation, drainage, storage, and light, and so it made avenues, gutters, tanks, and windows, and the result reads as urbanism because urbanism is only what the constraints look like from outside. It grew this. Then it died — long ago, in the middle of the plateau's long flattening, for reasons that are not recorded anywhere because nothing here records — and left behind the calcareous-siliceous body of itself, which is the town.

And the town is a stump too. The columns were taller once; you can see it in the way their tops are not tops but breaks, worn to soft lips, some of them worn all the way down to knee-height stubs whose interiors have filled with moss and standing water. Erosion has been working on this settlement for so long that it has become part of the landform. There is not one sharp edge in the whole of it. Every doorway is a lozenge. Every corner is a curve. It has settled into its final shape, which is the shape of something that will simply get lower.

## The Sound

You will hear it from an hour's walk away, and by the time you are among the columns you will not be able to hear anything else.

The wind up here is constant and comes across a flat with nothing to break it, and it enters the columns through their broken tops, through the old windows, through the cistern grilles, and the whole town is a rank of organ pipes with the stops pulled out. It is not a whistle. Whistling requires small apertures and there are no small apertures left — attrition has widened every hole past shrillness. What the Drone Ground makes is a bass so low and so loud that it arrives in your sternum before your ears, a sound with a floor in it, three or four fundamental notes beating against each other and producing slow throbs, a great patient breathing that swells when a gust crosses the plaza and sags when it passes.

At full wind you cannot shout to a companion two metres away. You will not try twice. Everything in this place that communicates does so by touch.

The pitch is not fixed. It is seasonal, and it is tended. And it is, on the largest scale, falling: the city's voice has been descending for aeons as its pipes widen, from something like a scream, through a long era of chorus, to this present drone. It is going toward silence by way of getting deeper. Another few million years and it will be a pressure you can feel and nothing you can hear.

## Water

Real rain comes perhaps twenty days a year and comes hard, sideways, brief. Everything else here drinks air.

Twice a day, near dawn and near dusk, cloud comes up over the rim of the plateau — not descending from above but rising from below, the valley's exhalation — and it moves through the columns as a wet grey crowd. The fluting on the outside of every shaft is a condensation surface. Water beads in the grooves, runs down, gathers at the base, enters the gutters. In the cold hours it does this as rime instead, furring every westward face with white needles that fall off with a clatter in the first sun and melt into the same channels. The cisterns are always about a third full. They are never full and never dry. In a place with this much stone and this little rain, the town's plumbing is the only reason anything grows.

## What Grows

Lush is the wrong word if you are thinking of forest. It is the right word if you kneel.

Around the foot of every column, exactly as wide as the drip-line, there is a garden. Cushion plants in mounds so dense you can stand on them, dark green, studded in season with flowers the size of a match head, sulphur-yellow, unscented. Moss in six or seven kinds — one of them a sheet moss the colour of old copper that grows only on the sound-shadow side of columns and forms mats a hand deep. Liverworts in the gutters. In the cisterns, a fine green wool of algae, and floating on the surface of the darkest tank, a single population of tiny cupped plants that close at night. Lichen everywhere, in mustard and rust and a pale bruise-blue, so slow-growing that some of the crusts on the plaza floor are older than any tree that has ever lived.

The gardens are ring-shaped and the pavement between them is bare, so from above the town would look like a chart of pale streets with green sockets in it. Nothing grows in the avenues. The runoff does not go there.

## What Moves

You could stand in the third plaza for an hour and see nothing alive but plants.

Then: a moth, thumb-sized, wingless-looking until it opens — greasy grey, a moss-eater, wholly deaf, navigating by nothing you can determine. There are a great many of these across a year and very few at any moment.

And there are the stoppers. Pale, low, six-limbed, warm-blooded so far as you can tell, no larger than a fist, and they live inside the columns, in the old chambers, in the layered mass at the north end. They are deaf too — they must be — but they are not indifferent to the sound. They carry moss. All spring they climb the interiors and pack wads of the copper sheet moss into cracks and side-vents, and all autumn they pull it out again, and what they are doing, whether they know it or not, is tuning the town. The drone in high summer is a different chord from the drone in winter. The stoppers are why. Whether the sound matters to them as sound, or as vibration, or only as a by-product of a nesting instinct that happens to be architectural, cannot be settled by watching.

And four or five times in a season, a bird: a broad-winged grey thing that comes up from below on the thermal off the rim, circles the town twice, takes a moth out of the air, and goes back down. It does not land. Nothing here has any reason to land.

## Rules

Nothing in this place is sharp. Not the stone, not the edge of the plateau, not the wind, not the notes. Anything that arrives sharp will be rounded.

Nothing new begins here. The ecology is closed and has been closed a long time: these mosses, that moth, those stoppers, that lichen, and no colonists. Seeds blow up from the valley constantly and none of them take. The soil in the ring-gardens is the same soil it has been.

Sound is a weather, not a signal. It has seasons, it has fronts, it has calm. On the rare still mornings — perhaps ten a year — the town goes quiet, and the quiet is so unfamiliar that it feels like an event, like a held breath, and you can hear the drip of the cisterns and the clatter of falling rime and, if you are very close, the small dry rustle of a stopper hauling moss.

The town is not a ruin, because it was never abandoned; the builder did not leave, it stopped. There is no distinction here between architecture and geology, or between decay and completion. This is what a thing looks like when it has finished happening and has a very long time left in which to be finished.

Then the wind returns across the flat, and the whole grey city takes it in through its broken mouths, and the ground begins again to hum.
