## The Made Ground

Come up onto it at midday, when the vapor has sunk back off the rim, and the first thing you understand is that the floor is wrong. It is pale — the pale of a broken bone, of the inside of a shell — and it is flat, and it is under an inch of water everywhere, so that the whole plateau is a mirror seventy miles across. But it is wrong because it has no grain. Stone has a grain. Stone remembers being pressed, folded, cooked. This has none of that. Put your face to it and you see instead a fine radiating pattern, like the underside of a mushroom cap, like the pith of a cut reed, repeated at every scale you can resolve. It was extruded. All of it. The floor is not the ground the plants grow on. The floor is what the plants have put down beneath themselves, layer on layer on layer, and it goes down four thousand feet to the cloud and does not stop there.

There is no bedrock in this country. There is only work.

### The Throats

They stand very far apart. That is the shock after the floor: how empty the made ground is. You can walk half a day and have only two of them in sight, one behind and one ahead, each the size of a cathedral district, and between them nothing but the water-skin and the light bouncing off it and your own reflection walking upside down beneath your feet.

Call them throats. Each is a single organism, though nothing about them suggests an individual — more a masonry proposal that got out of hand. From the base, buttresses splay out and fuse into the floor, so seamlessly you cannot find the join; walk up one of the low ribs and you are walking up the animal, or the plant, or whatever grammar you want. Higher, the mass hollows. It becomes flutes, sleeves, nested cylinders, galleries with pale ribbed ceilings, chambers that open into chambers. Rooms. Unmistakably rooms, with lintels and thresholds and window-slots ranked at regular intervals, and no one has ever lived in a single one of them. Nothing has. There are no nests, no middens, no smoke-blacked corners. The throats grow rooms the way a fern grows fiddleheads: it is simply the shape the growing does.

At the top, three hundred to eight hundred feet up, each throat opens into a crown of upturned funnels, and this is the point of the whole enterprise. The air here is warm and heavy with water — the plateau sits inside a permanent inhalation from the drowned lowlands, vapor rising off something enormous and hot down there that nobody on the high ground has seen. The funnels condense it. Water gathers in the crown-basins and falls, inside the throat, down the whole hollow height, through constrictions and over baffles and around the spiral galleries, and comes out at the base into shallow radial channels that feed the mirror.

The water is not clear. It is faintly milky, faintly sweet, and it carries dissolved stone in solution — the throat drinks it up again through the floor and lays it down as more throat. The mass that falls is the mass that climbs. In goes out. This has been in balance since before anyone was counting.

### The Sound

You will not have silence again. Understand that before you come up.

Each throat sings. Not metaphorically — the falling water column drives air through the flutes and the whole structure sounds, continuously, one note, and it has sounded that note without a semitone's drift for as long as there have been ears to check. Standing at the base of a large one you feel it in your sternum and the roots of your teeth; the water-skin around it is shivered into standing rings, fixed rosettes of ripple that never travel. At a mile the note is a presence like weather. At five miles it is the horizon humming.

And each has its own pitch, according to its size and the length of its flutes, and the pitches do not change, and so the plateau is mapped by ear. Travelers do not learn landmarks. They learn chords. There is a place in the northeast where four throats stand in a rough diamond and their notes make a sour, gorgeous, unresolvable stack that you can hear from twenty miles in any direction, and everyone calls that region by the sound of it, and there is no other name for it and no need of one. Children are taught intervals before they are taught walking distances. To be lost here is a specific and rare disaster: it means fog thick enough to swallow the near throats *and* a head-cold. It means you are hearing only the far chord, the plateau-wide drone, which points nowhere because it comes from everywhere.

People shout, in this country, as a matter of course. Speech at conversational volume is an intimacy, conducted with mouth at ear. Songs are built to sit in the gaps between the local pitches. Nobody hums.

### The Rule of No Age

Here is the strangeness that takes longest to sit down in you.

Nothing on the Made Ground is old. Every edge is sharp. Every surface is the pale of the first day, ungrayed, unlichened, unstained. Corners are crisp. The window-slots have no drip-runnels beneath them. Set your hand on a buttress that was standing when your great-grandmother's great-grandmother was born and it feels like fresh plaster, faintly warm, faintly damp, faintly giving.

Because the throats repair at precisely the rate the water wears them. Cut a notch in the floor with a shell edge — it takes hours, and it hurts your hands — and come back in eleven days and you will not find it. Not the notch, not a scar, not a discoloration. The radiating pith-pattern will be continuous through the place where it was. The plateau does not heal, which implies time and process. The plateau *declines the event.*

So there is no erosion here in any sense a lowlander would recognize. No gullies, no talus, no rounded shoulders, no soil — no soil at all, anywhere, not a handful, and the absence has a smell, or rather the absence of the smell you expect: no loam, no rot, no mushroom dark. The air smells of hot wet mineral and faint sweetness and nothing else. There are no dead throats. There are no young ones either. Nobody has ever seen one begin.

### What Lives Here Besides

Very little, and it is all small, and it is nearly all inside the water.

On the mirror, a film: a single-celled green so thin it reads as a stain, growing in slow gyres wherever the radial channels slacken. Grazing on it, the flatlings — palm-sized, translucent, edgeless, more like a piece of wet paper deciding to move. They can be walked past a hundred times unnoticed and then you see one and then you see forty.

Inside the throats, in the falling columns, the ascenders: finger-length, glass-bodied, with a ring of hooks. They ride the water down through the galleries, get spat out at the base, and then climb — for days, up the inner flutes, against the wet, in the loudest place in the world — to be dropped again. That is their whole life and no one has determined why. They are the only creature that seems to be *enjoying* the plateau.

And rarely, once or twice in a walking season, high up, the long-winged things that cannot land quietly. They come in from over the rim, circle a crown, and go. Nobody has seen one at rest. There is a persistent story about something large that walks the empty stretches at night, big enough to be mistaken for a small throat until it moves — and it is a story precisely because nothing here moves, so the imagination is starved and builds one thing and keeps it.

### Rules

- Nothing burns. There is not a dry gram of anything in a hundred miles, and no wood in any case, and no charcoal in any dig, ever, at any depth.
- Nothing is quarried. The floor cannot be taken up; the throats cannot be cut; a chisel dulls before the pith yields, and the yield closes anyway. So there is no building. There is only occupation — you may sleep in a gallery, hang a woven mat across a threshold, set a gourd under a drip. The mat is the only made thing you own, and you made it from your own hair and the fiber of a lowland plant you carried up.
- Nothing is planted. Food comes up from below, or comes out of the film and the flatlings, and there is a whole grim discipline of scraping that nobody up here talks about with pleasure.
- Water is never scarce and never abundant. There is exactly this much, always, everywhere, an inch, forever.
- Nothing has news. Weather does not arrive; it hangs. The vapor sinks at midmorning and rises at dusk with the punctuality of a held breath being adjusted. The seasons register only as a slight change in the angle of the glare.

What people do up here, mostly, is listen — for the thing everyone half-expects and nobody has recorded: one of the pitches shifting. A crown clogging. A flute widening. One note in the plateau chord going flat by a hair, which would mean the balance had broken somewhere, which would mean the Made Ground had finally started, after all this time, to become a landscape.

It has not happened. Ask anyone. They will lean in close to your ear and say, in the small voice they save for real speech: *not yet.*
