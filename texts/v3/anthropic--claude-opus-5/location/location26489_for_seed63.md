## The Kiln at the Ninth Turning

There is a mountain on this world that is not tall enough to hold snow — there is no snow anywhere — but tall enough that the air thins to a mean, scraping cold at night. Its flanks are grey scree, angular, unsorted, the kind of slope that takes a step and gives back a half-step. Nothing grows on it because nothing grows anywhere. What lives here does not need light and never learned to want it.

Near the summit, on the north side, the scree parts around a low doorway of stacked slabs.

The doorway was made. That is the first strange thing. Three courses of dressed basalt on each side, a lintel of the same, the whole thing pitched slightly forward as if bowing, its joints packed with a grey-white cement that on inspection is not cement. Above the lintel someone cut a shallow spiral, and beside the spiral, five vertical strokes. The strokes have weathered to soft grooves; the spiral is nearly gone. Whoever cut them has been gone long enough that the mountain has taken back the trail they must have used.

Go through. Stoop; the lintel is at hip height.

---

Inside, the heat arrives all at once, the way water would if there were water. It is a dry, radiant, ovenish heat that comes off every surface at once — floor, walls, the low ceiling of the entry crawl — pressing on the eyeballs, drying the inside of the nose in three breaths. The rock is too hot to rest a palm on for longer than a slow count of four. The air smells of scorched salt and something faintly sweet, like burnt sugar, and under that a mineral sharpness, sulfur and hot iron.

The crawl runs forty paces and opens.

The chamber is the size of a cathedral nave and shaped roughly like one: high, narrow, vaulted by the accident of a collapsed gas bubble in old lava. Two hundred paces long, forty wide, the ceiling lost above in a darkness that is not empty. Far down at the chamber's low end, a fissure in the floor shows a line of dull cherry light, no brighter than the memory of an ember, and this is the only light in the world here. It does not illuminate. It gives the far wall an edge and takes away every other detail. Everything else must be learned by touch, by heat on the skin, by sound.

Which is a problem, because the chamber is packed.

---

You cannot put a hand anywhere. That is the plain fact of the place. There is no surface in the Kiln that is not occupied and no volume of air that is not crossed. The walls wear a coat two hand-spans thick of living bodies, and the coat moves — slowly, with the patience of a tide — and the ceiling hangs with drapes of them, long swagged curtains of hooked limbs where individuals grip individuals grip individuals, a chain of forty thousand animals hanging in a catenary. The drapes brush the top of your head. Small ones travel through the open air with a dry rattling drift, not flying exactly, more like a seed's fall sideways, using the chamber's thermal columns as a staircase. They land on you and stay, and more land on those, and within a minute of standing still you are wearing them.

They are not insects, but insects are the nearest lie. Plated, flattened, palm-sized to thumb-sized, the colour of old candle wax, with no eyes at all and no pigment to protect. Their backs are ridged like radiators. Their legs end in a small warm hook, and where the hook contacts rock it leaves a bead of glassy residue, and that residue is the cement in the doorway's joints.

They have no water in them. Nothing here does. Their blood is a salt eutectic — nitrates, chlorides, a little sulfur — which is liquid at the temperature of this chamber and solid at any temperature the outside world can offer. This is the whole law of the place, from which everything else follows: **to be alive here is to be hot.** Cooling is not discomfort; cooling is setting, hardening, the slow arrival of the statue. A creature that wanders too near the entry crawl and dwells too long feels its limbs stiffen and grow eloquent with weight, and it either turns back or it stops forever where it stands.

They eat heat. More precisely they eat the *difference* — they stand across a gradient, hook-feet on hot rock and radiator-backs into cooler air, and run their chemistry on the fall of it, the way a mill runs on the fall of a stream. This is why the chamber is full and the mountain outside empty. This is why they arrange themselves in that particular obsessive way, jostling for the good gradients, thick where the floor is hottest, thin where the wall has gone dull. The living coat on the walls is a heat map. If you could see it you could read the mountain's temperature by the depth of its fur.

---

And they are almost silent.

That is the thing that undoes people. Millions of animals in a stone room, and the sound is a whisper — the sift of dry flour poured from one bowl to another, continuous, directionless, so even that within a quarter hour you stop hearing it and hear instead your own pulse. The rock ticks occasionally as it expands. The fissure at the low end gives, every so often, a soft cough of gas, a sound like a heavy book closed in another room. A drape releases from the ceiling and reforms, and that is a long dry rustle, four or five seconds, unhurried.

Nothing here shrieks. There is no reason to. With no eyes and no voices, they speak by touch and by warmth, and a swarm that communicates through the soles of its feet has no use for noise. Sound in the Kiln is a thing that happens *to* them. Shout — go on — and the drapes let go. All of them, the length of the chamber, in a cascade you can hear travelling away from you, and then you are standing in a rain of bodies to the knee, and the air is briefly clear and blindingly hot, and then the reassembly begins, taking the better part of an hour, a slow rising hum of climbing.

---

The floor is not rock. Or: it was, once, some hundreds of thousands of cycles ago.

It is a slope of packed and fused corpses, cemented by that same glassy foot-residue, compacted into a pale nacreous stone that rings faintly underfoot and crumbles at the edges into a gravel of individual plates and hooks. It is nine or ten times the thickness of a body wall. Wherever the floor rises to meet a cooler part of the chamber, the stone is white and dead and hard; wherever it lies over the hot fissure it is soft, worked, dug through with galleries. The dead are the architecture. There is no other architecture — except the doorway, and the eleven niches.

The niches are cut into the west wall at the height of a standing person, spaced by a stride, each a hand's breadth deep, each with a lipped floor. Eight of them hold shallow bowls of fired clay. The bowls never held water. They hold a crust of white salts, mounded, polished into a low dome by generations of hooked feet crossing over them. Somebody once carried salt up a mountain into an oven, in the dark, and set it out in bowls, at intervals, for reasons that did not survive them. The three empty niches are empty because the bowls are on the floor below, broken, half-swallowed by the pale stone. You could work out how long ago from the depth. It would not tell you anything you want to know.

---

There are hunters.

They walk the ceiling on ten legs, arm's-length long, and they are the only things in the chamber that hurry. They come along the drapes from above, from the unmapped dark of the vault, and they harvest — not one at a time; they eat the chain, running along a hanging curtain and taking every third or fourth body, and the drape sags, adjusts, closes ranks, and does not panic, because panic is not in it. A drape being harvested makes a sound like a fingernail going down a comb, very quietly, once, and then the sift resumes. The hunters are hotter than everything else. You know when one has passed above you because a bar of warmth crosses your scalp.

And the whole chamber breathes on a cycle of about half a day. When the outside night comes, cold air sinks in through the doorway and rolls along the floor to the fissure, and the population moves — the coats thin and shift downward, the drapes lengthen, the whole living mass migrates twenty or thirty paces toward the heat in a slow silent tide, and in the vacated upper chamber the walls tick and cool and shed a fine sand of stiffened bodies that did not move fast enough. When the outside day comes and the doorway warms, the tide returns upslope, and the fallen are trodden into the floor, and the floor grows by the thickness of a fingernail every few thousand cycles.

That is the whole world. Two hundred paces of it. Beyond the doorway, grey scree and a sky with no moisture in it to make a colour, and no other place like this for four hundred miles.

The Kiln is not going anywhere. It has found its shape and keeps it: heat in from below, cold in at the door, bodies distributing themselves across the difference like filings across a magnet, dying into stone at the margins, and the stone rising slowly toward the heat, and the heat, in its own slow time, sinking away.

When it finally cools, the floor will hold every one of them, in place, in position, mid-climb — an entire congregation set at the moment of its last warm second, in a room with eleven niches and a bowl of salt.
