## The Sill

Come up the cable and the first thing you notice is that the mountains are gone.

They were here. The stone under everything is the root of a range — you can see the grain of it where the escarpment breaks, folded strata standing on end like the pages of a book slammed shut and then, over an unimaginable duration, sanded to a table. What remains is a plateau at three and a half thousand metres, roughly the shape of a county, hemmed on all sides by cliffs that drop into a permanent white floor of cloud. Three stone knuckles rise from the surface, polished round, none of them higher than a mill chimney. They are called the Nubs. They are what is left of summits.

Nothing here is young. Even the erosion has finished; the plateau has reached its final form and stopped, and everything on it takes that as instruction.

## The Haulm

You do not walk on rock. The stone is two hundred metres down, sealed under the haulm: compacted living plant-mass, fibre laid on fibre for so long that the lower layers have gone to something between peat and hardwood, and the upper metre is a springy dense felt of stems, root-braid, and the year's new growth threading through last year's. It gives underfoot, half an inch, and pushes back. Bootprints close over in a fortnight. Cart ruts take a season.

The haulm is cool at the surface and warm at four metres down. It is not soil. There is no soil on the Sill. Nothing has died here badly enough to become dirt.

Out on the open plain the haulm carries a low heath of **fog-combs** — waist-high, grey-green, leaves like the teeth of a card comb, all held edge-on to the prevailing wind. They are the water system. Twice a day the cloud floor rises over the lip of the escarpment and comes across the plateau as fog, and the combs strip it, and the water runs down their stems into the braid and away underground toward the stands. There are no rivers. There are no ponds. There is one bucket of standing water in this entire landscape and it is on a windowsill somewhere, put there deliberately, by someone who wanted to look at it.

Between the combs: space. That is the character of the Sill more than anything else. It is a big place mostly consisting of the distance between things.

## The Stands

Every twenty or thirty kilometres, out of the heath, a stand of **flue-cane** rises.

Imagine bamboo grown to the proportions of a cathedral spire: two hundred metres tall, six to nine metres across at the base, walls of dense fibrous wood a hand's breadth thick, hollow inside, jointed at intervals by nodal floors you can walk on. Grey-white, faintly waxed, banded with the scars of shed scales. A stand is anywhere from nine to two hundred canes, leaning very slightly outward from one another, and the canes are the city.

They are drilled, floored, glazed, laddered, hung with iron. Bridges span between them at seven and eleven and fourteen levels, some of them cane-fibre cable, some of them grown — the canes have been coaxed for so long into meeting one another that many bridges are simply a limb of one cane fused into the flank of the next, with a walkway pinned inside it. Water-closets, tram halts, foundries, dormitories, the offices of the Pressure Assize. Fifty thousand people live inside plants on this plateau. Nobody thinks this is remarkable and nobody has ever done otherwise.

Nine stands are occupied. Fourteen are not — hollowed out, floored, wired, and empty, the windows still glazed. A cane lives about nine hundred years and then goes brittle at the base, and when the Assize condemns one, its district must move to a young cane, and a young cane needs ninety years to be fit. So the city is always partly evacuating and partly waiting, and it has been doing this so long that the direction of the drift has forgotten what it was.

## Pressure

There is no coal on the Sill. There is no fire on the Sill worth mentioning; the substrate is ninety percent water and the air is cool and wet and nothing catches. What there is instead is pressure.

A flue-cane at full transpiration lifts water two hundred metres. To do it, the vessels in its walls run at fifteen atmospheres, sometimes eighteen, and the whole industry of the plateau is a tap on that. Bronze thief-valves are driven into the cambium at the third node — three thousand of them in the largest stand — and the take is manifolded into iron mains that run down the inside of the cane like a spine. From there: pneumatic trams that fire along cable in the between-air and dock with a bang; drop-hammers; fibre presses; the great slow looms of the Draw, which weave cane bast into cloth of a grey that holds no dye; the retorts of Sparroway, where the resin is cooked down to varn, the varnish that keeps the rest of the world's ships from rotting, if the rest of the world still has ships.

Electricity too, weakly. There is a standing potential between the outer bark and the pith, and cambium taps yield a current that drifts and surges with the plant's own rhythm. The lights on the Sill breathe. They go bright and dim on a twelve-second cycle that everyone's heart has learned.

The whole system wakes at once. At dawn, when the stomata open across ten thousand hectares of canopy, the mains charge in about four minutes, and the city shudders — a rising groan in the wood, valves knocking all up the spine, dust jumping off every horizontal surface, and then the hammers start. This is called the Charge. Visitors sleep through it once. Only once.

## Sound

It is a loud place, and this is the first thing about the Sill that will not sit quietly in your head.

Some of the noise is simple. A hollow cane two hundred metres tall is an organ pipe; the wind never stops up here; a stand of ninety canes with holes cut in it at eleven levels produces a chord, and the chord shifts as the wind veers, and it is enormous, and it is under everything. Add the hammers, the trams, the shift whistles, the constant tick of condensation coming down the inside of the walls into the catch-gutters.

The rest of the noise has no source.

The haulm records. Nobody knows the mechanism and the Assize has stopped funding the question. Sound laid down in the plant-mass comes back up out of it, decades later, centuries later, thin but perfectly clear and in the wrong place. Out on the empty heath, twelve kilometres from any occupied cane, you will walk through the full clangour of a foundry — pour-bell, crane, men shouting in an accent no one alive uses — and walk out the other side of it in forty paces. Fossil noise. It has favourite spots. There is a stretch of the Ninestacks road where a shift whistle blows every day at 6:40 for a mill that was condemned in a year nobody can now name, and the carters take their break by it, because it is a good whistle, and it is reliable, which the rest of the plateau is not.

Two facts about the fossil sound that people find harder to be casual about. First: it is not always old. Occasionally a sound surfaces that is going to happen, and the interval is short and mean — hours, sometimes minutes. There is a small profession of men and women who sit in the empty stands and listen for their own names. Second: the Sill is loud because it is empty. The noise is the plateau's memory of a time when there were three times as many people, and the memory has not been informed.

## Rare Fauna

Almost nothing here is an animal.

The Sill is a closed vegetal world — three hundred species of plant, forty of fungus, no birds, no rodents, no insects in the sense you mean. When the ecosystem requires an animal function, it grows one. At blossom-season the canes bud **carriers**: pale six-limbed things the size of a cat, made of the same fibre as everything else, mute, warm, faintly waxy, with no gut and no blood. They do not eat. They photosynthesise, badly, and run the pollen from stand to stand in a mindless purposeful traffic, and at the end of eleven weeks they lie down wherever they are and go soft and are reabsorbed. Children keep them. Children are always told not to keep them.

Occasionally something arrives from below — a bird carried up on a thermal, a moth in a crate on the cable. It lives four or five days. Then it slows, and its outline stays, and it becomes cane-fibre in the shape of a bird, and in a month it is nothing but a hard grey lump in the haulm, and in ten years the plateau will grow another one exactly like it somewhere else, and possibly it will fly.

Because that is the plateau's habit. **It repeats shapes.** That is the whole of its genius and the whole of the problem.

## The Rules, As The Assize Publishes Them

**On distance.** Routes across the open heath must be counted aloud, in paces, by at least one member of every party. An uncounted distance does not hold its length. This is not superstition; it is in the survey code, with fines. Consequently the roads of the Sill are loud with counting — carters droning, work-gangs singing numbered songs, tram drivers ticking off nodes on a bell. The measured chains between stands are re-walked and re-counted every eleven days by paid pacers, and where a chain has drifted more than one part in forty, the drift is entered in the book and the road is closed and re-counted from the other end. Twice in recorded history a chain has come back shorter than the sum of its own segments. Those segments were burned out of the register. You cannot burn anything on the Sill. They tried anyway.

**On duplicate rooms.** Where the plant has repeated a structure — and it repeats structures constantly, since it has been growing rooms for people for eight thousand years and has learned the shape of a room the way it learned the shape of a leaf — the second instance is Crown property and must be reported. Do not sleep in it. Do not put your own furniture in it. There are perhaps four hundred such rooms known, in the empty stands mostly, each an exact recurrence of a room somewhere else: the same door, the same shelf-notches, the same wear-hollow in the sill where an elbow rested for forty years. Some of them contain a coat. The coats have been examined. They are made of cane bast, in one piece, with no seams, in the shape of a coat that somebody owns.

**On the Standing Crop.** The Sill will not keep the dead. A body committed to the haulm sinks and disperses within a year, and between one and nine years after that, at the margin of the nearest occupied stand, the plant grows a figure. Human-shaped, human-sized, fibre and wax, faceless in the details but unmistakable in the stance — the set of the shoulders, the turned-in left foot, the way a particular woman held her hands behind her back. They stand in the heath in loose scattered ranks, several hundred to a stand, and they move slightly, and over about thirty years they lean and go down and are taken back. They are not the dead. Everyone knows they are not the dead. It is simply that this plateau, which has stopped changing, cannot stop repeating, and it has learned people the way it learned the birds. Families visit. There is a rule against speaking to them, and the rule is universally broken, and no one has ever been fined for it, ever, in the whole history of the Assize.

**On names.** The oldest districts have no names. The signs are not weathered — the letters have *settled*, gone smooth and flush with the plate, the way the mountains went flush with the plain. When a name is lost the Assize does not reissue it. That would be a new thing, and the Sill is finished making new things.

## The Cable

At the north lip, at Draw End, the gondola line goes over the edge and down into the cloud floor: a hundred and forty iron cars on an endless loop, driven by a pressure engine the size of a church, running day and night. The loaded cars go down — board, bast cloth, varn in sealed crocks, the whole product of the plateau. The cars come back up empty.

They have come back empty for as long as the registers go, and the registers go back very far. Down there, under the white, there is presumably a receiving station and a road and a country. Nothing has come up the cable but weather and, once, a moth. No message. No refusal, either — the cars go down full and come up empty, so *something* is taking delivery.

Everyone works. The whistles blow, the hammers fall, the mains charge at dawn, the pacers walk the chains counting, and the shipments go over the edge into the cloud, and the plateau records all of it in its fibre to be played back to nobody in four hundred years.

## Weather

Full sun, most days. You are above the cloud, and the light up here is clean and cold and hard, a light with nothing in it, and it comes off the grey canes and the grey heath and the white floor with a flat glare that makes distance impossible to judge — which is, everyone agrees, half the problem with distance in the first place.

The fog comes twice: after the Charge and again in the last hour of light. It arrives as a wall over the escarpment lip, moves across the plain at the speed of a walking man, and swallows the stands to the ninth level. In the fog the chord of the canes goes soft and doubles, and the fossil noise gets much worse, and nobody walks the roads at all, counting or not.

Rain, perhaps nine days a year, and it is an event. Children are let out into it.

And once every century or so — the last time was recent enough that there are people who remember it — a cane sheds a scale from high up: a curved plate of bark eleven metres long, weighing as much as a tram car, that comes down through the bridges edge-on. There is a warning tone the wood makes about ninety seconds beforehand. Every person on the Sill knows that tone. It is the only sound here that everyone believes on the first hearing.
