You come to it downward, though nothing about the descent feels like descent. A split in a bank of clay after rain, the kind of gap a fox would ignore. You go in shoulder-first and the passage is short — three body-lengths of cool root-hair and wet grit — and then it opens, and you are standing in daylight.

That is the first thing to accept and the hardest. There is no shaft above you, no crack of sky, no glow-fungus, no fire. There is simply the ordinary light of an overcast morning, generous, even, entirely sufficient to read by, and it is coming through the walls.

The walls are leaves.

Not leaves scattered on a surface: leaves as the surface, as the fabric of the enclosure. Great overlapping panes of them, each the size of a door, each held in a lattice of green wood that has gone hard and slightly translucent like unripe horn. They are backlit. Behind each pane there is brightness — the diffuse, weatherless brightness of a sun somewhere above a cloud deck — and the light comes through the leaf-flesh stained faintly bitter-green, so that everything in the chamber has the color of a hand held over a candle. Shadows fall. They fall *outward*, away from the walls, toward the middle of the room, from all directions at once, which means the room has no shadows at its center and a hundred converging ones at its edges, and when you stand in the middle you cast nothing at all.

Behind the leaves, if you press your eye to the seam where two panes overlap, there is soil. Cold, packed, root-threaded, absolutely opaque. You are underground. The light is arriving anyway.

---

The chamber is large and almost entirely empty, and the emptiness is its most important quality. Call it forty paces across, though see below regarding measurement. In all that volume there is: the floor, which is moss — deep, dry, springy, ankle-swallowing moss the color of old brass; a single column rising off-center from floor to ceiling, a braided trunk of eleven or twelve strands twisted around a hollow core; a low swelling of wood near the eastern wall, waist-high, flat-topped, unmistakably the shape and height of a bench; and, on the moss about ten paces from the bench, a scatter of five objects.

Nothing else. No undergrowth. No litter. No bones, no burrows, no droppings, no webs. The air between these things is so unoccupied that it acquires a presence of its own, a held-breath quality, like the inside of a bell before it is struck.

---

Except it is being struck, constantly. This place is loud.

The sound comes mostly from the column and from the lattice of the walls, and it is the sound of things growing at a rate that ought to be impossible. In the braided trunk there is a surf: a rhythmic, arterial *hush-BOOM, hush-BOOM* of sap being driven upward under pressure enough to split stone, loud as waves inside a sea cave, and you can feel it in the floor through your heels. The walls tick and crack. New wood is being laid down fast enough to fail and re-form; every few seconds somewhere a fiber goes with a report like a knuckle, or a long fibrous *rrrip* as a leaf-pane expands and its edges tear free of the pane beside it. Above, in the ceiling-canopy where the panes are smallest and packed like fish scales, there is a continuous high seething, a sound like rain on rain, which is not rain.

Under all of it, at the threshold of hearing, is a murmur that the ear insists is a crowd. Many voices at conversational volume in a room next door. It is the stomata — several billion pores opening and shutting on the undersides of the leaves, exhaling. Knowing this does not stop it sounding like people. The words are always just about to resolve.

---

Twice in what you will come to think of as a day, the chamber breathes in a different way, and it rains.

You get about a minute of warning: the murmur drops away entirely, the sap-surf slows, and the air goes from warm-dry to warm-thick in the space of ten breaths. Then the ceiling lets go. It is not a downpour. It is the collected condensation of the canopy releasing in fat, cold, deliberate drops, thousands of them, hitting the moss with a sound like applause and soaking you through in ninety seconds. The moss drinks it instantly. There are no puddles. There is no runoff, no channel, no stain of water on the wood.

Between rains the place is *arid*. The moss goes crisp underfoot. Pollen lifts off the lower leaf-panes in yellow smokes when you brush past, and hangs, and does not settle, because there is nowhere lower for it to go. Your lips crack. Then it rains again and you forget the dryness the way you forget cold in a bath.

The second rain of each day falls twice. Once as described, and again — the identical rain, drop for drop, the same cold, the same applause — some unpredictable interval later, sometimes a few breaths, sometimes long enough that you have stopped expecting it. The moss is wet before the second one arrives.

---

Everything here is new. That is the other thing to accept.

Nothing in this chamber is worn. No edge has been rounded. The wood of the bench-swelling still beads sap at its pores, tacky, faintly resinous, and if you sit there you will need to peel yourself off. The leaf-panes have raw white margins where they are still unfurling. The braided column shows the bruise-purple of very young bark. The moss has no dead layer beneath it — dig a hand in and you reach clean pale rhizoids and then soil that has never held a root before, soil with the cut-open smell of a fresh spade.

This room was not found. It was *grown*, recently, in a single push — the way a fruit swells. The plant made a hollow inside itself and inside the earth, and it did so last season, and it is not finished.

---

The five objects on the moss:

A blade of knapped green stone, palm-length, worked on both faces, the flake-scars sharp enough to catch a fingernail. A thorn awl, black, six inches, socketed into a grip of bound bast. A hank of that same bast cord, twisted three-ply, coiled properly, the coil tied off with a professional little hitch. A fire-drill with its hearth-board, the notch charred. A wooden bowl, adze-hollowed, tool-marks still legible in the bottom.

There is no ash anywhere. There is no evidence anyone has ever been here. The objects lie as if set down mid-task by someone who intended to come straight back. And they are warm — hand-warm, blood-warm, warmer than the air, all five of them, always.

Take one. Carry it out through the split in the clay bank. It will be in your hand when you emerge, and it will be in the moss when you go back.

---

Rules, as far as they can be determined:

The chamber cannot be measured. Pace it out and you will get forty paces in one direction and nine in the other, and the nine will be the long way. Return with cord and the cord will be the wrong length in both directions. The lattice does not enclose a volume; it *implies* one, and the implication is generous.

The ceiling believes it is sky. Not metaphorically — functionally. Weather happens there. It has a wind that arrives from nowhere and combs the canopy in one direction for an hour. Once you will see, through the packed scales overhead, the shadow of birds crossing, a whole ragged skein of them going somewhere, unhurried, silent. There are no birds. There is soil.

Seeds fall upward. Small hard black ones, released from the canopy, drifting *up* out of sight, which is also the direction they came from.

Words spoken aloud here are absorbed. You will not notice at first. You speak and the sound goes into the moss and the wall-murmur gets very slightly louder and more nearly intelligible, and after a while you find you would rather not add anything more to it.

You cannot be here twice. You can enter twice. But the second time, the bench will be on the western wall, and you will not be able to remember which wall it was before, and both memories will feel like the original.

---

The fauna, such as it is, amounts to this. During the second rain — only ever the second, the repeated one — a small pale frog, translucent as a peeled grape, will be sitting on the flat of the bench with its throat going. It watches you with the total indifference of something that does not consider you a category. When the rain stops it is not there, and there is nowhere it could have gone. Twice a season a moth the size of a hand works its way along the lower leaf-panes, and it is always the same moth, with the same notch torn out of the left forewing, and it is always working in the same direction, and it never arrives anywhere.

And once — once only, and you will spend a long time deciding whether to believe it — the light behind one of the great leaf-panes will be interrupted. Something will move across it on the far side, on the soil side, close enough to press. A shape with an outline. It will pause. It will lay itself flat against the pane, and the pane will bow inward very slightly under the pressure, and the shape of five spread fingers will come through the green as a darker green.

Then it will lift away, and the light will be as it was, and the sap will go *hush-BOOM* in the column, and the murmur in the walls will be a fraction closer to speech.
