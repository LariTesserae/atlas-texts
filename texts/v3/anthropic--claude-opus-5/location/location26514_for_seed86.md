## The Hall Country

Begin with the fact that there is no sky, and then correct it: there are eleven skies, each of them small and round and very far away.

The country lies under a limestone plateau that was lifted out of a shallow sea and has not settled since. Above, there is forest — you can infer this without seeing it. Below, in the thickness of the stone, the water that came before the lifting hollowed out a system of galleries on a scale that has nothing to do with caves as most people mean the word. These are not passages. They are valleys with lids. A person standing on the floor of the largest of them cannot see either wall. The ceiling is ninety metres up and reads, in the dimness, as overcast weather.

The lifting drained them. That is the first and most important fact about this place: the water that made it has gone down to a lower level, into the sumps and the black rivers under the floor, and what is left is a country in a jar. Sealed. Cool. Finished.

---

### Terrain

The floor of the great galleries is flat, and it is pale, and it goes on. Fine sediment, the last silt of the last flood, laid down smooth and then left. In places it is still ridged in the low ripples the current made when it withdrew — corduroy hills two fingers high, holding their exact shape for tens of thousands of years because nothing has come to unmake them. No rain falls here. No wind runs the length of the halls with force enough to move a grain. The ripples are as sharp as the day the water stopped.

Where the sediment thins, the rock shows through, and the rock is *young* in the way a broken plate is young. Fractured blades, fluted knife-edge ridges, hollow scallops with lips like shell. The lifting cracked the limestone and the cracking is recent enough that the edges have not been rounded by anything. There is no soil to soften them, no frost to crumble them, no roots except in the eleven places where roots can reach. Walking barefoot across the bare-rock zones is not possible. The stone cuts. It does not cut the way weathered stone cuts, bluntly; it cuts like flint, in long clean lines, and the cuts do not heal fast in the cool damp.

Collapse has happened, is the other thing. In eleven places along the plateau, the ceilings of the galleries have failed and let the forest in. From below, each of these is a shaft — a chimney of blocks and hanging vines and green fog — and at the bottom of each is a mound of talus the size of a small hill, still angular, still unstable at the top, blocks the size of huts stacked with their fresh faces outward. These are the only slopes in the country. Everything else is level. You can walk for two hours in any direction and not gain the height of your own knee, and then you will meet a talus cone and climb forty metres of broken roof to stand in a shaft of daylight the colour of pond water.

### Light

Dim is the whole account of it. There is no bright and no black; there is the long gradient between the two, and the gradient never moves.

Under a skylight, at what would be noon above, the light is enough to read a hand by, greenish, filtered through eighty metres of hanging fig and fern and the mist that the temperature difference makes at the mouth. Fifty paces out from the base of the cone, the light halves. Two hundred paces, and it is the light of a room with the shutters closed. Six hundred paces, and it is the memory of light — you can tell floor from ceiling and nothing else. Beyond a kilometre from any of the eleven wells, in the deep bays and side halls, the darkness is total and has been total continuously since the roof went up, and things live there that have no eyes to be offended by the fact.

Because the shafts are vertical and narrow relative to their depth, the light never swings. No dawn side, no dusk side. The pool of grey under each skylight brightens and dims with the day above and with the weather above, and that is the only clock the country has except for the animals.

### Water

Damp, everywhere, insistently, and yet you could die of thirst here if you chose your route badly.

The walls sweat. Every surface in the country is faintly wet, a skin of condensation that gathers on the cold rock and beads and creeps. Where it collects, it drips, and the dripping is constant and uncountable and everywhere at once, a background hiss like static, the ceilings raining on the floors at a rate of a few millimetres a year. Under the skylights the drip becomes real rain: the collapse shafts carry the plateau's runoff down, so each of the eleven has its own private weather, a permanent fine drizzle at the base of the cone, and around each, a wet ring.

In the ring there are pools. Rimstone-dammed, shallow, absurdly clear, floored with white sediment, the water so still that the surface exists only as a change in the way the light behaves. And from a few of the pools, thin braided streams run out across the flat sediment floor, twenty centimetres wide, ankle-deep, wandering for a few hundred metres in loops before they find a crack and go down. They do not carve. They are too small and too slow and the country has been carved already. They lie on the floor like laid string.

Between the wet rings — dryness. Tens of square kilometres of dry pale sediment, cool and floury underfoot, and dust that lifts and hangs because nothing here ever pushes it away.

### The animals

And now the actual population of this country, which is the reason the air is loud.

Consider one roost. On the ceiling of the third great hall, over an area of roughly two hectares, the limestone is furred. Not spotted — furred, wall-to-wall, a pelt of bats hung belly-out and packed to the density of grain in a sack. Several million of them. They shift constantly, squabbling in a high dry chatter that carries down ninety metres and arrives as a hiss of grease in a pan. Below the roost is a cone of guano eleven metres high and steaming faintly, and the cone is *moving*. Its entire surface, continuously, at every hour: cockroaches in a shifting carpet, guano beetles by the tonne, blind mites in drifts, the whole mass rippling like a slow liquid, and the sound of it, close up, is a rustle you feel in the teeth.

Around the roost's edge, the predators of the roost's edge. Cave racers — pale slender snakes, two metres, three — draped in the ceiling cracks with their heads out, waiting; each will take a bat out of the air by touch alone. Amblypygids the size of a spread hand on the vertical walls, feeling ahead of themselves with whip-legs longer than their bodies. Centipedes the colour of raw liver, hunting the crickets. Cave crickets themselves in hundreds of thousands, legs like wire, coating the walls in the twilight zone in a moving stipple, and their scraping is the second layer of the country's noise.

In the shafts, swiftlets: tens of thousands per skylight, and they echolocate with sharp clicks, and when the colony comes in at dusk the clicks together make a sound like gravel poured continuously onto a drum, and the wings make wind that you can feel on the face.

In the pools: shrimp with no pigment, blind catfish that come to a hand held in the water and taste it, snails on the rimstone, frogs in the light-rings with skin the colour of stone, harvestmen on the wet walls in packed mats that pulse when disturbed and settle again.

Nothing here is rare. Everything here is in numbers that read as weather rather than as wildlife. And yet —

### Density

— and yet the country is mostly empty. That is the paradox of it and you feel it as loneliness even with the roar going on.

Life needs the two things this place hoards: light and food-import. So life is where the skylights are, and under the roosts, and along the string-streams, and nowhere else. The seams are saturated to the point of horror. The spaces between the seams are as clean and blank as a salt flat. You walk out of the sound of the third hall's roost and within fifteen minutes the noise has fallen behind you and the floor is unmarked pale silt with nothing on it, and it stays that way for an hour of walking, and the only living thing you meet in that hour might be one wandering cricket, and it will be moving in a straight line because it is going somewhere else.

Ninety percent of the Hall Country is a cool empty room. The other ten percent is a slaughterhouse.

### Vegetation

Only at the eleven wells, and only within the reach of the light.

At the top of each cone, hanging in from the shaft's rim: strangler figs with roots eighty metres long, dropped like plumb-lines and swinging fractionally; whole vine-curtains; ferns in the wet cracks. On the cone's flanks: pale seedlings — thousands of them, sprouted from fruit dropped by the bats and the swiftlets, and all of them stalled at a hand's height, etiolated, yellow-green, doomed, replaced. A permanent nursery that never graduates anyone.

On the flat floor around the wet ring: mosses in low green rings, a few metres wide, marking the exact limit of usable light. Algal film on wet stone, slick as oil. Beyond that, at the very margin of the visible, a scatter of grey lichens, and then nothing at all — bare rock, bare silt, for kilometres.

### Traces of hands

There are no structures. Not one. No wall, no shelter, no path, no mark cut in the stone.

But on a broad ledge at the base of the second skylight's cone, three metres above the wet, there is a smoke stain on the ceiling the size of a blanket, and under it: a coil of twisted bark rope, gone stiff; four bundles of split cane, cut with a stone edge and bound; a bamboo pole four metres long with a hook lashed to the end for reaching nests; a scatter of chert flakes and one broken blade; a gourd with a wooden stopper; a folded pad of leaves that were green when they were folded. Everything is cool and slightly damp and none of it has rotted, because nothing rots quickly at thirteen degrees.

That is the whole of the human presence. People come — up the shaft on rope, down the shaft on rope — and they take nests and guano and they leave the heavy gear behind because carrying it out is stupid when you will be back. They do not sleep here more than they must. Nobody has ever lived in the Hall Country. It is not that it is hostile. It is that it is *finished*, and there is nothing in it a person could change.

### Rules of the world

**Temperature is a constant.** Thirteen degrees, plus or minus a fraction, at every point in the country, in every hour, in every year. No seasons reach here. Cool enough that a still, wet body loses heat steadily; not cool enough to be called cold. You are never warm here and never freezing, and after a day the constancy becomes the most oppressive thing in the place, worse than the dark.

**Sound tells distance; light does not.** You navigate by ear. Every hall has its own voice — the third's grease-hiss, the fifth's drip-cathedral, the shaft-halls' gravel-clatter of swiftlets — and a person who knows the country can place themselves within a hundred metres from the mix of noises alone. Shouting is useless. The echoes in a space this size come back four and five seconds later, from three directions, degraded.

**Nothing above the floor is clean.** Under a roost, the rain is not water.

**The animals are the calendar.** Twice a day the bat tide: the outflow at dusk, a column pouring up through a shaft for forty minutes with a sound like a river in flood, and the inflow before dawn, the swiftlets crossing them in the opposite direction. This has happened at the same times, in the same order, in the same numbers, for as long as the country has existed, and the population does not grow and does not crash. The roosts are full because they have always been full. The guano cones are eleven metres high because eleven metres is where the beetles eat exactly as fast as the bats deposit.

**And nothing changes.** No new passage opens. No old one closes. The talus cones do not settle, having settled. The ripples on the floor hold. The stone edges stay sharp for want of anything to blunt them. The water level has not moved a hand's width in living memory or in dead memory either. It is a young landscape — raw, unsoftened, everything still showing the marks of its making — held in a state of perfect arrest, and it will look exactly like this when the people who left the rope on the ledge have been forgotten by the forest above.

Loud, cool, damp, dim, immense, teeming, empty, and absolutely still.
