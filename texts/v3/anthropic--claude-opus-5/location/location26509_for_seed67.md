## The Maintenance Plain

Stand anywhere on it and the horizon is a straight grey line with a faint upward curve at the extreme edges of vision, so that you understand, without instruments, that the floor you are standing on wraps a world.

The floor is the world. There is no soil under it, no bedrock, no crust in the geological sense — there is plate, and beneath plate there is void-frame and coolant gallery and the slow turning of shafts, and beneath that more plate, and it goes down as far as anyone has gone. Everything here was made. Not built over something; built *instead* of something. The distinction matters less than you'd expect after the first day.

### Ground

The plate comes in hexagons roughly nine meters across, sintered from a grey-green material with the density of basalt and the ring of porcelain. The seams between them are open a finger's width and are not flaws; they are the drainage system, and the whole plain is graded — one part in four thousand — toward gutter-lines that run for hundreds of kilometers before descending into intake throats.

Nothing rises far. The plain's tallest features are the cooling stacks, blunt truncated cones forty meters up, and the welds: long low ridges where two casting-fields met, standing eight or ten meters, with a knife-sharp crest and vitreous flanks that still hold the ripple-marks of pouring. You could walk the whole continent and never climb anything you'd call a hill. Elevation here is a matter of centimeters and consequences — which way the water goes, which seams run full, where the rime thickens.

The edges are *fresh*. That is the first strangeness a visitor registers, usually as a mild vertigo. Everything looks new-made. Corners are square. Casting-flash still stands on the underlip of vents in delicate frills. The welds have no talus at their feet. And yet the surfaces are polished — glossed to a soft sheen on every horizontal face by the passage of countless small feet — a polish that would take ten thousand years and would, in any honest landscape, have rounded those corners to nubs by now.

It hasn't, because wear here is answered. Somewhere beneath, at a rate exactly equal to abrasion, new plate is extruded and old plate withdrawn. The plain is not preserved; it is *replaced*, continuously, in place, at the speed of its own erosion. It is permanently one week old and has been for an unguessable duration.

### Sky and water

There is no visible sun. The sky is a high even brightness, the white of an overcast noon, coming from a condensation ceiling perhaps two thousand meters up — the plain's own exhalation, held there in a layer of stable saturated air, lit through from above by something the haze diffuses into total absence of shadow.

No shadows. Understand what this does. Your feet cast none. The stacks cast none. Depth has to be read from occlusion and haze alone, and distances lie constantly. There is also no way to tell the hour, because there is no hour. The brightness at what you would call midnight is the brightness at what you would call noon, measurable with an instrument as identical to four decimal places.

The water is everywhere and always. The plain runs at thirty-four degrees, humid to the edge of saturation, and every surface cooler than the air — the north flank of a weld, the shaded inner curve of an intake throat, the shell of any animal that has been resting — beads. Rocks weep. Machines weep. You weep, continuously, and cannot tell sweat from condensate.

Twice in each interval (the plain has intervals; it does not have days) the ceiling thickens and lowers and the *dew tide* comes: not rain, exactly, but a descending fog so dense that surfaces run in sheets, the seams fill and audibly gurgle, and the gutter-lines brim. It lasts about four hours. It is identical every time. The same volume, the same duration, the same rise in every gutter to the same wet-mark on the same plate. Between tides the plain dries to a film, never to dryness. Water enters the throats, is used for cooling somewhere below, and returns to the ceiling through the stacks as a slow inaudible plume. It is a closed loop with no losses. There is no weather in the sense of *events*. There is only weather in the sense of *circulation*.

### The rime

There are no plants. No leaf, no root, no stem, no green anywhere in the visible world — the word *green* is not useful here.

What there is instead is the **rime**: a crust, one to four millimeters thick, the color of weak tea and dried blood in patches, growing on every damp surface not scoured too frequently. Under a lens it is layered filaments and mineral plates, something between a lichen and a chemical stain, and it does not photosynthesize. It feeds on the plain. Specifically it feeds on the trace metals and reduced sulfur compounds that seep from the seams in the coolant condensate, and on the faint electrical potential between adjacent plates, which it shorts across with fine conductive threads.

The rime is the entire base of the food web. Everything alive here is, at two or three removes, eating the machine's waste products.

Its distribution is fixed. There are rime-fields kilometers wide, mapped, and they neither expand nor retreat. The grazing pressure exactly matches the growth rate. Someone arranged this, or it arranged itself so long ago that the difference has stopped meaning anything.

### The fauna

And now the thing that makes this place difficult to describe calmly: it is *crawling*.

Density here is not a figure of speech. Kneel and put your hand flat on plate and you will cover forty or fifty animals. Look along a weld-crest and the vitreous flank is furred with movement, layered — grazers on the surface, sifters in the seam beneath them, and in the gutter below, packed shell to shell, the filter ranks. Air-column too: at any moment there are two or three thousand small fliers per hectare, but they fly *low and slow*, in the thick wet air, like plankton in a warm sea.

A partial list, using the names the surveys settled on:

**Gasket beetles.** Palm-sized, flat, black, the most numerous large animal. They graze rime and secrete a waterproof sealant from the abdomen; the sealant is what keeps the plate seams from wicking coolant upward. They are eaten by nearly everything.

**Wickworms.** Translucent, up to a meter, living head-down in seams, drawing condensate up their bodies by capillary action and metabolizing the dissolved sulfides. Their protruding tails form a low pale nap over any wet seam, moving very slightly, all together, in the dew tide.

**Flange snails.** Fist-sized, shells of the same grey-green sinter as the plate — because they *are* plate; they build shell from ingested floor material. Their polish is the plain's polish. They are the single largest agent of erosion here and also, since their excrement is a fine sinter-dust that packs and cures in cracks, one of the largest agents of repair.

**Stack-swifts.** Not birds. Six centimeters, six wings, filter-feeding on the fog itself as they rise in the stack plumes and glide down. They never land. They are born in the plume and die in it, forty-one days later. Sixty million above one stack at a time, a haze inside the haze.

**Cistern crabs.** Half a meter, the plain's largest common animal, living in the intake throats where the water goes down. They eat what the water carries and they keep the grilles clear. They do not fight each other. There are precisely as many as there are grilles.

**Vent hounds.** The apex predator, if you can use the phrase. Blind, long, low, warm-bodied, hunting by tremor. One per twelve square kilometers. They take gasket beetles and snails at a rate that appears, when you sit with the numbers, to be *rationed*.

None of them are natural. If *built: total* is true of the plain then it is true of these as well, and the evidence is there for anyone who dissects: the ossicles that are machined, the gut that is lined with a catalytic mesh no lineage produces, the fact that a flange snail's shell is chemically indistinguishable from the floor.

And where they come from is not a mystery, only unpleasant to watch. There are **hatch-plates** — every few hundred meters, a disc of dull metal the size of a dinner plate, warm to the touch. When an animal dies, it is carried or crawls or is dragged to a seam and goes down; and within an hour or two, from a hatch-plate somewhere within a few kilometers, a replacement is extruded wet and folded and begins to unfold. The census is constant. Not approximately constant. *Constant.* Count the gasket beetles on one hexagon over a hundred intervals and you will get the same number, and the surveys that have done this stop doing it soon after, for reasons they describe as "morale."

### Sound

Here is the last strangeness, and for most visitors the worst.

All of this — a continent of machinery, a plain saturated with animals at forty per palm — is **quiet**.

The machines are quiet by design. The plain is a single continuous structure thousands of kilometers across, and any sustained tone in the audible range would find a resonance somewhere in it and eventually tear it apart. So everything below runs slow and huge and pitched beneath hearing. You do not hear it in your ears. You feel it in the soles of your feet and in the fluid of your chest: a note, unvarying, that you stop noticing on the second day and then cannot stop noticing on the fourth.

The animals are quiet because they have nothing to say. Nothing here needs a mating call, because reproduction is not theirs. Nothing needs an alarm, because predation is a fixed rate, not a risk. Nothing needs to defend a territory whose boundaries never move. They signal by touch and by chemistry, mutely, at close range, in the dense and constant press of each other's bodies.

What you actually hear, standing on the maintenance plain: water in seams. The small ceramic click of claws on plate, multiplied to a faint hiss like sand in a drum, so even that it registers as silence. During the dew tide, a soft general gurgle that rises and falls and rises the same way it did last time. And nothing else. No wind — the air is too heavy and too still. No thunder. No voices. No engine.

### The rule

Nothing here is going anywhere.

Everything that is worn is replaced at the rate of wearing. Everything that dies is remade at the rate of dying. Everything the machine excretes is eaten, and everything the eaters excrete is taken back down through the seams. Water rises, condenses, falls, drains, rises. The rime grows exactly as fast as it is grazed. The plain is one week old, permanently.

It is not dead. It is the opposite of dead — it is more thoroughly alive per cubic meter than any forest — and it is *finished*. Somebody, or something, solved the problem of a world and then set the solution running, and the solution has no next move, because it does not need one, and the sharpness of every edge out to the curved grey horizon is the sharpness of a thing that will never be allowed to become old enough to be interesting.

Visitors report that the hardest part is not the heat or the wet or the crawling. It is that after a while you begin to suspect that you are the only event.
