## The Sill

The land here is younger than the animals that live on it.

Forty years ago — perhaps sixty; nothing keeps count — the floor of a shallow sea was pushed up along a seam thirty kilometers long, and it came out of the water in sheets. That is the plain you are standing on: black basalt in overlapping lobes, each lobe the size of a market square, each one still holding the shape it had when it was liquid. Ropes. Folds. Coils like intestine. Where the crust broke as it cooled, the edges are still glass-sharp, and they will cut a foot open to the bone, because nothing has yet come along to blunt them. There has not been enough weather. There has not been enough time.

There is no soil. What passes for soil is a black-brown paste of dead insects, fallen leaf-flesh, and bat guano, two or three fingers deep in the hollows, absent everywhere else. Roots do not go down. They go *sideways*, over the rock, in flat gripping mats, and where they cross a fissure they plunge into it and disappear toward the heat.

The plain is low — barely above the sea that made it, which lies out past the eastern margin as a sound you feel in your sternum rather than hear. Ringing the plain, low cinder cones, none higher than a few hundred meters, red-black, smoking faintly at the crowns, their flanks already furred over with forest. From the top of any of them you would see the whole of it: twenty kilometers of canopy, absolutely dark, exhaling steam in slow columns, with the vent-glow pulsing underneath in dull orange patches like coals under ash.

## The Dark

There is no day here in the ordinary sense. High above, the ash veil from the seam's ongoing work has not cleared in living memory, and it turns the sun into a grey smear and the moon into nothing at all. Beneath the canopy — and the canopy is three-tiered and near-solid — the darkness is total by human standards. Not dim. *Total.* You could hold your hand against your own eye and not know it.

What light exists is made by the living. Fungal collars around the bases of the trunks give a green so faint it registers only after twenty minutes of adaptation, and then reads as a suggestion of vertical lines. Certain beetles carry a cold blue on their abdomens and pass overhead in loose constellations. Once or twice a night, a fissure opens somewhere in the plain and throws up a real orange light, and for a few minutes the forest is revealed — enormous, streaming, crowded — and every animal in it goes briefly silent, and then screams.

That is the only time anyone sees anything. The rest of the time, the world is heard.

## The Heat-Drinkers

The forest here does not eat light. It cannot; there is none.

It eats the seam. Every tree in the plain is rooted into a fissure, and what comes up the fissure is heat and steam and sulphur and hydrogen and dissolved iron, and the trees' root-mats are colonized, all through, by bacterial mattings that do the chemistry. The tree is a chimney with leaves on it. The leaves are not green. They are white, cream, translucent pink, the color of things grown in a cellar, and they are not for catching light but for *shedding* it — that is, for radiating heat away, and for condensing water out of the steam. They are broad and slack and cool to the touch on their upper surfaces, and they run.

Hence: lush beyond reason, and dark. Trunks the diameter of a room, soft-barked, warm as a body at the base and cooling as you go up. Between them, everything crowds. Vines with the texture of tendon. Pale bromeliad-things stacked forty deep on a single limb, each one holding a cup of condensate and a private ecology of larvae. Fern-analogues with fronds four meters long, so soft they cannot hold themselves up and lie across each other in layers you must wade through. Fungal shelves broad enough to sleep on, if you dared. A stalked, mouthlike organ growing directly from the rock — neither plant nor animal by any classification you brought with you — that closes on moths.

Density is the defining fact. In one arm's sweep you touch six species. Nothing here has any concept of empty space. When a giant falls, the gap is closed within a season, and the collapse itself feeds a thousand things.

## Water

It does not rain often. Perhaps eight or nine times a year, hard and briefly, when the ash veil lets a real storm through, and then the plain floods in sheets for an hour and drains completely, because the rock is full of cracks and the cracks go down to the heat, and the water comes back up as steam within the day.

The forest's water is condensation. The seam breathes steam; the pale leaves are cold; the leaves sweat; the sweat runs down the veins to the drip-tips and falls. All night — it is always night — the forest is raining on itself, a fine steady patter you would take for real rain if you had not seen the sky. Every surface is beaded. Every crevice holds a spoonful. There are no rivers, no ponds, no true streams: the water never gets the chance to gather before the rock swallows it. But nothing thirsts. You can drink from any leaf-cup in the plain, and the water tastes faintly of iron and cellar.

Damp, therefore, rather than wet. A place with no open water and no dry surfaces anywhere in it.

## The Noise

You will not be able to think here at first.

The plain runs at something between 105 and 120 decibels, continuously, and has for decades. There are three main contributors.

**The chorus.** Frogs — call them frogs; they are wet-skinned, four-limbed, and they call — at a density of perhaps four hundred per cubic meter of vegetation in the lower tier. Not four hundred per square meter of ground: *per cubic meter of leaf-space*, because the vegetation is stacked and every stratum is fully occupied. Their calls are short and metallic, and there are enough of them that individual voices vanish into a single sheet of sound like sustained applause, or like frying.

**The stridulators.** Above that, cicada-analogues and long-bodied crickets whose songs are pitched high and pure and land, collectively, as a scream. They synchronize in patches. A patch of forest half a kilometer wide will lock into phase and pulse — *whaaa, whaaa, whaaa* — and then the phase-lock breaks and drifts into the next patch, so that waves of intensity move across the plain all night like weather.

**The seam.** Under everything, the ground itself. Steam venting through wet rock produces a sound between a kettle and a groan; there are hundreds of vents; some of them are the size of a cathedral door. And every few hours the seam shifts, and there is a crack that arrives through your feet before it arrives at your ears.

Because there is no light, sound is not decoration here. It is the medium. It is how the animals see, hunt, court, and navigate, and the competition for acoustic space is as savage as the competition for root-hold. Species have been driven into every available frequency and every available rhythm. Some call only in the half-second gaps between the cicada pulses. Some have gone almost entirely ultrasonic. Some have given up on being heard and communicate by drumming the substrate — thudding a hollow trunk, slapping wet rock — so that the forest also *knocks*, constantly, from a hundred directions, and the knocking travels through the basalt faster and further than any voice.

There is a thing that imitates the sound of a fissure opening. Nobody has determined why. When it calls, the whole quarter of the forest goes silent and freezes, and the thing eats well in the silence.

## The Overwhelming

Fauna at saturation. This is what a young land with unlimited chemical energy and no predators-of-predators produces in half a century.

**The floor** is a moving carpet. Land crabs, primarily — pale, blunt, some the size of a dinner plate — in numbers that make the ground appear to flow. Between them, isopods, amphipods that have gone terrestrial and hop, blind carnivorous snails, and a flat-bodied, many-legged thing a meter long that hunts by pressing its whole underside against the rock and feeling for footsteps. You cannot walk here without killing dozens per step, and you will be walking on a surface that clicks and gives.

**The trunks and vines** carry the frogs, the tree-crabs, ropes of pale snake-analogues that hunt by heat and are consequently drawn to your face, and colonies of something insectile and social whose nests are not built but *grown* — a swelling on the trunk they induce and then inhabit, which is the only reason this landscape has no architecture despite having architects.

**The mid-canopy** is where the noise lives, and the noise-eaters: mantid forms with forelimbs like sickles, arm's-length centipedes, and the moths — moths in blizzards, moths in numbers that fill your mouth, some with wingspans wider than your two hands, all of them navigating by echo-deaf silence and dying by the million.

**The air** belongs to the bats. Or to the bat-shaped: leathery, warm-blooded, hook-thumbed, roosting in the fissure-mouths where the heat rises, coming out in columns at the slack hour, hundreds of thousands per column, and the sound of a column passing overhead is a physical pressure. They are the reason the ultrasonic band is as crowded as the audible one. There are moths here that jam bat sonar with clicks of their own; there are bats that have gone silent and hunt by listening for the moths' jamming.

**And the sea-edge**, out past the eastern lobes, where the plain drops into surf: colonies of something between a seal and a lizard, ten thousand strong, screaming at each other over a shoreline made of fresh glass, and the smell of them reaches four kilometers inland on the wrong wind.

Everything here is eating and being eaten at maximum rate. Nothing lives long. Biomass turns over in weeks. The forest floor is warm partly from the seam and partly from decomposition, and if you push your hand into the litter it comes out steaming.

## The Knappers

There are people here, if you are generous with the word.

They stand about a meter and a quarter, walk upright, are covered in short wet-looking fur, and have eyes so reduced that they are essentially decorative — two dark buttons that register only the fissure-glow. Their ears are enormous, mobile, and finely furrowed. Their hands are excellent.

They have no houses. They have no fire — there is nothing dry enough to burn, and no need; the ground is warm. They have no clothing, no containers, no beds beyond a pressed hollow in the fern-litter that lasts one sleep. Their entire material culture is acoustic.

They knap. They collect the glass-edged basalt and the true obsidian from the cone flanks, and they work it into blades, which they use for butchery and for cutting vine, and they carry these blades in the crook of the arm or between the teeth and lose them constantly and make more, because the raw material is everywhere and the land is new and generous with it. This is the least interesting thing they make.

What they make with real care are **strikers** and **plates**. A striker is a hardwood billet, hand-length, weighted at one end, worked smooth over a lifetime. A plate is a section of trunk from a particular species of heat-tree whose dead wood dries hollow and holds a note — they find these, test them, reject nine in ten, and the ones they keep they *tune*, laboriously, by scraping the inner wall with a basalt flake over a period of years. A tuned plate is the single most valuable object in this world. It is not owned; it stays where it grew, and the group returns to it.

Because: their language cannot be spoken. At 115 decibels, the human vocal range is useless. Their voices are for touching-distance intimacy only — a soft chirring at the ear. Everything else is percussion. They speak by hitting: the plates, the wet rock, hollow trunks, each other's cupped palms, the ground with a heel. Struck sound travels through basalt; the plain is a single continuous stone, and a message struck at the western cones is felt at the sea-edge in under ten seconds. They have a percussive grammar of some complexity. They can convey distance, direction, quantity, urgency, and the names of about ninety kinds of animal. They cannot convey anything abstract, as far as anyone can tell, but they have a call that means *the ground is about to do something*, and they are usually right.

They also drum for hours with no informational content whatsoever, twenty or thirty of them around a tuned plate, hammering, and the noise they generate carves a hole in the forest's noise, and for the duration of it every other animal within three hundred meters shuts up and moves away. This may be the point. It may be the only privacy available in this world: a hole in the sound, maintained by labor, inside which you can hear your own kind breathe.

**Traces** — the only marks that this landscape has been touched at all: the drum-floors, where the litter is worn down to bare polished rock in a circle eight meters across; the shell middens, low and long, gone soft with fungus; the paths, which are not cut but *worn*, faint grooves in basalt made by forty years of the same feet on the same route, and which the animals also use, so that the plain is threaded by a network of trails belonging to everyone.

## Rules of the Place

1. **Light is an event, not a condition.** When it happens, everything stops. Predators know this. Use the dark; the dark is safety.
2. **Silence is dangerous.** A hole in the noise means something has cleared it, and the thing that cleared it is either the Knappers or something worse.
3. **Nothing is more than sixty years old.** No ancient trees, no deep soil, no old bones, no fossils, no worn stone. Every edge is sharp. Every wound the land takes is still open. There is no past here to speak of, which is why there is so much present.
4. **The ground is a participant.** It is warm, it feeds the forest, it carries speech, and roughly once a month it opens somewhere and takes a hectare of everything down into itself. Then it grows back over, faster than seems decent.
5. **Water arrives from above, always, and never gathers.** Learn to drink from leaves. Do not look for a river. There will be rivers in a thousand years.
6. **Nothing is built.** Not by the Knappers, not by the social insects, not by the bats. Shelter is unnecessary in a warm wet dark, and so the intelligence here went into sound instead of into walls. If you are looking for ruins, you have come too early.

## A Night, Which Is All There Is

Slack hour — the nearest thing to dawn, when the frog-sheet thins and the cicada patches fall out of phase. The knocking gets louder in the gap. Steam stands in the vent-columns without moving; the air is at blood temperature and completely saturated, and your skin cannot cool, and you can feel condensation forming in your own eyelashes.

Then the bats come off the fissures in a column two hundred meters tall, and the pressure of them arrives, and every frog in the plain restarts at once — not gradually, *at once*, the way a rain-front hits — and the sound goes from 100 to 118 in the space of a breath, and the forest, which you cannot see, becomes a solid thing pressing on your ears from every direction, and somewhere three kilometers off a tuned plate begins to hammer out a rhythm about a collapse at the sea-edge, and you feel it in the soles of your feet before you hear it, and the whole young shining unweathered plain streams and drips and boils and eats itself and grows.

It will do this again in a few hours. It has done it every night since it came out of the sea.
