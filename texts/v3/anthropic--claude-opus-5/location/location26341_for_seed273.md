## The Shape of It

You come up out of the cloud and the first thing you understand is that every horizon line is a wall.

Not a ridge, not a scarp: a wall. Coursed brick, laid header-and-stretcher, running level for a mile and a half before it steps back and begins again forty feet higher. From below, out on the buttress-roads, the whole massif reads as a mountain — it has a mountain's mass, a mountain's weather, a mountain's habit of making its own afternoons. But the light comes off it wrong for stone. It comes off in stripes, because it is courses, and there is a shadow under every course, and the shadows stack, and that stacking is what gives the thing its peculiar velvet look from a distance, like the pelt of an animal lying down.

It goes up perhaps two miles above the cloud floor. It goes down — no one is sure. That is one of the two or three things here that a visitor cannot be talked out of finding strange, and the people who live on it will not help you, because they find your interest in the question mildly indecent, the way you would find a guest's interest in the depth of your well.

The terraces are wide. Not garden-ledges — districts. Each has its own grade, its own drainage, its own quarter of a sky. Some are three hundred yards deep, planted, gullied, built up with houses whose backs lean against the retaining wall of the terrace above and whose fronts hang out over the drop on brackets of cast iron, with laundry and lettuce and children on them. There are chasms — the Gullet, the Cold Slot, the Long Notch — that look for all the world like river gorges and are in fact the seams between two construction campaigns, unmortared, deliberate, dressed inside with black vitrified clinker so that the water that runs down them will not eat the brick. Standing on the parapet of the Cold Slot you can see the far wall four hundred feet away, and it is the same as this wall, courses and flue-mouths and fig trees, and somebody over there is beating a rug.

## Fabric

Three bricks, mainly. A sallow yellow one, soft, old, used deep in the fill. A liver-red one, harder, that takes lichen and holds it. And clinker: black, half-glassy, warped in the burning, laid at the wet edges of things, on stair noses and gutter lips and cistern rims, where it wears to a dull shine like the back of a beetle.

Everything else is iron, copper, tile, tar, and rope. Cast-iron brackets, gone to a brown crust that flakes off in plates the size of a hand and reveals sound metal beneath. Copper sheet on the windward parapets, verdigris green in the folds, polished bright on the ridges by two centuries of weather and pilgrim hands. Ceramic pipe — the great fixture of the place — in every diameter from a finger to a barrel, glazed brown, socketed, laid in runs that cross the terrace faces on little arcades of their own, so that from a distance the mountain looks stitched.

The mortar is the interesting part. It has been repointed so many times that in the old quarters the joints are wider than the bricks, and the mortar is younger than the brick beside it by four hundred years. In some walls the brick has become the minor material: a scatter of red crumbs held in a matrix of grey. This is not decay. This is the settled state. The mountain has found its angle of repose, its lichen crust, its root network, its schedule of shedding and being caught, and it has been in that state longer than any lineage on it can trace itself. The parapets are sanded round. The stair treads are dished. The iron rails on the inclines are worn to mirror on the top surface and rough as bark underneath. Nothing is falling apart; everything has finished falling into shape.

## Water

There is no river. There is an hour.

Most days, sometime between two and five in the afternoon, the cloud comes up. It comes from the lowlands as a white flood in the valleys, rises, finds the mountain's flanks, and climbs them the way water climbs a wick. For two or three hours the middle terraces are inside weather: visibility of ten yards, everything dripping, the air warm and white and so bright that people wear the same smoked-glass eyeshades in fog that they wear on the mirror-floors above.

Every windward parapet on that band is combed. The fog-combs are copper fins, a foot deep, set in ranks, and behind them are the leaders, and behind the leaders the ceramic runs, and at the ends of the runs the cisterns: barrel-vaulted rooms lined with tar and glazed tile, some of them the size of a chapel, with a stair down into them and a boat. The four o'clock cloud comes through the combs as vapour and leaves the mountain as water in pipes. That is the river here. It is a river that arrives sideways and takes three hours.

Above the cloud line it is dry. Truly dry — bleached brick, a crust of black and orange lichen, dust that smells of hot clay, and the drying-floors, where fibre and fruit and dye-cake are spread out under a sun that at that altitude has almost nothing between it and the ground. Below the cloud line it drips continuously, day and night, from every soffit and root and cornice, and the sound of that dripping inside a flue is one of the great sounds of the place: a hollow, hurrying applause that never resolves.

## Warm

You would expect cold. At that height, at that thinness of air, you would expect frost every night of the year. The mountain is warm, and it is warm from inside.

There are furnaces down there. There have always been furnaces down there. Their heat comes up through the flue system — thousands of vents, from finger-width weepholes to shafts you could drop a cart down — and it comes up as a steady, mild, slightly sulphurous breath that keeps the terraces at the temperature of a good greenhouse. On a still night you can see where the flues are by the standing columns of shimmer. In winter, snow falls on the upper works and does not lie except in figures: it maps the cold spots, the dead flues, the places where the mountain's circulation has gone quiet. Masons read those maps.

Nobody can tell you the whole plan of the flue system. The people who tend it work by inheritance and by ear — you strike a wall, you listen, you know.

## Green

Because it is warm and it drips, the mountain is a cloud forest that happens to be made of masonry.

Moss first, everywhere, in seven or eight kinds, from a hard black crust to a pale green plush an inch deep that squeezes water when you step on it. Then the epiphytes: rosettes wedged in every joint, holding their own little ponds, each pond with its own population of larvae and a frog the size of a thumbnail. Then ferns — tree-ferns in the flue mouths especially, where the warm air suits them, so that many vents look like they are growing a green fountain. Then the figs. Figs are the great architects here. Their roots go into a joint as a thread and come out fifty years later as a grey cable gripping four courses, and the masons long ago stopped fighting them and began to plant them where a wall was tired. Half the retaining walls on the middle terraces are held by fig.

And on top of all that, the crops: orchard terraces, fibre terraces, a dye-plant with leaves like wet leather, mushroom galleries in the disused flues, and the compost bins — great brick coffers, generations deep, where the leaf-fall of the whole mountain is layered and left, and cut after forty years as a black turf that burns clean and slow. The mountain fuels its own furnaces with its own fallen leaves, on a forty-year rotation. That is the closest thing here to a metabolism.

## Animals

Swifts. Hundreds of thousands. They nest in the vent throats, and at dusk they come home in a shrieking helix half a mile across, and the sound of it drowns the machinery. Their droppings are farmed — scraped, aged, sold — which makes the swifts the mountain's most valuable livestock and gives every vent-shaft an owner and a lawsuit history.

Bats in the deeper flues, coming out in a black rope. Geckos on every sunlit brick, so common you learn not to see them, so numerous that when you clap they go off the wall like a handful of thrown gravel. Grey mice with pale bellies that live entirely in cavity walls and have, someone will tell you, no wild population anywhere. Goats on tethers, kept to eat the seedlings off the parapets — municipal animals, with numbers painted on their flanks. Mules on the incline landings, patient as furniture. Herons that come up out of the lowlands to the open reservoir courts and stand there, absurd, at eleven thousand feet. Pale eels in the cisterns, which are eaten. Moths at night in the gaslight, the size of a spread hand, thudding.

## Work

Industry here has an old, slow, water-driven character. There are boilers, and their whistles rule the day. There are foundries in the deep courts, glowing at night, and a dye works whose runoff dyes a whole cascade brick-pink for two hundred feet. There are brick-crushers, because the rule is that no new clay comes up the mountain; brick is made from brick, ground and re-tempered and re-fired, so the fabric is endlessly re-eaten and re-laid and the mountain neither grows nor shrinks. There are chain pumps, screw pumps, and the great hydraulic accumulator in Stairhead Court, whose stone-filled counterweight descends a shaft over the course of one season and is winched back up over the next, and rings a bell once a week on its way down. That bell is how the mountain counts.

The inclines are the pride of it: water-balance funiculars, two cars on one rope, fill the top tank from the cistern and gravity does the rest, and the water arrives at the bottom where it is wanted anyway. Nothing wasted. Everything twice-used.

## Sound

Loud, and layered, and constant. The base is a hum you feel in your teeth from the deep works. Over it: water hammer in the mains, banging like someone kicking a door three terraces down; the mill grind; the whistles at the quarter-shifts; the drip-applause; the goats; the swift-scream at dusk and dawn; and above all of it, at the upper works, the wind, which at that height has a thin, hard, continuous voice like a blade being drawn.

You get used to it. People here are loud talkers and close talkers, and they read lips without noticing they are doing it.

## Rules

Do not dig downward. Not superstition — permit law, with a long, boring history of ruinous precedent behind it.

Condensation belongs to the parapet it forms on, and the parapet belongs to whoever maintains it. Divert another's drip and you will be fined, and worse, gossiped about.

Loads going up have right of way over everything, including funerals.

Every household owes so many days a year to the walls. When the flood-cloud comes in wrong, or a joint starts weeping, the wall is attended to before the house is.

And no new brick. Only brick from brick.

## Time

Change here is measured against the counterweight's bell and against people. The cloud arrives every afternoon; the shifts turn over; the swifts come home; and none of that is change. Change is a fig root reaching a fourth course. Change is a flue going cold and the snow, that winter, lying in a new shape. Change is a terrace whose orchard is being slowly replaced, one tree a year, by a variety that likes the fog better — a project begun by someone's grandmother and expected to finish under someone's grandchildren.

Go and watch a mason repointing on the Weeping Face. She is working a course that was pointed in her grandmother's time, and she can tell — the sand is a shade browner. She will take out about eleven feet of joint this season. Then she will move along. The face is a mile and a quarter long, and by the time anyone gets back to this spot, the sand will be a shade browner again, and none of it will look any different at all.
