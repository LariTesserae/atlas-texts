## The Pocket at the Top

There is no way in. Note this first, because it will save you the effort of looking: the cavity has walls, a floor, a ceiling that sweats, and no aperture larger than a fingerbone. You are already inside. You have always been about to have arrived.

It is a room. Not a cave system, not a chamber in a network — a single contained volume roughly the size of a modest kitchen, though pacing it out gives inconsistent results. Four steps in any direction returns you to where you started without your having turned. The walls are close enough to touch two at once if you stretch, and yet you cannot reach them by walking. Both of these are true and neither is a trick. The room simply doesn't participate in the arithmetic that rooms usually agree to.

### The stone

Everything here is new. That is the dominant fact of the surfaces: they have not been worn. The rock is fresh basalt and fresher glass, extruded in ropes and bulbs and paper-thin plates that ring like cheap porcelain when your knee brushes them. Edges are lethal. Every ridge is a green-black razor with its arris intact, unblunted, uncomforted by weather. There is no dust. There is no sand. Nothing in here has yet been ground down into the soft particulate that makes older landscapes habitable.

In three places along the lower wall the stone is still arriving. It comes out slow as honey, a dull glowing lobe pushing forward maybe a hand's width in an hour, skinning over into iridescent glass as it goes — peacock blues, bronze, the oil-slick sheen of a starling's neck. The skin cracks in fine crazing as it cools and the crazing tinkles. That is one of the sounds.

Above waist height the walls are hung with mineral: gypsum needles, sulfur crusts the yellow of a broken yolk, and long soda-straw stalactites, none of them longer than a finger, all of them brand new. Nothing here has had time to become a formation. It is all still becoming one.

### Heat, and water, and the contradiction of the air

It is hot in the way of a closed bathhouse gone wrong — a wet, pressing, insinuating heat that arrives at your skin and stays there, refusing to be sweated off because the air is already carrying all the water it can. 60 degrees, maybe 65 near the floor vents. Sustained. Your pulse becomes a project.

Water is everywhere and none of it falls properly. The ceiling weeps continuously, condensation gathering in fat lenses that let go and land — but some of the beads run *up* the ceiling instead, gathering into threads that climb toward the highest point of the vault and vanish into the rock there without a sound. The floor holds a pool the color of milk with a drop of blue in it, opaque with suspended silica, and the pool breathes. It fills over roughly thirty-five minutes until it laps at a particular shelf, then in about ninety seconds it drains away down its own throat with the sound of a bathtub much smaller than itself. Then it begins to fill again. It has done this forever and will keep doing it, and the whole population of the room lives by that clock.

And yet: the air is thin. This is the part that doesn't work. Your lungs report altitude — real altitude, the kind found above six thousand meters, the specific insufficiency where each breath is a debt. The steam is dense enough to drink and there is not enough of it to live on. Through the one hairline crack in the vault comes a blade of daylight that is unmistakably *high* light: hard, blue-edged, the light of somewhere with very little sky above it. Cold light in a hot room. It falls across the pool and does not warm the pool.

Nothing else confirms the mountain. There is no summit visible, no wind, no view. Only the light and your own suffering chest insist that this pocket is embedded near the top of something enormous, and that the enormous thing is young, and that it is possibly not finished.

### The tenders

Now the important thing. The room is crowded.

They are on every surface, in layers. Palm-sized, segmented, eleven or thirteen or nine plates to a body, carapaces of a translucent amber-grey through which you can watch the slow shunting of pale fluid. Each has a fringe of feeding cilia at the front margin and two hard black mandibles, and a variable number of legs — this is not a matter of species variation, it is a matter of when you look. They cling in overlapping shingles, three and four deep, so that the wall is not a wall but a pelt. They line the ceiling. They ride the pool's surface in mats. When the water drains they walk down after it in a slow unhurried tide; when it fills they walk back up ahead of it, and the whole room seems to shift a few centimeters in one direction and then back, like a lung with legs.

You cannot count them. This is not hyperbole about density. Count a patch of wall, look away, count again: a different number, reliably, and not because they've moved. Twelve becomes nine becomes seventeen. Two individuals will occasionally be, briefly, the same individual — you will be watching one groom its mandibles on the left wall and see the identical gesture, identically timed, in the identical creature on the right, and then one of them will stop existing without going anywhere.

They eat the mats. This is the base of everything: films of chemosynthetic slime, rust-red and cream and a deep bruise-purple, thriving on the sulfur and iron and hydrogen coming up out of the floor. That is the entirety of the plant kingdom here — flat, ancient, pre-botanical, oozing. It grows fast in the heat. It has to.

And there is a predator. It lives in the pool's throat and comes up with the water, a pale limbless length maybe as long as your forearm, moving with the patience of something that has never been in a hurry. It takes tenders from the underside of the mat. It has no visible eyes and no visible mouth and yet a tender simply *stops* and then is not there. When the pool drains, it goes back down. Nobody panics about it. The tenders make room.

### The tools

They knap.

This is what will keep you awake, later, if there is a later. A tender selects a plate of fresh glass from the new stone, braces it with four legs, and strikes it with the flat of one mandible at a considered angle, and takes off a flake. It examines the flake with its cilia. It strikes again, retouching the edge. Then it carries the flake in its mandibles and uses it to scrape mat off rock, or to lever a rival off a good grazing patch, or to cut something small.

The flakes are inherited. You will watch one tender, moribund, its carapace going chalky, pass a worked flake to another mandible-to-mandible before it goes still. The receiving animal does not treat it as food.

And some of the flakes are old. This is the impossible part and it is easy to verify: pick one up. Some flakes have hydration rinds, patina, edge-rounding from use — decades of use, centuries — and they are made of glass that came out of the wall this morning. The tradition is older than the material. The craft has been going on longer than the stone has existed.

They build nothing. There are no nests, no cairns, no walls, no shelters, nothing arranged. Not one artifact is set down anywhere on purpose. Every made thing in this room is being carried by something alive. When a tender dies its tool changes hands within minutes and the room returns to a state of having no objects in it at all.

### Sound

Almost none. This is the room's strangest generosity. Everything in here happens quieter than it should. The fumarole a foot from your ankle, jetting steam hard enough to move your hair, whispers. The pool's thirty-second drain is a suggestion of a gurgle. A plate of glass the size of a door letting go of the ceiling and shattering across the floor makes a single soft *click*, like a knuckle. There is no echo, ever, from anything; the walls take sound and keep it. Your own voice comes out flat and small and doesn't reach the other side of the room, which is close enough to touch.

What you do hear: the tinkle of cooling crazing. The dripping, offset and arrhythmic, hundreds of sources. A faint continuous rustle from the pelt of tenders, so soft it registers as a texture in the silence rather than a noise. And the knapping — thousands of tiny percussions per minute, so quiet individually that together they make a sound like distant light rain on a leaf you cannot find.

### Rules, as best they can be stated

Nothing here erodes. Things are broken, split, shattered, extruded, dissolved — but never worn. Sharpness is conserved.

Reflections run out of step. The pool's surface, and the mirror-black of fresh glass, show the room roughly one water-cycle displaced — usually behind, sometimes ahead. You can watch a tender knap a flake in the pool's reflection and then, forty minutes later, watch it happen. Whether this makes it happen is not a question the room seems to consider well-formed.

Steam holds shape. Occasionally a cloud coming off a vent will not disperse but will instead be, for two or three seconds, the crisp negative volume of a tender — the hollow where one *would* be, in perfect detail, at three times life size. The tenders orient toward these and do not approach them.

The room does not close. Its geometry is locally reasonable and globally impossible, and it is not embarrassed by this.

Dropped things return improved. Set down a flake, lose it, find it again: it will have been retouched.

You cannot leave, but you will not notice this happening, because leaving is not something the room forbids. It is something the room has no opinion about, in the way a page has no opinion about the room the reader is sitting in.

The heat continues. The water climbs and falls. The stone comes out of the wall glowing and skins over blue-bronze, and within four minutes the mats have found it, and within nine the tenders are grazing there, and within twenty someone has struck a flake from it that has been in use since before the mountain.
