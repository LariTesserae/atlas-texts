## The Roundness

**I. The top of the world, underwater**

Start at the edge, because the edge is the only place where the shape of the whole thing is legible.

You are standing — or hanging, the difference has worn away — on a lip of black stone at the rim of a tableland that goes on for the width of a continent. Below the lip, the water drops. Not water into air: water into more water, deepening from the warm green-gold you are breathing into blue, then into the blue that has no name, then into the flat unlit body of the lower ocean, six or seven miles down, which no one here has ever seen and which the plateau does not think about.

Above you the water gets brighter. That is all it does. There is no surface. Swim up for a day and the light thickens by a degree, the way afternoon thickens; swim for a year and it is a paler, wider, honey-colored nowhere, and the plateau is a grey smudge below you, and the brightness is still exactly as far off as it was. Everyone who lives here has stopped trying. Up is not a place. Up is a quality, like sweetness.

The water at this altitude is *thin*. This is the first strange thing your body notices. It has the density of a warm morning, barely more; you can swing an arm through it without resistance, and your hair does not float so much as drift the way hair drifts in a still room. Things fall here, slowly, as if through air. Silt falls. Leaves fall. But you can also push off the ground and go up thirty feet in a lazy arc and come down again, and the coming down takes a long time, long enough to think about.

The temperature is a bath left standing an hour: warm, near blood, faintly warmer near the ground where the old rock is still giving back heat it took in during an age nobody remembers.

**II. What the erosion did**

The plateau was mountains. You can tell by the bones of it — long parallel welts running away to the horizon, the roots of ranges, worn until they are gentle swells you could roll a ball down. Nothing here has an angle. There is not one sharp edge in ten thousand miles of stone. Every boulder is an egg. Every ravine is a groove, soft-shouldered, filled with moss to the brim. The whole landscape has the smoothness of a soap that has been used every day for a hundred years, and it has the same air of something almost finished with being itself.

But the erosion did not stop at stone.

It kept going into the other things that could be worn. It got into *sequence*. It got into *edges of the abstract kind*. Down in the grooves you find events that have been rounded off until they no longer have beginnings: a stone rolls, and rolls, and has always been rolling, and does not arrive. A drift of silt collapses in a small slow avalanche about every four minutes, forever, in the same shape, having worn a channel in the local behavior of time the way water wears a channel in rock. The people who used to live here — and there were people, or something with hands — called this *roundness*, and the whole country by extension: **the Roundness**, the place where all the corners went.

Rule of the world, first and largest: *anything old enough becomes round; anything round enough becomes everywhere.* A thing that repeats long enough stops being an event and becomes a feature. There are features here you can walk through: a soft warm patch that is the last exhalation of something that died in the Second Age, still going, spread out over an acre, thin as a thought. You can stand in it. It is like standing in a mood.

**III. Layers, and the problem of density**

The plateau exists more than once.

Not as history — as stacking. The same tableland, dozens deep, all occupying the same volume of water and all very nearly touching. You do not see the other layers, exactly. What you see is *too much of everything*: moss beds that seem to be growing at four different heights in the same place, the ghost of a hill inside a hill, tree-things whose trunks have a smeared quality as though drawn many times with a hand that could not quite settle. Density here is not crowding. It is *superposition* — many presences per volume, none of them willing to be the only one.

You slip between layers constantly and never notice. The tell is small and always the same: the moss you were walking on is a slightly different green; the sound of your own movement, which has no sound, has a different silence to it. That is all. There is no ceremony. Someone who set out east across the Roundness and kept a scrupulous count of hills would arrive back where they began having crossed nine hundred hills that were the same hill.

This is why the plateau is functionally infinite though it is only continental. Scale here is not distance. Scale is how many times a place is willing to be itself.

**IV. The green**

Lush, and lush in a way that has nothing to do with vigor.

The dominant growth is a moss the color of old bottle-glass, knee-deep, spread over the entire tableland like a pelt, and it is warm to the touch — warmer than the water — and it glows. Faintly. A low continuous green-gold luminescence, everywhere, so that the ground is dimly lit from below and the underside of every leaf above catches it. This is where the light of the Roundness mostly comes from; the brightness above is only a rumor by the time it filters this deep.

The moss glows because it eats its own light. This should not work. It photosynthesizes by the illumination it emits, a closed loop, a candle feeding on its own flame — and it works here, and it has worked for so long that arguing with it would be rude. Somewhere in the sequence of *light → sugar → light* a step has gone round and dropped out. The loop is one of the oldest features in the country. If you dig a hole in the moss and hold your hand over it to block the glow, the moss underneath goes on glowing, having already been lit.

Above the moss: the **standing forests**. Trees that were once trees, on land, when there was land at this altitude — hardwood-boned, huge-boled, with crowns like slow explosions — and which did not die when the water came. They adapted downward into something patient. Their leaves are broad, dark, faintly rubbery, and they hang rather than spread. Their bark is furred with white filter-hair. They do not sway, because nothing sways in water this thin and this still; they *lean*, all of them, very slightly, in the direction the current went a hundred thousand years ago, and they have never straightened up.

Between the trunks, hanging vines with air-bladders like grapes, and long combed curtains of a red weed that grows only in the shape of a curtain, and mushroom-things the size of chairs that fruit once per century in complete silence and are eaten by nothing.

Layer on layer on layer, dense enough that a mile of forest is a green dusk, and every gap in the canopy is filled with a slow snow.

**V. Weather**

The Roundness has weather, and the weather happens *inside* the water, which takes some getting used to.

**Snow**: the constant fall of white detritus from the bright water above — flakes of who-knows-what, sifting down forever, thicker in some seasons, thin in others. It is the sound of this snow landing on the broad leaves that constitutes most of the audible world.

**Rain**: heavier, saltier water, gathering somewhere in the upper glow, comes down in sheets — visible rain falling *through* water, greying the middle distance, drumming very softly into the moss, running off downhill in shining braided rivers that flow along the bottom of the ocean like rivers on a shore. There are rain-cut valleys. There are rain lakes: pools of dense brine lying in hollows with a mirrored top surface, water on water, and you can dip your hand into that surface and feel it break like the surface of a pond. Things drown in those lakes. Water drowns in them.

**The Breath**: once a year, or once a something, the entire body of water over the plateau moves — up, slowly, over the course of weeks, and back down. Ten feet of vertical drift across a continent. During the rise, the leaves of the standing forests all turn to face the direction they lean; during the fall, they turn back. It is the largest gesture the country makes.

**VI. Quiet**

Sound travels absurdly well here. A cracked branch would be heard a hundred miles away. So the Roundness has arranged itself, over an unspeakable amount of time, to be quiet — not out of etiquette but by wear. Loud things get everywhere, and anything everywhere becomes a feature, and any feature eventually rounds off into the general hum. So the noises of the past have all been absorbed: there is a bass tone in the water, far below hearing, that is the smoothed-out remainder of everything that has ever happened here. Every collapse, every death-cry, every hammer-stroke of the vanished builders, averaged into one warm note you feel in your teeth.

Second rule of the world: *nothing is loud twice.* Make a noise and it goes out from you across the plateau and does not come back, and thereafter that particular noise is a little harder to make. Shout the same word ten times and the tenth is a whisper. Shout it a hundred and you will find your mouth making the shape and nothing leaving it.

So what remains is: snow on leaves. Rain in moss. The creak of a very large trunk deciding to lean a degree further. Your own blood.

**VII. The rare ones**

Fauna is a thing that happens once every few days of travel, and it is always slightly the same animal.

The **mowers**: pale, flat, sail-finned grazers about the length of a boat, cropping glow-moss with a fringe of soft mouthparts, moving in a straight line at the speed of a shadow. They do not school. You will find one, and then in three days another, and the second has the same notch in its dorsal sail as the first, and the third is younger, and the fourth is very old and nearly blind, and the fifth has the notch again. There may be one mower in the Roundness. There may be one mower, encountered at all its ages at once, because the layers do not agree about when.

The **hollows**: not animals, but the shapes animals left in the water. Deep in the forests there are volumes of water that hold a form — a coiled negative space, a hollow with fins, an emptiness with the exact posture of something sleeping. The water there is stiffer. You can put your arm into the space where a throat was and feel the throat's absence resist you, gently, like memory foam. Some hollows still move: slow, tidal, breathing shapes going about a life that finished in the middle ages of the planet.

And rarely, at the rim, a **bird**. Dark, long-winged, wheeling out over the abyss with perfect competence, drowned so many ages ago that it has forgotten, and flying anyway. It never lands. It has never learned that it can't.

**VIII. The scattered built**

There are marks of hands, and they are all the same mark.

**Doorways.** Free-standing, two uprights and a lintel, cut from a dense black stone that comes from nowhere on this plateau, sunk to their knees in moss, spaced out across the country at intervals of days. No walls. No floors. No foundations, no rubble, no city that they were ever part of. Just a doorway, standing in a clearing, glowing faintly green from the moss on its sill.

And they *work*.

This is the third rule, and the builders knew it before they left or dissolved or rounded off: *doorways still function; walls do not.* Everything else in the Roundness has lost its edges — every wall those people built has softened and spread and become a general feeling of separation, thinly distributed. But a threshold is not an edge, it is a *decision*, and decisions are harder to erode than stone. Pass under one of these lintels and you arrive definitively. The layer you land in is the layer you intended. For one moment — the moment of crossing — the world consents to have exactly one version of itself, and you feel it, an almost painful clarity, like a held note resolving.

Then you are through, and it goes soft again, and you are somewhere, and there are nine hundred somewheres in it.

There are other artifacts, fewer: cupped hollows worn in boulder-tops, too regular to be rain; a spiral of thirty-one stones on a hilltop, each the size of a fist, arranged so that from one particular angle they line up and vanish into one stone; the occasional single step, one step, black stone, going up out of the moss and stopping. Nothing to read. They did not write. They may have felt that anything written would eventually be everywhere.

**IX. What the country is doing**

It would be wrong to call it dead. It is extremely busy — it is just busy at the pace of geology and with the sequence sanded off, so the busyness reads as stillness.

The rain is carving. The brine lakes are filling and evaporating downward, into the ocean, which is a thing they should not be able to do. The forests are migrating, three inches a decade, following the ancient lean. The moss is spreading its impossible loop of light into new hollows. The layers are settling against each other like books in a shifting stack, and occasionally two of them align completely and for an afternoon the world is *one*, sharp-edged, shockingly bright, every hill a single hill — and then it relaxes.

And everything is being worn down. Still. The Roundness is not finished; it is only very near finished, in the way a sphere is near finished. Somewhere ahead of it, at the end of a long enough time, there is a state in which the plateau has no features at all, and no events, and no layers, only the warm dim water and the low unhearable note of everything that already happened — and the plateau is going there gently, without grief, at the speed of a leaf falling in a thin sea.

If you stay, you will start to notice that you are rounding too. Your memories lose their corners first. Then your habits become features. Eventually you will be a warm patch, an acre wide, thin as a thought, and someone will walk through you and think: *there was something here.*

There is a doorway on the hilltop. Go through it now, while you still want a particular direction.
