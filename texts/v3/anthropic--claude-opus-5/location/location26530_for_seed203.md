## The Underlight

### The Lid

Begin with the ceiling, because everything here is a consequence of the ceiling.

It is ice, and it is not far overhead — four hundred metres in the thin places, two or three kilometres where it sags — and it is *clear*. Not the milk-blue of glacier ice packed with air, but something young and glassy that has not yet had time to bruise. Sunlight enters it at the surface of the world, travels down through it, scatters, and arrives everywhere at once. There is no direction to the light. It comes from above, from the sides, from beneath, bounced back off the mirror-canes and up again into the ice and down again, until the whole vault is one continuous luminous body and nothing in it casts a shadow.

This is the hardest thing to convey. Not brightness like a lamp, or brightness like noon. Brightness like being inside the filament. You cannot look at anything without looking through a haze of your own dazzled optic nerve. Distance is unreadable, because there is no shading to model form; a cane-tower ten kilometres off and a cane-tower ten metres off are equally flat, equally white, equally *there*. Depth arrives only through sound, through touch, through the slow correction of walking.

And it is cold. Roughly the temperature of a cellar in February. The ice above does not warm; it only shines. The light passes through the air without giving it anything, so you stand in an inferno of illumination with frost forming on your sleeve.

### The Floor

There is no ground.

Beneath is not rock. Somewhere far down, presumably, there is rock, but no one has touched it and no one will. What lies under your feet is root: a felted mat of living and half-rotted plant tissue, tens of metres thick in poor country, hundreds in rich, floating on cold black meltwater. It gives under your weight like a mattress. It exhales. Push a hand into it up to the wrist and the hand comes out warm — the deep mat ferments, and the fermentation is the only heat in this world that is not light.

Walk far enough and you find where the mat has torn open along a fresh rift, edges still bright and fibrous, no rot, no smoothing, the tear only days old. Below, water so clear and so still that the glare goes down into it undiminished and you cannot tell whether you are looking at a pool two metres deep or a shaft two kilometres deep. People have been known to step into these, believing them solid, because there is no shadow to mark a hole.

### The Emptiness

The scale is the second thing to understand. This cavity — this space between the mat and the Lid — runs on. Not for a valley's length or a country's. It goes far enough that its own weather is generated internally, that fronts form and cross and dissipate without ever reaching an edge, that a person walking their whole life in one direction will die still under the same ceiling.

And it is nearly empty. That is the paradox of the place. Vegetation is total — the floor is plant, the walls where the mat curves up to meet the ice are plant, the only architecture is plant — and yet the volume is a near-void. The cane-growths stand in isolated masses with fifty, eighty, two hundred kilometres of clear cold shining air between them. From the top of one mass you would see, if you could see through the glare, perhaps two others: white smudges on a white plain under a white sky. Mostly you see nothing but light and the faint dark grain of the mat running away to a horizon that is not a horizon but simply the point where dazzle wins.

### The Canes

The plant is called, by the few who name it, thousandfold cane, and it is possibly one organism, possibly a genus, possibly a behaviour that many unrelated things have converged on.

Its leaves are mirrors. Under this much light, photosynthesis is not a matter of gathering but of *refusing*, and the cane has solved the problem by cladding every surface in a silvered cuticle that throws away nine parts in ten. The working tissue hides beneath in thin green seams, sipping. So the forests are chrome. Whole groves of upright polished tubes, each the diameter of a chimney, ascending in tight bundles, and every one of them reflecting every other, so that the interior of a cane-mass is a hall of mirrors flooded with light from a source you cannot locate. Entering one without eye-protection blinds you inside a minute.

And they are fast. This is where the turbulence lives. A cane grows six to twelve metres a day, driven by absurd surplus, racing upward toward the Lid it can never usefully reach. It arrives. It presses against the ice. It cannot stop, so it bends, buckles, splits, and the plant answers by throwing out lateral bundles that run flat along the underside of the ceiling like a slow silver flood. The Lid, warmed a fraction by contact, weeps, softens, releases a slab.

The slab falls.

A quarter-hectare of ice dropping four hundred metres takes out a hundred canes, which spring back as they break and take out a hundred more, and the mass unzips itself in a chain of shearing collapse that goes on for hours and can be felt through the mat forty kilometres away. Then, in the crater of glittering wreckage, the surviving rootstock begins again, and within a season there is a new tower where the old one stood, differently shaped, in a subtly different place. Nothing here holds its form for longer than a few years. Maps are worthless past the season of their making. The country is constantly demolishing and rebuilding itself, at the pace of a slow catastrophe, everywhere, all the time, and mostly out of sight.

### Weather

Rain, here, falls upward before it falls down.

The mirror-forests bounce so much light back into the ice that they melt their own ceilings; meltwater beads on the underside of the Lid, gathers, drops. Over a great cane-mass it drips continuously, a cold fine ceiling-rain that never quite becomes a shower. In the emptier reaches, where the mat lies bare and dark and absorbs, thermals lift off in columns kilometres wide, hit the ice, spread, cool, and shed their moisture as needle-fog that sinks back down and hangs over the plain in flat white sheets. Fog is a mercy. Fog is shade. People walk in fog for the relief of it.

Then, in the collapse-zones, the ice comes down in dust as well as slabs, and for a day or two it snows indoors.

### Sound

Almost none. The mat swallows footsteps; the fibrous everything eats echo; there is no wind worth the name except in the thermal columns. You can hear your own blood.

Against that near-silence, the events: the *tick* and *tock* of ceiling-drip on a mirror-leaf, which carries an implausible distance. The occasional deep groan of ice adjusting. And then, perhaps four or five times a season, the far-off collapse — a sound like a door closing in a house the size of a continent, arriving as a pressure in the chest before it arrives as noise.

### Fauna

Rare to the point of rumour. A cane-mass the size of a city might hold: one colony of mirror-gnats, which are eyeless and navigate by the temperature gradient of the light; a scatter of flat pale grazers in the root-mat, chewing the soft seams; and, in the black water beneath, long ribbon-things that have never in their lineage encountered a photon and would boil in a second if they did.

And once or twice in a life, on the open plain, a **walker**: two metres at the shoulder, blind, silver-furred to survive the glare, moving in a straight line at the pace of a wading heron, sniffing for fermentation heat. It does not acknowledge you. It has never had a reason to learn what you are.

### The Shade-Keepers

There are people. Not many — the emptiness is not an exaggeration; you might cross the distance of a small nation and find only the compressed dimple where a camp stood last year.

They have no stone, because there is no stone. They have no metal, because metal comes out of stone. They have cane: dead cane, which cures hard as horn and takes an edge that will cut cane. So every tool is made from the thing it works, and the whole material culture is one substance in a hundred treatments — knives, needles, cordage, hooks, sleeping platforms, the long probing staff for testing whether the white ahead is floor or water.

They have almost no fire. Nothing here is dry enough. Warmth comes from burrowing: cutting into the hot rotting mat and lying in it, which is why their bodies are always faintly the smell of the deep floor.

And their architecture is the manufacture of darkness. That is the whole of it. A structure, here, is a device for producing shade — layered cane matting, seven and eight deep, woven tight and sealed with root-pulp, built up into a squat lightless drum with a crooked entry passage so that no glare can turn the corner. Inside there is one small black room, and in that room, for the first time, you have a shadow, and a face has features, and you can open your eyes wide.

These structures are scattered across the underlight singly and in twos, hundreds of kilometres apart, and they are the only built things in a space large enough to hold weather. Some are new. Some are old and half-crushed under a fallen slab, and travellers repair them without knowing whose they were, because to let a dark room collapse is the one thing here that everyone agrees is wrong.

The people wear slit-masks of thin cane and go about with their heads tilted down. They speak quietly, because the silence makes ordinary speech feel like shouting. They tell their children that the light will end — that far above the Lid the sun goes round and low and eventually does not come back, and that when it does not, the ice will go dim and green and then black, the canes will stop their frantic climbing, the ceiling will stop weeping, and there will be a season, a long one, of the thing they build their houses to imitate.

They call it the debt. Nobody living has seen it paid.
