## Gallery Four, the Weeping Landing

Two thousand seven hundred metres, and none of it is ground.

The chamber is bored into the north shoulder of the mountain, but the mountain has been evicted from its own rock. What remains is concrete — and the concrete is made *of* the mountain, its granodiorite crushed to aggregate in the plant that stood downslope for four years and has since been dismantled. So the stone is here, but only as ingredient, the way flour is in bread. Nothing you can touch in this room grew or settled or was left. Everything was poured, bolted, welded, or hung.

The room is small. That is the first surprise, given what passes through it. A steel grating platform, nine metres by five, cantilevered off the west wall at the head of the surge shaft. Handrail, kickplate, a ladder cage going down into dark, a second ladder going up to the louvre gallery. You can see the whole of it from the doorway. There is no vista. There is no elsewhere. Whatever the works are — and they are enormous, a scheme of shafts and penstocks and machine halls threaded through several kilometres of mountain — you will never see them from here. This is a pocket. A single room in a building with no visible exterior, and the room is all the world it offers.

## The sound

You do not hear this place. Hearing is for smaller sounds.

Below the grating, at a depth you cannot judge, water is moving down a shaft at four metres a second under two hundred and eighty metres of head, and the shaft is a flute with no finger holes. What comes up is not a noise but a pressure, a solid column of sound standing in the room like a pillar. It has a body. It has grain. It presses on the sinuses and the sternum and the soft place behind the knees, and it makes the fluid in your eyes tremble so that the lamps smear slightly at their edges.

Layered into it: the transformer note from the far side of the wall, a hard 100-hertz saw that arrives through the concrete rather than the air, and which you feel in your teeth if your mouth is closed and in your palate if it is open. Above that, the turbine harmonics, thin and pure and shifting a semitone or so as load changes on the grid five hundred kilometres away. And above *that*, riding on top of everything, an intermittent hiss-crack from the pressure-relief line where a fitting is not quite right and a jet of water is atomising itself against a baffle plate, ten thousand times a second, forever.

Speech does not exist in Gallery Four. A shout at ten centimetres from an ear is a warmth, a wetness, not a word. Everything is hands: *going down, coming up, hold, wait, two minutes, no, no, stop.* Men and women who work here develop a facial economy — the eyebrows carry the whole grammar of enquiry. They come out of the shift and stand outside the door in the corridor and do not speak for a while, because the corridor's quiet feels like being undressed.

## The water

Rated wet. It is wetter than that.

Cloud enters through the louvre gallery above, which was designed to admit air for the transformer bay and admits, on two hundred days of the year, the inside of a cloud. Cool air at four degrees meets air warmed to eleven by the machines, and the meeting happens exactly here, on this platform, in this room, which is therefore permanently the site of its own weather. Fog stands in the upper third of the chamber and does not disperse. It moves — it has slow gyres, it leans when the louvres shift, it comes down the ladder cage in a soft column when the pressure drops — but it does not leave. Below waist height the air is clear and colder. You work in the clear part and reach up into the white part.

Everything above sheds. The underside of every beam carries a fringe of droplets in a row, evenly spaced, physics having decided the interval. They fall in no rhythm you can predict and in an average you can: something like nine hundred impacts a minute on the grating, a constant sub-audible tick that is entirely buried in the roar and yet is felt on the back of the neck as a small cold randomness.

The concrete weeps too, and this is different water — it comes out of the wall rather than out of the air. It arrives carrying lime. Where it emerges from a construction joint it leaves a white bloom, and where it drips it has begun, in the four years since the pour, to build. The longest of the efflorescence straws under the west beam is eleven millimetres. Nobody removed it, and now people leave it alone on purpose, and one of the shift leads measures it with a steel rule every solstice and chalks the number on the beam. Eleven millimetres. It is the oldest thing in the room.

The floor grating drains to a gutter, and the gutter drains to a sump, and the sump is pumped, and the pump runs for forty seconds in every eleven minutes. When it stops, the room is *quieter*, which is the strangest sensation available here — a subtraction of one voice from a choir that remains deafening.

## Light

Nine lamps, of which eight work. LED panels in wet-rated housings, cold-white, each hooded so it throws down and not out. They make pools with distances between them. The fog above turns each one into a low dome of glow that does not reach the next dome. So the room reads as a scatter of separate small illuminations in a large darkness, though the darkness is only twelve metres away in any direction.

From the louvre gallery, in the middle of the day, in the season when the sun clears the ridge, a grey light comes down the ladder shaft and lands on the platform as a soft rectangle, slightly bluer than the lamps, quivering because the fog it passes through is in motion. It lasts about forty minutes. People stand in it without discussing why.

## What grows

The drip line under the louvre has moss. Two species, probably: a low bronze-green *Bryum* and something looser and paler. Together they occupy perhaps a third of a square metre in three separate patches, and they are the only soft thing in the chamber. Silt has collected around them — mineral dust from the louvre, plus lime, plus a little organic nothing — enough to call soil if you were generous.

There is a green algal film on the north wall where seepage runs, in a long streak two storeys tall, and the film is thickest where the lamp reaches it and absent where the lamp does not, drawing the shape of the light onto the wall like a slow photograph.

In the gutter, in a wedge of silt at a bend, a single grass has come up. It is fifteen centimetres and etiolated, drawn out and pale from reaching. It arrived as a seed in someone's boot tread or in the louvre air, and it will not set seed here, and it will die when the gutter is cleaned in autumn, and someone will notice.

No lichen. Lichen is a later chapter; four years is nothing to lichen.

## What does not live here

Nothing moves that isn't water, machinery, or a person.

There is a fly in the housing of lamp seven, dry, on its back, arrived by louvre. There is a cobweb in the corner of the door frame, on the corridor side, where the air is still — the spider is not in it and never came back. In the moss there are, statistically, nematodes and rotifers and the tardigrades that arrive wherever moss arrives, and you may count them as fauna if you wish, but nothing here has eyes, and nothing here is hunting anything, and no sound in this room was ever made by an animal.

The absence isn't eerie. It is simply a consequence: too loud, too cold, too new, too sealed, and nothing to eat.

## The cycles

The place is not static. It has four weathers, on four clocks.

**The load cycle**, seven to nine hours: the turbine note climbs as the grid wakes, and with it the shaft roar deepens and the platform's vibration moves up through a series of standing patterns, so that on a heavy afternoon the handrail at the north end buzzes hard enough to numb a hand and on a light night it is merely warm.

**The pump cycle**, eleven minutes: the sump voice on and off.

**The condensation cycle**, daily: fog thick and low at dawn, thinner and higher by mid-afternoon, dripping heaviest two hours after the machines ramp down, when the warm air gives up its water all at once and the platform receives what feels like the beginning of rain.

**The pour cycle**, seasonal and slow: lime out of the wall, salt onto the surface, straw growing downward by two and a bit millimetres a year. The only geology this place has, and it is being made in front of you at a rate you could watch, given patience.

## Rules

1. Ears are not optional. Twenty minutes unprotected costs you a range of frequencies you will not get back, and the ones you lose are the ones in which children speak.
2. Three points of contact on the ladders. Every surface has a film of water on it and every film has lime in it, and lime is soap.
3. Do not open the corridor door while the louvre damper is open. The pressure differential will take the door off your hand and the fog out of the room, and the room will need six hours to make its weather again.
4. Do not speak. Not as etiquette — as a fact of physics you will keep forgetting.
5. Leave the straw alone.

## The one strange thing

On the load ramp, twice a day, air is displaced up the surge shaft and out through the louvres, and for about ninety seconds the room exhales — a slow warm push of fog going up the ladder cage and out into the cloud that made it. Then, as the ramp finishes, it draws back in.

Everyone who works here has independently decided that the room is breathing, and everyone knows perfectly well that it isn't, and nobody has ever said so out loud, because nobody can say anything out loud in Gallery Four.
