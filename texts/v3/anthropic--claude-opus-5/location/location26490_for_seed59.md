## The Basin

It sits in a saddle between two ridges, at the altitude where cloud lives permanently rather than passing through. A shallow bowl, maybe six hundred paces across — you can walk its rim in the time it takes a pot of tubers to soften — and everything within it is either water, plant, animal, or some negotiation between the four.

The floor is peat. Not soil: peat, ten or twelve arm-lengths deep, black, cold, saturated to the point of refusal. Step wrong and your leg goes in to the knee and comes out smelling of ancient leaves. Step right and the whole ground flexes under you like the skin over a full belly, and the flex travels outward, and a hundred paces away someone feels you arrive. There are no streams in the basin because there is no gradient worth the name; water simply *is here*, held in moss, weeping from every cut root, sheeting invisibly under the vegetation, gathering at the center into a pool of tea-colored water called the Eye, which never freezes and never clears.

Above the peat, plants. That word is insufficient. There is no distinction here between forest and building material and ground and furniture and larder. There is only living mass, arranged at various degrees of intention.

## The Grown Town

Seven trunk-groups anchor the settlement, each one an old tree — twisted, gall-knuckled, wind-cropped at the top by the ridge weather — around which generations have pleached, bent, lashed, and coaxed the surrounding growth into rooms. Not built. *Kept.* A house here is a place where the vegetation has been persuaded, over eighty or a hundred years, into a shape with an inside.

The walls are hedge: three species of stiff-stemmed shrub interplanted and woven while green, now thick as a man's chest, cropped daily on the inner face so the interior stays hollow. Left alone for one season a wall closes inward by a hand's width. Left alone for three, the room is gone — filled, solid, indistinguishable from thicket. So the pruning is not maintenance. It is the house. Stop and you have no house; you have a place where a house was.

Floors are platform-work of split cane over root-buttress, and the buttresses go on growing, so the floors tilt slowly through the decades and are re-shimmed with wedges of bark. Roofs are shingled in the fibrous sheath-bark of a palm-like understory plant, layered eight deep, and every roof carries its own second landscape: a garden of epiphytes, ferns, bladder-orchids, cushion moss a hand thick, plus the ferns' passengers and the moss's populations. Rain does not fall on these houses so much as get absorbed and gradually re-released. Long after a storm, the eaves are still delivering.

There is no stone in the town except three boulders that were here first and are now so wrapped in root that only their crowns show, polished by generations of sitting.

There is almost no metal. A few thin copper things come up the ridge trail from somewhere lower and warmer — a needle, a rasp, an old awl — and they are treasured and greased with animal fat and they still corrode away within a handful of years, because nothing in the basin is ever dry. The real tools are chert flake and bone chisel, split-cane knives that are sharp for one afternoon and then thrown away, hardwood mallets, and cordage. Enormous quantities of cordage. A person here can twist fiber while walking, talking, arguing, eating. The town is held together by perhaps forty thousand paces of hand-rolled string, all of it rotting, all of it being replaced.

Fire is the difficult thing. Wood won't dry. Cut peat won't dry either, not properly, not in this air. So the hearths are clay bowls raised on posts at head height, fed with slow smouldering blocks of dense root and resin-rich heartwood chips, tended constantly, never allowed out, smoking with a sweet acrid patience. Every household's status can be read in the color of its rafters. The oldest houses are lacquered black inside, and their smoke-cured meat lasts a season longer than anyone else's.

## Sound

The basin is loud in a way that visitors find physically pressing, like a hand on the sternum.

Frogs first — thumbnail-sized, thousands of them per pace of moss, in the cushion layer, in the leaf-axils, in the water held by every bromeliad. Their call is a single high tick, and there are so many that individual ticks dissolve into a shimmering hiss that comes from every direction including underfoot. It rises at dusk, holds all night, thins toward mid-morning, never fully stops.

Over that: insects in three or four distinct registers, one of them so continuous that people stop hearing it and then get vertigo on the rare nights it lapses. Birds — small tanager-bright things in flocks of two hundred that arrive as a moving wall of chatter, strip a fruiting shrub in a quarter-hour, and go; and above them the raptor-whistles from the ridge; and in the eaves, bats muttering and shifting all day, thousands of them, treated as tenants who pay rent in guano for the roof gardens.

And over all of that, the town's own racket: the arrhythmic snick-snick-snick of pruning, which never stops anywhere in the basin from first light to dark; cane being split; the hollow-log drums used for announcements because shouting doesn't carry through this much vegetation; children; the pounding of fiber; argument.

Twice a day the sound changes wholesale. When the cloud comes up the western slope — a tide, arriving at roughly the same hour, thickening the air until you cannot see the next house — the frogs surge and the birds fall silent. When the cloud draws off in the late afternoon, the reverse. People time their work by these two shifts more than by the invisible sun.

## Turbulence

Nothing here holds still, and the town is designed around not holding still.

The rain comes hard most afternoons, brief and vertical, drumming the roofs into a roar that stops conversation. Wind crosses the saddle in sudden shoves that make the whole canopy heel and dump its stored water at once, a private cloudburst under a clear sky.

Growth is the real weather. Everything wants the space you are standing in. A path unused for two weeks must be recut. A cache left un-checked for a month is inside a plant. Ownership in the basin is not recorded, cannot be recorded, and does not need to be: what you own is what you are currently keeping open. The peat remembers, though. It holds the shapes of old post-holes, old hearth-clay, old walls, layered downward, and when they cut peat for the hearths they cut through their great-grandparents' floor plans and everybody knows it and nobody remarks on it.

And a few times a year the browsers come through. Heavy-bodied, ox-shouldered, cold-adapted, moving in loose bands of eight or fifteen, feeding on exactly the shrubs the walls are made of. There is no keeping them out — a hedge is a meal, a fence is fiber, the peat won't hold a ditch. So the town yields. Drums go, platforms are lifted, stores go up into the crowns, the population climbs, and the animals spend a day and a night walking through the houses, browsing the walls down to knuckles, churning the peat into black soup, defecating magnificently. Then they leave.

Then everybody comes down and begins again, and the walls come back thicker for the pruning, and the churned peat grows the best fiber-cane in the basin, and the year's calendar is reckoned from these visitations. The oldest woman in the town has lived through fifty-one. She describes the fourteenth as the worst and the thirty-second as the funniest.

## Rules of the Place

*Nothing is finished.* There is no word here for a completed structure. The nearest term means something like "currently being kept."

*Nothing is dry.* Plan accordingly. Store in resin-sealed gourds and smoke-blackened baskets. Assume rot as a constant tax, roughly a third of everything, every year.

*The dead go to the moss.* Wrapped, weighted, given to the Eye, which is cold and airless below a certain depth and gives nothing back. The pool is not sacred exactly. It is simply where people go.

*Silence means look up.* If the frogs stop and it is not the cloud-hour, something large is in the basin.

*You may take a room from the plants but you may not take a hill from the water.* The rim is the boundary. Below the western lip the slope steepens and the vegetation changes and the air warms and that is a different country, reached by one trail, and people go down it and come back up it with copper needles and salt and stories, and the basin absorbs them and closes over the gap they left, exactly as it does everything else.

Standing at the rim at the cloud's turning, you would see: grey-white light, even, shadowless, generous — bright enough to work by anywhere, not bright enough to cast a shape. Under it a bowl of green so dense and layered that the town reads at first as nothing at all. Then a line of smoke. Then another. Then the roofs resolve, and the platforms, and the fine dark stitching of ladders, and the movement, all of it, everywhere, in every layer, ceaselessly — and you understand that the whole basin is a single body, breathing water in and out, and that the people are one of the things it does.
