## The Hour

The country has a name that means a time of day. There is a reason for this.

It is a plateau, and it is high — high enough that the sky at the zenith has gone from blue toward a bruised indigo, high enough that the horizon is not a line of land but the flat white lid of a cloud sea, which lies below on all sides and does not move. Not "moves slowly." Does not move. The same cumulus knuckle has been resting against the same buttress of the Lip for however long the lichens took to grow, and the lichens grow at a rate that would embarrass a stone.

The plateau is sixty kilometres across, give or take, and it is *old*. Not old in the way of mountains that still have peaks to lose — old in the way of a face at the very end of a life, when the bones have become the only news. Three summits remain, and they are stumps, worn down to broad shoulders you could drive a cart over. Between them lies the great flatness: gently dished, gently seamed, so nearly level that a dropped ball would not know which way to go and would simply sit, and sit, and sit.

### The seams

Now, the light. It is good light, plain and hard and thin, the light of about the tenth hour of the morning, and it comes from the same place it has always come from. The sun does not travel. There is no evening here and no one has ever needed a word for one.

Because of this, every shadow on the plateau is a permanent feature of the geology. Each boulder has a lichened north face and a bare, sun-scoured south face, and between them runs a line so exact it looks ruled. Over the ages the difference in weathering has cut that line into the rock — an actual groove, a millimetre deep, the fossil of a shadow's edge. The herders call these grooves *seams*, and they are everywhere: seams around every stone, seams trailing from the legs of every standing animal, seams thrown by the horns of the Standing across the pan in long converging rays, so that from a rise the whole plateau appears combed.

Walk into a shadow and your skin registers the change immediately: a drop of several degrees, held there for millennia. The air is cool everywhere — the cool of high stone that has never once been warm — but the shadows are colder in a way that feels *aged*, like the cold of a cellar under a house that has fallen down.

### Ground

The rock underfoot is a pale, faintly rosy limestone, and if you kneel and look you will see what it is made of. Vertebrae. Ribs no thicker than a straw. Beaks. Toe bones. Whole small skulls the size of a thumbnail, packed shoulder to shoulder with no matrix between them, cemented by nothing but pressure and time. The plateau is not a place where animals died. It is animals, some kilometres deep, compressed into pavement.

Where the plateau dishes lowest there is the Pan: a bed of old salt, white going to grey, cracked into polygons a metre across. It is the ghost of a lake. There has been no water in it for so long that the concept has weathered out of the mineralogy. Dust does not blow across it, because nothing blows here.

### The Standing

And then there is the fauna, which is the whole point, and which is difficult.

There are animals everywhere. Not scattered — *packed*. The density is the density of a crowd at a gate. Large woolled quadrupeds, some two metres at the shoulder, matted, dun and iron-grey, horns spiralled to a completed shape, standing in loose ranks that run to the limit of sight in every direction. Between and beneath them: smaller things wedged into every gap. Rock-hares in every crevice of every scree, so numerous that a slope reads as slope until it turns out to be eyes. A felted crust across the open ground made of small hard-bodied insects, millions to the square metre, interlocked like chain mail, which yields under your boot with the faintest give and no sound at all.

And overhead, the Nailed. Birds — thousands of them, at heights from four to forty metres — fixed in the air. Wings out, primaries fanned, not hovering, not soaring, simply *positioned*. You can walk beneath a low one and take its foot between your fingers. It is warm. The scales of the tarsus are dusty. If you let go, the foot does not swing.

None of them is dead. This is the thing you have to understand slowly, over about a day, and then keep re-understanding.

They are warm. They are pliant. Their eyes are open and moist at the corner and their pupils will contract if you lean in and shade them. There is dust settled on the cornea that a blink would have cleared, and the blink is coming; it is simply not due yet. Every few years, somewhere on the plateau, one of the Standing exhales. It is the largest event in the world. The sound arrives as a long soft collapse, like a heavy door easing shut in another building, and afterwards the smell of warm wool and old grass hangs in the still air for a week without dispersing.

Under the Pan lies the Long One. Twenty-two vertebrae break the salt crust, each the size of a small hut, ridged and yellowed, running four hundred metres from a mound in the north to nothing at all in the south. It is not a fossil. Put your palm on it and there is warmth, and something below the warmth, slower than warmth. The rest of the body is beneath, and it is not known how much of it there is.

### The quiet

Sound behaves poorly at this altitude and even more poorly here. The air is too thin to carry much, and there is no wind, no water, no leaf, no cry. Your own footsteps arrive muffled and slightly late, as though heard through a wall. Speech travels perhaps ten metres and then simply stops, as if absorbed. What you hear instead is the hum: a pressure in the teeth and the sinuses, not quite a tone, which some visitors say is the sum of a hundred million heartbeats each about a decade apart, and which others say is the sound the plateau makes instead of a wind.

### What grows

Vegetation is sparse and entirely opportunistic. Cushion plants, tight grey rosettes, growing where a shadow-seam keeps the frost. Lichen in six colours — mustard, rust, black, a bone-white crustose that is the oldest living thing here and has not changed shape within record. A short blue-grey grass, one species, which grows exclusively in the wool of the Standing, in the lee pockets behind their shoulders where a little frost collects; the herds are lightly turfed, each animal carrying its own small meadow, and none of them will ever eat it.

Water exists only as this: frost, in the seams, in the shadows, on the north sides. It does not melt, because the temperature there does not change. It does not sublime, because — well. Wipe it off with your sleeve and it will not come back, and the bare patch will remain bare, and this is one of the ways you learn the rules.

### Rules

1. **Nothing here is beginning and nothing is ending.** Everything has arrived at its final form and is holding it. Wounds do not bleed, close, or worsen. A cracked horn on one of the Standing has been cracked to exactly that depth since before there were tally-marks to record it.

2. **The plateau is a fixed point, and change here does not accumulate.** You may move a stone. The stone moves; it stays where you put it; the seam it used to cast remains cut in the rock behind it, orphaned. Come back and the stone is where you left it. But you will find yourself uncertain, with a certainty that grows, that it was ever anywhere else.

3. **The herd cannot be counted.** Two careful tallies of the same slope, made an hour apart, with nothing having moved by a hair, will not agree. The discrepancy is never large and is never in the same direction.

4. **You cannot cross the plateau twice by different routes.** Attempt it and the second crossing will discover itself to have been the first: the same cairns, the same three-legged hare, the same order of stumps against the cloud lid. The plateau has one crossing, and it lends it out.

5. **Being here is contagious.** By the third day you will notice that you have not been hungry. By the fifth, that the cut on your knuckle from the first morning is neither healing nor hurting. By the eighth you will find yourself standing, facing along the seams, and the standing will feel like the most reasonable thing you have ever done.

### The marks of hands

There are people, or there were, or the distinction has stopped holding.

Their work is scattered, small, and unmistakable. Cairns: several hundred of them, waist-high, mortarless, sited without evident logic, marking no route and no grave. A drystone wall, eleven hundred paces long, one metre high, running from nowhere to nowhere across the middle of the Pan — built, as far as anyone can tell, to cast a shadow, and casting it, and having cast it long enough to cut its seam into the salt.

Bone tools, worked with stone: needles, awls, a comb. A coil of rope braided from the wool of the Standing, laid down in a neat spiral beside a hearth-ring that contains white ash which has never been rained on and which holds the print of the last hand that touched it. There is no charcoal in it and no wood on the plateau, and nobody has explained the fire.

Between the fourth and fifth vertebrae of the Long One, someone chipped a shelter into the salt and roofed it with hide, and swept the floor. The floor is still swept.

And on the boulder at the head of the Pan there are tallies: eleven thousand-odd scratches in eight distinct hands, in groups of five, counting something. The groups have been counted by visitors many times. The totals never match, and the marks have never changed.

Among the Standing, on the eastern slope of the middle stump, there are four figures. They are the right size and the right shape, and they wear plaited wool, and they face along the seams like everything else, and their eyes are open, with dust on them, and they are warm. One of them is turning her head. She has been turning it for some while — the seam thrown by her chin has begun to blur at one edge, a smear a few millimetres wide in the cut rock, which is the only evidence anywhere on this plateau that time is passing at all.

She is turning to look behind her, and there is no telling when she will get there.
