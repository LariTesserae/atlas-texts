## The Plateau of Long Machines

Begin with the fact that there is nothing to see. Not dimness — dimness implies a source somewhere, badly served. Here there is no source. The eye, if you bring one, receives nothing at all and after a while begins inventing, which is dangerous and everyone knows it. So set the eye aside.

You will know this place the way it knows itself: by sound, by warmth on the skin, by the grain of the rain, by the smell of hot stone and wet fibre, and by the long low note underneath everything, which is the note of the machines, and which does not stop.

---

**The ground.**

Three to four kilometres above whatever lies below, a highland spreads out farther than walking. Not a summit — a *province* of height, continental, with weather of its own manufacture and provinces within provinces. Its rock is a soft pale limestone that water has been working on for an unguessable time, and the work is finished, or as finished as such work gets. Nothing here is sharp. Every ridge has been rounded to a shoulder; every gorge has been fluted smooth; every slope carries the long shallow stairs of rimstone terraces, pool below pool below pool, each lip built up grain by grain out of dissolved stone by nothing more ambitious than water spilling over an edge for ten thousand years.

Walk it barefoot and you can read the whole geology through your soles. The terraces are the good country: warm shin-deep water, floors furred with slick mats, rims you can follow like a handrail for a day at a time. Then the sponge country, where the limestone has been eaten hollow and the ground answers your footfall with a resonance, a faint boom of some room beneath — and where, every few hundred paces, a shaft goes straight down into a warm updraft that smells of iron and old steam. Then the flute gorges, where the plateau's rivers have cut down through the pale rock into darker beds and run in tight loud curves you cannot cross for a week in either direction. Then the boiler-lands, where the stone is too hot to stand on and the ground exhales continuously and there is no vegetation, only a wide crackling silence with a hiss under it, and the bones of animals that misjudged their crossing.

Everywhere the rock weeps. Water is never more than a hand's depth under the surface, and in most places it is *on* the surface: seeping from cliff faces in curtains, pooling in every hollow, condensing out of the air onto anything cooler than the air, which includes you at first, and then does not.

---

**The lid, and the water that never leaves.**

Two kilometres overhead there is a ceiling. It is cloud, and it has been cloud for so long that it may as well be counted as rock. The plateau breathes warm vapour up out of its vents and its machines and its million pools; the vapour rises, cools against the underside of the world's cold upper air, and comes back down as a fine warm drizzle that has been falling for the entire history of anything that lives here. It falls with a grain to it — not drumming, more like the sound of a crowd very far away agreeing about something. On the terraces it makes a continuous hiss of ten million small impacts. Under the cane it makes a slower, fatter percussion of drips off leaves, and that sound is how you tell cover from open.

The water goes up, comes down, seeps in, gets heated, comes out of a vent, goes up. It has been the same water for a very long time. It tastes faintly of minerals and faintly of machine, and every living thing on the plateau is built out of it.

The lid makes its own storms. Convection stacks up beneath it, gets nowhere, and turns into weather that circulates rather than passes: warm columns that roar upward for hours and then collapse into cold downdrafts that flatten the cane and drop the temperature ten degrees in a minute and dump water in sheets. And thunder — real thunder, huge, rolling from horizon to horizon, arriving in the chest before the ears.

Never any lightning. That is the first strange thing you will notice and the last you will get used to. The charge goes somewhere; there is a discharge; there is an enormous noise. There is no flash. Nobody on the plateau considers this remarkable. When you ask, they say *the lid drinks it*, in the tone of someone explaining that water is wet.

---

**Growing without light.**

And yet the plateau is thick with growth — layered, near-touching, saturated with life to the point where in places you cannot push through and must go around.

Nothing here eats light. Everything here eats chemistry. The vents deliver sulphides and dissolved iron and warm hydrogen up out of the deep rock, and the base of every food chain is a mat: rubbery, tough, colourless in a way that has no meaning, growing in sheets and beards and pillows over every warm wet surface. The mats are the pastures. Above them rise the true flora, which are strange, because they are all built for a world where the sun is a *pipeline*.

There is the cane: a hollow-stemmed thermophyte that grows in seas covering whole provinces, rooted along buried conduits, tapping heat and mineral directly from the machine-lines the way a vine taps a trellis. It grows to four or five metres, packed close as reeds, and in wind it is deafening — a dry clashing roar over the wet hiss of the rain. Its stems are full of water under pressure. Cut one and it screams for a few seconds, then sighs.

There are the hanging curtains: root systems that grow *downward off cliff faces* into the vapour, kilometres of fine filament combing minerals out of the fog, so that walking the base of a scarp you pass through hundreds of metres of warm wet hair. Things live in the curtains. A great many things live in the curtains.

There are fungal towers in the sinkhole country, ten metres, spongy, warm to the touch, hollow, and hooting softly when the downdraft catches their vents — an accidental instrument, an entire landscape of them answering the weather.

And there is the moss that hears. It grows on the machine casings and on the great pipes and in the fluted gorges where the water is loudest, and it grows *toward* sound; it thickens where vibration is strongest; and its spore cases split on a particular low note. Which brings us to the point.

---

**The machines.**

Under the plateau, in it, through it, there is an industry. Nobody built it in living memory. Nobody knows what it is *for*, and asking that question marks you as new.

Its scale is geological. Mains the diameter of a gorge run for hundreds of kilometres under the terraces; you find them where the limestone has worn through and there is suddenly a curved wall of warm mineral-jacketed metal beneath your feet, ringing softly. Shafts that were once chimneys have been colonized by calcite until the turbine blades inside them are stalactite-thick, and still they turn, slowly, hugely, with a grinding that you feel in your teeth from four kilometres off. Pumps somewhere down in the dark move water up. Heat exchangers pour it back out as vapour. The vapour becomes the lid. The lid makes the darkness. The darkness made everything alive up here, and everything alive up here now maintains the machine: mats seal its leaks; calcite-precipitating growths plaster its cracks; the cane roots hold its buried conduits in place against the working of the water. It has been in this state so long that machine and ecology are no longer separable. It is not decaying. It is not improving. It simply persists, in balance, weathered into its own final form, like the rimstone.

None of it gives off light. Not a spark, not a glow, not a filament. Whatever ran on flame ran out of flame long ago; what remains is pressure, rotation, and heat, and heat is not light down here — the metal is warm, the exhaust is warm, and the dark is absolute.

The machines have a cycle. Roughly every eleven hours the whole plateau's drone shifts: the base note drops, there is a long ascending groan as some vast valving somewhere changes state, a hammering that travels across the province like a wave, and then the note settles half a tone lower and the rain thickens for an hour. Then, eleven hours later, it goes back up.

That is the day. That is the sun. The moss splits its spores on the down-note. The cane flowers on the up-note. Grazers move on the hammering. Everything alive on the plateau keeps time by the shift, and between the two notes there is a gap of perhaps forty seconds when the machine is quiet.

In the gap, everything freezes. Every animal, all at once, across a continent. Forty seconds of nothing but rain.

Predators hunt in the gap, because in the gap they can hear you.

---

**Animals.**

Abundance, and all of it loud.

The pastures carry herds of drum-backed grazers — six-legged, chest-high, plates of resonant keratin over the shoulders — that walk the terraces cropping mats and tapping continuously, each animal producing a slow click that maps the pool ahead and, incidentally, keeps it in the herd. A herd of four hundred crossing a terrace field sounds like rain on a tin roof, which is presumably the point.

In the curtains: climbers, small, fast, with ears the size of their bodies, that shriek constantly to see and go silent to hide.

In the flute gorges: eyeless swimmers, long as your arm, that navigate by the water's own noise and hunt by feeling the drag of your leg.

Above, all night, which is to say always: the vespers. Flyers with three-metre spans that ride the convection columns and navigate not by echoes off the ground but by *the machines* — by triangulating the drone, using the great pumps as beacons, so that their migration routes are the pipeline routes and their roosts are the warm chimney heads. When a pump section fails, a generation of vespers gets lost, and for a season the sky is full of confused voices.

And the mimics. The plateau's true horror, and its most honest citizens. Sound-eating ambush predators that learned, long ago, that in a world where reality is defined by noise, you can simply lie. They reproduce machine tones with terrible accuracy — the hammering of the shift, the sigh of a cut cane, the click of a grazer, the specific squeal of a bearing that everyone in the district knows and steers by. They will imitate a landmark. They will imitate a *place*. Travellers speak of following a familiar valve-hiss for two hours toward a shelter that was never there.

The rule everyone learns: if a machine sound is *new*, do not go to it. If a machine sound is *slightly wrong*, go the other way. The plateau's real machinery has been making the same sounds for ten thousand years and has no interest in you.

---

**What people have left.**

Very little, and all of it small.

There are counting-sheds at intervals along the mains, stone huts the size of a cart, roofed in cane, containing a bank of stops and levers and a slate wall of tally-marks cut deep enough to read with a fingertip. Some of the tallies are current. Some stop mid-column and the moss has been over them for centuries.

There are listener towers: stacked rimstone plates, four or five metres, with a hollow throat that concentrates the drone, where somebody sat and counted shifts and knotted the count into a cord. The cords survive well in this climate. There are cords in the towers that record eleven thousand shifts, and cords that record four.

There are knot-strings strung along walking routes at hip height, kilometres of them, hand-over-hand guides across the terrace country, maintained by nobody in particular, with knot-codes that say *water*, *hot ground*, *turn*, *gap coming*, *do not*.

There are hand-marks worn into the metal of certain valves, smooth depressions where generations have gripped, and the valves are kept clear of moss, and no one alive will tell you what they do, only when to turn them.

The people themselves are scattered, few, and eyeless — not blinded; simply, after enough generations, no longer manufacturing an organ with no function. They are extraordinary listeners. They speak in a language with a great deal of percussion in it, and they clap and click while they walk, constantly, the way you would breathe. To go silent on the plateau is to stop existing. To be silent and moving is to be a mimic, and is answered accordingly.

---

**Rules of the world, as they'd be told to you.**

Keep making noise.

Count the shifts. If you lose the count, sit still until the next one.

Never be in the open in the gap.

Water flows to the rim; if you are lost, follow the water down and you will reach the edge of the plateau in a month, which is not the same as reaching anywhere.

Do not follow a new sound.

Never strike a spark. This is the oldest rule and the one they will repeat twice. A spark, out here, is a beacon to every creature within a kilometre — and to some that come from farther, faster, and are never seen, because nothing here is ever seen. There are stories about a light on the terraces, and the stories all end the same way, and the plateau has been dark for a very long time, and it stays dark, and it stays dark, and that is not an accident, and no one will say more than that.

---

At the rim, the plateau simply stops. The terraces run out over an edge and become a warm rain falling two kilometres into cloud, and the sound of that is the loudest thing on the continent, a white roar you can feel in your sternum from a day's walk inland. Beyond it, nothing — no updraft, no echo returning, no floor. Whatever is down there does not answer.

Stand at the rim in the warm drizzle, with the cane roaring behind you and the vespers passing overhead in their thousands, following a pipeline they cannot see toward a beacon they do not understand, and the machines under your feet turning over into their next long shift, and the thunder rolling across a sky that has never once shown its face.

It is not a ruin. Ruins are things that stopped. This place did not stop. It arrived, a long time ago, at exactly what it is, and it has been being that ever since, loudly, warmly, in the dark, with nobody watching.
