## The Upper Shelf

Come up onto it in the early morning, before the cloud lifts, and the first thing you understand about this country is that it is new.

The plateau stands high — high enough that the air is thin in the chest and the sky, when you can see it, is a deep bruised blue — and it stands *up*, unapologetically, in slabs. Nothing here has been worn down. The ground is pale rhyolite and welded ash, and it has broken along its own grain into tables and steps and clean-edged benches, some of them a hundred feet high, all of them still holding their corners. Where a slab has parted from its neighbour, the gap is a straight-sided cleft you could drop a rope into, and the two faces still match. You could fit them back together. Nothing has rounded them off.

The talus at the base of the cliffs is angular, ringing, unsettled — glassy chips that slide underfoot with a sound like poured coins. Streams run across the flats in shallow braided sheets because they have not yet had time to choose a channel; they wander, they flood a hollow, they leave. At the plateau's edges they simply arrive at the drop and go over it, unnotched, no gorge cut back into the rim, just water leaving the lip in a clean line and becoming vapour halfway down. The waterfalls have not yet begun to eat the cliff. That is how young this is.

## Warmth from underneath

By elevation this should be a cold place — frostbitten, sparse, a country of lichen and grit. It isn't. The heat comes from below.

Somewhere not far under the pale rock there is something still cooling from the event that raised all this, and it keeps the ground at blood temperature. Bare stone in shade is warm to the palm. Puddles steam faintly at dawn. Springs come up at the base of every scarp, some merely tepid, some hot enough to scald, depositing rims of white and ochre mineral around themselves in flat overlapping saucers. Water seeps out of joint-planes all along the cliff faces and runs down them in unbroken films, so that whole walls of the plateau glisten like wet skin, weeping steadily, endlessly, from a source that never lowers and never rises.

The result is a warm, saturated, motionless air. Not tropical rot-heat — mild, about the temperature of a room, the same temperature at noon and at midnight, the same in what would elsewhere be summer and what would elsewhere be winter. There are no seasons here because there is nothing to make one. The cloud forms on the plateau every morning as the ground heat meets the thin cold sky, sits in the hollows until mid-morning, rises to hang three hundred feet above the flats, and stays there. Light comes through it white and even, shadowless, strong enough to burn skin. Twice a day, near dawn and near dusk, the deck thins and lets through slanting yellow light that makes every wet surface on the plateau flare at once — cliffs, sheet-streams, roofs, the whole shelf briefly one enormous pane of glass — and then it closes again.

Everything is damp. Cloth never dries all the way. Rope stays supple. Stone stays dark. The wet is not weather; it is simply the condition of the place.

## The green, and what is missing from it

Vegetation covers perhaps a third of the ground, and it covers that third thickly. The rest is bare pale rock, and the boundary between the two is abrupt: a mat of green with a rolled edge, sitting on stone like something laid down rather than grown out of it.

That is more or less what it is. There is no soil to speak of. Nothing turns the ground over — nothing digs, nothing burrows, nothing tunnels. So the dead plants pile up where they fall, ferment slowly in the warm wet, and become a spongy brown peat that thickens upward for a few hand-spans and then, on the steeper faces, peels away from the rock in one great sodden sheet and slumps into a stream bed. The plateau is therefore patchy by nature: mats accumulating here, sloughing there, bare rock waiting.

Where the mats hold, the growth is generous. Tree ferns eight and ten feet tall, their trunks furred with wet black fibre, the crowns holding standing water in their centres. Clubmosses in dense low cushions, so springy that walking across them is like walking on a bed. Long tough grasses and sedges gone to seed, their pollen shed to the air, their seeds plumed for the air, because the air is the only courier there is. Liverworts pasted flat on wet stone in overlapping green scales. Around the hot springs, mats of cyanobacteria in ribbons of blue-green and rust, slippery as oil, exhaling small bubbles.

There are no flowers worth the name — a few dull green catkins, some pale wind-shaken tassels, nothing coloured, nothing scented. There is no fruit anywhere in this country. Nothing needed to be sweet, so nothing became sweet.

And every leaf is whole.

This is the thing a visitor notices last and then cannot stop noticing. Not a bite taken, not a hole, not a mine, not a gall, not a curled tip. No thorns anywhere, no spines, no bitter sap, no defences at all, because nothing has ever needed defending. The ferns stand up in the open with their soft new fronds unrolling and nothing comes for them. Nothing has ever come for them.

There are no animals on the Upper Shelf. Not birds, not insects, not a worm in the peat, not a fish in the pools, not a mite in the litter. The mats are plant and fungus and bacterium and nothing else. The pools are clear and empty. This is not a mystery the people here contemplate; they have no word for animal, and no need of one.

## The silence

Because of that absence, the plateau makes only three sounds, and it makes them constantly.

Water is the first — not a rush, but a texture: a thousand cliff faces seeping, ten thousand fern-drips falling onto peat, the distant unvarying hush of the rim falls, which is so steady that after a day you stop hearing it and only notice it when a fold of the land cuts it off. The second is wind in fern crowns, a dry rattle in a wet world. The third is the small clicking collapse of talus, somewhere, occasionally, once or twice an hour, the plateau settling a fraction further into itself.

There is no birdsong. No insect drone. No rustle in the undergrowth, ever. At night the silence is total in a way that is difficult to sit inside. A dropped stone can be heard across a whole basin.

## The town

There is one town, and it has no name, because there is nothing to distinguish it from.

It occupies three broad natural benches on the south flank of the plateau, where a warm spring comes out large and constant, and it is built of the country: rhyolite split along its own planes with wooden wedges driven dry into drilled sockets and then soaked until they swell and the block parts with a sound like a hand-clap. The walls are dry-laid, courses tight, no mortar, because in this air mortar would never cure. Roof timbers are tree-fern trunk, lashed with plant cord; over them a thatch of fern frond; over that a peeled peat mat, laid green side down, weighted with cobbles. The mats keep growing on the roofs. Half the town is turfed and mossy on top, and from the bench above it looks less like a settlement than like an unusually orderly patch of the plateau — rectangles of green with pale stone edges, wisps of steam.

There is no metal. Blades are obsidian, struck from the black glassy lenses in the ash beds and so sharp they part skin before they are felt. Heavier work is done with ground basalt adzes and with fire and water. There is fired clay in quantity — pipes, gutters, basins, storage jars — because clay is one thing the plateau makes abundantly, in white beds under the pumice.

What the town is, more than anything, is a machine for moving water sideways. Every street is stepped and cambered and grooved. Every roof drains into a clay channel; every channel into a runnel; every runnel into a stone-lined drain that goes under the houses and out over the bench edge in a small private waterfall. Hot spring water is led by open stone flumes to bathing basins, then, cooled, along the growing terraces, where it warms the root zone of the crops: a grain like millet that pollinates itself on the wind, a starchy tuber, fern crowns cut young, and, in shallow warm tanks, harvested mats of the blue-green algae, dried into dark cakes. There is nothing else to eat. There has never been anything else to eat. No leather, no bone, no wool, no fat, no eggs. Cloth is bast fibre, retted in the warm pools, and it is grey-green, and everyone's is the same.

The population is a couple of thousand, and the town has room for four. This is deliberate and very old. Courtyards are wide, plazas are half-empty, whole terraces of houses stand sound and swept and unoccupied, doorways open, floors warm. You can walk for ten minutes through built streets and pass two people. They will nod. They will not raise their voices, because a raised voice here travels to the rim.

No wheels — there is nothing to pull a cart, and the streets are stairs anyway. Goods move on drag-sledges over wet stone, which is nearly frictionless, hauled by two people with a chest strap, in silence, at a walk. There is one instrument in the town, a hollowed log slit-drum on the upper bench, and it is struck perhaps once in a generation.

## The rule of the place

Nothing here changes, and that is the whole architecture of the world.

The spring delivers the same volume it delivered to the grandparents of everyone living. The cloud comes up at the same hour. The temperature at the doorsill is the temperature it has always been. The crops do not have a season; they are sown and cut in a continuous rotation, a little every day, forever. There is no store against winter because there is no winter, and no famine in memory, and no surplus either — the terraces produce what the terraces produce.

So the town does not grow. When a wall fails, it is rebuilt on the same footprint, block for block; the plan of the town is memorised in the way a song is memorised, and the empty quarters are maintained empty. Nobody can say what the extra room was ever for. The best answer anyone gives is that the town is the right size, and that a thing that is the right size does not need to become another size.

The only real events are geological, and they are rare and small: a slab parts from a cliff and comes down in the night with a noise the whole plateau hears once and then never again; a spring shifts twenty feet along a joint and a flume must be re-cut; a peat mat lets go and takes a path with it. These are dated and remembered. There is not much else to date.

Stand at the rim at last light, when the cloud thins and the whole wet shelf ignites gold. Below, past the falls, past the drop, there is more country — dim, blue, layered with cloud, presumably as young and as still. Nobody has been down. There is no reason to go down, and nothing down there wants anything up here.

Behind you the town drains, quietly, into the evening, at the rate it has always drained.
