## The Young Range at First Light

Nothing here has finished happening.

Start with the air, because the air is what you notice first — how little of it there is, and how much room it has. At this height the atmosphere is a thin grey solution, half water, and it holds the light the way frosted glass holds it: everywhere, from no particular direction, dim enough that shadows are only suggestions. The sun is up somewhere behind eleven kilometres of cloud deck. What arrives is a colourless daylight, blue-shifted, about the brightness of a room lit by a north window in November. It will not get much brighter. By midafternoon it will be dimmer.

Below that light: a valley thirty kilometres long that does not yet know it is a valley.

---

**The rock.**

The walls are new. Not new in the sense of clean — new in the sense of *unfinished*. This limestone was seafloor. You can walk up a ramp at fifty-two hundred metres and find, in a slab tilted on edge, the coiled sections of shells that lived in warm shallow water, now crushed into a stone that is being lifted four millimetres a year and does not have time to weather.

That is the central fact of the place: uplift is outrunning decay. The mountains are rising faster than the world can take them apart. So the rock stands in forms that are structural rather than sculptural — bedding planes still legible as bedding planes, joint sets meeting at their original angles, fracture faces flat and bright and sharp-edged, with the pale unpolished look of a broken thing rather than a worn one. There are no rounded summits. There are no smooth spurs. There are no soils. Nothing has had a chance to be softened, and so the geometry of the deep earth is still showing through the surface, the way a frame shows through fresh plaster.

Colours: the wet grey-white of the limestone, going ochre where iron seeps out of it. Bands of a dark green schist lower down, slick as a wet blackboard. Nothing red, nothing rusted, no long-oxidised patina — that takes millennia and there haven't been any.

---

**The water.**

The mountain sweats.

It carried a sea's worth of water up inside its fractures, and it is still full — a perched, fractured, high aquifer, pressurised, leaking. Walk the base of any wall and you will find spring lines: horizontal seeps where a bedding plane meets the air and simply weeps, all day, all night, in a hundred separate threads too fine to make noise. The rock is dark and glistening for three metres above the talus. Where the seeps freeze at night they build glassy aprons, translucent, faintly blue; where they thaw at ten in the morning the aprons detach and go down in a soft crash of glass.

Everywhere the ground is saturated. The valley floor at the toe of the ice is a cold marsh at forty-nine hundred metres — flat, grey, braided with a thousand shifting channels of milky water, cushioned with moss where the flush is steady, sucking underfoot, breathing out mist. Meltwater lakes fill in the hollows between rubble ridges: turquoise, opaque, silt-loaded, and temporary. Some drain in a single afternoon when the ice dam behind them fails, and the whole marsh reorganises itself, and by evening there are channels where there were none and the mosses that took eighty years to establish are gone under half a metre of fresh grit.

The atmosphere is at its dew point most of the day. Cloud forms *in* the valley, out of nothing, at eye level: a coherent white body sliding along a contour, dissolving where it touches sun-warmed stone. Everything you own becomes damp. Everything you look at is either wet, or ice, or about to become one.

---

**The machinery.**

Here is the thing that takes a while to see, and then cannot be unseen: this valley is a plant. Not built by anyone. But it is running, and it is running at industrial scale, and the process flow is completely legible.

The intake is atmospheric: moisture stripped from a monsoon airmass by an obstruction seven kilometres high, precipitated as snow across a catchment of nine hundred square kilometres. Delivery is continuous and enormous — call it a metre of water equivalent a year across the whole accumulation zone. The snow compacts, recrystallises, becomes ice, and the ice moves, because that much ice on that much slope cannot do anything else.

What follows is comminution. The glacier is a crushing bed forty metres deep, loaded with rock and dragged over rock, and it produces — steadily, unstoppably, at a rate of some millions of tonnes a year — a graded product: boulders, cobbles, gravel, sand, and a flour so fine it will not settle out of still water for a month. The output is sorted hydraulically at the terminus, coarse fractions dumped as ridges, fines carried off in suspension by an outflow river the colour of skimmed milk. Some of the flour is dried on the flats, lifted by afternoon wind, and exported downrange as dust, where it will fertilise forests four hundred kilometres away.

There is a freeze-thaw stage too, working the walls, day-shift and night-shift: water into fissures at noon, ice expanding at three in the morning, and every dawn a scatter of fresh quarried blocks at the foot of the cliffs. There is hydraulic mining under the ice, where pressurised meltwater cuts channels in bedrock. There is even a power supply — gravitational potential, four thousand metres of head, converted by falling water into the work of grinding a mountain into mud.

The strange part is the quiet.

You expect this much machinery to be loud. It is not. Industry at geological scale is almost silent: the ice moves at the pace of a fingernail growing, and moving ice does not shriek, it groans very occasionally, a low sound at long intervals, felt in the sternum. What you actually hear, standing in the middle of it, is the outflow river as a distant continuous hush, wind pulling around an arête, and long stretches of nothing at all — a silence so complete you can hear individual grains of sand ticking down a slope of scree. Then, three or four times an hour, from somewhere, the clatter of a rockfall you cannot locate. Then nothing again.

Nothing here is built and everything here is manufacture.

---

**The life.**

There is very little of anything, per cubic metre. That's the other governing fact. The volume is essentially empty: standing on the marsh, you can look up eleven hundred metres of clean wall and across four kilometres of ice and see, in all that space, no organism whatsoever. The place is hollow. You feel it as a kind of ringing in the attention.

And yet life is not scarce — it's *concentrated*, in films and pulses, so that the density is nearly zero and the abundance is enormous, and both facts are true at once.

The films: the surface of the glacier, in the ablation zone, carries something like a hundred thousand springtails per square metre in the meltwater grooves — dark, sesame-seed-sized, thick enough in places to look like a spill of pepper. They eat algae, and windblown pollen, and each other's dead. Feeding on them: glacier midges that walk rather than fly, and small pale jumping spiders that live at fifty-three hundred metres eating whatever the wind brings up from below, an entire predatory economy running on aerial fallout. Red snow-algae bloom on the old névé in patches the size of ponds. Black crustose lichens are the pioneers on rock less than a century exposed, growing a millimetre a decade, doing the first slow work of soil that will not exist for ten thousand years.

The pulses: at dawn, geese. Bar-headed, in skeins, coming over the col at seventy-two hundred metres in air with a third the oxygen of sea level, wingbeats visibly hard, calling to each other — a thin honking that arrives from directly overhead and passes on south in ninety seconds and leaves the sky empty again. This happens twice a year, and this is one of those weeks, and it happens all morning: hundreds, then thousands, in waves, threading the one low notch in the range.

Between the pulses: choughs, thirty or forty of them, working the updraft along a buttress for the sheer pleasure of it, glossy black, the only genuinely noisy things in the valley. A lammergeier at the top of the airspace, a slow crossbow shape, going somewhere else. Snowfinches nesting in a rockfall cavity. Down at the valley mouth where the moss becomes something like turf, twenty blue sheep in a line, grazing cushion-plants and saxifrage, alert; and above them, on a shelf, a wolf lying down with its chin on its paws, doing arithmetic.

---

**The rules.**

Uplift beats erosion. Every landform you see is younger than the idea of a landform.

Water is never absent and never still and never warm.

There is no soil, so vegetation lives in cracks, in flushes, and on stone; and everything that eats, eats something the wind delivered.

Nothing has been made. Everything is being made.

And the whole apparatus — intake, crushing, sorting, export — runs in a dim grey light, in near-total silence, with almost nothing in it, at a temperature just above freezing, in a valley that in a hundred thousand years will be beautiful, and is currently only raw.
