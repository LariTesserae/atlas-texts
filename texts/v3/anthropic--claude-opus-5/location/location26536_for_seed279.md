## The Chord Flats

You hear it a day before you see it. Not as sound at first — as a wrongness in the air, a pressure behind the sternum, the sensation of standing near a large animal breathing in a dark room. Then, coming down off the old terraces, it resolves: a low sustained tone, several tones, layered, with slower waves rolling through them where the pitches beat against each other. By the time the basin opens below you it is loud enough that if you speak you will not hear yourself.

The basin is perhaps thirty kilometres across and shallow, a saucer rather than a bowl, floored in pale grey-white stone. Cloud lies on it in a flat lid at maybe two hundred metres, so the whole place is lit as if an hour before sunrise, permanently, and the brightest thing in view is the ground.

### The floor

The stone is carbonate, freshly made and still making itself. It has the texture of cauliflower and boiled milk skin, blistered, lobed, crusted with crystal that will open the pad of your thumb if you drag it. Nothing here is rounded. Nothing has had time. Break a lobe and the inside is chalk-soft and wet, and it hardens over a season into something that rings when struck.

Out of this floor come the tubes.

They stand everywhere, from ankle height to four metres, hollow, walls a finger thick, mouths ragged with new growth like the rims of thrown pots. They are spaced a hand's width to a stride apart, and they cover the entire basin: not a grove, not a field, a *saturation*. Standing among them you can never see more than twenty or thirty metres in any direction, because the tubes crowd and overlap and the mist collects between them. Above knee height the space is nothing but pale vertical shafts, and below knee height it is nothing but their bases fusing into drapery, terraces, small rimmed pools, and the flat coral-like sheets that link one tube to the next. Density is the fact of the place. There is no volume of air here without three or four mouths in it, and each mouth is exhaling.

That is what they are: mouths. Beneath the basin the crust is young, fractured, still settling, and an aquifer under pressure comes up through it carrying dissolved lime and a great deal of gas. Each fracture that reaches the surface builds its own chimney out of what it carries, and each chimney sings, because the gas leaves faster than the water and must whistle to get out.

### The sound

It is not a roar. Roaring implies chaos. This is a chord — an enormous, stable, unhurried chord, produced by some millions of pipes each holding whatever note its own length gives it.

Close to a large tube the note is a physical object. You feel it in the jaw and the small bones of the ear, and the mist visibly organises itself into bands over the mouth, standing waves made visible. In the shallow pools the water shivers into fixed rosettes and stays there. Put your ear against the wall of a tube and it will hurt; put your palm there and you will feel the stone working, a fine sandy vibration, which is the sound slowly doing the same thing to the tube that a river does to a valley.

The chord is loud but it is not violent. There are no crashes. Nothing here happens suddenly. Over an afternoon, as the pressure lid rises and falls, the beating between pitches slows and quickens, and the whole basin seems to breathe through a set of intervals that are almost harmonious and never resolve. Occasionally a tube clears its throat — a slug of water lifted and spat, a wet gulp, a half-second of a wholly different voice — and then rejoins. Once a season, somewhere, a tube grown too tall for its walls fails, and there is a crack like ice and a long descending sigh as the pipe unlearns its note.

Because sound fills the place, sight goes second. The eye is nearly useless anyway in this light and this crowding. The ear does the work. Every part of the basin has its own pitch signature, its own local chord, and you learn the flats as a map of pitch: the drone-quarter, where the tubes are tallest and slowest; the wasp-ground, thickets of tubes no higher than the shin, all of them shrill; the long seam of stopped mouths where the fracture beneath has silted and there is a corridor of near-quiet four kilometres long, which is the only road anyone has ever used across the middle.

### Water

Water is everywhere and nowhere deep. The table sits a finger's width below the crust; step firmly and your footprint fills. Between the tubes lie thousands of rimmed pools the size of a cupped hand, and larger ones the size of a room, all of them still, all of them very clear, all of them lined with fresh crystal so that they look enamelled. They are cool. Everything is cool: the air, the stone, the water, the exhalation. Only directly at a mouth is the gas a degree warmer than the air, and that degree is enough to keep the basin under permanent condensation, which is why the cloud lid never lifts and the light never comes.

The whole place is therefore always freshly rained on without it ever raining. Every surface weeps. The tubes are slick and beaded. Your clothes are wet within the hour and never dry, and you learn that being wet and cool is simply the condition of being here, the way elsewhere one is dry and warm.

On the northern lip the basin floods out into a shallow lagoon, and the tubes go on underwater, and there the sound changes completely: the singing stops, and instead the surface is covered from shore to horizon with columns of rising bubbles, hundreds of thousands of them, a hissing seething white noise, silver in that dim light. That is the loudest and simplest part of the place, and the least loved.

### Light, and things that shine

Twice I have seen a tube-mouth flicker. Certain crystals at the rim, when the stone is working hardest, give off a faint blue-green light in time with the note — very weak, visible only when you have been in the dim for hours, so that a tall pipe seems to have a slow cold pulse at the top of it. Whole quarters of the flats do this in a bad wind. There is no reason to be frightened of it and everyone is.

Otherwise the only light is the ground's own paleness, and at night that goes too, absolutely, because there is no wood in this world. Nothing grows here. There is no fuel, so there is no fire, so there has never been fire, so no one who lives here has cooked or seen a flame or made metal or made anything hot. Night is not dark. Night is deletion, with the chord still going.

### The living

Almost nothing.

Grey-brown films in the still pools, feeding on the gas and the sulphur, slick as spit, not green anywhere. On those films, in some pools but not others, pale flat crawlers the size of a fingernail, blind, ancient in design, grazing. That is the base and nearly the whole of it.

Above them: rarely, a *listener*. They stand a metre and a half at the shoulder, four-legged, hairless, skin the colour of wet chalk, with a broad shovelled head and no visible eye — only a wide flat plate of a face that they hold against tube-mouths, presumably feeling the note, presumably drinking the spat water. You may go a fortnight and see none. Then one is standing eight metres away in the mist, unmoved, and has probably been there since before you arrived.

Where a listener walks, tubes break. A herd of them crossing the drone-quarter over a summer will snap two hundred pipes and silence a chord that has held since before anyone's grandmother, and the fracture beneath will then spend forty years building the tubes back, and the note will come in slightly lower than before. The animals are a geological process with legs. They are the fastest thing here by an order of magnitude, and they move like something being poured.

### The scattered work of hands

People have been here. Not many, not now, not with tools worth the name.

There are low walls, waist-high, built of broken tube-plates stacked flat without mortar, running for a hundred metres and then stopping — windbreaks, or fences for nothing, or the edges of ownership. There are cairns at intervals along the quiet seam, each with a smooth stone on top that has been handled so often it shines.

There are the *silences*: circles ten or fifteen metres across where every tube was snapped off level with the floor and the stubs plastered over with soft chalk, which then set. Inside one you can hear a companion speak. The chord is still there but it has become a wall around you rather than a thing inside you, and after a week on the flats the effect of stepping into a silence is so strong that people sit down. Most silences have a low bench, and the bench is worn.

There are five or six beehive huts, no taller than a crouching person, built of the same plates, corbelled inward, with the door always facing away from the local dominant note.

There are stopped mouths, tubes plugged with a fist of clay and a flat stone, which is how a family tunes the ground around its hut. Everyone understands that a stopped mouth is somebody's decision. You may not stop a mouth unless you can name what the chord will be afterwards, and you may not unstop one at all, ever, because someone chose that quiet and may still be listening to it.

And there are handprints, thousands of them, pressed into the soft new carbonate of tube walls at the height of a standing adult, hardened now, fossil-hard, small ones and large ones, some layered over each other three deep where the stone kept growing and a hand kept coming back. Nobody living does this. Everyone living knows exactly what it means, and they will not say.

### The rule of the place

The flats do not permit speech, so nothing is agreed by argument. Touch does the work: a palm on the back of the wrist, a knuckle to the sternum, a hand laid flat over another hand for yes. Direction is not north and south but *uppitch* and *downpitch*, and children are taught the basin as a melody before they are allowed to walk in it.

And the last rule, which is the shape of the whole world here, is that the note is falling.

Every tube lengthens by about the width of a hair each year. A longer pipe sounds lower. So each mouth, over centuries, sags in pitch, and the great chord of the basin has been descending since it first opened — very slowly, imperceptibly, catastrophically. The old terraces on the rim are dead stumps, silent, weathered, and they were the high voices; they finished. The wasp-ground will be the drone-quarter in some thousands of years, and the drone-quarter will by then be so low it is only pressure.

People here know this the way people elsewhere know about winter. They say the flats are one enormous animal exhaling one enormous note, and that when the note drops below hearing the exhalation will be finished, and the water will stop coming up, and the whole pale crowded singing basin will stand there hardening in the mist with nothing in it, wet, cool, dim, and finally quiet.

They do not think of this as an ending. They think of it as the pitch.
