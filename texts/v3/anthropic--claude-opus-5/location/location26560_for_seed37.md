## The Pocket

Nine metres down, in a body of pale limestone that has not yet agreed to be a cliff, there is a room that is not finished being a room.

It is about the size of a small chapel, or a large larder — you could cross it in seven steps if the steps behaved. The ceiling is low enough at the eastern end that you would stoop, and open enough at the western end that something could nest up there, and something does. The overall shape is that of a lung, or of a bubble caught in cooling glass, and in fact it is a bubble: an air pocket in stone, at the exact age where a bubble is deciding whether to become a cave.

Nothing dug it. Water is doing that, but slowly, and mostly later.

---

**The stone.**

The floor is not rock yet. It has the consistency of hard cheese, or of a wheel of chalk left in a cellar — pale grey-cream, cool, faintly greasy to touch. Press a thumb in and it takes the print and keeps it. In four hundred years someone will find that print and it will be flint-hard and they will argue about it.

The walls are bedded in strata, thin bands of ochre and bone and a single startling seam of oxblood red — and the red one *moves*. Not fast. Over the course of a morning it will migrate a hand's breadth upward through the beds, sliding through the other layers like a shadow behind a curtain, then settle, then reconsider. The strata here have not yet fixed their order. They are still being laid down, and the past is not finished arriving.

At the north wall there is a fold that hasn't happened. You can see where it will be: the beds bunch and pucker slightly, prefiguring a crumple that requires a mountain-building event still some tens of millions of years out. The stone is bracing.

---

**The light.**

This is the strangest thing, and everything else in the pocket has arranged itself around it.

The pocket is *bright*. Not glowing, not phosphorescent, not the soft blue of ice or fungus — bright the way a whitewashed room is bright at eleven in the morning with a north window open. A clean, even, slightly cool daylight, plenty to read by, more than enough to see the veining in the rock and the individual hairs on a moth.

There is no source. There is no shaft, no crack to the surface, no seam of luminous mineral. The light does not come *from* anywhere; it simply is here, in the same way that the temperature is here — nine degrees, always, damp and steady.

The best explanation, and it is not a good one, is that this stone was once seabed, and seabed is a shallow thing, a lit thing, and the rock has not yet accepted that it has been buried. The light is a memory that hasn't been corrected.

Shadows in the pocket lag. Move your hand and its shadow arrives on the wall a moment behind, then over-shoots slightly, then settles. The shadows of the animals are the most honest clocks in the place.

---

**The water.**

A single seep runs the length of the ceiling crack. It gathers, it hangs, it falls: one drop every eleven seconds, tirelessly, into a puddle in the floor's lowest dish.

The puddle is the width of a spread hand. It is deeper than the pocket is tall. You can look down into it and the light goes down with you, illuminating a shaft of clear cold water that continues past any reasonable floor, past the limestone, into a dark green that has no bottom. Something lives down there. It comes up sometimes — a pale eel-shape as long as your arm, which cannot possibly fit in the puddle and does, surfacing, taking a breath it does not need, and sinking away. It has been doing this for a while. It is patient with the geometry.

Elsewhere: condensation. Every surface wears a film of it. Every eleven seconds, somewhere in the pocket, a bead of it gathers enough weight to run, and leaves a bright track through the dust, and the dust closes over the track within the hour. This is the whole erosive economy of the place, and in eighty thousand years it will have carved a hall.

Twice a day — the pocket has days, though nothing here has ever seen a sun — a low fog forms at ankle height, sits for two hours, and lifts. It smells of wet slate and, faintly, of cut grass, which is a smell that should not be available nine metres down.

---

**The quiet.**

The drip is silent.

You watch it fall. You watch it strike the puddle and make its ring. There is no sound. Then, perhaps forty minutes later, standing across the room, you hear a single clear *plink* with nothing to have caused it.

This is the work of the glasswrights. They are grey, translucent, eight-legged, about the size of a walnut, and they string their webs across every corner and interval of the pocket — filaments so fine that you find them with your face. The webs do not catch insects. They catch sound. A footfall, a breath, a drip: the noise strikes the filament, snags, hangs there as a faint pearl of tension, and the glasswright comes and drinks it, or bundles it, or — if the web is disturbed — lets it go, late and displaced and slightly out of tune.

There are perhaps four hundred glasswrights in the pocket. The result is a silence so complete it has texture, like felt against the ears, punctuated at random by orphaned noises: your own cough, released an hour after you swallowed it; a footstep from the far side of the room where no one is; occasionally something much older, a low grinding, the sound of the rock folding, which has not happened yet and which some patient web has caught in advance.

---

**The fauna.**

Despite all this, or because of it, the pocket is *crowded*. It is one of the most populous rooms in the district.

**Sifters** — thumb-sized, six-legged, the colour of milk — graze the soft floor in loose herds of thirty or forty, ingesting the unset limestone and excreting it as tiny clear crystals, which accumulate in drifts along the walls like sugar. There is a low bank of this stuff a foot high in the west end. It sparkles savagely in the sourceless light. The sifters are why the floor will eventually be a floor: they are chewing it into hardness.

**Velvet moths**, wingspan of a fingernail, entirely without scales — matte, dark, absorbent. They roost in the high western vault in a carpet of about nine thousand and they are the only genuinely black thing here. They fly at fog-time, silently, and they do not eat, and no one has determined what they are for.

**Chalk shrews**, blind, quick, five of them, territorial and furious, patrolling the crystal bank. They kill sifters by the dozen and eat perhaps one. They are the only animal in the pocket that is ever in a hurry.

**The glasswrights**, as described, farming the silence.

**The eel in the puddle.**

And, on the ceiling near the seep, a colony of small pale **swallows** — genuinely swallows, genuinely feathered, birds of open air and summer evenings, nesting in mud cups nine metres underground with nowhere to fly to and no insects to hawk. They do not seem distressed. They come and go through a gap in the crack that leads only to more stone. Nobody has followed them.

---

**The plants.**

Sparse, but present, and each one an argument.

A single hart's-tongue fern in the north seam, dark green, glossy, thriving. A palm of moss on the wet lip above the puddle. One blade of meadow grass growing out of the floor near the threshold stone, eleven centimetres tall, bending in a draught that does not exist.

And in the exact centre of the pocket, in a handful of grit, an oak seedling. Two true leaves. It has been two true leaves for a long time. There is no room for it to become anything else and it does not appear to be trying.

A lichen grows on the light itself — visible only edge-on, as a faint mottling in the air, feeding on brightness. When it is thick, the pocket dims very slightly, and the shadows catch up.

---

**The marks of people.**

There are no people. There have never been people. Nevertheless:

At the eastern, low end, set into the floor, there is a **threshold stone** — a slab of harder grey rock, dressed square, worn into a deep smooth saddle at its middle by the passage of many thousands of feet. It leads nowhere. There is no doorway above it and no wall around it. The wear is real and the feet are still to come.

Beside it, a **cairn of seven stones**, stacked with care, sized in order.

Halfway up the north wall, a row of **tally marks in chalk** — forty-one strokes, grouped in fives, the last group incomplete. They are not weathering. They are getting *clearer*.

On the crystal bank, a small **clay lamp**, unglazed, unfired, its wick dry and unlit, holding a thimbleful of oil that has never evaporated.

And near the fern, a single flake of flint with one worked edge. It may be accidental. It has three retouch scars along it, which is two more than accident usually manages.

These objects are not old. They are early. The pocket is running its erosion and its history in the same direction as its rock: forward, but not from the beginning. It has the wear of use and the innocence of never having been used, and it holds both without difficulty, the way a room can be both empty and expectant.

---

**The rules.**

1. Sound is a substance, and it is farmed. Anything you say will be repurposed.
2. Light does not travel here; it is simply local, like weather. It will run out eventually. In the deep future the pocket will be dark and will not remember why this seems wrong.
3. Water is the only agent of change with any real authority. Everything else is decoration or livestock.
4. Wear precedes use.
5. Nothing here is finished. This is not decay running backwards; it is a slower thing, a *setting*, like plaster, like an argument reaching its terms.
6. Count the walls twice and you will get different answers. This is not a trick. The pocket has not settled on its shape and is trying options.

---

**What it is doing.**

Being made.

There are, in this district of limestone, several thousand pockets like this at various stages, none of them connected, none aware of the others. Most will close. Some will merge and become passages and then chambers and then, in due course, a cave system with a name and a gift shop and a handrail.

This one is bright, and quiet, and full of animals, and has a doorstep worn by people who will not exist for a long time. It is nine degrees. Every eleven seconds a drop of water falls without a sound, and somewhere else in the room, at an unrelated moment, a small clear noise arrives to no one.

The red seam slides a little further up the wall.

The oak keeps its two leaves.
