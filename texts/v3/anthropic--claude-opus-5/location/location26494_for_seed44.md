## The Culm Works

**Where it sits**

Twelve metres above the sea, and the sea forty kilometres off, which in this delta means nothing: the water table stands a hand's breadth under the topsoil, and in the fifth month it stands above it. The whole works occupies a single low terrace of grey clay, an old river-bend deposit about four hundred metres long, held between two sloughs that were once one river and are now two lazy arms of the same brown thought. You can walk the perimeter in eleven minutes if the boards are dry. They are not dry.

This is the entire place. There is no hinterland to speak of — beyond the sloughs the bamboo closes and does not open again for a great distance, and nobody here has much to say about what is out there except that it is more of the same, unmanaged.

**The vegetation, which is the architecture**

Say it plainly: there is no surface here that is not plant, or is not held by plant, or is not being slowly taken by it.

The dominant organism is a giant clumping bamboo, culms as thick as a man's thigh at the base, forty of them to a clump, rising fifteen to twenty metres before they bow. The clumps are on a fourteen-year rotation and have been for longer than the ledgers go back, so the stand is not a forest but a colonnade — old stools swollen into knuckled masses a metre and a half across, each one a socket that the works has been drawing from for generations. Between and above them, three species of fig. The figs are the masons. They germinate in the crotch of a bamboo or in the crumbling mortar of a chimney course, send down roots that flatten and braid and fuse, and after fifty years the thing they have grown around is inside them. Two of the ten buildings here have no walls of their own left. Their brick is a lining, a kind of interior render, and the load goes down through fig.

Everything else is upholstery. Liverworts in flat green scales on the north brick. A moss with red sporophytes that fruits so densely in the wet months that the handrails look rusted. Ferns in every gutter, every joint, every abandoned bolt hole; a small epiphytic orchid that grows only on cast iron, apparently indifferent to whether the iron is turning. Duckweed on the retting tanks so complete that the tanks read as lawns until a bubble breaks them. Sedge and reed at the water's edge, cut back weekly, growing back weekly. The paths are mown by feet.

The trimming is constant and it is the real work of the place, more hours logged than the machines take. Two people whose whole occupation is the canopy: they open light-wells over the drying racks and close them over the tar shed, they cut fig roots that reach for the flywheel pit, they leave the ones that have taken load. There is a rule about that, and it is not sentimental. *You may not kill a root that holds a beam.* The engineer's ledger has a column for it. Some entries are ninety years old and read simply *bearing, sound*.

**The Walk**

The single feature that organises everything: a rope walk, ninety-two metres, oriented along the terrace's long axis, roofed but not walled.

Its ridge is a live bamboo arcade. The culms on either side were bent toward each other while green — three generations of bending, the technique settled long ago — and lashed at the apex where they have thickened and locked. The result is a nave of nodes, twenty metres to the crown, with the light coming down in bars, and everything in it is green-lit and steaming. Beneath that, a plank floor on piles with a fall of one in two hundred, because water gets right of way here and always did. Beneath the floor, running its whole length, a plank-lined channel takes the drainage to the tail slough.

Down the centre, the ropes. Fibre from the retting pits, hackled and combed at the north end, spun to yarn, the yarn drawn out the length of the Walk on hooks, then twisted, then closed into strand and strand into cable by a travelling top-cart that grinds down the boards on a rail at the speed of a slow crawl. This is the loudest thing. The jack-wheel at the north end runs off a belt off a line shaft off a horizontal steam engine, and the whole assembly — gear, belt, cart, rail, ninety metres of fibre under tension — makes a sound like a long throat clearing continuously, with a heartbeat in it, the top-cart's rail joints, four seconds apart. You feel it in the piles through your boot soles anywhere in the works. People here pitch their voices over it without noticing they do, and they are said to be loud elsewhere.

**Industry**

The plant is small and it is old and it is complete.

*Boiler house.* Two return-flue boilers, brick-set, fired on the year's waste — culm tops, hackling tow, spent fibre pressed into bricks and dried on the racks. The stack is nineteen metres and has a fig on it at the twelve-metre course; the fig has been there since a photograph that hangs in the office, and in the photograph it is a seedling. The stack smokes a pale smoke that does not rise so much as spread, because the air here will not take on any more water and the smoke sits in it, layered, at about roof height, drifting toward the tail slough on the terrace's own faint breath.

*Engine house.* One engine, cross-head, flywheel four metres, kept in a condition that is beyond clean and into the territory of devotion. It has a name. Its bedplate sits on a masonry block that goes down five metres to a raft of bamboo piles driven into the clay, and the piles are perfectly sound because they have never been allowed to dry. That is the fundamental physics of this place: *nothing rots that stays wet, and nothing rots that stays moving.* It is only the things at the boundary — the tide line, the half-buried, the seasonally exposed — that fail. All the ruin here is at that line, a horizontal band of spalled brick and crumbled iron at roughly knee height along every wall, as if the works had been dipped.

*Retting.* Six tanks, concrete, warm — they run at blood heat off the boiler's exhaust steam and they have run at blood heat for a very long time. This is where the fibre is freed from the culm by controlled bacterial rot. It smells. The smell is the ground note of the whole terrace: sulphurous, sweetish, vegetal, not exactly foul but absolutely insistent, and it is why nobody's clothes ever quite smell of anything else. The tank water leaves black with tannin and lignin and takes the pH of the tail slough down with it.

*Electricity.* A small dynamo on the line shaft, carbon-filament lamps strung on insulators screwed into fig trunks, and the fig has grown around the insulators so that the wires appear to come out of the wood. Light after dark is amber, sparse, and doubled everywhere in standing water. Sixty lamps for the whole works.

**Water**

Everything drips. The bamboo condenses; the fig leaves shed in single fat drops that hit tin and boards at a rate that never resolves into rhythm. Rain, when it comes, is a wall of white noise on the canopy that arrives thirty seconds before the water does, filtered and slowed, so that inside the Walk it rains long after it has stopped raining outside. The rock here is not rock — it is clay, and the few stone elements, the sluice cheeks and the engine block, weep continuously along their joints and grow their own moss beards. Steps have a runnel worn down their middles and a slick of algae in the runnel; you learn to walk on the outer edge, and everyone does, so the treads are worn hollow at the sides as well.

**Fauna, or its absence**

This is the thing visitors notice and cannot place. No birdsong. The air is dense, hot, green, absolutely full of life, and it is nearly silent of animals.

The tannin and the retting liquor have made the sloughs a poor place to be a fish for a long time. The clumping bamboo flowers rarely and hosts little. There are ants — two species, one that tends nothing and one that tends a scale insect on the figs. There is a translucent shrimp in the tail-race below the sluice, in numbers, feeding on what the tanks send down, and it is the only abundance. A grey heron comes perhaps every ninth or tenth day, stands on the sluice cheek in the machine noise for forty minutes, takes shrimp, and goes. People stop to watch it. There are rats in the tow store, one lineage, dark and unafraid. A single tokay-type gecko lives in the engine house and has for years — the same one, they say, though it cannot be — and it barks at dusk, three or four times, and it is the loudest animal in the works and the only one anybody would recognise as a voice.

Otherwise the sound is: engine, belt, cart, water, and the bamboo. The bamboo is not a small contributor. Culms rub at their nodes when the air moves and produce a squeal like a badly hung gate, and in gusts a whole clump will knock hollowly against itself, a wooden chattering high up, and this is the noise that people who leave here say they miss.

**Rate of change**

Nothing here happens fast, and nothing here has happened fast for as long as anyone can reconstruct. The rotation is fourteen years. The engine has been rebored twice. The fig on the stack is at the twelve-metre course and moving up at perhaps eight centimetres a decade, and there is an ongoing disagreement, conducted at intervals of a generation, about whether it will one day have to come down, which it will not, because the rule holds and it is bearing. The tail slough is a hand's width shallower each decade with tank silt; the head slough a hand's width deeper. Two of the ten buildings are, in the strictest sense, gone — dissolved into their own supporting figs — and are still in use, still swept, still lit, still warm.

Equilibrium is not the same as stillness. The works and the vegetation are pushing against each other with almost exactly equal force, and have been for so long that the shape of the pressure has become the shape of the place: mown paths, cut light-wells, tarred cuts, bearing roots, a nave of bent culms holding ninety-two metres of rope under tension in a green half-light that steams, and the long clearing of a throat that goes on and on and does not stop.
