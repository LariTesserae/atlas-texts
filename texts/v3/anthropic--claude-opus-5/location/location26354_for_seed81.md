## The Kettle

The basin sits at three thousand metres, a bowl of pale altered rock scooped into the shoulder of a range whose peaks you never see. You never see them because the basin has a lid. Not cloud exactly — cloud implies weather that arrived from elsewhere. This is the basin's own breath, held.

Underneath, a few hundred metres down, there is a body of magma that has been cooling for longer than anything here has had a name. It has not cooled much. Groundwater reaches it, flashes to steam, rises through a thousand fractures, meets the thin cold air of the high altitude, and falls again as rain. Rain soaks in. Reaches the heat. Rises. The whole place is a single closed circuit standing on end, eight kilometres wide, and it has been turning over like this at a steady, unhurried pace for a very long time.

The floor is hot enough that you feel it through boot soles as a pressure rather than a temperature, a hand pushing up. Air at the floor sits around forty-two degrees and is saturated; you sweat and the sweat does not go anywhere, so you learn to stand still and drink. Two hundred metres up, on the terraces, it is cooler by a little. At the rim, where the light comes in sideways under the lid, there is frost on the north-facing stone every night of the year. The engine of this place is that difference, and the city was built to sit inside the difference like a hand in a glove.

## Ground

There are no plants. Not stunted ones, not lichen crusts, none. The reasons are stacked: the ground is too hot at root depth, the water is acid enough to strip enamel, and the rain that would carry a spore in is manufactured on site and carries nothing.

What there is instead: sinter. White, faintly translucent, laid down layer on layer wherever hot water has run and cooled, so that the lower basin is a landscape of terraces like flooded rice paddies gone to bone. Rimstone lips a few centimetres high hold pools the colour of skimmed milk. Walk on them and they ring, then crack, then hold. Under the white there are seams of sulfur so yellow they look lit from within, and bloom-fields of iron oxide, and kaolin clay so pure and soft it takes a fingerprint and keeps it.

And on the warm wet stone, in the shallows, a film. Colourless, about the thickness of a fingernail's worth of grease, slightly iridescent, sliding away from your touch as one piece. It is doing chemistry with sulfur and iron. Whether to call it alive is a question the place does not answer and does not seem to consider interesting. In the hottest pipes there are filaments — long, pale, moving slightly against the flow — and the local word for them, sintered onto a maintenance plate near the north outfall, is *hair*.

## The city

It stands over all of this on legs.

Understand first that it has almost no mass. It reads as urban — dense, layered, organised, an infrastructure that goes on past the point where you can hold its plan in your head — and yet you could carry a cubic metre of it under one arm. It is built of mesh, screen, lattice, tube, and thin plate. Its walls are woven wire hung on frames. Its floors are grating. Its towers are open-work cylinders forty metres high with nothing inside but cold air falling and hot air climbing past each other in the dark. A city with the mass of a birdcage, and the same acoustics.

This is because it is a condenser. Every surface is a surface for water to form on. The screens are hung at the calculated angles; the gutters at the base of every wall are worn concave and lined with white rind; the streets are not streets but aqueducts with catwalks along their sides. Water is the product. Everything else is scaffolding for water.

The districts are spread very far apart. This is the thing that most disagrees with the word *city*. You stand at a junction on the second terrace and there is a condenser tower a kilometre off to the west, and another two kilometres beyond it, and between them nothing but catwalk, pipe, and the white ground going on. The intervals are deliberate: each tower needs its own volume of undisturbed air to work. So the settlement is enormous and hollow, an organisation of emptiness, and you spend forty minutes walking between one arrangement of structure and the next with nothing but the sound of your own grating underfoot.

The Cold Face is the main works. It is a north-facing cliff of the inner rim, eleven hundred metres of it, in permanent shadow, and the vapour of the whole basin condenses on it in sheets. The builders dressed it: cut flutes into the rock, hung a hundred thousand square metres of screen on it, gathered every runnel into troughs and every trough into headers. It weeps continuously. You can hear it from a kilometre away as a single unbroken hiss, and standing at its base you are rained on by a cliff, cold water on hot skin, the only relief in the basin.

## Machinery

Industrial, and slow, and almost silent, and nobody runs it.

Nothing here burns anything. There is no need — the heat is already under the floor, and the cold is already at the rim, and any machine placed between them will move on its own. So: thermosiphon loops the size of bridges, water going up one leg and down the other forever. Bimetallic strips two metres long acting as valve gear, bending in the afternoon, straightening at night, opening and closing sluices on a daily rhythm without a hand touching them. Rows of hot-air engines with flywheels that turn once every four seconds, driving thermoelectric stacks that light the lamps.

And the knockers. Pulse pumps — a slug of steam admitted into a water column, collapsing, and the water hammer lifting a portion of the flow up to the next terrace. Each one thumps roughly every forty seconds. There are perhaps three hundred of them across the basin, all slightly out of phase, so what you actually hear is a soft irregular knocking from various distances, like something in a wall in another room in another house. Days go by and you never hear two together.

The lamps: dim, orange, filament, most of them dead, the living ones in no pattern, hung on the lattice at intervals that were probably once regular. They come up in the late afternoon when the temperature differential peaks and the stacks surge, and they fade through the night as the basin equalises. The brightest hour is dusk. The brightest thing at any hour is not the lamps but the ground, in a few places, where a crack lets you see far enough down: a dull cherry glow, and above it the air ripping upward.

## The overhead lake

Here is the rule that takes the longest to accept.

Rain forms under the lid, high up. It falls. Two hundred metres above the basin floor it meets the column of heat coming off the ground and stops falling. It hangs. Drops shrink and reform and hang, and the layer of them thickens, and by mid-morning there is a stratum of suspended water twelve to thirty metres deep, spread flat across the whole basin, shining faintly, shifting like something looked at through wobbling glass.

So the streets are mostly dry, and there is a lake overhead, and it is held up by nothing but heat.

At intervals — a vent silts up, a pool crusts over, the column falters somewhere — a patch of it collapses. This is called a fall. You get about nine seconds of warning: the light changes, the knocking stops, and there is a sound like a held breath let go. Then forty degrees of water in a mass, for anything from a minute to an hour, and one district under half a metre of it, and the aqueducts running over their lips. Then the ground reheats, the column re-establishes, the layer knits itself back together from below, and by evening the overhead lake is whole again and the district is steaming and slightly whiter than it was.

## What the water leaves

Nothing here rots. Everything here petrifies.

Silica-saturated water touching anything cool deposits on it. Over months, a rind. Over years, a shape. So the basin is not a ruin — it is a cast. There is a chair on the fourth terrace that was wood and is now white stone with the grain still legible in it. There is a coil of rope like a coil of bone. There is a glove on a railing, and the railing has grown into the glove, and inside the glove there is nothing at all, which you can verify, and which does not help.

The plumbing does this to itself. Pipes narrow with rind until they burst; the burst weeps and the weep sinters over and re-seals, thicker and crooked; and after long enough, the pipe run is no longer a pipe run but a swollen, knuckled white thing following approximately the original line, still carrying water, repaired ten thousand times by the substance that broke it. This is the balance the place has reached. It builds itself at the same rate it destroys itself, and the form it has settled into — bulbous, ropy, weirdly anatomical — is the honest shape of that arrangement. It looks less designed every year and works no worse.

## The living

Rare, and specific.

In the warm pools: a flat pale crustacean thing, thumbnail-sized, eyeless, grazing the film. Dense in a few pools, absent from all the others for no reason you can find.

On the wet lattice, at night: ashwings. Grey, moth-shaped, but they walk more than they fly, and what they eat is the mineral film off wire. They gather on a screen panel by the dozen and rasp at it, and that rasping is the loudest biological sound in the basin, and it is very quiet, and it stops when you are still six metres away.

And once or twice a day, high up, swifts. They come in over the rim and fly a long fast circuit through the underside of the overhead lake with their mouths open, drinking, and they leave, and they never come down, and nobody knows where they nest. They are the only things here that treat the basin as a place they pass through rather than a place they belong to, and watching them do it is the closest this landscape comes to offering you an opinion about yourself.

## Sound, finally

It is quiet. This surprises people who hear "industrial" and expect noise. But steam eats sound, and lattice does not resonate, and everything moves at the speed of thermal expansion.

What you get: the hiss of the Cold Face, always, from whichever direction the Cold Face is. The knocking of the pumps, offbeat, near and far. The drip of a hundred gutters. Grating underfoot, absurdly loud, yours. In the deep night, when the differential drops and the machines all but stop, the basin becomes quiet enough that you hear the sinter growing — a fine irregular ticking off the terraces in every direction, thousands of it, the sound of the ground getting slightly larger.

And then before dawn the lid greys, the floor heats, the flywheels pick up, the lake reassembles overhead, and the circuit turns over again, on schedule, for nobody.
