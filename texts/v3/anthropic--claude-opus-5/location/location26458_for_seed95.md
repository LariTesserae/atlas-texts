## The Warm Roof

### 1. The flatness

You come up through cold and arrive in warmth, which is the first thing that is wrong here.

The climb is ordinary — a notch between two shoulders of grey rock, air thinning, breath shortening. Then the ground stops rising. It does not level off so much as *give up*, and what opens ahead is a plain so wide that its far edge is a matter of opinion: a pale band where the land and the sky agree to stop arguing. Sixty kilometres, maybe more. In the middle distance, other tables of the same height float above their own haze, the severed tops of the same vanished range, all cut off at the same altitude as though something had been drawn across the world at four and a half thousand metres and everything above the line taken away.

And it is warm. Not sun-warm — the sun here is thin and violent, a white nail — but warm *underneath*. Sit down and the stone gives heat into your legs. Lie down and it comes up through the ribs. The plateau is the cooling root of the mountains, a granite body the size of a province that has been letting go of its last heat for eleven million years, and it has another million to go. The peaks are gone. Their fire is still here, banked and slow, a hand held under a table.

### 2. The domes

The plain is not empty. It is crowded to the point of difficulty.

It is covered, from your boots to the horizon, in low domes. Each is somewhere between the size of a loaf and the size of a haycock. Each is a shallow curve rising off the flat, and each has, around its lower margin, a fluted skirt of ridges and channels running down to the ground. They stand nearly shoulder to shoulder. In places you can walk between them; in places you must step over. They are the colour of the stone, which is the colour of old bread, and they throw crisp small shadows in the crisp hard light, and the shadows are the only reason your eye can tell that there are, in any hundred paces, perhaps four thousand of them.

Some of them are stone.

Water and warmth working together for ten million years on a granite flat with no gradient left to speak of will produce exactly this: rounded corestones weathered out of the bedrock, then grooved by dew running down them, again and again, until they become fluted domes. That is one third of what you see.

The rest are animals.

### 3. The dew

Nothing here rains, much. Perhaps nine days in a year, and then hard and briefly, water flung sideways with no cloud to speak of. The plateau's water arrives every night instead, and arrives from the air.

At sunset the thin atmosphere drops its heat immediately — the temperature falls twenty degrees in forty minutes — while the ground, warmed from below, does not fall at all. The boundary between the cold air and the warm rock becomes a shallow layer of fog about knee-deep, and it condenses on everything with a surface. It condenses most on things that are fluted, because flutes give surface area and give the drops somewhere to go.

So: every night, the whole plain sweats. Every dome, living and dead, streams quietly. The water runs down the channels of the skirts and gathers at the base, and in the base of each dome — under it, in the dark, in the warm — is a pocket of standing water no bigger than a hat. Four thousand hats per hundred paces. Ten million per plateau.

That is the water budget of this world: no rivers, no lakes, no springs. A film of moisture that appears at dusk, is drunk before dawn, and is exhaled again from ten million small warm bodies to make the next night's fog. The plateau is a closed lung. It has been breathing the same water since before there were mountains here to lose.

### 4. The green

Vegetation is a minority opinion.

On the north-facing flute of each dome, in the groove where the water lingers longest, there is a thread of black-green crust — an alga, or a lichen, or something in between that has not bothered to become either. It is perhaps two millimetres thick and it grows two millimetres in a human lifetime. It is the base of every food chain here and it is nearly indestructible; it will not burn, it does not rot, and it is still doing photosynthesis three years after being scraped off and left in a sealed jar in the dark.

Between the domes, in the grit, there are cushion plants the size of fists, hard as knuckles, with grey hairs that comb the fog. Where three or four have grown together they form a mat you could sit on and would not want to; there is nearly always something living underneath.

There are no trees on the plateau surface. There are trees in the wells.

### 5. The quiet fauna

The animals do not make noise, and this is the second thing that is wrong.

There are more animals per hectare here than in a coral reef. There are so many that "how many" stops being a useful question and you begin instead to think in terms of biomass and packing, of how many kilograms of slow warm life can be stacked into a volume before it becomes a single organism by accident. Turn over any slab and there is a mass of pale, flattened, patient things beneath it, all of them touching. The grazing herds — if that is the word for an aggregation that moves one body-length per hour and takes a season to cross a valley — number in the tens of thousands and are indistinguishable, at any distance over a kilometre, from a change in the colour of the ground.

And it is quiet. You can hear your own blood.

The reason is water. To make a sound you must move air across something wet, and here the air is thin and thirsty and every breath spent on noise is moisture lost. Vocalisation is a luxury of wet lowlands. On the Roof it was priced out of the market a long time ago, and everything that speaks, speaks another way: by touch, by posture, by the slow raising and lowering of a fluted skirt, by the release of scent so precise and so local that you can walk through an argument without knowing it. The big grazers communicate by leaning on each other. Whole conversations, conducted at the speed of pressure.

The third wrong thing takes longer to notice.

They are all the same shape. Not similar — the same. A shallow dome, a fluted margin, a soft dark underside. Under the shells and the fleeces and the chitin they are radically unrelated: there are molluscs here, and arthropods, and things with warm blood and vestigial fur, and at least two lineages that no one has managed to place at all. Every one of them has arrived at the identical body: the optimal dew-catcher, the optimal heat-shedder, the optimal low profile in a place with no cover and a wind that never stops and never rises. Convergence has run to completion. Evolution on the Warm Roof finished its work, sat back, and has had nothing to add for four million years.

This is why the domes of stone and the domes of flesh are the same shape. The same physics carved both. The rock is doing, over geological time, what the animals did over evolutionary time, and they have met in the middle and now stand together in a crowd, indistinguishable, at rest.

You learn to walk here by watching for the slight difference in shadow-edge. Living domes have a hairline gap at the base. You learn this quickly, because the animals do not move when stepped on. They have no reflex for it. Nothing has stepped on them in four million years.

### 6. The wells

The flatness is broken, perhaps twice in a day's walk, by holes.

They are collapse features — the roof of the granite is riddled with old dissolution cavities where the last heat cooked the rock from inside — and they are round, and steep, and forty to two hundred metres across, and they go down eighty metres into shade. Warm air stands in them like water in a cup. Fog fills them at night to the brim and does not clear until nine in the morning, and so the wells are the only wet places on the plateau, and the only places where anything grows tall.

Look down into one and you see canopy: a dozen or twenty trees, thick-trunked, small-leaved, none of them more than four hundred years old and none of them, at the moment they die, ever replaced by anything different. The floor of the well is deep leaf-litter over standing water. The density down there is obscene. Things fly in the wells — the only place on the Roof where flying is worth the energy — and they fly slowly, in layers, at different heights, thousands of them, and they are silent too.

There are wells that are dry, and they are worse. In dry wells the trees are standing dead and grey and the floor is a fine white dust of ten thousand generations of small skeletons, and it is warm down there, and the dust holds a footprint for a century.

### 7. The scattered work

There are walls.

Low ones, knee-high, dry-laid from flat slabs, running in long open spirals and arcs across the plain. Some of them are four kilometres long. They are set exactly crosswise to the night wind, and their windward faces are grooved, and at the foot of each wall the crust-lichen is thick and black and healthy for a hand's breadth on either side. They are dew-catchers. They work extremely well.

Nobody knows what made them. There are no tools on the Roof; there have never been tools on the Roof; nothing here has a hand, and the stones are the size a person could carry, and the joints are careless but not random. Two possibilities are equally uncomfortable: that people came up here once, made walls with their bare hands, and went away; or that something slow and patient and shelled has been dragging slabs for a very long time and will continue to, and that the walls are a nest.

Elsewhere, more definite things. A dozen corbelled huts, scattered across a hundred kilometres, each a beehive of dry slabs with the roof still on, each with a doorway too low to enter upright and a floor of packed dust and a shallow basin cut — no, not cut; *worn* — into the stone at the centre, positioned under the hole in the apex where the night fog comes in and gives up its water. The basins are polished. The doorjambs are polished too, greasy-smooth to shoulder height, from something leaning against them a great many times.

In one hut there are bones, arranged. In three, nothing at all. In one, on the inner wall, a patch a metre square where the rock has been rubbed pale by the repeated pressure of something soft, over and over, for a period of time that must be measured in centuries. No pattern. Just the patience.

### 8. Sound

An inventory of what you will actually hear, in a day:

The tick of stone. Granite expanding through the morning and contracting after dusk, a dry irregular clicking that comes from everywhere and nowhere and stops, entirely, twice a day, at the two moments when the rock is neither warming nor cooling. Those two silences are absolute and last about ten minutes and are the reason travellers here go strange.

Drip. Constant, everywhere, from midnight to about eight, ten million points of it, which does not sound like dripping at all but like a single soft grey tone with no source.

Wind that has too little body to make a noise. It moves your hair and pushes gently on your chest and says nothing.

The herds, which are not a sound but a pressure — a feeling on the skin of the face, from very large numbers of very slow bodies displacing very thin air.

And, perhaps once in a season, at the bottom of a wet well, one long descending note from something that never comes up.

### 9. Time

The fast cycle is the night: fog, sweat, drink, dry. Twelve hours.

The middle cycle is the year: nine days of hard rain, a slight greening of the flutes, a slight thickening of the crust, and the extremely gradual migration of the herds toward whichever quarter of the plateau the rain favoured. They will arrive in about fourteen months.

The slow cycle is the heat. It is going out. Every century the plateau is a fraction of a degree cooler, and every century the night fog forms a little later and a little thinner, and every century a few more wells go dry. In three hundred thousand years the temperature differential will fail, the fog will stop, and the whole closed lung will exhale for the last time and not inhale. Everything here knows the shape it will die in, because everything here is already in it. There is no adaptation left in the system, no variation to select on, no direction to go. The Warm Roof has reached its final form and is simply holding it, in warmth and silence, until the warmth runs out.

It is not a sad place. It is a *finished* place, which is different, and rarer, and much harder to walk through without lowering your voice.

### 10. Rules

- Everything is domed, and the domes are half alive.
- Water comes from the air at night, and belongs to whatever has the best grooves.
- Sound costs water. Silence is not peace; it is thrift.
- Nothing here is in a hurry, because nothing here has anywhere further to get to.
- Do not sit on a dome with a hairline gap at its base. It will not object. That is the problem.
