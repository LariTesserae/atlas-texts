## The Works at Sowder's Bottom

### Approach

The land here has no ambition. It lies down two hundred metres below the ridge road and stays lying down, a shallow basin scooped out of clay long enough ago that its edges have gone soft as a mouth. You come at it from the north, through cut-over scrub, and the first thing you meet is not a sight but a pressure against the sternum — a low continuous *working* sound, the sound of something large exhaling through a narrow place, and under it a grinding, and under that the tick of water falling on water.

The basin is perhaps three hundred paces across. You could walk its rim in twenty minutes if the rim were walkable, which it is not, because the rim is where the growth is thickest.

### The substrate

There is no ground at Sowder's Bottom. There is only plant.

Where you expect soil there is instead a mat — three metres deep in the middle, according to the one probe-hole anybody ever cut — of living and half-living tissue: root laced through root, rhizome bedded on rhizome, the whole thing warm to the touch and faintly springing back under a boot like the flank of an animal. Step and the impression fills with water. Step again and the first impression is gone, closed, healed. The mat drinks, holds, and slowly presses; nothing that falls here rots in the ordinary way. It is *incorporated*. A branch that comes down in April is a pale strand in the weave by autumn, still recognisable, being drawn inward.

Above the mat: columns. Above the columns: a ceiling.

The columns are drivewood, and there are about forty of them in the central ring, each a hollow laminated pillar the thickness of a hogshead, banded like a coiled cable, the fibre wound in a visible left-hand spiral from base to crown. They do not branch until eight metres up, where they let out a single tier of horizontal blades. The blades interlock across the ring and knit into a roof of overlapping fronds that admits a great deal of light, but only after the light has been through six or seven layers of green and has agreed to be green itself.

So the hollow is bright. This surprises people who have heard about it. It is bright the way the inside of a bottle is bright.

### The engine

The ring turns.

All forty drivewood columns stand on one continuous root — a torus, a great living wheel-rim buried a metre down — and the torus revolves against a bed of gritted rhizome studded with silica teeth. It makes one full revolution in a little under fourteen months. You cannot see the motion. You can measure it: scratch a mark on a column, mark the wall behind it, come back after a fortnight and the column has moved the width of your two hands.

What turns it is water and heat and patience. On the sunward arc of the ring the columns transpire hard in the warmth and their tissue tightens; on the shaded arc they take up seep from the basin wall and swell. The whole ring is therefore always slightly shorter on one side than the other, always leaning, always falling forward into its own lean, and the silica bed grinds beneath it. That grinding is the deep note you felt in the sternum from the ridge road. It never stops. It has not stopped, by the tree-count in the wall growth, for something like four hundred years.

Between the columns run the belts: strap-vines, flat as harness leather, three fingers wide, stretched from a boss on one trunk to a boss on the next. They are hygroscopic. In the morning, with the condensation on them, they hang slack in shining curves. By noon they have dried and drawn tight and begun to sing — a hard, thin, humming pitch, forty different pitches, a tuned rack of them. They transmit the ring's turning inward, to the middle, to the press.

And the bellows. Set into the mat all around the inner ring are the pod-lungs, low grey-green sacs the size of a hay-cock, each one a fermentation chamber where the mat's harvested surplus is broken down by something anaerobic and enthusiastic. Gas accumulates. The sac swells, over about nine hours, from slack hide to a taut drum. When the internal pressure passes a threshold a sphincter of woody lips at the top gives way and the whole charge is fired up through a stack of hollow reeds two storeys tall.

This is the loudest thing in the hollow, and it happens every few minutes, somewhere. It is not a bang. It is a *chord* — six to eleven seconds of enormous organ tone, a fifth and a flattened seventh and something underneath that isn't a pitch at all, ending in a wet clatter as the lips reseat. The reed-stacks are tuned by their own growth and drift out of tune over decades, so that the hollow's harmony is slowly modulating across a timescale no single listener will hear the whole of. There are stacks that have been going flat since before the Napoleonic wars.

The waste heat and the vented steam rise into the frond-ceiling, condense on the undersides, and come back down as a constant fine indoor rain, warm as bathwater, tasting faintly of yeast. That rain waters the mat. The mat feeds the pod-lungs. Nothing here needs the sky.

### The press

At the centre of the ring the belts converge on a thing that everyone who has written about Sowder's Bottom has described differently, because it is difficult.

It is roughly the shape and size of a lime-kiln, and it is made of layered lignified plates and a cabling of tendon-vine, and it is a hydraulic press. The belts drive a screw. The screw is a spiral of hardened cellulose with a thread pitch of about two centimetres, and it is being turned at a rate of one thread per nine days. Under it, in a bed of pale packing tissue, sits a chamber the size of a fist.

In the chamber, a seed is being made.

The seed takes fifty-eight to sixty-four years. Everything else in the hollow — the ring, the belts, the lungs, the drum-note and the chord and the rain — is the apparatus for delivering something over two million turns of that screw to one small object. When it is finished the seed is walnut-sized, black, and absurdly heavy: a metallic-dense knot of laminated tissue, so compressed that its internal structure is glassy. The press opens. The mat parts. The seed drops through the mat, through the clay, down to whatever it is doing down there. Nobody has ever recovered one. The bay-walls of the hollow are ribbed with the healed scars of previous openings, and you can count them: nineteen, twenty, twenty-one, going up the northeast slope like the rings of a cut trunk.

Between openings, nothing changes. That is the character of the place. Enormous, ceaseless, deafening activity, arranged to produce nearly nothing, extremely slowly, and to keep producing it for as long as the water table holds.

### The built things

There was a fuller's mill here, and then a bone-black works, and the second one had a rail spur.

You find the spur first: two rails still gauged and parallel, running eleven metres out of the north wall of growth and stopping, the ties gone entirely to mat, the rails themselves furred with a rusty lichen and slightly bowed upward where a root has been thinking about them since the 1880s. Twenty paces on there is a brick culvert mouth, elliptical, beautifully laid, with a date-stone: **1847**, and beneath it a mason's initials, and beneath that a hand-cut channel through which good clear water still runs out of the wall and into the mat. The mat has not blocked it. The mat has *lipped* it, grown a clean collar around the brickwork, and left it working.

And then, if you push into the west arc between two drivewood columns, you find the flywheel.

Cast iron, four metres across, six spokes, an English wheel from a beam engine, and it is turning. It is turning because a strap-vine has been laid over its rim — laid there, wound three times, in a way that is difficult to attribute to accident — and the ring is driving it, and it is doing nothing at all with the rotation. Its shaft goes into the mat and ends. Its bearing is a socket of grown wood, packed with a waxy exudate, and the socket is *maintained*: you can see the fresh pale tissue where wear has been made good.

This is the part people argue about. One reading: the plant copied the machine. Another: the machine, in its working years, established the tempos and the geometries that the growth then optimised into, so that the hollow is a fossil of an industry, running on. A third reading, which the local phrasing prefers: the works never shut. It only changed hands.

Other artifacts, scattered, incidental: a row of eleven iron hooks in a rock face, with the shoulder-seams of aprons still on three of them. A ledger, lignified through, sitting on a shelf of growth, opened and swollen into a single block — its pages are legible in cross-section as darker and lighter laminae, and someone has counted them: 412. A boot sole. A ceramic insulator. A brass nameplate reading **ROBSON & SON — MAKERS — LEEDS**, which has been absorbed to the depth of its lettering and no further, held out at reading height like a caption.

### Fauna

Almost none. This is the other thing visitors notice, after the noise: no birdsong, no midge-column over the water, no rustle in the mat.

The hollow is a closed vegetable economy and it has very little use for animals. It pollinates by pressure — the chord vents shake pollen loose and the indoor rain carries it down. It disperses by gravity and by press. It does not need to be eaten by anything to complete anything.

What lives here:

The grease-beetle. Fat, blind, the colour of old lard, about as long as a thumb joint. There are perhaps two hundred in the whole basin. They live in the bearing-sockets and the silica bed, and they secrete the wax that packs those sockets, and they eat the wear-debris. Without them the ring would seize in a decade. They are the only creature the plant has kept, and it keeps them the way a mill keeps an oiler.

A rain-frog, brown, palm-sized, heard rather than seen — three or four notes in an afternoon, from up in the frond-ceiling where the condensation pools. Nobody knows how many. Possibly nine. Possibly one, very old.

Something that has left tracks twice in a hundred years of note-taking, in the soft margin by the culvert. Heron-ish. Larger than a heron.

And a pale flat spider that does not spin, lives inside the reed-stacks, and rides the chord out and back.

### Rules of the place

The rain never stops indoors and it never falls outside; step past the rim-growth and you are in dry scrub in ordinary weather, three metres from a warm downpour.

Nothing you drop here comes back. Give it a season and it is a strand in the weave, and the weave is under compression, and the compression goes one way.

The ring's path is a metre-wide band of ground-down silica, absolutely bare, the only bare surface in the basin. Nothing grows in it. Stand in it long enough and you will feel it: the whole rim leaning at you at the speed of a fingernail.

The sound cannot be got away from within the basin, and it stops abruptly at the rim, and for about an hour after you leave you will hear it in the ordinary sounds of things — in a passing lorry, in a kettle, in your own breathing.

The tally marks on the inside of the culvert brickwork run to 4,180 and are cut in at least four different hands, in groups of five, and the last group is a group of three, and the cuts are old but not as old as you want them to be.
