## The Bell-Cellar

It is a room, and it is only a room. You could walk its floor in eleven strides if the floor allowed walking, which it does not, quite. Everything else about it — the mountain it is inside, the vane on the ridge above that swallows the cloud, the shaft that drops the cloud down here to be wrung out — all of that is elsewhere, out of reach, inferred. This is one organ of something larger, and the larger thing has not been seen entire by anybody in living memory. What can be seen is this: a warm wet chamber, dim, loud past bearing, thick with life, three thousand and some meters above any sea, and made. Made all the way down. There is no rock here. There is no soil. Whatever is under your hand was poured, sintered, drawn, or grown by intention, and then left alone for a very long time.

**Getting in.** The way is a duct, oval, the height of a kneeling person, with a lip worn into a shallow trough by the passage of hands and hips and the dragging of water-skins. The wear is the only clock in the place that reads in human units. It says: many, over long. The duct's material is a grey-green ceramic with a slight nap to it, like the bloom on a plum, which is not a coating but the material itself gone soft at the surface from ten thousand years of condensation. Rub it and it squeaks and your fingertip comes away faintly bitter. There are no tool marks. There are no seams except one, and that one is a scar — a place where the wall was broken, long ago, by something, and afterward closed itself, leaving a raised weld like the ridge on a healed shinbone. Nobody living has seen the room do that. The room has not needed to.

**The floor.** Terraces. The condensate that runs off the flutes above carries dissolved lattice out of the machine's own body, and it lays that lattice down again in rimstone tiers, the way a hot spring builds travertine, except the tiers are the pale bluish white of bone china and ring under a knuckle. They step down from the walls toward the center in shelves a hand wide, each shelf holding a film of water two or three millimeters deep, each rim scalloped and delicate and strong enough to stand on. The tiers grow, by the best reckoning of the people who come with wax in their ears, about the width of a fingernail every hundred years. Which means the floor is a long slow record of the room's own dissolution and re-deposition. The room is eating itself and excreting itself in the same place, at a rate that has not changed in a very long time. This is what balance looks like here: not stillness, but a rate that matches a rate.

Between the tiers, in the drowned angles, there is a black-brown organic mat, ancient, deep as a mattress in places: guano, moulted chitin, drowned wings, bacterial felt, the dead of a hundred thousand generations. It is the closest thing to earth in the Bell-Cellar and it is entirely biological in origin. It is warm to the wrist. It steams.

**The water.** It arrives as breath. Far up the shaft, out on the ridge, the vane takes in the cloud that piles against the mountain's western face all afternoon, and drives it down here at body temperature, and this room's whole purpose — as near as anyone can tell — is to make that breath let go of what it carries. The walls are fluted for it: hundreds of vertical vanes, each the depth of a hand, each surface engineered at a scale below sight so that water beads on it in a particular reluctant way and then runs. The flutes are cool. They should not be cool; the air is warm, the mat is warm, the drain is warm. They are cool anyway, steadily, without machinery behind them, and if you press your palm flat to a flute you feel the heat leave your hand in a direction that does not feel like down or out but like *away*, and after a few seconds you take your hand back. That is the first of the room's quiet impossibilities and the people who come here have stopped remarking on it.

The runoff gathers in grooves finer than a hair, hundreds of them, invisible until you tilt your head and catch the shine. Some of those grooves run upward. Not many. Perhaps one in forty. They climb four or five centimeters against gravity to clear a rim and then rejoin the general falling, and the water in them moves faster than the water going down. Nobody has ever explained this and it is not, at this point, considered a mystery so much as a mannerism.

Everything ends at the lip-pool: a basin at the room's center, oval, waist-deep, so clear that its bottom looks reachable and is not. It spills over a low weir into the drain, and the drain is a round dark throat as wide as a barrel, and the drain is warmer than the room, and no plumb-line has found its bottom. Water goes in. Water does not come back.

**The sound.** You cannot speak here. That has to be said before anything else about it. The shaft above is a column of air a hundred and some meters long with a vane singing across its mouth, and it holds a note — a low one, near the bottom edge of hearing, so that mostly it is not heard but *had*, in the sternum, in the teeth, in the water of the eye. On top of that note sits the hiss of the intake, a white roar like surf. On top of the hiss sits the weir, which is not a large fall but is close, and close water is loud. And on top of everything sits the animals, whose contribution is a continuous shrieking chatter of an intensity that in any quieter place would empty a village.

Total: enough to make your ears ache in minutes and ring for two days. Visitors plug them with wax and beeswax-soaked lambswool and still cannot hear each other shout at arm's length. They communicate by hand, and by pressing forehead to forehead, where the skull carries speech directly. There is a whole small gestural language that exists nowhere but in this room and in the memory of the four or five families who use it.

**The animals.** They are abundant and they are, almost without exception, deaf, or as good as.

Nothing here hunts by ear; there would be no point. So the ecology has gone over entirely to touch, taste, and tremor. The tiers crawl with a flat pale crab the size of a coin — thousands of them, they are the room's grazers, they scrape biofilm from the china and read the world through the plate-hairs on their legs, feeling the footfall of a predator through the rimstone six shelves away. There is a fly that has given up flying; its wings are two chips of mica it uses to fan condensation off its back. There is a mite in the mat in numbers that make the mat itself seem to shimmer. There are worms in the fine grooves, thread-thin, half a meter long, that hunt by feel and can hold themselves in an upward-running groove and let the water bring them dinner.

Nesting in the flutes, layered in tiers of their own, are the birds — a swiftlet-thing, sooty, with a fringe of stiff bristles around the gape that they use to feel air pressure the way a blind man reads a doorway with his fingers. They fly in the dark by touch on the moving air. They nest here in tens of thousands because no owl, no snake, no ear-hunting thing can tolerate the noise. Their screaming is the loudest layer of the din and they cannot hear a note of it. They do it anyway, or they do something with their throats that in an ancestor was screaming.

In the shallows of the tiers: a newt, pale rose, palm-sized, breathing almost entirely through the skin, which is possible only in an atmosphere this saturated. It courts by pressing its throat to the rimstone and pulsing. The china carries it. In the pool, something larger moves that gets seen once a decade — long, and unbothered.

And then the tenant: a single warm-blooded animal, cat-sized, big-eyed, hand-footed, that lives somewhere up the shaft and comes down at intervals to rob nests. There is only ever one. Nobody knows what happens when there are two.

**The plants.** Almost none, and that is the room's honest character: this is a wet warm place with no sun. Six species of moss on the drip-lines, one liverwort in a mat the size of a table, algae films in the shallows, a hair-grass rooted in the weir's crest that whips constantly in the outflow and is the only thing in the Bell-Cellar that looks agitated. And one vine, come down the shaft from the daylight, hanging forty meters, leaves the size of a thumbnail because there is nothing here to feed leaves, fruiting perhaps twice in a decade to enormous local celebration among the crabs.

**The light.** Almost none of that, either. Far up, at an unguessable distance, a smear of grey daylight where the shaft turns. In the walls, in the deepest seams, a dull persistent ember-colour, red-brown, not flickering, not warm, present since before record. Enough to see the shine of water and the movement of pale animals and not enough to see colour. Eyes adjust for twenty minutes and then the room opens up considerably, and stays modest.

**The rules, such as they are.** Take water from the lip-pool; never from the weir. Do not put anything into the drain, ever, including the dead. Do not repair anything; it does not need you, and the one scar in the wall is evidence enough of what it does instead. Sit on the third tier, not the second, because the second belongs to the newts in the wet season. Leave before your ears stop hurting, because when they stop hurting it means something has gone quiet, and things going quiet in the Bell-Cellar is not restful, it is a symptom.

And the last one, which is less a rule than an observation carried down: the room does not require witnesses. It made the terraces and the note and the whole crawling deaf ecology of it during ages when nobody came at all. It will keep the rate. That is what it is for, apparently — to keep a rate — and the birds and the crabs and the wax-eared families have simply arranged themselves inside the doing of it, the way lichen arranges itself on a bell that is still being rung.
