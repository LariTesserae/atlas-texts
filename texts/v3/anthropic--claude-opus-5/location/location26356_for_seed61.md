**The Chamber Under the Gutter**

Begin with the sound, because the sound was here first and will outlast the reading of this.

It is not thunder. Thunder has a shape — it swells, breaks, retreats. This is a flat wall of noise, a white weight, the collapse of a river into a hole. It enters the body at the sternum and stays. Speech is impossible in the open floor of the chamber; two people standing an arm's length apart cannot exchange a word. The noise has been at exactly this volume, exactly this timbre, for longer than any counting the people here have kept, and this is the first fact of the world: **nothing changes.** Not the water level. Not the temperature, which sits at a permanent cool that beads the skin but never chills to shivering. Not the pitch of the fall.

---

The chamber is small. This is the thing hardest to convey, because the sound suggests something cathedral-vast and it is not. It is a room. Two hundred paces along its long axis, perhaps eighty across, and its ceiling in most places low enough that a tall adult walks with a slight duck of the head. A single feature, contained. A blister in the limestone, ninety fathoms under a plateau nobody down here has seen or believes in particularly.

The water comes in at the eastern end through a throat in the roof — the Gutter — a rounded shaft polished to the smoothness of a worn tooth, and it falls perhaps four times a man's height into a plunge basin of dark green-black water, and the sound is the falling. It leaves at the western end through a slot too narrow to enter, wide as two hands, at the base of the wall. Between inflow and outflow the water spreads shallow across the floor, ankle-deep, shin-deep, running fast at first and then slow and glassy, and the whole city stands in it.

Stands *in* it. There is no dry land. This has to be understood.

---

Everything here is stone or is made from stone or comes down the Gutter. There is no wood. There is no soil worth the name — only a fine grey silt that accumulates in the slack water and is dug out and fired into hard grey pots, the only manufacture the chamber truly owns. There is nothing green. There has never been anything green. The word for *plant* here, if it exists, is a word for a kind of debris.

But the walls are not bare. Everything wet and undisturbed carries a film — a pale, faintly luminous crust, the colour of skimmed milk, slick as raw egg to the touch. It is alive in the way that a stain is alive. It does not grow toward light because there is no light; it grows toward the minerals weeping out of the rock, and it gives back, for reasons of its own, a low greenish glimmer that is not enough to read by and is enough to walk by. The whole chamber is lit at the intensity of a room in which someone has left a door ajar three rooms away. Faces are recognizable at four paces. Beyond that, shapes.

Hands have rubbed the film off the frequently touched surfaces — doorframes, stair treads, the ropes — so the city appears in negative: dark tracks of use through a soft pale glow. You can read the traffic patterns of centuries as an absence.

---

The buildings are corbelled: flat plates of limestone, split from the wall along their bedding planes, stacked with each course oversailing the last until the walls lean in and close. No mortar. Every joint packed with the grey silt, which swells when wet and never dries, so the seals are permanent and the whole city is technically a single mud. The roofs are false domes, thumbprint-shaped. There are perhaps three hundred of these dwellings and they are pressed against one another like knuckles, sharing walls, stacked two and three high where the ceiling permits, connected by ramps and by ladders of stone rungs socketed into the masonry. Where the ceiling comes down, the houses are simply cut off flat and continue as tunnels. In several places a stalactite has been left running straight through a room, floor to roof, and the room built around it, because to break it would be an obscenity — the drip-figures are in balance here, dissolving precisely as fast as they accrete, neither growing nor shrinking, and the people take this equilibrium as the model of all correct behaviour.

Every threshold is raised a hand's width above the water. Every floor is a stone platform on stone piers, and beneath every floor the water runs quietly, cool, forever. You sleep on stone with a river six inches under your ear.

---

The city's plan is not laid out for sunlight or wind or defense. It is laid out for **acoustic shadow.**

The noise from the Gutter is enormous, but it is also fixed, and a fixed noise casts stable shadows. There are places in this chamber — behind a certain buttress of flowstone, in the lee of a particular ceiling pendant, inside a niche whose curve happens to cancel the reflected wave — where the roar drops away to a hush, and human voices work again. These pockets have been mapped with an exactness no other feature of the world receives. They are the only rooms where anything can be said. Every one is built over, roofed, floored, fought over, inherited. The quiet places are the wealth; the loud places are the streets.

So the city is organized like this: a dense clot of dwellings in the western third, where the fall's noise has spent itself, and then a scattering of small precious structures in the eastern roar, each one clinging to its acoustic pocket like a limpet — a shrine, a birthing room, a place where agreements are made. Between them, corridors of noise that everyone crosses quickly and no one lingers in.

And because the streets cannot carry speech, the city speaks with its hands, and with its feet on the resonant stone, and with cord. Stone-flax cord: the amphibole veins in the north wall comb out into a silky white fibre, and the combing and spinning of it is the great craft here. Cords run everywhere overhead, hundreds of them, waxed with lamp-grease, each with its knots and beads, each with a tension and a length, and a tug at one end rings a hanging pot at the other. The chamber's ceiling is a nervous system. In the dim you see them as pale threads crossing and recrossing, and hear, cutting under the roar at intervals, small hard clocks of fired clay knocking together. That is the only conversation audible in the open.

---

Nothing here makes food. That is the whole economy.

Everything edible enters through the Gutter, delivered by the water: silt-borne organic muck, drowned beetles, wads of surface leaf-litter, once in a while a whole rat or a frog or a fish that took a wrong channel far above. Across the plunge basin's outflow stand the Screens — frames of lashed stone slats hung with stone-flax netting, nine of them in series, each finer than the last, the finest so tight it takes the greenish film as well as the silt. Tending the Screens is the work of the world. The catch is drained, sorted, some of it eaten as paste, most of it fed to the tanks.

The tanks are shallow basins cut into the flowstone in the western quiet, and in them live the animals: a blind white crustacean, thumb-length, translucent enough that you can see the dark thread of its gut. They are slow and they are few and they breed grudgingly, and they constitute the only meat, the only fat, the only lamp oil. Each tank holds forty or fifty; the whole city's herd, if you can call it that, would fill two of the grey pots. Nothing else moves in this chamber except one kind of pale flat-bodied insect, ubiquitous and inedible, which grazes the wall film, and — very rarely, at the edge of the lamplight in the outflow slot — something long and grey that nobody has ever caught and everybody has agreed to consider a rumour.

---

There is no weather. There is no season. There is no day. There is no upstream news, because the Gutter delivers only debris, and debris says nothing about the world it fell out of except that such a world exists and is careless.

So time is kept in bodies, and only in bodies. A person's age is the only calendar. Depth of history is measured in the polish of the stair-treads and the thinness of the worn thresholds, and since the rate of that wear is unknown, history is understood to be simply *long* — a single undifferentiated long, without eras, in which the roar was already the roar and the water was already exactly at this level, and the ancestors did precisely what is done now, in these rooms, at these Screens.

The people believe the chamber is in balance and that balance is fragile in one direction only: their own. The stone does not err. The water does not err. The stalactites hold their length. Only hands err — by breaking a drip-figure, by widening the outflow, by taking two crustaceans where one was owed. Their ethics is entirely conservation and has no concept of improvement. To ask what might be *better* here is a category error, like asking what a stone weighs in the dark.

---

Stand at the eastern end, in the roar, wet to the knee, water pushing gently past your shins toward the city. Look up into the Gutter's throat, where the falling column is a hard grey rope shaking in place, apparently motionless because every part of it is identical to every other part. Feel the sound in your ribs. Notice that your ears have already begun to stop hearing it, that within an hour it will have become silence, and that this is how everyone here lives — inside a silence that is deafening, built out of a single sustained noise that has never once, in the whole history of the world, gone up or down.

Then turn west, where the pale film glows on three hundred stacked stone domes packed close as barnacles, where the cords cross overhead and the clay bells knock, where somebody is combing white fibre out of the north wall by feel, and understand that this is not a ruin, not a refuge, not a stage in anything. It is a completed place. It arrived at itself a very long time ago and has had no reason since to be anything else.
