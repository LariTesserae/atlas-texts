## The Late Plain

Begin with the fact that there is nothing to fall from and nowhere left to fall.

You are standing nineteen kilometres above the mean surface of a world that no longer bothers with oceans, and the ground under you is flat. Not smooth — flat. The distinction matters. Underfoot is a pavement of interlocking pebbles, each one seated in its socket, each one turned so that its most weathered face is up, and the whole mosaic lacquered a red-black by a varnish of manganese and time. You could roll a marble in any direction for eleven kilometres before slope became a factor. Beyond that it is still flat, only differently flat, tilted a hand's width per league toward some drainage that stopped draining before the first lichen was invented.

This is what a mountain range looks like when it has finished. Everything vertical has been paid out. Somewhere below the varnish are the roots of peaks that once had weather systems tangled in them like wool in a comb; what remains of them are quartz stumps, polished flush with the plain, appearing as pale scars a few metres across, sometimes with the faint fossil swirl of a fold still visible, the way the grain of an old table shows the tree. Erosion did not stop here because it was finished. It stopped because there was nothing left for it to want.

The air at this altitude is a rumour. Nine per cent of a lowland breath. Sunlight arrives undiluted and unforgiving, so that the lit sides of stones are warm enough to sting bare skin and their shadowed sides carry frost that never once, in the entire history of the plain, has melted into water. It goes straight from ice to nothing. The zenith is the colour of a bruise going black. The horizon is a hard, curved, colourless line, and because there is no haze in the upper air you can see that it curves, and see the far edge of the plain lift very slightly at the ends like the corners of a mouth.

## Turbulence without sound

Now: the wind.

It moves across the plain at speeds that would flatten a lowland forest — two hundred, three hundred kilometres an hour in the long afternoon gradients. At this pressure it has almost no weight. It leans against you like a persistent child. It cannot carry sound, or barely; it can carry dust, because dust is what it is made of.

So the loudest thing here is you. Your own pulse in your ears. The tick of your boot on varnish, arriving oddly flat and close, with no reverberation, as though the world declines to take the sound and hand it back. Grains hiss along the pavement — a papery, continuous *shhhh* at ankle height that you stop hearing after the first hour and then can never stop hearing again. When the sun crosses the terminator of a large stone, the stone contracts and *clicks*. That click will be, for the next several hours, the most significant event within a hundred metres.

And yet nothing here is still. The plain is the most violently active landscape on the planet, and it conducts its violence in near-silence.

Watch a middle distance — five kilometres out, say, where the pavement blurs into a plum-coloured shimmer. A column of dust stands up. It does not wander like a dust devil; it *organises*. In ninety seconds it has thickened, plumbed itself vertical, sprouted a flange, then four flanges, then buttresses. In four minutes there is a tower out there a hundred and eighty metres tall with a fluted shaft and a cornice and something like a stair spiralling its outside, and its surfaces are matte and grey-gold and hold the light the way a moth's wing does. In six minutes it has grown neighbours. In eleven minutes there is a city, or a cathedral, or the enormous detailed model of the branching of a lung — the vocabulary varies — occupying a square kilometre of plain and rising in tiers.

Then a shear-line comes through and the whole thing goes down at once, all of it, without a sound you could name. A faint sift. A softening in the light. The dust lies back down on the pavement and is pavement again, and the pebbles beneath were not disturbed, and there is no trace, and eight kilometres to the west a new column is already standing up.

This happens continuously, everywhere, all across a plain the size of a continent. From the edge of the escarpment at dawn you can count two hundred simultaneous events and know that you are seeing perhaps a hundredth of them.

## What the dust is

It is not weather. It is machinery, in the same sense that a forest floor is agriculture.

Somebody, at some point that the plain's own geology cannot bracket, seeded this world with builders too small to see and too numerous to count, and gave them the mandate to make things. What survived of that mandate, after the erosion of instruction sets by cosmic ray and by simple copying error over an interval long enough to flatten mountains, is the *make* and not the *what*. The dust remembers form the way a river remembers meander: structurally, compulsively, without reference.

It works dry. There has never been water for it. Its chemistry is silicate and metal and electrostatic latching — grains that grip each other, at the corners, with a strength that shouldn't be possible and isn't stable, and let go the instant charge redistributes. That is why the towers rise in minutes and why they cannot last a day. It is also why the air above the plain glows, on cool clear nights, with silent cold fire: sheets of violet corona standing over the assembly-fields, discharging at pressures too low for thunder. You see lightning that makes no noise, over cities that will not exist by morning, and there is no rain, and there was never going to be.

Two things about the dust that everyone who crosses the plain learns in the first week:

It copies what it touches. Leave a bootprint and it will be gone by evening, replaced by a print two metres long in the same rippled tread, and by the following noon there will be a ridge of them marching off downwind in diminishing scale, a range of footprint-shaped hills, each a faithful ruin of the last. Set down a cup and by dusk there is a colonnade of cups. Sleep on your side and wake beside your own shape rendered five times, in grey, larger each time, all of them collapsing.

It stops at skin. Nobody knows whether this is prohibition or indifference. It will copy a glove and not a hand. It has never been observed to model anything that respires. This is the single most reassuring fact in the region and the source of most of its religion.

## Scattered permanence

Real structures — things that stay — are rare enough that they are landmarks and cheap enough to describe.

Every few hundred kilometres there is a **marker**: a shaft of sintered basalt, waist-thick, standing four metres, with no inscription and a bevelled top polished by nothing but wind. They are not aligned to any compass. They are aligned, as far as anyone can determine, to each other, and only to each other, and the network they define is not a grid and not a line.

There is a **doorway** on the western third: two jambs and a lintel, freestanding, framing four square metres of identical plain. The stone is not from this world. Something was there once, and the dust has been faithfully making rough copies of the doorway for a geological interval, so the ground for forty kilometres downwind is littered with half-formed lintels in dun powder, each collapsing, each recopied, each generation further from the original — a long smeared genealogy of a single memory of a door.

There is a **listening station** near the northern rim, and it is still working. A low dome, glassy, unpitted after all this time, with an aperture that tracks something. Put a palm on it and feel a vibration too slow to be a hum, one cycle every eleven seconds, patient as a tide on a world that has no tides. It does not answer. It is not for you.

And there are **rings**, dozens of metres across, of black slag, which are the only visible evidence that the plain was ever hot.

## The living, spaced out

Life here is a matter of enormous distances between very small events.

Lichen, in the lee of stones — crustose, black-green, three species that anyone has bothered to distinguish, growing at a rate best measured against tectonics. Every second or third kilometre, a cushion: a hand-sized hemisphere of tight grey rosettes, hollow-hearted, insulated by the corpses of its own previous seasons, drinking frost directly out of shadow in the hour before dawn through hairs that are not quite crystal and not quite tissue. Where the dust-fields are most active there is a fourth thing, which grows *on* the assemblies — a glassy prostrate scale with translucent lobes, seeding itself onto towers that will fall by nightfall, riding them up, cashing in a few hours of extra sunlight, going down with them. It has no other strategy. It has needed no other for a very long time.

Fauna: you will go days. Then there will be tracks — a straight line, unhurried, crossing your path at some angle that has nothing to do with anything, going from one horizon to the other. Follow them and you will not catch it. Occasionally, high up, at the very edge of the thin air, there is a **soarer**: a black flake with a four-metre span that does not flap and, in local accounts spanning generations, has never been seen on the ground. It exploits the standing waves off the escarpment. It presumably eats the dust-grazers, which are the size of a fingernail, transparent, and which spend their entire lives on a rising tower, ascending, feeding on the loose fringe of the copy-process, and are put down gently when it falls.

## Rules

The plain will not change. Nothing you do to it will change it. This is not a poetic assertion; it is the observed behaviour of a landform that has already spent everything it had and reached the shape from which no further shape is possible. Bore a hole and the varnish will heal it in a decade.

Everything on the plain changes constantly, completely, and silently, and none of it persists past a single rotation of the world.

The dust will copy whatever you leave. Leave carefully.

At night — and the nights are cold, and clear enough to see the dark limb of the world eat the stars — the assembly-fields go on working, unlit, and the corona stands over them in slow lilac sheets. From the rim, looking out across three hundred kilometres of it, you will see the flicker of cities being born and cities coming apart, over and over, at the rate of some hundreds per hour, in perfect quiet, on a floor that has not moved since before there were eyes.

And there is no water in any of it. Not one drop, in any direction, forever. Only frost, in the shadows, for one hour, being spent.
