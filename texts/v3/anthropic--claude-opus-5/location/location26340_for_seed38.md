## The Sill

Come at it from the low country and you will not find an edge so much as a weather system with a base to it. For two days' travel before the escarpment, the sky ahead is not sky: it is a grey-green wall going up out of sight, streaked vertically, faintly luminous, and always faintly moving. That is water leaving. The whole rim of the plateau weeps. Not in rivers — there are no rivers up there, as will be explained — but as a continuous exudation, a sweat, seeping out of the cut face of the tableland and falling four thousand metres in a haze so fine that most of it never lands. It reascends. It rains on itself. The escarpment has its own permanent inverted weather, a standing cylinder of cloud that circles the continent, and the sound of it at the base is not a roar but a hiss, immense and even, like the inside of a shell held up to the ear of a god.

The rock of the face is the last honest rock in the country. Above it, nothing.

---

Up on top, there is no ground.

This must be said plainly at the start, because everything else follows from it. The Sill is an old mountain range — the oldest, a range that stopped rising before there was much on the planet to notice — and it has spent an unthinkable duration wearing itself down to a shelf. There are no summits. The relief across a thousand kilometres is measured in tens of metres, long low swells you cross without knowing you have crossed them, the way you cross the ocean's swell. All the peaks became gravel and the gravel became mud and the mud went over the rim in that slow weeping. What remained was a flat wet warmth at high altitude, and into that condition life poured itself, and it has been pouring for so long that the plant matter is now deeper than the erosion was.

The floor of the Sill, where you stand, is between two and nine hundred metres of accumulated living and half-living plant. Below that, peat compacted to something like brown coal, still soft, still exhaling. Below that, presumably, the bones of the mountains. No one has been down. Nothing goes down. Whatever the surface produces, the surface keeps.

So: you walk on a body. The uppermost two metres is a mat the local grammar would call *quilt* — a knit of moss-analogues, thread-roots, leaf litter that never quite becomes litter, and a pale flexible tissue like wet felt that binds it all. It holds you. It gives about a hand's depth under each step and closes again behind you, and if you stand still for a quarter of an hour it will begin, without malice, to grow over the tops of your feet. Beneath the quilt is water. Beneath the water, more quilt, older, in layers, going down through amber and brown into black, each layer once a surface.

---

Vertically, it is architecture.

The great columns are not trees, or not any longer. They began as trees, presumably, in some era when there was soil to root in; now they are structural organs of the mass, trunks that stopped bothering with roots because the whole body feeds itself by contact. They rise sixty, eighty metres, smooth as bone, and at height they do not branch so much as **fuse** — column running into column, the canopy a continuous vaulted ceiling with no separate crowns in it. Look up and you see a groined roof going away in every direction, ribbed, dripping, panelled with leaf-plates the size of doors. Light comes through it in a dim submarine green, and shafts of brighter green where a column has died and let the ceiling sag.

Between floor and ceiling: the galleries. Fifteen, twenty stacked storeys of hanging and standing vegetation, each with its own climate and its own inhabitants. Ropes of vine thick as thighs, spanning; sheets of epiphyte hung like tapestry, holding their own private ponds; hollow stems you could crawl inside for a kilometre; whole shelves of pale fungal-looking growth that is not fungus but a plant that has given up on light and lives on the exhalations of its neighbours. Density is the rule. Not a jungle-tangle — it is too old and too settled for tangle — but a filled space, arranged, layered, everything near-touching, everything slightly damp with everything else's breath.

And it is one thing. Cut anywhere — into a column, a vine, the quilt, the pale shelf-growth — and the same sap comes: thin, warm, faintly sweet, faintly metallic. The Sill is not an ecosystem containing plants. It is a plant containing an ecosystem.

---

Water does not flow here. It is held.

Rain — and it rains most nights, warm and vertical — never reaches the quilt. It is intercepted at every level, absorbed, passed inward, and it moves after that only by seepage, cell to cell, at speeds better measured in seasons than in hours. There are lakes: wide open eyes in the quilt where the mat has thinned, black, still, warm as blood, ringed by a lip of raised living moss so that they sit slightly *above* the surrounding country like water held in a cupped palm. There are veins: channels a metre or two below the surface, running slowly, full of things without eyes. There is the general saturation, which means that everything you touch is wet and nothing you touch is cold, and that within a day your skin begins to soften and take on a faint green cast, and within a month the Sill has started, politely, to make you part of itself.

Once a year — the year here being an unhurried thing — the whole tableland swells with retained water and rises perhaps four centimetres, then subsides. Continental breathing. You cannot feel it. The animals can.

---

It is dim, and it is quiet, and the two are related.

Dim: a good noon on the Sill is the brightness of dusk in ordinary country. The eye adapts and then discovers that the Sill also glows a little from below. Down in the peat, decomposition runs at a low warm smoulder, and certain deep tissues fluoresce with it, so that the quilt at night has a faint mottled underlight, the colour of moonlight on beer. You see your own footprints filling with it behind you. There is also lamp-growth: pendant bells, a few per hundred metres, which brighten for an hour after any disturbance of the air. Walking at night, you are preceded and followed by a slow lighting-up and a slow going-out, as though the place is checking who has come.

Quiet: the moss eats sound. Nine hundred metres of soft wet plant beneath you and twenty storeys of soft wet plant above you make the deadest acoustics in the world. Clap, and there is no echo, no reverberation, only the clap and then a silence that arrives faster than it should — an unnerving punctuality of silence. And so the fauna have all gone silent too, because in a room this dead, a sound is a position, and a position is a death. Nothing calls. Nothing sings. Courtship here is conducted by scent, by light, by touch, by the slow patient tapping of one body against a stem in a rhythm too low to hear at ten paces.

What you do hear: dripping, constant, various, the base note of the country. The creak of a column adjusting. And rarely, at intervals of days, a sound from below — a deep soft *whumph* as a pocket of gas that has been assembling in the peat since before your grandmother finally rises and lets itself out through the quilt. The mat lifts, sighs, and settles. Birds — there are things like birds — go up in silence.

---

The animals are abundant and almost all of them are soft.

There is no stone up here and no bone-mineral to speak of, so the fauna have largely done without skeletons. Everything is turgid rather than rigid: pressure-bodied, translucent, moving with the deliberate hydraulic grace of a caterpillar or a starfish. The grazers of the upper galleries are pale ribbed things the size of sheep that flow along the underside of the leaf-plates and rasp them, leaving neat scallops. The mound-browsers of the quilt are larger — bulk of a cow, no legs worth the name, a creeping foot like a snail's — and they crop the moss in wide arcs and lay down their own manure-gardens, and the moss grows back over their tracks in a few years, and one browser will spend a century going round a single lake. The predators are ambush-only. They cannot chase across a substrate that grips. There are hunters that grow into the quilt themselves, becoming a patch of surface with a mouth in it, and wait — patiently, in this patient country — for a foot.

In the water veins: eels of a kind, blind, pale, wonderfully long. In the sub-quilt galleries where the roots make a wet crawlspace: whole societies of small blind pad-footed things that have never in their lineage seen light and that eat root-hairs and each other. In the air: silent broad-winged fliers with no keel-bone, gliding under the vaulted ceiling like fish under ice.

---

Scattered through all of this — and this is the thing that makes travellers uneasy — are the structures.

You will come, every few days' walking, on a doorframe. Two uprights and a lintel, waist-high or full height, made of the hard mineralised heartwood the Sill produces at depth and sometimes surfaces. Standing alone. No wall attached, no floor, no rubble, nothing on either side but quilt. You can walk around it. People do walk around it.

You will find low circular walls, a course or two high, enclosing nothing. Cairns of the rare nodules the plant secretes, stacked with obvious care. A flight of four steps going up out of the moss and stopping. A bench. A threshold-stone, worn concave in the middle as thresholds are worn, sitting in the middle of a moss plain with no house behind it and no path in front of it.

The generous reading is that there were people, long ago, and the Sill has swallowed everything except the hardest parts, and these are the last teeth of drowned villages. This is likely true of some of them.

The other reading, which the older guides prefer and which explains the ones that are *made of living wood, still attached, still growing*, is this: the Sill repeats. It has enveloped so much, over so long, that its tissue has a habit of reproducing the shapes it has enveloped, the way scar reproduces the shape of the wound. Somewhere in the deep peat there is a doorway. Nine hundred metres above it, the plant makes a doorway, out of nothing, for no one, in slow annual accretions, and then makes another one a little further on. It does not know what a door is. It knows what a door is shaped like.

There is no fire on the Sill. Nothing is dry enough, ever, and there is no flint and no free stone. Nothing here has ever been cooked, smelted, fired, or lit. This is the whole reason for the Sill's condition: fire never happened, and therefore nothing that follows fire ever happened. Whatever people were, they were people without embers, and what they left is this — thresholds, benches, four steps, a wall around nothing — the built forms that need no heat.

---

Time.

Two clocks govern. The first is the breathing: annual, four centimetres, imperceptible.

The second is the Long Flower. Somewhere on the Sill, at all times, there is a front of blossoming — a line of it, running roughly north and south, hundreds of kilometres long, advancing at something under two kilometres a year. Ahead of the front the country is green-brown and ordinary. At the front, over a band twenty kilometres wide, every level of the architecture flowers at once: the ceiling opens white, the vines put out sprays the colour of raw liver, the quilt itself throws up millions of small yellow bells, and the air becomes unbreathably sweet, and the pollinators arrive in silent clouds, and the light doubles because the blossom is pale and reflective. Behind the front, for a decade, the country is exhausted, littered, shockingly bright, sagging, and then it closes over and is ordinary again.

The Long Flower has been walking since before the low countries had names. It goes round. Given the size of the Sill, a given hectare flowers roughly once every eleven thousand years. If you are lucky enough to be alive near the front, you may walk with it, and if you walk at the speed of the flowering — which is to say, if you stand nearly still, and shift your camp a few hundred paces each spring — you can spend an entire life inside one continuous white event, and never see the ordinary country again, and die inside a smell.

Some people do this. Their thresholds are found later, worn concave, alone, with nothing behind them.
