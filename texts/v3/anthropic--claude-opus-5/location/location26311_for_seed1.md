## The Sump

### I. The lie of the land

It reads, at first, as nothing: a grey dish of country some forty kilometres across, so gently rimmed that you only understand it is a basin by watching where the water goes. The hills that hold it are not hills anymore. They are swellings, long low shoulders of stone with no edges left anywhere on them, the way a bar of soap has no edges. Whatever mountains stood here spent themselves so long ago that their gravel has been ground, sorted, re-cemented, and ground again. The land has finished. It has arrived at the shape it will keep, and the shape is *almost level*, and being almost level it holds water everywhere, in a hundred thousand shallow figures.

You walk it and your boots find no soil. There is no soil, because soil requires roots and rot and there has never been either. What lies underfoot is a pavement of packed grit, dark as wet slate, and if you crouch and take a pinch of it and let it fall grain by grain onto your palm you will see what the grit is made of. Cogwheels. Not cog-shaped stones — cogwheels, four millimetres across, eleven teeth, hubs bored through, some seized in pairs, some worn to smooth discs. Beside them: hexagonal nuts the size of poppy seeds, fragments of coil, splinters of laminate iron with the striations still countable. The whole plain is scree off machinery, milled fine, sorted by water into bars and beaches and long tailing-fans, and nothing built any of it.

That is the first thing to accept here. There are no structures. No walls, no chimneys, no rails, nothing set upright by a hand. There never were. The machinery is not built; the machinery is *bedrock*. Down under the grit, in the layers, matter here crystallizes into mechanism the way elsewhere it crystallizes into quartz — hexagonal, predictable, obeying some law of form that decided long before life that the natural state of dense cold metal is the gear, the piston, the armature, the sealed bearing. Break open a boulder on the eastern swells and inside is a cross-section of gearbox, oil-black, still greased with something that has never been alive, teeth meshed and unturning, a fossil of an engine that was never assembled and never worked.

Most of them never worked. Some still do.

### II. Water

Water is the medium of the place. It is 2 degrees, maybe 3, and it does not freeze except in skins at the edges, because it is thick with dissolved iron and salts and a faint sourness that catches the back of the throat. It stands in the millions of shallow pans. It seeps from the swells in slow beads that hang on the rock and never quite drop — the stones weep and the weeping is continuous and no one drop ever completes. It runs in the troughs, and the troughs are the best feature of the basin: long parallel channels, twenty metres wide, half a kilometre to fifty kilometres long, spaced with a regularity that makes the eye insist on intention. They are not canals. They are the exposed teeth of a rack-and-pinion formation as wide as a county, the grooves between iron ribs, each rib worn to a rounded hump the height of a barn, glazed with wet.

The air is at saturation and stays there. Everything is beaded. Your sleeves bead. Your eyelashes bead. Breath does not plume so much as join. There is a fog that lives about knee-height in the mornings and about roof-height by afternoon, and it travels in slabs, and where a slab passes over warm ground it lifts and you get a moment of clarity in which you see how far the basin goes, and it goes further than you were prepared for.

### III. Light

Dim. Continuously dim. There is a sun somewhere behind eleven kilometres of grey; you can locate it as a slightly less grey patch, and it moves, and by its moving you know the day. Nothing casts a hard shadow. Everything casts a soft one, doubled and tripled by the second light source, which comes from below.

The ground glows. Not brightly — the way a wristwatch glows an hour after you turn out the lamp. It is strongest in the wet places, where the water conducts, and it pulses. Long slow pulses, fifteen or twenty seconds, greenish, travelling across the pans in fronts, so that at night the basin is a slow ocean of dim green weather crossing itself. In the troughs, where the iron ribs are close, the pulses concentrate and sometimes discharge: a crack of blue at ankle height, a smell of ozone and burnt hair, gone. These are common. You learn to keep out of standing water when the ground is counting up to something. The animals know it perfectly and you never will.

### IV. Sound

It is loud here and the loudness has no source you can walk to.

Underneath everything is the Hum: sixty-ish cycles, felt in the sternum, arriving through bone as much as ear. Above that, a stratum of working noise — the sub-audible knocking of enormous slow reciprocation, somewhere down at three hundred metres, coming up through the wet grit as a rhythm you can set a pace to and then can't stop setting a pace to. Then the surface layers: water hammer in the seep-channels, sharp bangs like someone striking a pipe with a spanner, hundreds a minute across the basin, each one echoing off the fog-slabs. Rivet-pops as thermal stress works the ribs. A high wet whining in the west that varies with the wind and is not the wind. And on some afternoons, unmistakably, the sound of a shift ending: a huge falling siren-tone, four notes down, twenty seconds long, so loud it flattens the fog, and afterwards a silence in which every animal in forty kilometres stops moving.

Nothing ends. No one leaves. The siren sounds anyway. It has been sounding at the end of nothing for a length of time the erosion suggests is measured in the hundreds of millions.

### V. What lives

No plants. Not one. No moss, no lichen you would call lichen, no green anywhere except the ground-glow. What stands in for the base of the food chain is film: mats of something simpler than either fungus or algae, pearl-grey to bruise-purple, growing in sheets over any surface where current leaks. They eat electricity. That is the honest description. They oxidize dissolved iron and they take up potential difference across their membranes and they thicken where the ground is hot, and in the trough-bottoms they grow in ropes and curtains a metre long that stir in the flow like hair.

Everything else eats the film, or eats the eaters. And there is a *great deal* of everything else.

**Shovels.** Pale segmented grazers, isopod-plan, hand-sized to dog-sized, armoured in something with the lustre of galvanized steel. They move in rafts of thousands over the flats, scraping film off gravel, and the sound of a raft feeding is a hiss like rain on a tin roof. They are the biomass of the basin. Everything is about them.

**Trough-eels.** Two metres, black, no eyes, a lateral line of pit-organs that reads voltage. They hunt shovels by sensing the animal's own nerve-fire. When they strike they discharge, and the discharge lights the water dull violet for a body-length around, and you can watch a hunt at night from the rib-top as a series of soft violet blooms travelling up a trough.

**Flitches.** They fly. They are the size of a starling and they are not warm and they are not exactly present; they read as flakes of brightness that move in flocks of ten thousand and bank as one and settle on wet iron in a rattling sheet to lick condensate. When a flock passes overhead, the Hum wavers in pitch. Whether they cause that or ride it is a question the basin does not answer.

**The Slow.** On the northern flats, mounded things four metres high, film-crusted, indistinguishable from boulders until one of them has moved a hundred metres in the time it took you to eat. They filter grit through some ventral apparatus and excrete sorted metal in neat conical piles, each cone graded by grain size, tidier than anything a person would bother to make. They are the oldest animals. They are possibly the *only* animals, in a sense addressed below.

Predation, breeding, moulting, migration — it is all running, all of it, hard, all the time, at full biological pace, in a landscape that has been geologically finished for aeons. The living part of the place is frantic. The stone part is asleep. They share a floor.

### VI. Rules

Here is where the basin stops behaving.

**Counting fails.** You cannot count the shovels in a raft, and this is not a matter of difficulty. Count a small group of nine; count again, get nine; have a companion count, get nine; count together aloud and you will not finish, because at some number between six and eleven the sequence declines to proceed. Numbers work here up to about five. Past five the basin substitutes *many* and will not be argued with. Every quantity larger than a hand is genuinely, physically indefinite, and the ecology takes advantage of this: predator and prey both hunt inside the ambiguity, and both are sometimes correct.

**Repetition.** Walk east for two hours and you will come to a pan four metres across with a rib of black iron at its northern lip and one shovel dead on its back at the shallow end, legs curled. Walk another two hours: the same pan. The same shovel, same legs, same angle of the same dead light in the same water. It is not a similar pan. It is the pan. The basin has a limited vocabulary of places and speaks them over and over, thousands of times, and the erosion is identical in each recitation down to the pitting. This is why the surveying never closed. Distances re-tally behind you. The basin is forty kilometres across in every direction from wherever you stand, and it does not add up, and the arithmetic that would show it doesn't add up is the arithmetic that stops at five.

**Tense leaks.** Dew forms on the ribs about ninety seconds before the cold front reaches them. Water-hammer bangs arrive, then the pressure surge that causes them. A trough-eel's strike-flash precedes the strike so reliably that the shovels have evolved to flinch at the light — which means the flinch also precedes, and the eels have evolved to strike where the flinch will be. The whole predator-prey relation runs backwards from its own outcome and stabilizes there, and it works, and the eels are fat.

**Singularity of the fauna.** Watch The Slow long enough and you will notice that no two are ever visible in the same glance. Turn your head; the one behind you is the one in front of you; the crusting patterns match. The abundance is real — the biomass is enormous, the noise of it is deafening — and it is possible that the abundance is one animal, encountered many times, the way the pan is one pan. The basin does not distinguish between *many things* and *one thing repeated*. Nothing in its physics requires it to.

**Product.** The machinery, where it still turns, produces. Not objects. It has no output chutes, no yards, nothing to load. What it manufactures is *occurrence*: the fog-slabs, the fronts of green light, the discharges, the four-note siren, the weather. These are made goods. They come off the line at intervals, and the intervals are industrial — regular, quota'd, indifferent — and you can stand on a rib-top at dusk and watch a fog bank come off the western flats in a form so uniform, so obviously the ten-thousandth identical unit of its type, that the word *natural* falls out of your hands. The basin is a plant. Its product is its own afternoon. It has been running the same shift since before there was anything to sell it to, and the shift ends every day, and nothing ends.

### VII. Standing there

Late, then. The green fronts crossing. Rain of shovel-feeding on the near flats and violet blooms going up the trough. The Hum in your ribs, the deep knock underneath keeping your heart's time whether or not you consent. Everything wet, everything cold, nothing green, nothing built, nothing tall, nothing new — and the place so full, so *packed*, film on every stone and animals on every film and machinery under every animal and water over all of it, that there is no gap anywhere to set down a thought of your own.

What is strange is that it is not desolate. Desolation requires that something be missing. Nothing is missing here. The basin has everything it has ever needed and it is using all of it, loudly, at capacity, forever, and it settled into this exact final form so long ago that the question of what it was before has worn away along with the mountains.

The siren goes. The fog lies down. In the pause you can hear, very clearly, water finding its way through iron — and then the second pause, the smaller one, when every shovel in the basin is still, and there is only one of them, and it is looking at you.
