## The Shelf Town

Above two thousand metres the mountain stops climbing for a while and puts out a shelf — a floor of pale grey limestone about the size of four or five large fields, tilted just enough that water knows which way to go. On three sides the shelf ends in air: not sheer drops but long slabbed shoulders, terrace after terrace of rock lying in the position it settled into a very long time ago. On the fourth side the mountain resumes, a wall of the same grey stone streaked darker where moisture runs, going up out of sight most days into cloud.

This is the whole of the place. You can walk its boundary in half an hour. Everything here is inside that walk.

---

**Water arrives sideways.** It rains perhaps two days in ten, thin cool rain that never becomes weather in the dramatic sense, no thunder, no downpour, just the sky lowering itself onto the shelf and being present for a few hours. The rest of the water comes as fog. The cloud that sits against the mountain wall drifts across the shelf in the mornings and again toward evening, and it leaves itself on everything: on lichen, on the stone, on wool, on the fine mesh screens the townspeople have strung between posts on the western edge, six of them, each about the height of a man and three times as wide, woven from split reed brought up from far below generations ago and repaired since with horsehair and gut. Water beads on the mesh and runs down into channels cut in the rock, and the channels run into cisterns.

There are eleven cisterns. Each one is a chamber hollowed out of the limestone beneath a house, reached by a stair, lidded with a single slab. The insides are smooth as the inside of a mouth. The water in them is very cold and tastes faintly of stone and more faintly of the ferns that grow at the lips of the overflow gutters. The level in each cistern is marked with a groove, and the level is at the groove. It is always at the groove. Whether the townspeople have arranged this or the shelf has, no one seems interested in explaining.

---

**The town** is thirty-one buildings, and they stand far apart. This is the strangest thing about it at first — an urban thing, plainly a town, with lanes and thresholds and drains and a public floor, but spread thin across the shelf like the last pieces on a game board. Between houses there are sixty, eighty paces of bare stone, worn into shallow paths by feet, the paths as fixed as if they'd been carved. You can stand in the middle of the town and see all of it, and see mostly sky and rock.

The buildings are limestone, dry-laid, no mortar, walls a full arm's length thick with rubble packed in the cavity. Roofs are the same stone in overlapping slabs, corbelled inward so each roof is a shallow stone dome from inside — cool, dim, dry, humming very slightly when the wind comes over. Doorways are low and lintelled with a single long block. The stone has weathered to a soft mineral grey with rust-coloured bleeding around iron pins, and the south faces are furred with grey-green lichen in maps that have not changed in living memory.

Underneath the houses, older houses. The shelf has been built on and rebuilt on so many times that in places you go down two or three floors of packed rubble and buried thresholds before you reach the rock itself. Cellars open into other cellars that were once ground floors. There are stairs that end at a ceiling. Nothing has been demolished; things were simply built over, and the town rose on itself a little at a time, until it reached the height it is now, which is where it stopped.

---

**The tools** are stone, wood, bone, and a small quantity of copper — chisels, a few adzes, the pins that hold roof slabs at the ridges, needles. Copper comes up the mountain rarely and is never thrown away; a worn chisel is beaten and re-tempered until it is a stub, and the stub becomes a wedge, and the wedge becomes filings for making something else. There is no forge here hot enough for iron and no fuel to feed one. Wood is precious: the only trees on the shelf are eleven junipers, all of them ancient, all of them low and clenched and leaning east, and no one cuts them.

What the town does have is masonry, and it has masonry the way some places have religion. The craft is not building. The craft is **replacement in kind.** When a roof slab cracks with frost, a mason goes to the quarry notch at the base of the mountain wall, cuts a slab, and dresses it to the exact dimensions of the broken one — not similar, exact, checked against a rod of measurements handed down. The new slab goes into the place of the old. The old slab goes into the rubble fill of a wall, where it becomes part of the thickness. Nothing here is ever new and nothing is ever lost. The town is the same town it was three hundred years ago, made of different stones.

---

**Life is scarce and deliberate.** Cushion plants in the crevices — hard green pincushions that flower briefly in a pale yellow and are grazed by nothing. Ferns in the wet lines below seeps. Lichen everywhere, in nine or ten kinds, which the townspeople distinguish by name and use for dye. Two walled gardens of imported soil, each about the size of a room, growing a hardy grain and a bitter leaf, roofed with mesh in the fog hours to collect on the crop directly.

Animals: eleven goats, small and grey-black, kept in a fold on the east edge. A dog. Choughs — glossy black, red-billed, absurdly good at flying — who come in over the drop in the afternoon in twos and threes, walk about on the roofs looking into things, and go away. In some years a fox works up the terraces and is seen once and not again. Under stones there are pale spiders and a slow black beetle. That is the ecology. It is complete; nothing is missing from it, because nothing has been here to be missed.

---

**Sound.** The dominant sound is wind on stone, which is not a howl at this height but a broad low shushing, with a note in it where it passes the mesh screens. Under that: dripping. Every cistern has its drip, and each drip has its own interval, and the intervals do not change. Someone who grew up here can tell which house they are near by the rhythm of the water under the floor. Then goat bells, dulled by wool wrapping. Then feet, and the sound of a chisel — a small precise tapping, carried a long way in the thin air, four or five taps and a pause, four or five taps and a pause. Voices are quiet, not from reverence but because the air carries so well that there is no need to raise them. A conversation from across the shelf arrives faint and legible, like reading.

Light is plain daylight, cool and even, slightly silvered by the moisture in the air. When the cloud comes across, the light does not dim so much as become directionless, and the shelf goes soft and small and near, and the far houses vanish, and the town is briefly the size of a room. Then the cloud passes and it is thirty-one houses again on a floor of grey rock, with the drop on three sides.

---

**The rule of the place** is not magic. It is simply that this shelf found its equilibrium a long time ago and has not been given a reason to leave it. The water arrives at the rate it arrives. The stone erodes at the rate it is replaced. The junipers do not grow and do not die. There are enough people for the work and the work is enough for the people, and the number of both has been the same for as long as records — knotted cords in a stone niche — have been kept. When a child is born, an old person is usually dying, and the town notices the coincidence without remarking on it.

Nobody here talks about the future, because the future is visibly the same as this. Nobody talks much about the past either, for the same reason. What they talk about is the day's stones: which one cracked, where its match will be cut, who will carry it. It is not a sad place, and not a sleeping one. It is a place that has finished becoming, and has stayed to be that, at height, in cool air, in the coming and going of the cloud.
