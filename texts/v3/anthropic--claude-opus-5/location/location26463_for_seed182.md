## The Cut

You come down nine meters and you are in another climate.

The stair is a spiral of cast treads bolted into rock that was still hot when the bolts went in — you can see where the drill scored the stone and where the stone, being young, simply broke instead of wearing. Forty-odd years ago the ground here opened along a seam, exhaled, and stayed open. The town above went on with its market days and its wet winters. Down here the seam kept breathing, and the town, which is thrifty, built into the breath.

The rock is fresh. That is the first thing your hands learn. Every fracture face is unrounded, unhealed, edged like a struck flint — run a palm along a wall and it takes skin. There are ribbons of glass in the darker courses that will cut a boot sole. Nothing has had time to become smooth. Nothing drips long enough in one place to have carved a channel; the channels are all cast iron, all made, laid in by men who did not want to wait a thousand years for the water to find its own way. The result is a chamber with the geology of a wound and the plumbing of a city.

It is hot. Not the hot of fire, which flickers and can be stepped away from, but the hot of a held breath, even and total, a temperature that has no direction. Ninety-six, ninety-eight degrees, and never less, and the air thick enough that your first hundred steps feel like your last hundred steps of a long day. Sweat does not evaporate. It simply accumulates, until you and the walls are wearing the same film.

---

The whole space would fit inside a modest cathedral, or under a market square, which is in fact what lies above it. That is its entire extent: one hall, roughly kidney-shaped, three hundred paces at the long axis, with the vent-end narrowing to a throat of pipework and the sump-end widening into a flooded apse. There are boundaries and everyone knows them. You can walk the perimeter in the time it takes bread to rise.

Within that small compass there is no unoccupied volume. This is the second thing you learn, and you learn it with your shoulders. The Cut is packed. Terraces of glazed brick step up the walls in four tiers, and every tier is trellised, and every trellis is loaded, and between the tiers run catwalks of perforated plate, and beneath the catwalks hang trays, and through the trays run pipes, and along the pipes grows moss, and in the gaps between pipes someone has wedged clay pots of cuttings because there was, technically, a gap. Roots come down through the ceiling — the plane trees of the market square have found the warmth and sent their taproots nine meters to drink — and rather than cut them the wardens have trained them onto frames, tapped them with brass spiles, and hung buckets. The surface drinks here and pays rent in water.

You do not walk through the Cut so much as insinuate yourself along its remaining seams.

---

Light: about as much as a shuttered room at dusk. Along the main catwalk there are filament lamps in wire cages, and they are kept deliberately faint, a brown-orange glow that reveals the shape of a leaf but not its color. The lamps are throttled because the current is thin and because bright light is a waste here — nothing in the Cut needs it.

This is the strangeness of the place, the rule that reorganizes everything: the vegetation does not grow toward light. It grows toward heat. The sun of the Cut is a boiler junction, a steam main, a flue-brick still warm from the seam below. The leaves are thick and dark and leathery, veined pale, and they arrange themselves like hands held out to a stove. Walk along a hot pipe and the foliage leans in from both sides to meet over it, forming a green tunnel around the metal; walk along a cold return and the plants turn their backs and you can see the wall. Whole species have specialized. The boilerfern grows nowhere but within a handspan of scalding iron and dies of the cold at two handspans. The sulfur-fig, whose fruit takes eleven years to ripen and tastes of iron and ash, sinks its roots directly into fissure gravel and metabolizes the seam's yellow breath. There is no photosynthesis worth the name down here. There is chemistry, and there is warmth, and the plants are green mostly out of habit.

And there is gauge-moss. It grows only on warm metal, and its pile thickens exactly with temperature: bald at blood heat, a felt at eighty, a deep bottle-green plush where a pipe runs near boiling. The wardens do not carry thermometers. They read the moss with a fingertip and know their machine to within a degree or two. Over the decades the moss has been bred and coaxed and shaved and encouraged until every hot surface in the Cut is a dial, and the whole works is legible by touch in the dark.

---

The machine at the center is called the Slow. It is a beam engine of ordinary design geared down past all reason — the flywheel takes a quarter of an hour to come round, and the beam's stroke, top to bottom, is about the length of a slow meal. You can watch it and not be sure it is moving. You can put a finger on the crosshead and feel the world's most patient pressure.

It does two things. It drives an Archimedes screw that lifts condensate from the sump to the upper cistern, and it turns a dynamo that keeps the lamps at their brown ember. Its belts are not leather but woven from the fiber of the wick-vine, which is the Cut's crop and its reason. The pith of the wick-vine, dried and drawn, makes filament. The town above burns the Cut's harvest in its street lamps and its parlors. The district that produces the light lives in the dim. Nobody down here finds this remarkable; it is simply how the trade lies.

Because everything is geared so far down, the Cut is quiet. This surprises visitors more than the heat. There is no clatter, because clatter requires speed. Below a certain velocity, machinery stops making noise and merely settles, and the Slow is well below it. What you hear instead: the tick of condensation on plate steel, irregular, everywhere. A long breath from the throat vent when the pressure differential shifts. Leaves moving against leaves as a warm current turns over — a sound like paper being considered. Once in a while, dulled by nine meters of rock, cartwheels crossing the market square above, which is the loudest thing in the Cut and sounds like weather.

The wardens speak, when they speak, beneath the level of the drip. There are four of them. They wear wet linen and move at the pace of the engine, and they sleep in bunks let into the terrace brick among the pots. They tap pipes to signal each other — one ring for the vent-gate, two for the sump. It is not a rule that you must be quiet. It is that hurry costs air, and shouting costs air, and the air is the one thing here that is not abundant.

---

Rain comes twice a day, at the gate. The vent-gate is opened at the second bell and the fourth, and cool surface air falls into the throat, meets the held breath of the Cut, and gives up everything it is carrying. For about nine minutes it rains indoors: a fine warm rain, straight down, sweet-smelling, ringing on the perforated plate, filling the gutters, running the cast channels to the sump. The plants take it in through their leaves, which are cupped for it. Then the gate closes and the ceiling goes back to weeping in its own slow arithmetic. Everything between the rains is condensation, and condensation is enough.

---

Fauna is nearly absent, and everyone notices the absence rather than the presence. No ants — no ant will cross the sulfur. No rats, no birds, no flies. What there is: a pipe-moth, palm-sized, dust-colored, that lays in gauge-moss and hatches out of it, of which you might see two in a week; a population of blind pale shrimp in the cistern, whose bodies keep the water sweet and whom no one eats; and, under the sump grate at the flooded apse, one large toad-like animal that has been there longer than any warden and is fed table scraps by tradition, and which nobody claims to have looked at closely.

---

The gradient of the place is a cooling. The seam gives up about one degree a decade. You cannot feel it in a lifetime, but the moss can, and the plants can, and over forty years the whole ecology has crept perhaps twenty paces toward the throat, where the deep heat still comes hardest. The wardens move the trellises to follow. There are terraces at the sump-end, brick and glazed and beautifully built, standing empty now, cold as a cellar, and in them a different moss, the grey kind, the kind that grows anywhere.

The arithmetic is known and written in the ledger. Somewhere around the year eighty the last hot pipe will drop below the boilerfern's line, and the Slow will be walked up the stair in pieces, and the wick-vine will be taken as cuttings to wherever the ground has opened next. The Cut will be left to fill with its own water and go quiet in the fuller sense.

Nobody hurries about it. That is the last rule, and the deepest: things in the Cut that hurry break. Forced fruit rots on the vine. A man who runs faints in nine steps. The engine, if you ungeared it, would shake its own bolts out of the young rock in a week. So the place proceeds at the speed of the beam's stroke and the fig's eleven years and the stone's one degree, and its inhabitants — four wardens, a moth, some shrimp, a toad, and an entire leaning green district oriented toward warm iron — proceed with it, damply, in the dark, punctually rained upon, making light for a town they can hear overhead.
