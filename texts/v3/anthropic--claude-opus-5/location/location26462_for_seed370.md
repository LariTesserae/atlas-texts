## The Hum

You see it first as smoke, from a day away.

There is no fire up there and nothing to burn, but the summit wears a brown veil that never lifts and never drifts — a plume standing perfectly still against the wind, which should be impossible. It is dust. The place exhales a fine flour of itself, always at the same rate, and the plume has reached its own equilibrium: as much powder settling out of it as rising into it. Under that veil the sun is a copper coin. You climb toward a dimness at the top of a bright world.

The last three hundred paces of the slope are scree, and the scree is not stone. It is potsherd and brick-crumb, graded by size the way a beach grades pebbles, though no water has sorted it. Vibration sorted it. Your boots sink to the ankle and everything under you sings faintly, a dry chittering of ceramic on ceramic, and you understand that you have not yet arrived — that this loose skirt is only the outermost fringe of the sound, where the sound has finished with things.

Then the pressure begins.

---

It arrives as a physical object more than a noise. First in the sternum: a low tone, somewhere near the bottom of hearing, that takes hold of the cavity of your chest and plays it. Then, layered above it, a stack of overtones climbing into a shrillness that puts a taste in the back of your throat, like biting foil. Between the low and the high there is a band that has no describable quality at all, only effect — it loosens the joints of your jaw, it makes your eyes water without emotion, it strips language out of you at about eighty paces from the outer wall.

You will not speak again until you have gone back down. Not because of a rule. Because you cannot hear a shout you make with your own mouth an inch from your own ear.

---

The whole summit is made. There is no natural substrate anywhere in it — dig down and you find brick under brick under brick, laid by hand for a depth no one has ever bottomed out. Perhaps eighty paces across, all told. A hamlet's footprint. A cathedral's insistence.

It is a warren: thousands of small chambers, corbelled, each roofed by stacked rings of unfired brick stepping inward to a keystone plug. Cell after cell after cell, sharing walls, packed with no interstice, no corridor that wasn't itself a cell before its ends were opened. Nothing here has room to be beside anything else. Every chamber is a resonator with a single throat — a fired clay tube, thumb-wide to fist-wide, angled through the outer skin to the sky.

The wind at this altitude is a permanent laminar river. The ranges to the west are shaped, by ancient accident, so that they deflect it identically forever; it does not gust, it does not veer with season, it simply *is*, the way a floor is. It crosses the throats. The throats speak. They have been speaking without pause or variation since before the idea of a year.

---

The tone made the architecture.

Consider what a standing wave does to unfired brick over deep time. At the antinodes, where the air's motion is greatest, the surface is scoured — not by grit, but by its own trembling, molecule shaken loose from molecule. Anything at an antinode became dust and left through the throats. At the nodes, where the air is still and only pressure varies, the material is pressed and consolidated, polished, made dense as a river-stone.

So: everything erodible has eroded. What remains is exactly, precisely, the negative of the sound. The walls survive as lenses and webs and thick sagging membranes, fat where the tone is silent, absent where the tone is loud. Whole ranks of cells are open on one flank, their missing side a smooth-lipped absence with no rubble beneath it. Pillars stand that taper to a waist you could close your hand around and then swell again — pinched at antinodes, bellied at nodes. It looks like a ruin caught mid-collapse. It is nothing of the kind. It is finished. It has been finished for a very long time. There is nothing left in it that the tone can touch, and so it will hold this shape until the wind itself changes, which the mountains have arranged never to do.

This is the first rule of the place: *only what agrees with the sound remains.*

---

Inside, the light comes through the throat-holes. Thousands of them, each a needle of copper daylight, all slanting the same way, crossing the dim rooms in a parallel grain. Where a beam meets hanging dust it becomes visible for its whole length, so the interior is ruled and re-ruled with faint bright lines like a comb drawn through smoke. It is dim enough that you move by touch. It is bright enough that you can see the dust is not behaving.

The dust hangs in shapes. Sheets, rosettes, nested shells, long braided cords of powder suspended in the air, motionless. The vibration will not let it fall and the pressure field will not let it wander; it collects in the still places between the loud places and stays there, held, a three-dimensional map of the tone rendered in flour. Some of the forms are the size of your hand. Some fill a chamber like a tree fills a courtyard, branching, frondlike, obscene in their resemblance to something growing.

Nothing is growing. There is no life here at all — no lichen, no spore, no film of anything on the warm brick. The tone is well past any threshold a membrane can hold together across. This is a place that is sterile the way the inside of a bell is sterile, and it is sterile actively, continuously, by pressure, not by poverty.

But the dust-forms will look to you like a garden, and if you break one by walking through it, it will reassemble in the time it takes you to turn around. The field imposes the shape; the shape has no history to lose. You cannot leave a mark here. There is no *here* that accumulates.

---

It is warm. That surprises people at this elevation, with the air so thin it makes your climb a series of small drownings. The warmth is the tone's own waste: friction of air in ten thousand clay throats, of grain against grain in every wall, the endless conversion of motion into heat. The bricks are at blood temperature. Lay your palm flat and you cannot feel where you end. Lay your cheek against a pillar and the note enters through the bone and goes around behind your hearing, and for a moment it is not sound at all but simply the condition of being.

The air is bone-dry. It has been dry so long that the only water left on the summit is chemically bound inside the clay, and even that is leaving — a few molecules per brick per century, wandering out through the throats. At the throat-lips, salts left behind have grown into pale whiskers, fine as hair, an inch long, absolutely rigid, that would break at a breath if any breath here were different from any other. They are the only clock in the place. Read them if you like. They say: *very old, and slower than you.*

---

The makers left three things.

Handprints, inside cells too small to have been entered by anyone who could have made them — which means the cells were built around the hands, which means the hands were reaching in from a scaffolding of wet brick that no longer exists.

Obsidian scraper-marks on the interior lips of the throats, thousands of them, each throat shaved and shaved and re-shaved. No metal anywhere on this mountain, ever. Someone tuned a mountain with a flake of volcanic glass and a memory of a pitch, working by feel because by then nobody could hear anything but the whole.

And cord impressions in the brick — the plaited fiber of a plant that does not grow at this altitude and may not grow anywhere now, hauled up on someone's back, pressed into clay, gone.

There is no fourth thing. No burials, no goods, no marks that mean. Whatever this was for, it was not for after.

---

The second rule: *the tone is the weather.* There is no other element here. No rain, no frost, no season, no night that differs from day except in the color of the needles of light. Nothing here is going anywhere. The plume stands, the dust-fronds hold their shapes, the whiskers of salt lengthen at a rate below noticing, and the note — the single, unvarying, deafening note that the summit has been sustaining since before there was anyone to be deafened by it — does not so much as waver.

You will not stay long. Nobody does. Twenty minutes in and your hands shake and your vision has begun to pulse at the edges in time with something you can't isolate. You go back down through the singing scree, and the pressure peels off you in layers, and at some point on the slope you notice you can hear your own boots again, and then your own breathing, and then the wind, ordinary wind, thin and cold and blessedly stupid.

The note stays in you for three days. Then it thins. Then it is only a thing you know about.

Up there it is still going. It was going while you slept last night. That is the whole of it, really — a small made place, high and warm and dark and full to the brim, holding one sound perfectly still forever, having long ago worn itself into the only shape that sound permits.
