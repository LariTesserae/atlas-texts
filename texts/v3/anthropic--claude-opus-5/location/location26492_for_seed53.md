## The Shelf of Combs

At this altitude the air has given up most of its body. It is still air, it still moves, but it has become thin enough that sound cannot lean on it; a shout carries perhaps thirty paces and arrives as a whisper of itself, so that the whole place behaves as if it were indoors. Everything you hear, you hear close.

The shelf is a shoulder broken off a much larger mountain, high enough that the mountain's summit above it has stopped mattering. It measures perhaps two hundred paces along its length, ninety across at the waist, and it is bounded on three sides by a fall that does not resolve — the eye follows the fall down through a hundred feet of ochre bedding-planes and then loses purchase in the white. On the fourth side stands a wall of dark hornblende rock, glazed with old ice in its cracks, leaning slightly outward as walls do when they intend to remain. The shelf is the whole world here. Beyond it there is only weather and light.

## The white below

Most days a cloud-sea stands two thousand feet under the shelf, unbroken, faintly convex, and this is the second sun. Direct sunlight comes down hard and violet-edged from a sky so dark-blue it seems bruised; but the light that comes *up* off the cloud is soft, dense, and constant, and it is this upward light that gives the shelf its strange quality of having no proper shadows. The undersides of ledges are the brightest surfaces. Overhangs glow. Where a slab has fallen and rests at an angle, the sheltered space beneath it is not dark but luminous, like the inside of a shell. True darkness exists only in the deep seams where two pillars have grown into each other and left a hair-thin gap; those seams hold the only night on the shelf, a black line, a few finger-widths deep, that never opens.

Because the light arrives from both above and below, things here cast two faint shadows that meet and cancel. A standing figure appears to hover very slightly. This is only optics, but it is difficult to unlearn.

## The combs

The floor of the shelf is not a floor. It is a grove.

They are called combs, in the marks left by the people who came, or at least the mark used for them is the mark for a thing with teeth. They stand between the height of a man's chest and four times a man's height, and there are hundreds of them, packed closely enough that you must turn your shoulders and set your feet with intention to pass, and in places closely enough that you cannot pass at all. They are pale — the pale of old fat, of milk-quartz, of an eggshell held to a candle — and they are translucent to a depth of about a thumb's length, so that when the sun crosses them at low angle each one lights from within and the whole grove becomes a room full of standing lamps.

They are not stone in the way the wall behind them is stone. They are sinter: layer laid on layer, a rind at a time, each rind a season. Broken ones show it plainly in cross-section — a thousand concentric laminae, some clear as horn, some clouded with a dusting of red where a hard wind brought grit up from ranges to the south. You can read a pillar the way you would read a cut tree, if trees existed anywhere within a thousand miles of here. They do not. Nothing on this shelf has ever been alive, and the resemblance of the grove to a grove is a coincidence produced by water, cold, and time doing the only things they can do in this place.

Here is how a comb is made. Snow lands. It does not melt in bulk; the air is too cold and too dry, and most of it simply leaves again, vanishing directly into vapour under the hard sun. But a thin film of it, in the hour when direct light strikes wet rock, becomes liquid — a film no thicker than a fingernail's edge, running down the flank of a standing form. That film carries dissolved calcite, wind-borne, from the pale dust that arrives here as sediment from somewhere else. The film travels a hand's breadth, freezes, thaws, and dries, and leaves behind a deposit too small to see. Repeat every day of every bright season for a very long time. The forms grow upward and outward, fed by their own shape: a comb that catches more fog grows faster; a comb that grows faster catches more fog. Their surfaces are fluted vertically, in rounded ribs, like melted candle-wax or like the inside of a mussel shell, and the flutes are the paths of the films, worn and rebuilt by their own water.

Growth is slow. A finger's width in a lifetime, on the fastest of them.

## Waists and falls

The grove is not increasing. It has arrived at itself and stayed there, which is a different condition from stillness.

At the base of each comb, where the meltwater collects and lingers and turns slightly aggressive with dissolved air, the pillar is being eaten. The classic form here is an hourglass: a broad crown, a slender waist a foot or two above the floor, sometimes narrowed to the thickness of a wrist. Combs stand for centuries on these waists. Then one gives. The fall is almost soundless in this air — a dull tap, a rattle of shards, and then the extraordinary quiet afterward. The fallen crown lies on the floor, and within a few decades its own films have welded it down, and the grove begins to build a new comb on top of the ruin, using it as a footing. Every third pillar, if you scrape the crust away at the base, has an older pillar lying dead inside it, crossways.

So: the floor is rubble, cemented; the rubble is former grove; the grove is standing on itself, tier on tier, and if you could take a section through the shelf you would find perhaps forty feet of interlocked pale wreckage before you struck the hornblende. The whole shelf is the grove's own accumulated body. This is what *established* means. Nothing here is new and nothing is finished.

## The frost tide

The single most alive thing about this place happens every clear morning, and it takes about four hours.

At night everything freezes. The grove wakes furred — not with heavy snow but with a bloom of hoarfrost, needles and feathers of ice a half-inch long, grown out of vapour onto every fluted rib, so that at first light the combs look soft. Then the sun clears the eastern rim, and the frost on the lit flanks goes directly to vapour, hissing so quietly you feel it in your teeth rather than hear it. The lit flanks dry. The shaded flanks stay furred.

And the shade moves.

Through the morning a boundary travels across the grove: a slow, ragged, west-going line, wet on one side and dry on the other, with the frost fur standing intact ahead of it and steaming away behind it. Because the pillars are dense and near-touching, the line does not travel smoothly; it snags, splits, runs down corridors, pools in cul-de-sacs, leaves islands of frost behind columns that persist for another hour before being taken. Where the boundary crosses a wide comb, the films run, and the shelf makes its only sustained sound: dripping, uncountable, from hundreds of overhangs at once, none of it loud, all of it near — a sound like a held breath with texture in it.

By midday the grove is dry, blazing, and silent. By late afternoon the shadow tide runs back the other way, eastward, and re-freezes what it passes. Twice a day the wet line crosses the world. Standing still in one place, you are damp, then dry, then damp again, and never wet.

On fog days — when the cloud-sea rises and swallows the shelf, which happens perhaps one day in five — none of this occurs. The grove simply stands in white, condensing, gaining weight, at its work. Fog days are growth days. In fog the light is even stranger than usual: sourceless, total, shadowless, and so bright that you close your eyes and still see white.

## The marks of the people

They did not live here. There is no fuel on this shelf — no wood, no dung, no oil, nothing that burns — and therefore no fire, and therefore no nights. What they left is the residue of visits.

Near the eastern rim, where a broad fallen crown makes a table, someone hollowed a basin. It is a hand-cut cistern, oval, three feet across and a foot deep, and the tool-marks in it are still legible: hundreds of overlapping bruises from a hammerstone of grey quartzite, no metal anywhere, no polishing. Short channels were pecked into the rock above it to lead five separate drip-lines into the basin. The channels still work. The basin still fills, every clear morning, during the passage of the wet line, to a depth of perhaps two inches. Then the sun takes most of it back.

Twenty paces west, three walls of a shelter, built of stacked sinter slabs, corbelled inward as though a roof had been intended; it may never have had one, since it faces the up-light and would be brighter inside without. It would hold four people sitting.

Inside the shelter, cut into the flattest slab, are tallies. Not a tally of days — the groups are too large and too irregular. The best reading is that someone sat here and counted drops.

There is a length of cord, half-fused into the floor crust, spun from the fibrous mineral that occurs in green seams in the wall behind — a stone that can be shredded into wool and twisted. It has not rotted, because there is nothing here that rots. It has simply become part of the ground.

And there are two carved figures, each the length of a finger, both of them representations of combs: fluted, waisted, crowned. It appears that when these people made images, the thing they made images of was this place.

Why they came is not written anywhere, but the cistern answers it well enough. This is water that has never touched soil, never passed through anything, never been anywhere. Every drop of it was vapour an hour ago and dust before that. Somebody, at the cost of a very hard climb, came up here to fill vessels with the emptiest water in the world, and sat in a roofless room counting it into the basin, and went down again before dark.

## The one animal

There is no fauna. In a broken comb near the west end, though, at a depth of about two hundred laminae, there is a wing.

It is a moth's, or something's — two-thirds of it, membranous, brown, entombed in the clear layer and magnified slightly by the curve of the stone above it. It was blown up here from a world with air thick enough to fly in, and it landed on a wet flank, and the flank grew over it, and now it is a fossil roughly three hundred years old, held at eye height, lit from below by cloud-glare.

Everyone who has ever stood in front of it has stood in front of it for a while.

## The rules of this world, plainly

Nothing grows that is not mineral, and everything mineral grows.

Water arrives as vapour, works for one hour a day, and leaves as vapour. It is never abundant and never absent; the shelf is permanently damp and has no puddles.

Light comes from above and below, and shadow exists only in seams too narrow to enter.

Sound does not travel. Whatever you hear is happening within a few paces of you.

Every standing thing is standing on a fallen thing.

The frost tide crosses west in the morning and east in the evening, and this is the closest thing here to a pulse.

And the grove is not waiting for anything. It reached this arrangement a long time ago, and it will maintain it, comb by comb, waist by waist, fall by fall, in the bright cold, for as long as dust continues to blow up from the south.
