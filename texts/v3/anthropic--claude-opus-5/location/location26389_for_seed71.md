## The Sheet

There is a river here, but you will not find its banks. Some unthinkable stretch of time ago it had a channel, then a valley, then a broad shallow trough, and then it wore even that away, and now it is simply the way water lies on the plateau: a film three millimetres deep, moving at the pace of a slow thought, extending in every direction past the curve of sight. They call it the Sheet. You walk on it. It comes over the welt of your boot and does not reach the lace. It is so thin that it takes the temperature of whatever is under it, so that the water is warm over dark stone at midday and locked to grey ice in the shadow of a wall, and the boundary between the two states drifts across the land all afternoon like a slow tide of glass.

Because the Sheet is everywhere and nearly nothing, the whole plateau is a mirror. Look down and the sky is under your feet, only dimmer, and interrupted by your own soles, which appear to be standing on the far side of an enormous window. In the still hours the reflection is so clean that people walking towards you seem to be wading in cloud up to the ankle, and the horizon becomes a seam rather than an edge — the actual difficulty of the place, the first thing a newcomer must learn: *the ground looks like distance.*

## Air

Cold, and thin, and wet in a way that has nothing to do with rain. At this height — and the height is the reason for everything, this is a shelf lifted so far that the lowlands are a rumour in the oldest songs — the air is a dark blue that does not brighten much at noon. Stars stay faintly visible all day, particularly if you shade your eyes with a hand: not many, just the loud ones, hanging there rudely in the afternoon.

Moisture arrives sideways. Fog comes up out of nowhere, condenses on every surface, and drips. Frost forms and sublimes and forms again. Real rain happens perhaps nine times a year and is an event: the towns go out and stand in it. Otherwise the water in this place has to be *harvested*, and the plants do the harvesting.

## The green

Lush, but you will not call it lush at first, because lushness here has been decoupled from crowding.

The dominant life is cushion: enormous domed mounds of a moss-like organism, dense as felt, some the size of a cart, some the size of a plaza. Kneel and press a palm into one and the palm goes in to the wrist, and comes out wet, and the depression fills back over the course of an hour. The cushions are not really individual. Cut one open and it is all the same tissue from one end of the plateau to the other, a single sheet of it under the stone skin, surfacing where there is enough condensation. Everything green here is one green thing with many hats.

And spaced across the cushion-fields, at intervals of hundreds of metres, the fog-trees: black, wet, six or seven metres high, and structured not for photosynthesis but for combing. Each is a candelabrum of fine fibrous rakes, splayed into the prevailing wind, and each stands in a small permanent puddle of its own making, ringed by a brighter richer green. In the morning you hear them working. It is the loudest sound in the world here — the *tk, tk, tk-tk* of drops falling from a thousand fibres onto leaf and stone, one tree at a time as the fog reaches it, so that the sound moves across the plateau like something walking.

Between them: emptiness. Hundreds of paces of wet mirror-stone with nothing on it. This is not barrenness. It is spacing. Everything alive here has settled into the exact distance from everything else at which it can be sustained, and has held that distance so long that the distance has become a fact of the world, like the freezing point of water.

## No animals

There is nothing with a spine. Nothing with legs. No insects in the cushion, no worms under it, nothing in the Sheet but suspended grit and the occasional slick of algae. The fossil beds in the town quarries are dense with them — plates of shells, small skulls with beautiful teeth, wings — but that was in the time when the rules were sharper.

What you get instead are the fetches. On cold mornings, where the fog is thickest and the cushions have breathed out overnight, coherent shapes form in the vapour and *move with intent*. A four-legged thing pacing along a wall. Something low that startles and pours itself sideways when you turn your head. A flock's worth of small quick pressures moving through the air above a fog-tree. They are made of nothing; put your hand through and there is only a chill and a smell of wet stone. They do not react to being walked through, but they react to being *watched* — they always break and diffuse under sustained attention, which is why nobody in the towns looks at them directly, and why children are told the polite thing is to keep walking.

The best current thinking, held by the surveyors and disputed by the potters, is that the fetches are not ghosts of the animals. They are the *habit* of animals, left in the weather. The plateau did this for so long that the air learned it, and the air is slower to forget than the bones were.

## The flattening

Here is the actual condition of the place, and everything else is downstream of it.

Erosion is not confined to rock. Given enough time — and this plateau has had all of it, this is the oldest surviving surface, the final draft — the rules wear down too. What wears out of a rule is its *sharpness*: its capacity to distinguish.

So: temperature still exists, but gradients here are strangely lazy; a hot coal and a cold stone in contact take an unreasonably long time to argue about it, and stew in their difference for hours. Sound still exists, but the law that carries it has been ground shallow, and a shout dies at forty paces as if it had hit a curtain. This is why the plateau is quiet, quieter than the thinness of the air can account for. Two people can hold a conversation. Three can, if they stand close. A crowd cannot; a crowd here is a silent thing that communicates by touch and by placard and by tugging on cords.

And *before* and *after* have been worn nearly flat against each other. Not abolished. Sequence still mostly holds. But it has slack in it, the way an old hinge has slack, and in the slack things arrive early. Bread comes out of the oven at the moment the dough is set in. A cord snaps a half-breath before the strain. A woman calls her son's name and he is already answering, already has been answering, and neither of them finds this remarkable; they find it *convenient*, and they build their day around it, timing tasks so that the useful ones land in the slack.

The deepest wear, though, is on identity. The rule that made two similar things *two* things has thinned almost to transparency. So if you cut two blocks to the same dimensions, from the same seam, and dress them with the same tool, and set them in the same orientation, they will begin — over weeks, over months — to be one block that is in two places. Scratch it in the north and the scratch is in the south. Warm it here and it is warm there. Speak against it in one town and, faintly, in a room nine hundred kilometres away, in the same room, someone hears you.

## The towns

That is the infrastructure. That is the whole civilisation, in fact: a people with hand-tools, hard stone, fired clay, rope, a little soft copper, no wheel worth the name — and a telegraph made of carefully identical rocks.

The towns are low and broad and dry-laid, no mortar, courses of dark plateau-stone fitted with a patience that is difficult to look at for long. They are built and rebuilt on their own rubble, so each town sits on a slight swelling of its own former selves, six or eight metres up out of the mirror, and these mounds are the only topography left in the world. From a fog-tree's height you can see three towns as three low blisters on an absolutely flat wet plain, and nothing else, and the sight of it is the closest thing this place has to grandeur.

Every town has a Common Room: a chamber whose nine walls are cut to the standard, and which is therefore *the same room* in every settlement on the plateau. It is kept scrupulously bare, because clutter breeds confusion about which town's floor you are standing on. Messages are chalked on the walls with the sender's town-mark. Marriages are conducted there between people who will never be within four hundred kilometres of one another. There is one well, and every town has it, and every town draws from it, and it is going dry at the same rate everywhere, and this is the great slow political problem of the age.

The stone is worked with stone and with copper chisels that are re-forged constantly, because copper here is soft even for copper — the metals have been worn towards a kind of general grey obligingness. There is no iron. There may never have been.

## Measurement

The other consequence of the flattening: distance does not add up reliably. Walk a hundred cords east, a hundred north, a hundred west, a hundred south, and you will arrive somewhere within sight of where you began, usually, but not at it, and the discrepancy is not random and not constant. It has weather.

Hence the surveyors, who are the only priesthood, and who are the reason the towns hold together. They walk in pairs with knotted cords and a plumb bob and a slate, and they re-measure the plateau continuously, forever, and post the corrections. The main roads are marked not with milestones but with counted cairns that get recounted every season. A journey is planned the way a sea-crossing is planned: with drift accounted for, with margin, with a willingness to be somewhere else.

A surveyor's cord is dressed with grease and lamp-black. A surveyor's greeting is to state, aloud, the distance to the nearest cairn, and to be corrected, and to thank the corrector. Their prayer, if it is one, is a long recitation of the current known length of the north-south trunk road, and it is different every year, and the difference is the point.

## What still moves

Nothing here is dead. That is the thing visitors get wrong — they see the flatness and the silence and the empty stone and assume they have arrived at an ending. They have arrived at a *final form*, which is not the same.

The fog comes up every night and is combed down every morning. The Sheet moves, immeasurably slowly, in a direction nobody has established, because the direction is longer than the plateau is wide. The cushion breathes and encroaches and dies back with the seasons in slow green waves that take eleven years to cross the map. Ice grows on the north faces and lets go with a tick. The fetches pace the walls at dawn. The wells go down a finger a decade. The identical stones deepen their identity, and occasionally two of them merge so completely that one of them is simply *gone*, leaving a socket of dry air, and the surveyors come and measure the socket and write it down.

And four times a year there is the Rehearsal: a wind out of the flat west that carries, unmistakably, the sounds of a day that has already happened — footsteps, a hammer, a woman laughing forty paces off where nobody is standing. It lasts an hour or two. It is not frightening. The towns stop work and listen, since listening is difficult here and worth doing when the air is briefly generous, and then the wind drops, and the *tk, tk-tk* of the fog-trees resumes, and everyone goes back to fitting stones that will still be fitted when the sun has changed colour.
