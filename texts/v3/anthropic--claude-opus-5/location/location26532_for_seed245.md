## The Thumbprint Plain

### Ground

There is no rock here. Nowhere on this world, at any depth anyone has reached, is there a stone that formed by itself. Go down through the floor — you can, in places, where the surface is still soft enough to open with a rib of fired clay — and you find more floor: courses of hand-laid brick, then a layer of packed grog, then a slab bearing the sweep-marks of a paddle wider than a room, then more brick. Turtles all the way down, and every turtle made by hand.

The surface itself is leather-hard clay, the stage between wet and dry when a body will hold an edge but still take an impression. It is the colour of a liver in dim light — a brown-red with blue-grey bruising where the slip has pooled. Under bare feet it is warm, faintly yielding, and everywhere textured. Thumbprints. Not scattered: *tiled*. The whole continent has been smoothed by pressure from something with a thumb, and the whorls of that thumb are as long as a forearm, and they repeat with the patient irregularity of real work — overlapping, some doubled, some dragged where the maker slid rather than pressed. Water stands in the low spirals. Walk any direction and you walk across a fingerprint the size of a province, and then across its neighbour, and the whorls change hand somewhere out of sight.

Nothing has worn away. This is the first thing a visitor fails to understand. The edges are *new*. A brick laid at the founding has the same crisp arris as one laid an hour ago. Set down a tool and its outline remains in the slip until something deliberately removes it. There is no weather that takes; only weather that adds.

### Heat and the wet

The heat comes from underneath. It arrives through the floor as a steady, animal warmth, and in the seams and unmortared joints it arrives as a dull breath you can see — a shimmer, a smell like hot ceramic and pond. The world is being fired from within. Not fast; nothing here is fast. The kiln under the floor has been climbing toward temperature for a duration the plain has no word for, and the surface is only ever the outside of a vessel wall.

Because the underneath is hot and the ceiling is cold, this world's whole meteorology is condensation. There is no sea. There is a sky that sweats.

Water gathers on the underside of the lid — the grey vault far above, which is not cloud and not stone but something with the sheen of unfired porcelain — and falls in long, warm, vertical strings. It falls slowly. It falls in *slip*: water thickened with suspended fines it picked up on the way down, so the rain is milky, and it beads on skin, and it dries as a fine pale film on everything, which is why the plain always looks slightly dusty and is always slightly damp. In the low ground the slip-rain pools into flats that never drain, only thicken — mudglass, tacky, breathing. Beyond the ankle-deep edge you would go down and not come up, and the surface would close and be smooth again, and there would be no mark, and this is the only erasure the world reliably performs.

The rocks weep. All of them, being brick, wick water up out of the near-surface table and hold it in their faces, so that any raised structure has a dark waterline that rises and falls with no schedule and stains upward past its own top.

### Light

Dim, and from the wrong direction. Roughly a third of the light is a diffuse grey from the lid — enough to see by, never enough to cast a shadow. The rest comes up: through hairline cracks, through the mouths of open seams, through the porous places where the brick has been laid loose on purpose. It is orange, and it moves. Standing over a seam, your face is lit from below like someone bending to a hearth, and your shadow is thrown up onto the underside of the falling rain, and hangs there, spread and wobbling, until the rain moves on.

So: a world where every shadow is above you.

### The scattered things

For a very long walk there is nothing but floor, rain-string, and steam. Then something.

A wall, shoulder-high, three hundred paces long, going nowhere, with a doorway at the exact middle and nothing on either side of it. The brickwork immaculate. The mortar not yet skinned over.

A stack of tools: ribs, scrapers, cord-wrapped beaters, all of them fired clay because there is no wood and no bone and no metal on this world at all. The tools are made of the world, fired harder than the world, and used to shape the world, and when they break their pieces are crushed for grog and go back into the body. Everything here is tempered with its own past. Sherds of older tools turn up in every fresh cut, and you can read them: this rib was once part of a floor; this floor was once a wall; this wall was once a rib.

A cord-loom of extruded threads — clay drawn out fine, dried leathery, twisted into rope that holds a knot and stinks of wet earth.

A kiln, cold, with its arch collapsed inward and no ash inside, because there has never been anything here to burn.

An armature: a lattice of fired rods lashed with clay cordage, standing four times the height of a person, describing in air the shape of something that has not been given a surface yet. Rain drips off its joints. Nothing has begun to fill it in. Nothing has stopped, either.

And then floor again, for hours.

### The work

No one is here. This is not the same as saying nothing is happening.

The making proceeds. You can watch it. A patch of floor perhaps twenty paces across will darken and go wet — slumping to slurry, the thumbprints dissolving — and then set again with new prints in a new alignment, and the seam where the old texture meets the new is as raw as a fresh cut. Somewhere out in the steam a wall goes up over the course of a morning, brick by audible brick, each with its little wet knock of placement, and nothing is visible near it. Somewhere else a wall comes down.

The rate is high and the noise is low. That is the thing about handwork at continental scale: it is a great many quiet events. Ten thousand knocks a minute across a landmass sounds, from where you stand, like almost nothing — a tick, a settle, a distant slump, a long silence, the string-rain hissing where it hits the hot places. Then a collapse somewhere far off that you feel through the soles rather than hear, and then quiet again.

And the work is *drafts.* This is the plain's most obvious madness. Ridges come in threes: the same low ridge, the same profile, laid out parallel within a few hundred paces, two of them abandoned partway with their courses left ragged, the third continuing. A doorway will be attempted eleven times along one wall, all eleven finished, the wall now more absence than presence. Whatever is making this world is not building; it is *practising*. Nothing has yet been approved. There is no reason to think anything ever will be.

### Rules

**Scale is artisanal.** Distance is measured in arm's-lengths, and the world enforces the measure rather than the metre. An object made by a hand appears hand-sized at any remove; the far wall does not shrink, it stays the size of the reach that made it, and so the horizon is crowded with things you cannot judge, and things a day's walk away can be touched with a stretch. Travellers learn to trust their feet and disbelieve their eyes.

**The mark precedes the hand.** Impressions appear before pressure. A thumbprint fills in the wet clay, the ridges rising, and only afterward — sometimes hours after — does something press down into it, exactly, and the fit is perfect, and nothing was ever visible. The causal order here runs mark first. This is why the plain feels less like a construction site and more like a page being written in a language whose grammar puts the wound before the knife.

**Up is provisional in some districts.** There are regions, always low-lying, always steaming, where the vertical has not been fixed. Rain arrives on the horizontal. Water stands in sheets on nothing. Set down a tool and it settles into the floor sideways. You can walk through these places if you keep your eyes on your own hands, and out the other side, and the sensation afterward is of having briefly been an unfinished draft yourself.

**Nothing decays; things get overwritten.** The only aging available on this world is being remade. Which means that everything you see is either brand-new or has been brand-new for an unmeasurable span, and there is no way to tell which, and asking how old the plain is produces the answer: *not yet.*

### The blooms

There is no life. The plain grows nothing, hosts nothing, is visited by nothing that eats.

But where slip-rain stands on hot brick, the water goes and the salts stay, and the salts creep. White, feathery efflorescence spreads across a damp wall face in structures of increasing complexity — filaments, then branchings, then something like a fruiting body, then something like a colony with a boundary it defends against a neighbouring colony's boundary. The blooms move up-gradient toward damp. They avoid one another. They are, when examined, entirely mineral: salt, fines, water, no membrane, no metabolism, nothing that could be called an inside.

The blooms are the only things here that were not made on purpose, and they are the only things here that behave as though they want something. Whatever is making this world scrapes them off. They come back. They are the only argument the plain has ever lost.

### The seam

Walk far enough in the direction the thumbprints lean and the floor ends.

Not a cliff — the elevation here barely varies, there are no heights on this world worth the name. It ends the way a table ends. A last course of brick, mortar still bright, laid dead level. And beyond it: a low, level expanse of grey unworked body. Pug. Prepared clay, wedged, de-aired, waiting, going to the edge of sight and past it, steaming gently, with the string-rain falling into it and not disturbing it at all.

That is not emptiness. That is stock.

The seam is where the knocking is loudest, which is to say audible: a slow, patient, near-inaudible tick of courses being added, one brick, then a pause, then one brick. The world grows outward at the pace of hands. It has been growing for the whole of its incomprehensible duration and it is not yet finished with its practice pieces.

If there is a plan, it is on the far side of the firing. Everything currently visible — the walls to nowhere, the eleven doorways, the armature with no skin, the great tiling thumbs, the salt colonies fighting their patient war on a wet brick face in orange light from below — all of it is the state of a thing that has not yet been *made*, only worked, and is warm, and is damp, and holds an impression.
