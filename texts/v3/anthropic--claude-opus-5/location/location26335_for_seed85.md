## The Flat

Begin above it, because the approach explains everything.

At four thousand metres the range comes to an end not in peaks but in a rim — a ring of black glass teeth, freshly split, each face still bearing the conchoidal scallops of the fracture that made it. Nothing has weathered them. There is no lichen on them, no soot, no stain of rain, no grit at their feet. They are as clean as a broken bottle two seconds old, and they have been that way for a very long time.

Inside the ring lies water. It has no surface texture at all. Not calm in the way lakes are calm — calm lakes breathe, they carry the ghost of a swell, they pucker under a passing thermal. This does not. From the rim it reads as a hole in the world, a disc of absolutely nothing, because it is a perfect mirror and the sky above it is unlit. The line where water meets stone is the sharpest line you will ever see. You could cut yourself on the definition of it.

Step in and the mirror does not break. It parts around you with a seam that closes and is instantly indistinguishable, and your wake — you will look for your wake — is gone before you have finished turning to look for it. The water forgets you as fast as you disturb it.

## Descending

The lake is shallow: sixty, eighty metres over the summit plateau. You are underwater and still at altitude; the pressure at the bottom is barely more than sea-level air, and the cold is a thin, hard cold, five or six degrees, exactly, everywhere. Say that again, because it is the central fact of the place: five point eight degrees, *everywhere*, to more decimal places than instruments usually bother with. Not five point eight at the bottom and five point nine near the rock. Not a warmer skin under the surface where the day fell in. There are no gradients here of any kind. No thermocline, no haloclines, no pressure-driven drift. And where there is no gradient there is no convection, and where there is no convection there is no current, and where there is no current there is no erosion, and so the mountain beneath stays young forever.

The water is not quite clear. It carries a suspension of mineral fines, particles so small they will never settle — held up by their own thermal jitter against a gravity they are too light to obey. This gives the water a body, a faint grey-blue smoke that swallows sightlines at about forty metres. It is why the place is dark. What little light reaches the surface is scattered into a general dimness before it gets halfway down, so that the bottom exists in something like the last eight minutes of dusk, permanently.

## The city

It sits in a basin a kilometre and a bit across: perhaps three hundred structures, and space between all of them. This is not a dense place. You can walk — you do walk, the water is too thin and cold to swim in comfortably and the floor is flat glass — for two minutes between one building and the next, seeing nothing but the haze and the mirrored ceiling.

The structures are low. The tallest reaches maybe a third of the way to the surface. They are made of a material that reads as basalt glass until you get close and find it has no grain, no bubbles, no inclusions, and edges that do not exist at any magnification you can bring — corners that simply stop being one surface and start being another with no radius at all. Nothing has fouled them. There is no biofilm, no dust, no calcification. Run a hand along one and there is no drag; the water beside the wall is not slowed by the wall.

The plan of the city is not a grid and not organic. It looks like the answer to a problem you cannot see the statement of. Buildings stand at distances from one another that feel measured, and turned to angles that feel measured, and there is no street because there is no traffic, and there are no windows because there is nothing outside worth looking at, and there are doorways — many doorways — that are only doorways, standing free, framing the same haze on both sides.

Some of the structures do have interiors. Enter one and the water inside is a different water: the suspension is absent, the view goes clear to the far wall, and there is a sense — you will not be able to defend this in a report — that the water in there is older. It sits inside its room the way a held breath sits in a chest.

## What it is for

The city is a machine for preventing something.

You infer this slowly. The isothermy is not an accident of a dead place; a dead place would still have a gradient, would still leak heat to the peaks at night and take a little sun by day. This is *maintained*. Somewhere under the glass floor, or in the walls, or in the arrangement itself, there is engineering that moves heat from where there is too much to where there is too little, continuously, at a resolution finer than a millimetre and a precision finer than a millikelvin, and it does this everywhere at once and has done it without interruption for however long the rim has been unweathered. The energy budget of that does not close by any accounting you know. You can measure it. It does not close.

The best guess anyone offers: entropy production in this basin has been driven near enough to zero that nothing can *happen* here. No chemistry can run. No engine can turn, including the very small engines that living cells are, which is why there is no plant, no alga, no mat, no spore, nothing green or brown or slick — not because life was killed, but because life is a gradient-eating process and there is nothing here to eat. It is not post-life. It is pre-nothing. It is a place where the arrow of time has been laid flat and pinned at both ends.

And something is being kept, in the middle of that flatness, from resolving. That is the shape of the intuition. Some event, some decision, some measurement, held open in a room with older water in it, undecided for as long as the stillness holds.

## Sound

Very little. Water carries sound four times better than air, so what you hear, you hear with unreasonable clarity and no direction.

Your own noise dominates: the crunch of glass grit under your boots (there is grit; the mountain is young and broke recently, and nothing has moved the pieces since), the creak of your suit, the appallingly loud liquid business of your own body, which is the warmest and most disorderly object for a kilometre in every direction.

Beyond that: a tone. Once every four hundred and some seconds, a single note, very pure, quite low, from no locatable source. It does not echo — the mirror ceiling should give an echo and does not. It is best understood as the machine checking itself, saying *still flat*, and receiving no answer because no answer is needed.

And occasionally, rarely, a tick — like a fingernail on ice, from a long way off, once, never twice.

## The ceiling

Look up. The underside of the surface is a flawless mirror, and it doubles everything. Above the city hangs a second city, hanging downward, exact, and the space between the two is the true volume of the place — a slab of dim grey water with a settlement on the floor and a settlement on the roof and nothing at all between them but the smoke of unsettling dust.

Walk, and your reflection walks upside down above you at the same pace, sixty metres up, feet to feet.

Nothing ever ruffles it. There is no wind above because the rim shields the basin and the air over the lake is as isothermal as the water, and a wind is only a gradient with somewhere to go.

## Fauna

There are animals here. Not many; you might see one in a week.

They are visitors, all of them, because nothing can live in the Flat, only pass through it carrying its own weather. The commonest are the shell-things: palm-sized, translucent, with a hard cap under which they keep a small pocket of warmth and disequilibrium — a battery of chemistry brought in from outside. They cross the basin in slow purposeful lines, trailing a filament of very slightly warmer water that the city erases behind them within a few centimetres. They cannot stay. They come in over the rim, cross, and go out the other side, and what they are doing is not known. On the glass floor you can sometimes find a spent one: a clear cap, a small mineral sediment, no rot, no scavengers, no decay, lying exactly where it stopped. Some of them have been lying there a very long time and look brand new.

Once, in the records, something larger: a long pale shape, ten metres, moving without any visible propulsion, passing under the ceiling so that it and its reflection met at the vertical middle of the world. It did not disturb the mirror. It did not disturb anything.

## Rules

Whatever you bring in is brought to five point eight degrees, faster than it should be.

Whatever you stir un-stirs.

Whatever you break stays broken exactly as you left it, forever, sharp.

Nothing rots, nothing rusts, nothing settles, nothing grows.

The light does not change from hour to hour or season to season, and the water does not change, and the temperature does not change, and the tone comes every four hundred-odd seconds and will do so after you have gone.

You are the only event.
