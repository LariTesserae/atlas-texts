## The Kettle

Nineteen years ago this was a bubble of gas inside a running flow of basalt, three hundred metres across and sixty high, holding its shape only because the rock around it cooled faster than it could collapse. Then the flow stopped, the bubble stayed, and the mountain closed over it. Four years ago a survey drill came through the roof and gave it air.

The floor is ropy pahoehoe, black and glassy, buckled into long coils like rope dropped from a great height and never picked up. In places it has slumped into shallow basins; in places it has cracked open into fissures a hand wide that still breathe warm. Nothing has had time to soften. Every edge is sharp. Where a boot scuffs the crust it takes off a flake of glass, and the flake does not weather — it just lies there, sharp, for years. There is no soil anywhere in the Kettle except what people carried down in sacks.

The walls are vesicular, riddled with holes like frozen foam, and they are warm to the touch — forty-eight, fifty degrees in the Low Quarter near the eastern vents, dropping to a merciful thirty-four up at the shaft mouth where the cold comes in. Ambient in the middle tiers holds around forty-three. Everyone wears wet cloth. Everyone re-wets it at the standing troughs every half hour, and the habit is so deep that people re-wet their collars in their sleep, reaching out of the hammock without waking.

---

The town clings to the walls in seven tiers.

There is almost no building on the floor — the floor is for the fleeces and the traffic and the sun-walk — so the settlement went upward, cast in slag brick and sheeted in board. The board is made here: they take the wall itself, feed chips of it into the melt-eye, and spin it into fibre. Basalt wool, grey-brown, itchy as sin, pressed into panels, woven into mats, drawn into thread. The Kettle is a place that turns the mountain into cloth. Every wall of every room is the room's own bedrock, reprocessed.

The tiers are stitched together by catwalks and ladder-runs and one good stair called the Spine that switchbacks the whole north wall. Density here is a matter of arithmetic: four hundred and ten people, in a volume the size of a large church, in a heat that makes crowding dangerous. So the buildings are narrow and tall and touch each other, and the alleys between them are shoulder-width, and the whole thing is hung with fibre curtains to break the radiant heat off the rock — layers on layers, so that walking the third tier is like walking through a closet of grey coats. You feel the town more than you see it. Doorways breathe past you. Someone's condenser drips on your neck.

---

**Water.** There is no river. There is no rain. There is the rock, exhaling.

The basalt above is still cooling, and groundwater from the mountain's flank seeps toward that heat and comes through the ceiling as vapour, invisible, through a thousand hairline cracks. The Kettle's entire economy is the interception of that vapour before it escapes up the shaft.

The instrument is the fleece: a hanging panel of basalt-fibre mesh, three metres by two, strung on a frame and kept cool by a coil of pipe running shaft-air through its top edge. Water beads on the fibre, runs down, collects in a gutter, drips into a jar. One fleece yields perhaps nine litres a day. There are four hundred fleeces. They hang across the whole floor of the chamber in ranks, grey and softly luminous under the lamps, and walking among them is the quietest thing you can do here — the fleece-field absorbs sound the way snow does. You hear only your own feet, and the drip, and, far off, the pumps.

Ownership rules are simple and absolute: the water belongs to the fleece, the fleece belongs to a household, and moving another household's frame — even a hand's width, even to sweep behind it — is the one offence the Kettle punishes with expulsion up the shaft.

There is also the Weep, a seep in the southeast wall that produces about forty litres a day of hot brine, undrinkable, useful. It supplies the salt trade and it feeds the boilers.

---

**Breath.** Twice a day the surface pressure changes and the Kettle inhales and exhales through its single shaft.

On the in-draft, cold air pours down the shaft in a visible white column, hits the hot floor, and the whole chamber fogs from the ankles up in about four minutes. The fleeces run heavy. Visibility drops to twenty metres. Lamps become haloes. This is the good time, the working time, and the bell rings for it.

On the out-draft, the fog is drawn up and gone, and the walls bloom.

This is the part visitors do not believe until they see it. As the humid air leaves the porous rock, the brine in the pores crystallises, and white needles of salt grow out of the wall — visibly, in minutes, a fur of fine glassy hair spreading across whole square metres. In an hour there can be a finger's depth of it. It is beautiful and it is a nuisance: it blocks the vapour cracks, it fouls the fleeces, it makes the catwalk rails cut your palms. So every household sweeps its own frontage twice a day, and the sound of four hundred people sweeping salt off basalt with fibre brooms is the closest thing the Kettle has to a chorus. It is a dry, soft, sibilant noise, and it lasts about twenty minutes, and then it stops all at once.

The salt goes to the pans. Some of it goes back into the fibre as a binder. Nothing is thrown away, because the shaft is four hundred metres of ladder and every kilogram costs a person's back.

---

**Light.** Dim, deliberately. Light is heat and heat is the enemy, so the lamp-strings are run low — a string of small orange filaments along each catwalk, enough to see edges by, not enough to read by. Most reading is done in the melt-house, where the eye of the furnace throws a white glare that will burn your retina if you stare at it, and where the fibre-spinners work in goggles.

And once a day, for about forty minutes in the early afternoon, the sun crosses the mouth of the shaft, and a coin of true daylight travels across the fleece-field on the floor.

They call it the sun-walk. Its path is charted on a board in the Assembly. Children are taken to stand in it. Old people arrange their day around it. The rules around the sun-walk are courtesy rules, not law: you don't crowd it, you don't stand in it more than a count of thirty if others are waiting, you don't bring work into it. It is the only part of the Kettle that is not managed.

---

**Green.** There is not much. Total living plant mass in the Kettle is perhaps two hundred kilograms.

Ash-cress grows in the fissure cracks of the third tier, pale and rubbery, tolerant of the heat, harvested young. There are eleven beds of it. There is a stand of reed in the brine pans, planted for the fibre, doing badly. There is a wall of grey lichen-analogue in the cold corner by the shaft foot that no one planted and no one can quite explain, and which is left alone on principle.

And there is the fig.

It stands at the shaft foot in a cast tub of imported soil, in the coldest three metres of the Kettle, watered with condensate from a dedicated fleece, and it is nine years old and four metres tall and it has never fruited. It is the tallest living thing below the surface for a hundred kilometres. People touch its trunk on their way to the ladder. The leaf-litter it drops is collected, weighed, composted, and returned to it, so that the fig is very nearly a closed system with itself.

---

**No animals.** None. Not one.

Not by accident — by policy and by youth together. The Kettle is nineteen years old and sterile; nothing has walked or crawled or flown down four hundred metres of shaft on its own. And nothing is permitted to. Everything that descends is heat-treated at the shaft head, and the descent log records every crate. There are no rats, no roaches, no flies, no ants, no spiders in the corners, no birds at the light. The fig is hand-pollinated with a squirrel-hair brush by a woman named Osme who inherited the job from her mother, and who has never seen a squirrel.

The absence is the first thing an outsider notices, though it takes them a day to name it. No animal sound. No animal movement in the periphery. The stillness of the fleece-field is complete — the panels only move when the chamber breathes, and then they all move together, four hundred at once, a single slow shrug across the whole floor. It is the most unnerving thing in the Kettle and the residents find it soothing.

---

**Sound.** The town is quiet. The fibre eats noise, the crowding makes people considerate, the heat makes shouting expensive. The steady layer is: the pumps, two of them, at eleven strokes a minute; the drip of four hundred fleeces into four hundred jars, which averages out to a soft continuous static; the hiss of the vents.

Above that layer, the rock talks. The basalt is still cooling and still cracking, and it ticks and pops at unpredictable intervals — a sharp report like a knuckle, sometimes a long tearing groan from somewhere in the ceiling. There is a duty called the Listener, a two-hour watch kept round the clock in a chair at the centre of the floor, whose entire job is to write down what the rock says and when. Nine times in four years the Listener has called an evacuation of a quarter. Six of those were wrong. Three of them opened new vents. The Listener's log is the most-read document in the Kettle.

On the out-draft, if you are alone in the fleece-field, the shaft draws air past the vent-cracks and the whole chamber produces a low tone, somewhere near the bottom of hearing, that changes pitch as the salt closes the cracks one by one. It is not a voice. Everyone agrees it is not a voice. The younger workers have words for what it says anyway.

---

**Rules of the place**, as posted at the shaft foot on a plate of cast slag:

Wet your cloth.
Do not touch bare rock below the third tier.
Sweep your frontage.
Another's fleece is another's water.
Log everything you bring down.
When the Listener calls, go up.

Below that, scratched in by somebody and never removed:

*Nothing lives here that we did not carry.*
