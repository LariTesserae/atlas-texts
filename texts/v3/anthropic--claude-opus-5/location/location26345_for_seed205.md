## The Thatch

There is no ground here. Understand that first, because everything else follows from it.

Nine days' walk in from the rim, the living surface is roughly two thousand metres above the hot brown plains you came from, and every one of those two thousand metres is plant. Not soil with plants in it. Plant: cane and cane-corpse, fibre laid on fibre, root threaded through the mummified stems of roots that grew when the plains were a sea. The Thatch is a continent that grew itself upward and then stopped, having reached a height at which it was comfortable. It is not a mountain that vegetation colonised. It is vegetation that became a mountain, and it wears its own weather like a hat.

### The surface

Walk out onto it at first light and it looks almost empty, which is the great joke of the place. Underfoot: a springy ochre nap of cane-tips, each stem the thickness of a wrist, cut off flat by wind and heat at knee height, packed so close that the whole plain resolves into a single texture, like the cropped pile of an enormous rug. It gives about a hand's width under your weight and pushes back. Walking is easy and strange; you feel that you are being handed forward.

And above that surface — space. Emptiness. You can see thirty kilometres and count perhaps four things standing in it.

Those things are the Standers. Each one is a single cane that, for reasons nobody has settled, kept going: eighty, ninety, a hundred and forty metres of grey-green column, jointed like bamboo, swollen at the nodes into rooms. They lean. They are all leaning slightly downwind, and because the wind on the Thatch has run the same direction for a geological age, they lean in parallel, so that the whole horizon is faintly italic. A Stander is an island. Its own bracket-fungi, its own lodger vines, its own quarrelling population of things with claws. From a distance, the great ragged mops of leaf at their crowns are the only green worth the name; everything else is the colour of old rope.

Between them: nap, nap, nap, and the shimmer of heat, and the sparse punctuation of vent-mounds where the interior exhales.

### The Draw

By the third hour after dawn the sound has begun, and by noon you cannot speak, cannot think in words, cannot hear your own scream if you make one.

This is the Draw. Two thousand metres of standing water column in the Thatch's vessels, pulled upward by a sun at 2.8 of anything's scale, evaporating out of numberless stomata — and the cane, being hollow and jointed, whistles. Each stem's note is inaudible. There are perhaps ten to the fourteenth power of them. The result is not a whistle but a wall: a roar that arrives from below and from every direction at once, a white-and-brass noise with a shuddering low beat under it, felt in the sternum and the teeth and the wet parts of the eye. Sand-grains dance on the nap. Dropped objects buzz.

You learn to read it, the way sailors read water. Thin bright roar: shallow mat, deadwood close beneath, a bad place to camp because there is no cool to be had. Fat roar with a wobble in it: deep vessels, good tapping. A patch of *quiet* — a hole in the noise, a soft grey circle of near-silence a hundred metres across — means a hollowing: the mat beneath has died and rotted and you are standing on a lid. People have gone into hollowings and not come out. The quiet is the most frightening thing on the Thatch. Everyone here has a superstition about it and none of the superstitions agree.

At dusk the Draw slackens, sighs down through a long descending series of beats, and for about twenty minutes there is something like calm. Then the night-things start, and it gets loud again, differently: insect, throat, wing, the *ratcheting* of the deep choir in the galleries below, which is a sound with the texture of gravel poured for hours down a metal chute.

Nobody on the Thatch has ever heard silence. There is a word for it and it is used as a word for death.

### Water

It almost never rains on top. That is the other joke: the wettest thing in a thousand kilometres is the inside of the plant you are standing on, and the surface is a desert.

But every afternoon the Thatch sweats a cloud. The transpiration column rises, cools, and lids the whole continent in a low white overcast, thick enough to take the murder out of the light — which is why the light here reads as ordinary, mild, an even shadowless white, a permanent late-afternoon. The plateau shades itself. It has been managing this for longer than there have been people to notice.

And then in the third hour of dark the lid drops its water back down as fog, and the fog beads on the cane-tips, and the nap runs with condensate and turns almost black, and the whole plain gleams and ticks. That is the Dew Hour. Everything that lives above the surface does its living in it. Everything that drinks, drinks then, licking stems, sucking mats of collected fur, cupping leaves. By the second hour of light it is gone.

Real rain belongs to the rim, where the monsoon breaks against the escarpment and undercuts it. The edges of the Thatch calve. A cliff of black lignin, sheer and fluted, three hundred metres of compressed dead cane like slate that will burn — and every few years another slab of it lets go and goes down onto the plain with a sound the interior carries inward for a full day. The rim is where the Thatch is losing. The centre is where it is winning. Between them, across a whole continent, it comes out even, and has come out even for a very long time. Nothing here is growing. Nothing here is dying. Both, at identical rates, in balance, weathered into a shape it has no reason to leave.

### The inside

At noon, when the Draw makes the surface uninhabitable, you go down.

The vent-mounds are entrances: raised collars of scar-tissue cane, worn smooth by generations of shoulders. Under them the Thatch opens into the Weft, and the Weft is the actual world.

It is cool. Fifteen, sixteen degrees, and damp, everything furred with condensate, and the noise up there becomes a distant industrial hum through metres of fibre. Down here the light is a dim green-brown filtering through translucent internodes, plus whatever the lantern-galls give off. The architecture is entirely accidental and entirely coherent: canes crossing canes, fused at the crossings, so the space is a lattice of galleries and rooms and chimneys and slumps, on scales from a fist to a cathedral. Nobody built it. Nobody could. It has been added to and rotted through for ten thousand years and settled into something that feels designed.

And it is *full*. This is where the fauna went.

The overwhelming population of the Thatch does not live on it; it lives in it, in the cool wet dark, six hundred metres of habitable depth stacked in layers, and the biomass per cubic metre down there is a number that makes no sense from above. Blind pale grazers rasping at the inner walls, whole herds of them, hand-sized, moving in sheets. The nymphs of the choir, packed into their cells like grain, forty years of them, all stridulating. Rope-snakes hunting in the ceiling lattices. Things with too many legs and no eyes and an apologetic manner. Trumpeters — the big ones, three metres at the shoulder, shaped something like a tapir made of loose hide, who lever their way through the wide lower galleries and whose calls come up through the vent-mounds at night as bass blasts that make the nap ripple. And insects at every scale, and mites on the insects, and the whole thing wet and clicking and warm-blooded-smelling and absolutely alive.

You can walk from one side of the Thatch to the other without ever seeing the sky. People have. There are families who consider the surface a place you visit.

### The people, and their small marks

There are not many, and they are scattered, and they will not add up to a civilisation.

They work in wood and bone and horn and gut and glass — glass, because cane-ash flux and a hollowing fire will give you a rough greenish bead, and because they need pipes. Their whole material culture is a plumbing culture. The great craft of the Thatch is the tap: a bored horn spigot, driven with a mallet at the correct angle into the correct vessel of the correct cane, at the correct depth, which you find by ear. A good tapper is a person who can hear water eighty centimetres inside a living stem while standing in a noise that would rupture your ear if you were not born to it. A tap yields for a season, then the cane walls it off. Bad tapping kills a stem; killing a stem is the only crime everyone agrees on.

Their structures are small and few, and they are almost all at Standers. A woven cane platform in a node-room, forty metres up. Drying racks. A ladder that is three centuries old and has been entirely replaced twice. Rain-combs — vast loose brooms of split cane lashed near a Stander's crown to strip fog out of the night air and run it down a spiral gutter into a horn cistern; from a distance a combed Stander looks shaggy, wrong, like a spear wearing a wig. Cairns of harvested nodes marking a route. A single leaning post, carved, where somebody's people once wintered.

And the husk-houses, which are not theirs.

### The castings

Here is the thing about the Thatch that visitors are not told until they have been there a while.

The mat grows around what falls on it. A dead trumpeter, a lost basket, a person — the nap closes over in a season and the cane grows through and around, and the flesh goes into the fibre, and after thirty or forty years, at the surface, the Thatch extrudes a shape.

They are hollow, and made of fused cane, and roughly right. A trumpeter's casting is a trumpeter-shaped shell the size of a hut, ribbed, hollow, with the legs wrong. A person's casting is person-shaped and about a third too large, and the hands are always in a different position than the hands were. The people of the Thatch do not treat this as horror and do not treat it as a gift. They treat it as weather. You will find castings scattered across the empty nap, twenty kilometres apart, standing alone in the shimmer, and if a casting is big enough and sound enough somebody will cut a door in it and store rope in it, and camp in it during Dew Hour, and leave it for the next party, and nobody will feel any way about that at all.

There is a school of thought among the tappers that the plateau is not recording anything, that this is only the way a fibre grows around a rot-void, that the resemblance is the resemblance of a footprint and means nothing. There is another school which points out that the castings sometimes appear for things which were never lost on the Thatch at all. Animals from the plains. Shapes of shapes nobody knows.

Both schools tap the same canes, and neither one goes into the quiet places, and the Draw comes up every morning at the third hour and drowns the argument out.
