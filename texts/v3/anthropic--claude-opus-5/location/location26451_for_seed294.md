## The Bowl

Begin at the rim, because there is no other honest place to begin. You come up out of the scree country — cold, thin, blue-grey — and the ground stops being ground. It becomes a wall. Not a cliff: a wall, six or seven courses of it still visible above the waterline, each block longer than a man lying down, set without mortar and worn until the joints have gone soft as old soap. The wall runs left and right until distance eats it. It encircles something like a valley, and the valley is full of water.

The water is the colour of pewter under cloud, and there is always cloud. At this altitude the sky is a lid rather than a space; the light that arrives has already been strained twice, through vapour and through the standing haze of suspended mineral flour that the Bowl never quite settles. Call the light *dim*, and mean it kindly. You can see to read the shape of the far rim, maybe nine kilometres off, as a smudge slightly darker than the smudge above it. You cannot see your own hand's shadow. Nothing here has cast a sharp shadow in a very long time.

Go under. It is easier than it sounds; the shallows shelve gently, in steps.

---

The steps are the first thing to understand. The entire floor of the Bowl is terraced. Not eroded into terraces — *cut*, or laid, or both: broad shelves descending in irregular flights from the rim toward a central depth, each shelf faced with fitted stone, each riser between one and three metres. In places the flights are wide enough to be plazas; in places they narrow into ramps and dogleg down through gaps in retaining walls. There are sluice channels, dozens of them, radiating outward and downward, floored with slabs, walled with slabs, roofed in a few stretches with corbelled slabs that have slumped into low tunnels you could swim through if you were braver than sensible.

There is no natural rock anywhere in the Bowl. This is not a figure of speech. Where the terracing has failed — and it has failed everywhere, in the patient way things fail over unthinkable time — the collapse reveals more construction beneath: older facing, older fill, packed rubble that turns out on close inspection to be *dressed* rubble, the broken bones of some prior version of the same idea. Dig, and you find building. Dig deeper, building. Whatever substrate the Bowl once had is gone or was never there, and the question of a foundation stops being a question and becomes a kind of vertigo.

The stone is a grey-green material, dense, faintly translucent at broken edges, cold to the palm in the way of very old things. It takes water-polish beautifully. Every horizontal surface in the Bowl has been buffed by seventy or eighty million overturnings into a slick, slightly convex sheen, so that the terraces read less like architecture than like the interior of a shell — the same geometry, everywhere the corners rounded off, the sharp intent of the original design blunted into something almost organic. Right angles have become obtuse. Steps have become slopes. The whole made world is halfway to being a smooth bowl again, and it will get there, and there is no urgency in it.

---

Nothing grows. Not because life is absent — life is thick here — but because there are no plants and never were, or none that left a record. It is too cold and too dim; the light that reaches the deep terraces would not fuel a leaf. What coats the stone instead is a film so thin you'd call it discolouration: bacterial varnish, mineral-eaters, chemosynthetic mats in shades of iron-red and bruise-purple where warm seeps push through the seams of the masonry. In the sluice mouths, where the seeps are strongest, the mats thicken into low crusts and ropy drapes, and around each seep the fauna gather like a congregation.

And the fauna are abundant, and they are all builders.

This is the thing that makes the Bowl what it is. There are no tools here, no fire, no devices, no wheel, no wire — and yet everything is constructed, because construction is simply what animals do in this water.

The commonest are the **mound-makers**: flat, palm-sized, plated things, more like a trilobite's rumour than a crab, that crawl the terraces gathering silt and cementing it with a secretion into chimneys and cones and long low walls. They work in loose colonies, hundreds of metres apart, so that the terraces are dotted rather than covered — a field of chimneys, then two hundred metres of bare polished stone, then a single ancient mound the size of a cottage, hollowed and re-occupied and re-built by forty generations. Their cement, when you break it, is chemically indistinguishable from the packing in the original terrace fill. Make of that what you like. Nobody in the Bowl is going to help you decide whether the builders left and the animals imitated them, or whether the animals were always the builders and there was never anyone else, and the terraces are just what happens when a mound-maker lineage has a hundred million years and a valley to work with.

There are **sift-swimmers** in loose vertical columns, thumb-length, silver-black, hanging in the water in their thousands with their filtering fans out, all facing the same current, spread through the dim in sparse galactic sheets. There are **sluice-eels**, blind, pale, patient in the corbelled tunnels. There are things like white sea-spiders four feet across that walk the risers with immense slowness and eat the mats. There are **tenders** — that is the only word — small hard-shelled animals that clean the joint lines of the old walls with their mouthparts, obsessively, the way an ant milks aphids, because the mats grow best in the joints and the tenders farm them, and in farming them they keep the joints clear, and in keeping the joints clear they preserve the walls, and so the architecture of the Bowl is maintained by animals that have no concept of it.

Density stays low. There is a great deal of empty water. You can swim for a long time over polished vacancy between one congregation and the next, and the emptiness is not lifeless so much as *spaced* — the Bowl is not crowded, it is populated at intervals, like a plain with villages.

---

It is quiet. Underwater quiet, which is a particular thing: a soft continuous hiss of grains moving on stone, the faint tick and rattle of mound-makers at work, the low organ-note of water pressing through a sluice. Nothing calls. Nothing sings. Every so often, without warning, a riser somewhere in the Bowl gives up and a hundred tonnes of facing stone slides two metres and stops, and the sound of it arrives through the water as a single dull *knock* that you feel in your sternum before you hear it, and then the hiss resumes.

And yet nothing here is still. This is the last and largest thing.

The Bowl is turbulent to a degree that has nothing to do with noise. It is a high cold basin under a sky that dumps katabatic wind down its walls in irregular gouts, and the water responds as water does at scale: it slops. There are seiches in the Bowl with periods of forty minutes and amplitudes of two metres, so that the shallow terraces are alternately submerged and drained, alternately submerged and drained, forever. There are cold plumes falling off the rim shelves that gather density and then let go all at once, and when one lets go the entire water column overturns — a slow toppling avalanche that starts as a change in the taste of the water and becomes, within an hour, a silt blizzard in which you cannot see your own forearm, in which the sift-swimmer columns are torn apart and scattered halfway across the Bowl, in which fresh mounds a season in the making are erased down to their footings and the mats are stripped from the seeps and flung upward into the dark to snow slowly back down for days.

Then it passes. Then the water clears from the top down, in strata, like something settling its thoughts. And the mound-makers, wherever they have landed, begin again — with no memory of the site they lost, cementing silt into cones on whatever polished shelf they now find themselves standing on, following the seep-smell, walling the joints, farming the film.

That is the rule of the Bowl, and it is not a metaphor, it is just the physics of a cold made basin at altitude: *everything is destroyed at intervals, and everything is rebuilt continuously, and neither process is going anywhere.* The terraces get smoother. The mounds get built and unbuilt. The wall stands one course lower than it did a million overturnings ago. The light stays dim. The water stays cold and grey and full of turning.

If there were ever anyone here who meant the terraces to mean something — irrigation, ritual, agriculture, a garden at the top of the world — the Bowl has forgotten it so thoroughly that the forgetting has become the architecture. What remains is the shape of an intention, worn convex, tended by animals, and stirred without pause by weather that has no interest whatsoever in the difference between building and ruin.
