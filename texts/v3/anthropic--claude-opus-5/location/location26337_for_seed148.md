## The Draft

**1. Approach**

You come to it low. Everything about the place is low — it sits in a basin that has not yet decided to be a valley, the way a bruise sits before it colours. There is no horizon. There is a ceiling, perhaps ninety metres up, and the ceiling is made of the same thing as the ground and the walls and the streets: a single plant, or a thing that was a plant before it acquired ambitions.

The whole settlement would fit inside a large parish. You could walk its perimeter in an hour, if the streets held still, which they do not always trouble to do.

It is cool — six degrees, perhaps eleven where the machinery runs. Your breath shows. The air smells of cut grass and cold iron and something faintly sweet and medicinal, like a cough syrup left open in a cellar.

**2. What is underneath**

There is no rock here. Not buried rock, not soil, not sand — none of the products of a world that has been worn. Beneath the plant's floor-tissue lies a grey paste called, by nobody, the protolith: a wet mineral batter that smells of struck flint and eggshell. Put your hand in it and it is neither cold nor gritty. It is *undecided*. Crystals begin in it constantly — you can watch a feldspar assemble itself over ten minutes, six-sided, hopeful — and then lose confidence and go back to slurry.

Nothing here has ever been broken, so there is no dust. This is the first thing that unsettles visitors and the last thing they can name. Every edge in the Draft is original. Every corner is as sharp as the hour it was extruded. There are no pebbles because there are no fragments; there is no mud because there is nothing that has finished being a stone long enough to fail. The town is old and looks brand new and always will, and the effect is not freshness but a kind of held breath.

**3. The substrate**

The plant is everything, and the plant is almost entirely empty.

Its structure is a foam at architectural scale. Cell walls stand a metre thick and run for two hundred metres: pale, dry, faintly translucent, ribbed like the inside of a bone. Between them are the lacunae — chambers the size of naves, of ballrooms, of flooded quarries — and they are full of nothing but cold air. This is the paradox you have to carry with you: total vegetation, near-total vacancy. The living mass is enormous and it is arranged as *space*. You will walk for twenty minutes down a gallery whose walls are alive, whose floor is alive, whose vaulted ceiling is alive, and pass not one leaf, not one insect, not one drop.

Then, at the twenty-first minute, a beetle.

**4. The town**

Somewhere, at some point, the plant contracted a city.

That is the only honest way to describe it. It did not host a city; it caught one, the way a lung catches a shape from the thing it breathes. Growing out of and into the lacunae are streets — real streets, kerbed, cambered for drainage, with gutters — made of cellulose laid down in courses that mimic brick so exactly you can see the false pointing. There are doorframes with lintels. There are stair treads worn in the middle, except they are not worn; the plant *grew* them concave, having understood that stairs should be concave, without understanding why.

There are three hundred and forty doors in the Draft. There are three hundred and eleven doors in the Draft. Count them on the way out and you will get a different number, and both numbers will be correct, and this bothers no one who lives here because no one lives here.

Rails run through it: two parallel woody ridges of exactly gauge width, sprouting from the floor tissue, dark green, greased with their own exudate. They lead to the Mill.

**5. The Mill**

The Mill is the largest single organ and it is only two-thirds finished, and it has been two-thirds finished for as long as the Draft has been able to remember, which is not long, because memory here is also under construction.

It works. That is the trouble.

Sap-pressure drives it. Vast turgor bladders in the ceiling fill during the wet hour and discharge through valves of pure lignin, and the pressure turns a wheel eleven metres across whose spokes are xylem bundles and whose rim is a hoop of self-grown resin. From the wheel: shafts. From the shafts: belts of woven phloem fibre, flapping and slapping, running up through the dark to loom-frames and drop-hammers and something that is unmistakably a lathe. Copper is here too — the plant hyperaccumulates it, sweats it out, lays it down in filaments and busbars, and where two filaments meet there is a low blue arc and a tang of ozone.

The Mill produces the town. That is its output. It extrudes kerbstone, doorframe, stair tread, rail, signage. It is a factory whose product is the factory's own city, and it has never once produced anything a person could use or eat or sell.

The signage is written in a script that has not yet chosen its letters. Watch a sign for a minute and the characters shuffle through candidate forms — a serif grows, thinks better of it, retracts. The meaning stays constant. The meaning is always some variant of: *this way, or something like it*.

**6. Light**

Almost none.

The ceiling is a hundred metres of opaque living tissue and above that, presumably, more. What light there is comes from three sources. The arc-joints in the copper, blue and intermittent, casting hard shadows that swing. A dull orange glow in the Mill's bearing-races where friction warms the resin to the edge of luminescence. And the lamps — bulbs the plant grew, gourd-shaped, filament inside, hung along the streets on stems. Perhaps one in nine still lit, each about as bright as a candle seen through a curtain.

Between lamps: full dark, for two hundred metres at a time. You navigate by the camber of the road under your feet, by the temperature of the air on one cheek, by the drip.

**7. The wet hour**

Water comes and goes on a cycle of roughly nine hours. The tissue is cool; the air that the Mill's motion drives through the lacunae is warmer and carries moisture up from the protolith; the moisture condenses on the ceilings and hangs there in a fur of droplets, growing.

Then the fall. Not rain — too slow, too separated. Individual drops, each the size of a grape, released over the course of forty minutes across the whole town: a soft irregular percussion, tock, tock, on kerbstone, on rail, on the crown of your head. Gutters run for an hour afterwards. Cisterns in the leaf-bases at street corners fill to a hand's depth and are drunk down by morning, if morning is the word.

Between wet hours, the Draft is a papery, static, condensation-cold dryness where your lips crack.

**8. Fauna**

They are numerous and you will hardly see them, and they are silent, and there is a reason.

In hollow galleries a hundred metres long, sound travels perfectly. So the ecology has organised itself entirely around not being heard. The beetles — thumb-sized, pale, each carrying one drop of water on its back like a lens — walk on pads of setae and make no noise at all. The wall-grazers, slow flat-bodied things the size of sheep that scrape films of algae off the cell walls with a rasping tongue, have evolved to rasp *in the wet hour*, under cover of the drip, and to fast the other eight. The moths do not fly. Flight is loud. They walk, and their wings are folded into insulating quilts, and they walk very slowly, and they are everywhere and you will step on one perhaps twice in a day.

There is a great deal of life per square kilometre and almost none per cubic metre, because everything has spread as thin as it can.

And there is the reason for it. The Listener works the void spaces. Nobody has seen it whole. It is described by its effects: a sudden absolute cessation of the drip in one district; a smell of hot copper; a beetle's single drop, unspilled, sitting on empty floor. It hunts by ear and it is patient and it is the only thing here that is not made of plant.

**9. The rules**

Say them plainly, because they are the plainest thing about the place.

*Erosion has not been invented.* Nothing wears. Nothing rounds. Nothing crumbles into anything else. Consequently nothing here can be older than it looks, and nothing can be dated, and the concept of *before* is thin.

*The town is a draft.* Regions of it are unspecified. Walk far enough down a side street and detail runs out: the kerb stops having a top surface, the wall stops having a colour, and your foot finds not a floor but *the average of a floor* — supportive, featureless, temperature-neutral. Push further and the specification thins to a grey where you cannot say whether your eyes are open. Everyone turns back. Everyone comes back with the sense of having been *inconsiderate*.

*Tense is negotiable, causation is provisional.* You will find your handprint in fresh green callus on a rail you have not yet touched. You will hear the Mill start after it is already running. Walk a street twice and your footprints from the second passage will be waiting at the start, going the wrong way. None of this can be used. It is not a mechanism, it is a symptom: the world here is a first attempt and the sequencing is not clean yet.

*Do not be loud.* Not a superstition. A rule of the world in the same category as gravity, and enforced.

**10. What the place is doing**

It is building. Continuously, quietly, in the cold and the dark: the wheel turns, the belts slap, the copper arcs, the extruders lay another six metres of kerb along a street that as yet leads nowhere, and the drip falls on it, and the wall-grazers rasp under the cover of the drip, and no one arrives, and no one leaves, and the protolith beneath tries again and again to become granite and cannot yet commit.

It is not ruins. That is the thing to hold onto, standing in the blue flicker with your breath showing. It has all the emptiness of ruins and none of the history. Nothing here has fallen.

It simply hasn't been finished, and it is working on it, and it is not in a hurry, and it would prefer you kept your voice down.
