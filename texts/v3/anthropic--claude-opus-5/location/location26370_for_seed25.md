## The Basin Under the Lid

There is a sky, but you will never see it. Three hundred metres up, the fog stands in a ceiling so flat and so old that it has its own geology — a lower face pocked and lensed, hanging in slow scallops like the underside of ice. Light comes through it the way light comes through a closed eyelid: a smear, grey-brown, without direction. Nothing casts a shadow here. Nothing has cast a shadow for a very long time.

Below that lid lies the basin: sixty kilometres of pale rock, low, level, worn round. From its rim it looks like a drained thing, a floor rather than a country. There are no peaks. The highest point is a knuckle of chalk you could climb in ten minutes. Erosion here finished its argument with the stone long ago and the two settled — every edge is soft, every hollow is a bowl, every ridge has the shape of a shoulder that has been leaned on for ten thousand years.

The stone is carbonate, off-white to the colour of a bad tooth, and it is porous the way bread is porous. It is made of bodies. Not fossils — *bodies*, still arriving. Every death in the basin becomes floor, and the floor keeps rising, and the basin keeps deepening its own bed by the weight of what it holds. Underfoot the surface crackles: a gravel of hollow bones, shed plates, the calcite husks of the grain. Below the crackle it is hollow, and it booms.

There is no plant. There has never been a plant. Nothing in the basin has ever been green, and nothing here would know what to do with a leaf.

---

**What the world eats**

Along every joint and crack in the rock, water seeps — not rivers, not rain most days, but *fog-milk*, condensing on the underside of the ceiling and falling in a fine, permanent, almost-not-there drizzle, and gathering along the fractures where it meets the sour breath of the deep stone. Where the two meet, crusts grow. Black rinds, brown rinds, rinds like wet felt and rinds like split fruit. They eat sulfur and iron and give back a slick, sour biomass with the consistency of tripe.

This is the bottom of everything. A world's entire economy, in seams no wider than your hand, ten centimetres tall, wrinkling out across the pans.

And on the rinds: the scrapers. Broad, flat, blind, the size of doormats and the size of doors, moving over the seams in overlapping ranks, rasping. Their mouths are files. A single scraper rasping is a sound like a knife on a whetstone. There are, at any moment on the open pan, perhaps four hundred thousand of them, and their combined rasp is the floor of the noise — a granular roar that never stops, never rises, never falls, so constant that within an hour you stop hearing it and only feel it in your teeth.

Above them, in the air, the grain: small hard-bodied things, three millimetres, uncountable, drifting in curtains and columns, feeding on rind-spore and on each other's dead. They fall constantly. They are the snow of this place. They collect in the hollows of your ears, in the corners of your eyes, in the pits and flutes of the rock, where the drip cements them into a pale mortar.

And above the grain: the clatter. Leathery flyers, none larger than a dog, packed in the underlid in colonies of millions, hanging from the fog-scallops by claws set into the accreted crust of their own leavings. When a colony wakes, the noise arrives like a wall falling. When a colony feeds, the sky rains grain-husk and droppings and small warm corpses, and the scrapers on the floor turn their fileless faces up and rasp faster.

Then the listeners, low and long and patient, walking the seams. Then the things that hunt the listeners. Then the mothers — great slow soft-bodied bulks, four metres, five, wedged into the gullets and the throat-holes, who eat whatever falls into them and who have not moved in decades, whose flanks have grown their own rind-crusts, their own scrapers, their own weather.

Density is the fundamental fact. There is no cubic metre of this basin that is not occupied. Lie down on the pan and you are lain upon. Reach into a crack and something in it is already reaching out.

---

**Sound as sight**

Here is the rule that shapes everything: nothing in the basin sees with light. There isn't enough. Everything sees with sound.

So everything makes sound. Constantly, deliberately, as a condition of existing. The scrapers rasp; the clatter shrieks in pulses too high for you and clicks in pulses you feel as pressure behind the eyes; the listeners tick; the mothers moan, long descending notes at the bottom of hearing, and the moan is how they know the walls of their own throats. To go quiet is to go blind. To go quiet is also to *become invisible*, and this is why silence is the most frightening thing in the basin: a hole in the noise, moving. Predators here do not stalk in shadow. They stalk in hush. The scrapers can feel a hush coming the way you'd feel a cold draught, and the whole rank will break and scatter from an absence.

The volume, in human terms, is intolerable. Standing on the open pan, unprotected, the pressure is that of a working quarry — a continuous, layered, saturated roar with structure in it, spikes and shears and sudden collective silences that are worse than the roar. Speech is impossible. You would communicate by touch. You would bleed a little from the ears by the second day and then, mercifully, hear less.

---

**What the voices have built without building anything**

Nothing in this basin is constructed. There is no tool, no wall, no path, no dwelling, no fire, no shaped thing. And yet the place looks *made*, and this is the strangest thing about it.

Because sound erodes stone, given enough of it and enough time.

The pillars of the north pan are fluted like organ pipes, and the flutes are not water-cut — they are the standing pressure-nodes of a chorus that has roosted in exactly that place for longer than the flutes have existed. The rock has learned the shape of the noise. The bowls of the seep-flats are lens-perfect, and they focus the rasping of their own scrapers onto their own centres, which are consequently the deepest points, which consequently gather the most water, which consequently grow the richest rind, which consequently hold the most scrapers. Every feature in the basin is a feedback loop that reached its answer and stopped.

And the basin, as a whole, has a note.

It is somewhere near the bottom of your hearing, and you register it first as nausea and then as a pressure in the sternum, and then one evening you simply notice that it has been there the entire time. Sixty kilometres of hollow carbonate under a fog lid, driven by a hundred billion animals: the whole bowl resonates. It has resonated at that pitch for so long that the animals are tuned to it. The mothers moan in it. The clatter pulse at its harmonics. The rock has been ground into a shape that sustains it and the creatures have been ground into shapes that sustain the shape. Geology and biology here are not two things. They are one very slow organ, playing one very long chord, and it is in balance, and it has been in balance for so long that the word *established* is the only honest description.

---

**Quiet-wells**

The exception. The wound in the system.

Scattered across the pan there are sinkholes, twenty to a hundred metres across, lined with a soft grey porous rock — a diagenetic sponge, a rot of the carbonate — that does not return echoes. It swallows them. Stand at the lip of a quiet-well and half your world goes missing: on one side the roar, on the other a smooth blank nothing, an amputation.

Anything that stumbles in goes blind and mute in the same instant. It cannot call. It cannot map the walls. It circles, quieter and quieter, and dies, and its body pads the lining, and the well becomes a little quieter still.

The basin is old enough that some of the wells have been deepening for ten thousand years, and their floors are metres of soft compacted silence. The animals know them by the absence of an echo where an echo should be, and they walk the rims without ever crossing, and no animal in the basin has ever learned to be curious about one.

---

**The cycles**

Twice a day the fog lid breathes: it lowers, thickens, and the drip becomes a wet grey mist through which the grain moves in denser curtains, and the temperature drops a degree or two — nine, ten degrees, no more, always cool, always damp, always the sensation of a cellar.

And perhaps once in twenty days the lid tears somewhere, and it rains properly. Rain is the great flattening. Rain raises the noise floor so high that no creature can see anything at all: a whiteout in the medium of hearing. Everything stops. Predator and prey lie down within a body's length of each other on the pan and do not move, and the hunting instinct goes dormant, and the basin becomes a single blind congregation under a hammering of water, breathing, waiting. The truce lasts as long as the rain does. When it thins, the roar resumes with a collective shudder, and the killing picks up exactly where it left off, and the water runs into the gullets and the throat-holes and feeds the mothers, and for a week afterward the rinds grow fat.

At the far rim, where the ground lifts a hundred metres and the fog lid thins to a haze, the light strengthens to something like true dusk, and the noise falls to a murmur, and the rinds fail, and the scrapers thin out, and the grain does not drift. The last few kilometres before the rim are almost empty. Animals reach that edge and turn back. Not from fear. There is simply nothing out there to hear.
