## The Warm Room

You come to it from the flats, where the ground is the colour of wet ash and holds your footprint until you come back that way and find it still there, unchanged, filled with water that has neither drained nor evaporated. The land here is barely above the sea. There is no horizon to speak of, only a lowering of grey into grey, and the air is body-warm and thick enough that you breathe it more than you inhale it.

The entrance is not dramatic. A limestone brow, no higher than a doorway, with a lip of pale mineral spilling out from under it like something poured and then thought better of. You have to duck. Then the ceiling lifts, and you are inside a space about the size of a large barn or a small church, and you stop, because there is nowhere to walk.

The room is *full*.

---

### Terrain

The floor is not a floor. It is a field of travertine — columns, cones, fluted drapes, knobbed masses like stacked melons, all of it the colour of old wax, of tallow, of a fingernail held to light. They stand shoulder to shoulder. Some are ankle-high, some reach the ceiling and fuse with the stalactites coming down to meet them, forming pillars you could not put your arms around. Between them the gaps are hand's-breadth, forearm-breadth. To cross the room you turn sideways, lift a knee, set a foot in a hollow the size of a bowl, and find that the hollow holds two fingers of warm water. Every hollow does. There is no dry surface anywhere in the room and no surface that is not stone.

The water is everywhere and almost never falling. It *seeps*. It comes out of the walls in sheets so thin they read as a change in gloss rather than as liquid, and it moves down the flanks of the columns at the pace of a slow thought, and it fills each basin to the exact height of that basin's rim, and then it goes over the rim into the next basin, and so on, and so down, and eventually out under the brow of the entrance to make the pale spill you saw from outside. Nothing splashes. The whole system moves perhaps a cupful a minute through ten thousand small transfers, and each transfer is silent.

At the room's low end there is a pool, roughly circular, roughly four paces across, rimmed by a raised collar of its own making. It is clear to the bottom and the bottom is three feet down and paved with the same wax-coloured stone in a pattern of overlapping scallops. The water in it is 38 degrees. It is 38 degrees at the surface and 38 degrees at the bottom and 38 degrees in the winter and 38 degrees in whatever passes for summer on the flats. Put your hand in and there is that startling non-sensation of water at blood heat: your hand disappears from your inventory of yourself.

### Air and light

The warmth is the room's chief inhabitant. It comes up from below with the water, and because the entrance is low and the room is a bowl, the warmth cannot leave. It sits. Everything is beaded — every column has a skin of condensation over its skin of seepage, and the ceiling wears a coat of droplets so dense it looks furred, and if you exhale hard the fog of your breath hangs and drifts and does not disperse for a long time.

Light comes in one way only: under the brow, off the outside spill, off the pool, and up. So the room is lit from below and everything is wrong-side-shadowed — the undersides of the drapes are bright, the tops are dark. On the ceiling there is a slow net of reflected ripple-light, greenish, moving in a pattern that has no rhythm, and it is the only thing in the room that moves quickly. It is enough light to see faces by, not enough to read by. Your eyes take about four minutes to accept it and then it seems generous.

### Living things

No plants. Not a leaf, not a moss, not a fern in the light-well of the entrance — the flats outside are mineral for miles and nothing has arrived to try.

What lives here does not photosynthesize much and does not care. Where the water comes out of the wall hottest there are mats: white, cream, in two places a rust-orange, laid down in sheets a few grains thick and slightly slick, like the inside of a cheek. Bacteria and their consortia. They do not spread. The white mat ends where it ends, along a line as fixed as a tidemark, and it has ended there long enough that the stone below the line and the stone above the line are different colours. In the pool, along the collar, a fringe of the same white waves very slightly in the outflow, filaments an inch long, and that is the closest thing to grass in this world.

Animals: rare, and you will probably see none. There is a small blind isopod, a translucent grain of rice with legs, that grazes the mats — you might find three in an hour of looking, always in the same three basins. There is a snail with a shell like a chip of thumbnail, and no snail has ever been seen moving. Twice a year, perhaps, a moth comes in under the brow, circles the ripple-light, and drowns in the pool, and its body is taken up by the mats within a day and by the stone within a season, so that if you look closely at the collar of the pool you can find, sealed in the travertine, the ghost-shapes of wings.

### The marks of hands

People have been here. Not many, not recently, not never.

Just inside the entrance, on the left, someone stacked slabs into a low wall — knee-high, six feet long, dry-laid, no mortar, the slabs pried from a bedding plane at the back of the room where you can still see the negative shapes they came out of. The wall has been coated by the seep until it reads as a single fused object, a bench of pale stone with a suggestion of courses in it. You can sit on it. It is warm. It is the only place in the room where a person can sit.

Beside it, a channel: a groove cut into the travertine floor with a hard stone, taking the overflow from one particular basin and delivering it to a hollow that has been deepened and squared. The tool marks are still visible under the glaze — short, chopping strokes, the work of an hour, done well. A dozen chert flakes lie in a crevice nearby, and the core they came from, and a small hammerstone of some dark rock brought in from elsewhere, all of it now gently cemented in place, the flakes' edges softened to the touch.

At the pool there is a step. Not built — worn. A single scallop in the rim, rubbed to a satin the rest of the stone doesn't have, exactly foot-shaped, and beneath it, in the water, a second. Two steps, going in. Whoever used them used them for a long time.

And on the pool's floor, near the middle, a bowl: fired clay, undecorated, upside down, encased now in a quarter-inch of the same wax-coloured stone so that it has become a smooth mound with the ghost of a rim. Next to it something long and slender, thickened by the same process, that was probably a wooden ladle and is now a thing shaped roughly like a ladle, in stone, weighing three pounds.

No metal anywhere. No writing. No image scratched on a wall.

### Sound

Mostly quiet, and the quiet has texture. The seeping makes nothing. What you hear, standing still, is: a drip, from a single stalactite near the back that has for some reason failed to make contact with its basin's meniscus, falling at an interval of about nine seconds; a hiss so low it might be your own blood, which is the sound of the hot inflow entering the pool from below; and, if you hold your breath, a faint irregular *tick* from all over the room, thousands of small releases as droplets on the ceiling let go and land, none of them near you, all of them somewhere. Outside, nothing. The flats have no wind worth the name and nothing to be windy in.

### The rule of the place

This is the important thing, and it takes some sitting on the warm bench to feel it.

The room is finished. The water that deposits stone deposits exactly as much as the water that dissolves stone takes away. The columns are neither growing nor shrinking. The pool has stood at its present level for longer than any record and will stand there. The mats hold their borders. The heat holds its number. The light holds its angle, because the entrance faces the flat grey nothing and the flat grey nothing is the same brightness at all hours and all seasons — dim, even, sourceless.

So the place has no clock. Erosion has already done its work here and reached its terms; the room is old and *settled*, not decaying. Nothing here is in the process of becoming anything else. The bowl on the pool floor will be a stone mound of that shape for as long as there is a room. The footprint you left outside will still be there.

This is why people came, presumably, and why they cut a channel and stacked a wall and wore two steps into rock: not for shelter, which the flats need but the room doesn't provide much of, and not for food, of which there is none. For the sensation of sitting inside something that is not moving. In a world of tides and rot and weather, this is a warm packed room where you can put your hand into water and take it out again and there will be no difference in anything, including — for a while, sitting there, breathing an atmosphere too full to disperse your breath — in you.

Then you duck out under the brow, and the air is cooler by four degrees, and you find your old footprint, and step in it.
