## The Sump House

You find it low down, where the land has almost finished being land. A floodplain of alder and reed, the kind of flatness that reads as water pretending to be ground. In the middle of it there is a swell no higher than a barn — not a hill, more a place where the field has been thickened from underneath — and in the flank of that swell, a doorway with no door, framed in brick worn to the softness of soap.

Cold air does not come out of it. Warm air does, and with the warm air a pressure you feel in your sternum before you understand it as sound.

Fourteen steps down, each one dished in the middle like a worn coin, each one running a thin sheet of water. Then the room.

---

It is a single room and it is the whole world. Perhaps twenty-five metres across, vaulted, and vastly, extravagantly empty — the sort of interior volume that a cathedral would use for God and this place uses for nothing at all. Air. Warm mist standing in the upper third like a second, softer ceiling. The vault is brick, laid in a herringbone that has half-dissolved; the individual bricks are no longer really bricks but nubs, rounded lozenges, each one a low dome, the mortar between them recessed into shallow gutters. Nothing anywhere in this room has an edge. Every corner has been coaxed by six or seven centuries of warm water into a curve, and the curves have found the shapes they wanted, and stopped.

Light comes down in three columns from three shafts in the vault — proper daylight, ordinary and generous, grey-gold, the light of an overcast afternoon anywhere in the temperate world. The shafts were glazed once; you can see the seatings, the iron frames reduced to a fur of rust that has gone as far as rust can go and now just sits there, stable, mildly orange, not eating anything anymore. So the light falls in unfiltered, three soft pillars of it standing in the mist, and where each pillar lands the floor is green.

---

The sound.

At the far end of the room the floor opens into a rectangular sump, twelve metres by five, black, and into that sump falls water — not a waterfall exactly, but a full-bore discharge from a culvert mouth the size of a wagon, a solid glassy rope of it, arriving with the whole weight of the drained country behind it. And from the sump, an Archimedes screw as thick as an oak rises at thirty degrees through the wall, turning, lifting that water back out and away.

The noise of this is not a roar. Roar suggests variation, gusts, something animal. This is a single sustained tonnage of sound, flat as a wall, occupying every cubic centimetre of the room equally. It has a low body you feel in the teeth and the backs of the knees, a mid-range hiss from the aerated water, and above that the screw's iron song — a steady grinding note, one note, because the screw turns at one speed and has turned at that speed forever.

You cannot speak here. Not shout — speak is simply not a thing this room permits. If two of you came down, you would find yourselves using your hands within a minute, and finding it easy, and later, up in the alders, you would both be hoarse without having said anything.

After ten minutes the sound stops being sound. Because it never changes — not by a decibel, not by a semitone, not in the seven hundred years — it fails to carry information, and the part of you that listens gives up and goes quiet. What is left is a kind of enormous silence with a weight in it. People who have sat here describe the same thing: that it became peaceful, and that they lost track of how long they stayed.

---

Water is not weather here. It is architecture.

Every surface weeps. The vault sweats and lets go in slow lines that have carved their own channels down the walls — shallow flutes, a hand's breadth apart, hundreds of them, each one polished. The floor is not flat but a fine landscape of basins and lips, all of it slicked, all of it draining eventually toward the sump. The mist above never clears and never thickens. Warmth comes up out of the water itself and out of the wall behind the screw, where something under the floor has been converting the fall of the water into a low hum and a steady blood-heat for as long as the screw has turned; the temperature in this room is twenty-four degrees in January and twenty-four degrees in August, and the humidity does not have a number, it just is total.

Which is why the room is a jungle in a country that has no jungles.

The green is arranged strictly, by light. In the three columns where daylight lands, there are mats of moss thirty centimetres deep, wet and springing, so saturated they release water when you kneel. Out of the moss come hart's-tongue ferns in rosettes the size of cartwheels, and maidenhair — real maidenhair, growing wild on brick, in a latitude where it has no business — and filmy ferns like wet green tissue plastered flat to the fluted walls, translucent, one cell thick, only possible in air that never dries. Liverworts sheet the lower brickwork in overlapping scales. In the deepest, dimmest corner beyond the sump, where light arrives only as a rumour, the growth thins to pale threads of algae and a black bloom on the stone that is neither plant nor mineral.

And one fig. A single fig tree has come down through the eastern light-shaft, seeded God knows when by a bird, and grown *downward*, hanging in the shaft, roots gripping the brick, its crown a low grey-green cloud in the mist about four metres above the moss. It is not large. It has not been large for a very long time. It fruits, small hard figs, and drops them, and they are gone by the next morning.

---

Almost nothing lives here, and what does is few and pale and far apart.

Cave crickets: maybe forty in the whole room, straw-coloured, long-legged, spaced out along the walls with two or three metres between each. They do not sing; there would be no point.

One species of spider, thin-legged, sheeting the moss with webs that catch condensation and hold it as beadwork.

Eels in the sump. Silver eels come up the culvert on their way inland and arrive here, in this black rectangle, and cannot pass the screw. Some number of them — nine, eleven — live down there permanently, thick as forearms, ancient, going round and round in the churn. You see one every twenty minutes or so: a coil surfacing in the aerated white, unhurried.

And sometimes a wren, in through a shaft, hopping the ferns, its beak opening and closing on a song that this room deletes entirely. It never stays long.

That is the whole census. Forty crickets, some spiders, eleven eels, an occasional silent wren. In a hall this size, that is nearly nothing. You can stand in the middle of the room and be, in every meaningful sense, alone with a noise.

---

The rules here are few and they are absolute.

Nothing accumulates. There is no leaf litter, no silt bar, no wrack line, no bones, no bottles, no fallen brick lying where it fell. Everything loose was carried to the sump and lifted out and away centuries ago, and the room is still doing it, patiently, every hour. The emptiness is not neglect. It is the product of relentless housekeeping by moving water.

Nothing decays further. The rust has finished rusting. The brick has finished rounding — it is at the shape water can no longer improve. The iron of the screw has reached the thickness at which it wears no faster than the water can polish it. Everything in this room has arrived at its final form and is holding there, not out of strength but out of exhaustion, which turns out to be the same thing given enough time.

Nothing changes. Not with the season, not with the hour, not with the weather above. Rain in the fields does not raise the sump; the culvert is fed from a reservoir of groundwater so large it has no moods. Winter does not cool the room. Night is the only visitor — the three pillars of light go out, and the sound stays exactly as it is, and the ferns wait, and at dawn the pillars come back and land in precisely the same three places on the moss they have landed on for seven hundred years.

There is nobody to maintain it and nothing that needs maintaining. Whatever the screw was built to keep dry is presumably dry. The fields above it grow alder and reed and are grazed in July. The people who graze them know there is a hole in the swell and that you can hear it if you put your ear to the ground, and they call it the Hum, and they do not go down, mostly because there is nothing down there.

They are right. There is nothing down there. It is one warm, roaring, bright green room, twenty-five metres across, perfectly finished, and it will be exactly this tomorrow.
