## The Shelf

Come at it from the sea, in a low boat, and the first thing you meet is the sound — before the land is even a shape in the vapour, there is a wall of noise standing offshore, and you row into it as into a current.

Then the shape: a pale apron, a mile and a half of it, running out from the foot of a dark escarpment. Terraces. Hundreds of them, stacked in shallow risers like the rings of a poured candle, each rim scalloped and lipped and beaded, each holding a pool that spills into the pool below. The whole shelf is the colour of old teeth and cream and, where the mats grow thick, of rust and mustard. It is warm to the touch. It is wet everywhere, always, with no distinction between rock and river; the water is only a hand's depth over most of it, sheeting, and you cannot look at any square foot of the place without seeing it move.

At the top, against the black cliff, the springs. Nine principal mouths and some hundreds of minor seeps, all of them delivering water at just above blood heat, saturated with lime and faintly sour with sulphur. The flow does not vary. Not with the year, not with the rain that falls on the highland behind, not in living memory nor in the memory of the flowstone, which is a better witness. The escarpment is old. The shelf below it is being made right now, at the rate of a fingernail's thickness a month, and this is the central fact of the place: nothing here is worn down. Everything is being added to.

## Air and Light

There is no sky in the ordinary sense. The steam off the terraces meets the warm sea air and stands over the shelf as a low white ceiling, three or four hundred feet up, lit from above by whatever the day is doing. What arrives below is a soft, sourceless, permanent dusk — bright enough to work by, never bright enough to cast a shadow with an edge. The pale stone takes this light and gives it back upward, so the underside of the vapour glows faintly, and the whole shelf lies inside a shell of its own dim reflection.

The air is at saturation. Everything drips. Cold objects are wet objects; there are no cold objects. Breathe and you taste chalk and iron and rot-egg. Stand still an hour and your forearms grow a whitish rime you can rub off in rolls, like the sloughing of a sunburn. Stand still much longer than that and other things begin.

## What Grows

Nothing green. There is not a leaf on the shelf, nor a root, nor a seed, nor the memory of one; the highland behind the cliff has some grey scrub, but nothing that lives up there can live down here, and nothing here has ever needed the sun.

What lives here eats stone-breath. The mats do: filamentous sheets in every pool, white where the water is hottest, then ochre, then a long orange belt, then rust and liver-brown at the cooler margins near the sea. They lie in the pools like wet flannel, like wrung cloth, in strata a hand deep, and they smell — the whole shelf smells — of a hot skillet and a broken egg. They are farmed. Every pool in the inhabited districts is a field, walled and weeded and cropped in rotation, its temperature managed by opening and closing runnels with a stone paddle. The colours are the crop varieties. Nobody planted them, ever; they were always in that pool at that heat, and moving them does not work.

On the mats: shrimp, in the billions, translucent and eyeless. Flies, midges, some blind, hatching in columns so thick they can be scooped out of the air with a hoop of stiffened net and pressed into cakes. Crabs the width of a palm, white, that clatter over the rims in sheets and are herded rather than hunted. Eels in the warm runnels, thick as a wrist, unbothered by anything. And in the lowest, broadest pools, where water spreads out lukewarm before it reaches the sea: the pale oxen. Great smooth grazers, salamander-shaped, five hundred pounds of them, entirely blind, mouths like a scraping trowel. They mow the mats. They are docile past all reason. They are yoked to sledges with rope harnesses and they haul the city's stone, walking at the same slow pace their whole lives.

And above everything, the criers. Tens of thousands of them nesting on the escarpment's ledges, grey-white, short-winged, feeding on the fly columns — dropping into them, wheeling out, screaming continuously. Not calling. Screaming, all of them, all the time, day and night, one unbroken sheet of sound. Their guano is the city's second most valuable substance and their eggs the third.

## Sound

You will not be understood if you speak. This must be said plainly: the shelf is deafening. There are three layers to it and none of them ever stops.

The bottom layer is the water — hundreds of thousands of small falls, none more than a few feet high, summing into a flat white roar that has no location, that arrives equally from every direction, that you feel in the sternum. The middle layer is the criers, a shrill unresolving braid laid over the roar. The top layer is made by the people: the tapping. Thousands of chisels, tens of thousands of strokes a minute, ringing on wet stone.

Children can hear. Adults, mostly, cannot, and this is not considered a loss any more than the loss of milk teeth. Speech is done with hands and face at close range, a fast full-body language with a grammar built for a world where you can always be seen and never be heard. For distance there is the tapping — because the entire shelf is one continuous mass of stone, welded to itself, and a struck rim rings through it. A hammer on a terrace lip in the upper districts is felt through the soles of the feet a quarter mile down. Every household keeps a striker. There are perhaps two hundred formal signals, and beneath them an enormous informal traffic of gossip, hazing, and lullaby, and beneath that the working rhythms — the chipping, which is not communication at all, which is simply the sound of the city keeping itself from closing.

## The Work

The stone comes out of the ground soft. Cut a block from a fresh terrace and it has the density of hard cheese; it saws with a toothed shell, it shapes with a wooden mallet, and it can be carried wet by a child. Left in the air a week, it hardens. Left in the water, it does something better: it plates. Water flows over a wall of new-cut blocks, and the lime comes out of solution into every joint, and in a season the wall is not a wall of blocks any more, it is one stone with faint seams in it. Nobody here has ever needed mortar. Nobody here has ever needed timber, which is fortunate.

So the city is built of itself. Corbelled domes, thick-walled, low-doored. Whole districts sunk into disused pools and roofed over flush with the terrace, so that from above the upper town looks like a floor of lids. Tenements grown up the risers in five and six shelves, near-touching, connected by slab bridges and by ladders of stiffened rope — rope dipped in the spring on purpose, and left to plate until it stands rigid as a bone. Aqueducts everywhere, hand-cut channels shunting hot water to the mat-fields and cool water to the streets, running along walls and overhead across the lanes on stubby arcades, dripping into everything below. There is no dry ground. Floors are laid with a fall to a drain. Beds are slatted. Nobody owns anything soft.

Metal exists in small amounts, traded up from the coast: copper for the good chisels, kept oiled with ox fat, kept obsessively. Everything else is stone, shell, hide, bone, gut, rope, and the strange plated objects that are made by leaving a woven form in a runnel for a year — baskets that ring, cradles like coral, a lattice hat.

And every day, all day: the scraping. Because the water plates the good with the bad. It closes drains, seals shutters, welds the ladder to the wall, glues the sledge runner to the stone, thickens the doorway an eighth of an inch a season until the lintel is at your collarbone. Every household is responsible for its own frontage and its own share of the public channels. This is not maintenance, in the sense of repairing damage. It is the removal of unwanted stone, forever, at exactly the rate it arrives. The chipping is the sound of the town holding its shape against a patient, mindless, tepid generosity.

## The Rule

Nothing may be left still.

It is the whole law and it is enforced by the ground itself. A tool put down in a runnel and forgotten is a fixture in a month. A boat drawn up on the lower flats and not turned is a boulder by the next year. The lanes are swept not for cleanliness but for movement.

And the dead are given to the flow. Not buried — you cannot dig here, you can only be covered — but carried up to the ledges below the springs and laid in the sheeting water in the attitude the family chooses. The lime does the rest. A month, and the features are softened; a year, and there is a white form; a generation, and there is a swell in the flowstone with a shape inside it. The upper ledges are terraces of these swells, thousands of them, in ranks, wave upon wave, the oldest reduced to gentle risings that hold pools of hot water where the mats grow orange and the shrimp swarm and the crabs come to feed. They are shown to visitors as the ancestors and they are also, plainly and without contradiction, the best mat-fields on the shelf. The city eats what the city was. Nobody finds this remarkable.

## Equilibrium

There is no history here in the sense you mean. There is depth — the flowstone under the lowest district is nine hundred feet thick, and somewhere down at the bottom of it is the first wall anyone laid — but nobody digs, because digging is impossible, and so the past is not a place you can go, only a direction.

Above that depth, the town is the same town. The spring flow is fixed, so the mat yield is fixed, so the population is fixed, and has been fixed, at something under twenty thousand, for as long as the tapping-signals have had names. The lanes cannot move; they are made of the thing they are cut into. The pools cannot be relocated; their heat is their address. There are no seasons — the vapour ceiling does not lift, the water does not cool, the criers do not migrate, and the light at what the coast calls midwinter noon is the same soft nowhere-dusk as at midsummer midnight.

Newcomers find this unbearable for about a year, and then find it unbearable to leave. It is not that nothing happens. Enormous amounts happen: children, quarrels, floods of midges, a collapsed arcade, a good year for eggs. It is that all of it happens inside a shape that will not be changed by it. The stone comes up warm and wet and endless, and the hands go on taking it away, and the noise stands over the shelf like a roof, and out at the low western edge the last lukewarm sheet slides off the final rim into the sea, and the sea takes exactly as much as the springs bring, and has, and will.
