## The Pit Lake

Start at the surface, though the surface is not where you are going.

It is a plane of water perhaps four hundred metres across, held in a bowl of stepped grey rock, and it is so flat that the first impression is of something solid — sheet metal, or a floor. There is no wind down in the bowl. There is almost never wind down in the bowl; the rim stands sixty metres above the water on every side and the air inside sits in the hollow like water in a cup, cool, still, faintly metallic on the tongue. The lake's edge does not lap. It meets the rock in a hairline, and the hairline does not move, not seasonally, not by a centimetre, because forty kilometres away a set of pumps in an operating shaft is drawing the regional water table down at a rate that exactly matches what this basin receives in rain and groundwater seep. Nobody arranged this. It is a coincidence of hydrology and quota, and it has held for thirty-one years.

Go under.

---

The water is unreasonably clear. Fifty, sixty metres of horizontal visibility — the clarity of distilled things, of laboratory glassware. That clarity is not health. It is the transparency of water that nothing lives in. There is no plankton to cloud it, no silt suspended by burrowing, no film of bacteria on the rocks that a hand would come away slick with. Light falls through it almost unhindered, and what arrives at ten metres is still recognisably daylight: a flat sourceless brightness, tinted the pale green-blue of bottle glass held up to a window, strong enough to read a serial number by.

The temperature drops in two distinct steps. Just below the surface, eleven degrees. At nine metres, a step down to seven. At nineteen metres, another step, and below that, six degrees forever — the same six degrees in February and in August, the same six degrees this decade as last. That deep layer has never mixed with the layer above it. It is heavier, saltier with dissolved sulphate and iron and aluminium, and the two bodies of water rest on each other like oil on vinegar, permanently, unbothered. The boundary between them is visible if the light is right: a haze, a horizontal smear about a hand's breadth thick, where the refractive index changes. Push an arm through it and the arm bends. Ripples travel along it and do not break it. It is the most stable thing in the landscape, and it is made of nothing but the reluctance of two waters to become one.

---

The floor of the basin is not a floor but a stair. Benches — the working terraces cut by the machines — descend in concentric rings, each one ten to fifteen metres high with a flat shelf of six or eight metres, spiralling down and inward. Under water they have gone soft-edged. Thirty years of frost-shatter above the waterline and slow chemical loosening below have taken the sharp lip off every bench and spread it as a talus fan across the shelf beneath. The angles have found their rest. The fans are neatly graded, coarse at the top, fine at the toe, and they will not change again in any timescale that matters, because there is no current here to move them and no creature to disturb them.

The rock is a banded grey siltstone, split by seams of something rust-red that bleeds. Where the red seams outcrop, the stone below them is stained in long ochre drapes, like candle wax, like the tidemarks in an unwashed basin. Iron coming out of solution as it meets the slightly less acid water of the upper layer. The drapes have a texture — soft, floury, they smoke off the rock at a touch and then hang, a small orange cloud, taking twenty minutes to settle, and then they are exactly as they were.

pH here is 3.1. It does not vary.

---

There is life. Not much, and it is easy to walk past.

On the shallowest bench, where the light is strongest, the rock wears a thin biofilm of a dull yellow-green — a single-celled acidophile, one of the Euglena, spread so thinly that it reads as a discolouration rather than a growth. In three or four places on that bench the film thickens into mats the size of a tabletop, quilted, slightly gelatinous, trailing filaments a centimetre long that all lean the same direction because they grew that way and nothing has since suggested otherwise.

Higher, in the splash zone where the hairline meets stone, a scatter of moss cushions on the north wall, no bigger than fists, sixteen of them, spaced metres apart. Above the rim, out of the water and out of this account, birch scrub has taken the spoil heaps. Below, nothing rooted. No reeds, no pondweed, no Chara meadow. The chemistry forbids the shells and the soft tissues both.

And no animals. This is the flat fact of the place and it takes a while to feel rather than know. No fish, obviously; no shrimp in the talus; no chironomid larvae in the sediment, no oligochaete worms, no water mites, nothing walking the biofilm mats and grazing tracks in them. The mats are unbrowsed. The sediment is unturned — and so the sediment is *layered*, thirty-one distinct annual couplets of ochre and grey, undisturbed, legible, like the pages of a book nobody has opened. In an ordinary lake, worms would have stirred all that into a homogeneous grey mud within a year. Here the record stands. Bioturbation is a thing that happens where there are bodies, and there are no bodies.

What this means, practically, is that nothing moves except what falls. That is the whole kinematics of the place.

---

Now the machines.

They are the only made things, and they were not built here so much as abandoned in place, and the distinction matters. There are no *structures*. No walls, no huts, no jetty, no pump-house, no railing, no sign. The basin itself is a made thing, but it was made entirely by taking away — a hundred million tonnes of removal, an architecture of subtraction — and subtraction leaves no roofline.

What remains is scattered and heavy:

On the third bench down, a conveyor drive drum, two metres in diameter, lying on its side with the shaft still in it, the rubber lagging perished away in sheets so the steel shows through crazed with rust. Beside it, ten metres of the conveyor's steel frame, and the frame's idler rollers, and — the detail that stops you — each roller still turns. Not by itself. But push one and it goes round with a grinding reluctance, sealed bearings full of grease still doing their job in six-degree water at pH 3.1, and then it stops when you stop it.

On the fourth bench, sixty metres from the drum: a haul truck tyre, three metres tall, standing upright, leaning against the wall where it rolled to rest. Rubber is nearly immortal here. The tread blocks are crisp. The moulded lettering on the sidewall is perfectly legible and describes a load rating in a language of numbers still in use.

Deeper, past the chemocline, in the six-degree layer: a dragline bucket the size of a small room, upside down, teeth up. A hundred metres of wire rope, laid across two benches and down the talus between them in a long lazy S, each strand of it individually distinguishable. A steel rail, bent. Four steel drums, one of them still sealed and still buoyant enough to be sitting on the floor rather than lying on it, canted at an angle, held in that attitude by a half-litre of air inside that has not found its way out in three decades.

They are spaced widely. You swim for a minute between one and the next through empty green-grey water, and that emptiness is most of what the place is: much more space than presence. The machines are not a scrapyard. They are more like erratics, boulders dropped by a glacier — geology now, with serial numbers.

---

Sound.

Mostly it is quiet in the way that only a dead lake is quiet: no snapping, no clicking, no chorus of invertebrate noise that fills every living body of water and that you do not notice until it isn't there.

Into that absence come four things.

A hum. Continuous, extremely low, felt in the sternum more than heard — the pumps, forty kilometres away, arriving through bedrock. It never stops and never changes pitch. It is the sound of the industry that made and now maintains this place, entirely offstage, present only as vibration and as the unnatural constancy of the waterline.

Ticking. Metal in the shallows contracting minutely as the afternoon light leaves the west wall. Two or three ticks a minute for about an hour, then nothing.

A belch, rare — once or twice a day — as a pocket of gas works free of the sediment and goes up in a string of pearls, and the string makes a sound like a finger drawn round a wet glass rim.

And, occasionally, from far above: a crack, then a long descending clatter, then a plunge, then a slow settling — a stone letting go of the dry rim, falling ninety metres, entering, sinking, joining the talus. This is erosion. It is the only event the place has. It happens perhaps forty times a year and each time it changes nothing that could be measured.

---

The rule of the world here is simple and worth stating plainly: **everything that could have happened has already happened, and what is left is holding.**

The pit was dug. The water came up. The chemistry sorted itself into layers and locked. The rock walls slumped to their angle of repose and stopped. The machines fell where they fell and corroded to the point where the corrosion products themselves protect what's underneath, and slowed, and effectively stopped. The animals never arrived because they cannot. The plants arrived to the exact extent they can and no further. The pumps hold the level. The stratification holds the temperature. Nothing is dying here, because nothing much is living; nothing is decaying much, because decay is mostly a thing that microbes and worms do and they are not invited.

It is not frozen. It is not preserved by force or by cold or by any exceptional circumstance. It is simply a system that ran to completion, arrived at a state that satisfies all its constraints at once, and has no remaining reason to move.

Cool, clear, lit, quiet, empty, and finished.
