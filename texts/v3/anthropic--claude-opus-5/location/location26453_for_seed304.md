## The Mound

Come at it from the north, across the flats, and you will not believe there is a town until you are inside it.

The marsh out there is grey-green and shallow, water standing in sheets barely deeper than a hand, cold enough to numb the ankles by noon. Nothing grows tall. Wind moves over it without obstruction and takes the heat out of everything. Then, ahead, a low pale swelling in the plain — a broad blister of stone maybe two hundred paces across and eight or nine man-heights at its crown, white as a fish belly, and above it a column of mist standing upright in the still air like a held breath.

That is the whole world of this place. Everything happens on that blister.

---

### What the ground is doing

The mound is not old. Nothing here is old. At the top, in a hollow ringed by terraces the colour of boiled bone, there is a vent the width of a cartwheel where water comes up blood-warm and heavy with dissolved lime. It does not gush. It swells and slumps, swells and slumps, in a slow pulse with a period of about forty heartbeats, and each swell spills a thin skin of water over the lip and down the flanks in a hundred braided threads.

Where that water meets the cool air, it gives up its load. It builds. Every thread of overflow lays down a film of new stone thinner than a fingernail paring, and every film sets on the one below, and so the mound climbs — perhaps the width of a thumb each year, sometimes more in a wet fog-heavy winter, less in a season of hard drying wind.

So the ground is fresh. There is no soil anywhere on the mound, no gravel, no dust worth the name. The rims of the terraces are sharp enough to cut a bare foot, scalloped in flowing lobes and fans, and where the water runs steadily the surface is polished to a wet glaze; where it only wicks and weeps, the stone puffs up into a coral-like froth, brittle as burnt sugar. Everything drips. The undersides of ledges hang with pale stubs and beads. Press your palm to any wall and it comes away damp and slightly slick, and if you leave your palm long enough — a season, say — the wall will have begun to take a mould of it.

### The town

There is a town on the mound because the mound is warm, and for forty days' walk in any direction nothing else is.

It is packed. There is no other word. Houses are not built beside one another; they are built *into* and *onto* and *through* one another, sharing walls, sharing floors, sharing the roof-terrace that the neighbour above sleeps on. The lanes between them are shoulder-wide, roofed over half their length by the rooms that have grown across them, so that walking the town is mostly walking through a warm wet tunnel with pale light coming down at intervals in slots and vents. In some places you go on all fours. In some places you must turn sideways and breathe out. The population is somewhere around three hundred, and every single one of them is within earshot of everyone else, and this is the quietest place you will ever stand in.

They build with the mound itself. At the spring lip, where the deposit is only days old, the new stone is *curd* — soft as hard cheese, cuttable with a shell-edged spatula, sliceable into blocks by a woman with a bone saw and a bowl of grit. Carry the block down out of the water, into the cool dry air, and by the second evening it has set. By the fourth day it will turn an antler chisel. This is why there is a town of stone here made by people who have no metal beyond a few soft traded scraps kept as heirlooms and needles: the stone gives itself away while it is young, and then hardens into their walls. The whole architecture is a record of how fast a person can work before the material stops permitting it. Blocks are small, hand-sized, laid quickly. Ornament is scratched, not carved — a moment's work with a thumbnail while the surface still yields, and it lasts forever after.

Everything is white or the yellow-white of old tallow, streaked ochre where iron comes up in the water, streaked grey-green where the seep-algae get a foothold in the shade.

### The rule

Here is the rule that governs every hour of life on the mound:

*The town is closing.*

The same water that gives them the stone falls on the stone they have already laid. Every wall thickens. Every doorway narrows. Sills rise. Floors swell up around the legs of stools. Steps become ramps and then become slopes and then become walls. Left alone for a decade, a room would seal itself into a solid lump with its furniture inside it, like a fly in amber.

So they scrape. This is the central labour of the place, more constant than food. Every household holds a scraping-right to certain surfaces: this doorway, that stair-tread, the vent above the sleeping shelf, the four hand-spans of lane outside the door. The tools are wooden paddles edged with shell or with the mound's own hard shell-stone, and they are used with a short, damp, hissing stroke. It is done at dawn and at dusk and it is the sound of the town — a soft rasping from everywhere at once, unhurried, like a large animal grooming itself in the dark. The scrapings are collected, wet, in baskets, and re-used: mixed with crushed snail-shell into a putty for patching, or spread out on the west terraces to make paving.

Fog thickens the growth. Dry wind slows it almost to nothing. So a hard blowing day in late autumn is a holiday: nobody scrapes, everybody climbs to the roofs to be cold and idle, and the town is briefly noisy, and you can hear children shouting from four streets away, which for the rest of the year would be unthinkable.

What is not scraped is chosen not to be scraped. Down in the lowest layers, under the present floors, are rooms nobody cleaned — their grandparents' rooms, filled and sealed and grown solid, some with the bowls still standing in the drowned stone, visible through the translucency like objects under ice. A man wanting a new cellar takes a pick and digs *down* into the house of a woman four generations dead. There is a word here for that work which also means *remembering*.

And they do not bury. A body is laid out on a terrace where the overflow runs thin and steady, arms crossed, and left. In three years it is a soft white bundle of shape. In fifteen it is a smooth pillar. The pillars are moved when they are hard enough to lift, and put where a pillar is needed, and doorways of a certain age are held up by people. This is not felt as grim. It is felt as the only reasonable arrangement, and the pillars are patted in passing.

### Sound

Air here carries sound badly — it is wet, it is layered with warm and cold, the porous froth-stone drinks noise like felt. Voices die within twenty paces. But the shell-stone rings. Struck with a knuckle, a wall gives a clear woody bell-note, and the note travels through the solid fabric of the town, wall to floor to wall, faster and further than any shout.

So they talk by tapping. Two knocks and a scrape at the door-jamb means *food is ready*; a long roll of the fingertips on a load-bearing pillar means *someone is hurt*; there is a five-note figure that means *the pulse has changed* and it is the only signal that will bring every adult out of doors at once. Children learn the taps before they learn to speak clearly. Strangers find the town eerie and half-empty, and only realize later that they were being discussed the whole time, through the floor, under their feet.

### The living

Sparse and strange. The water is too alkaline and too warm and too eager to petrify. There are three plants of consequence: a stiff blue-grey rush that colonizes the cool marsh at the mound's foot and is cut for baskets and roofing; a fat succulent rosette that lives on the dry southwest slope where the seep is thin, harvested for its sour water; and, at the crown, one tree. A single low crooked willow-thing, hardly taller than a man, standing where a cold freshwater seam happens to cut across the hot vent. Its trunk is half-encased. Two households hold the hereditary right to scrape its bark, and do it every eighth day with wooden spoons, and have done for as long as anyone counts. If they stopped, it would become a pillar in eleven or twelve years. Everybody knows this. Nobody has ever tested it.

Animals are mostly absences. Insects hardly bother with the place — too cool, too caustic. There are blind pink fish the length of a finger in the warm channels, taken with wicker traps, tasting faintly of chalk. There are snails with thick knobbed shells that graze the algae film and are farmed loosely on the shaded northern terraces; their shells go into the patching-putty and their bodies into the pot. Once or twice a day, a tall pale wader stalks the shallows — the town's only regular visitor, tolerated, occasionally rescued, because a bird that stands too long in the wrong place will find its legs sheathed and stiffening. There is a ritual for freeing them, done with a wooden mallet and great care, and it is considered lucky, and the birds do not learn.

At dusk, in some seasons, a lean marsh-cat comes to the edge of the light and sits, and does not come closer, and is gone. Perhaps eight times a year. People mention it for weeks.

### From above

Picture it near sunset in early spring: the flats a sheet of dull silver, the sky low and colourless and bright, the mound sitting in it like a loaf of white bread in a grey dish. The mist column bending eastward. Three hundred people packed into a lump of stone the size of a large barn, warm, damp, mostly silent, tapping on the walls. The rasp of the evening scraping starting up terrace by terrace as the light goes.

And under all of it, that slow forty-beat pulse in the throat of the spring — swell, spill, slump — laying down another film of stone over the town, over the tools left out, over the pillars of the dead, patient as anything, willing to wait.
