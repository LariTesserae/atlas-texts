# The Unwearing

## Where it is

There is no down. Whatever floor the world has lies beneath a deck of heavy grey gas some four thousand meters below the lowest place a foot can stand, and that gas is not water and never was; it is something denser and colder, the color of an unlit lamp, and it does not rise. Everything habitable is above it: a regional sprawl of ridges, shoulders, and unnamed high basins, a hundred kilometers of upness, arranged like the wreckage of a much larger idea.

The rock is new. This is the first thing a hand learns. Every edge is a blade. Every fracture face is fresh as a struck flint, and the scree is not gravel but shatter — plates and splinters and prisms, none of them rounded, none of them dulled, because nothing here has ever been rubbed by anything. There is no dust. There is no soil. There is no mud, no clay, no silt, no delta, no rounded cobble, none of the soft consequences that a world with water accumulates. The mountains are still in their raw drafting: crystalline, faceted, sheared, and everywhere slightly wrong in the way of a thing that has not yet been checked.

## The drone

The sound arrives before the place does, and it never stops.

It comes up out of the rock. The ranges are rising — a finger's width a year, a hand's width in a lifetime — and rising rock is not quiet. What you hear is the continuous grinding self-argument of a mountain being made: a fundamental somewhere below hearing that you take in through the sternum and the teeth, a mid-register roar like a river that is not there, and above that a bright metallic seething from the fields of bell-grass that resonate with it.

It is deafening. Speech is not possible. A shout at a hand's distance from an ear is a movement of air and nothing more. And yet it never changes. The drone holds its note for generations. Old makers can tell you — by scratching it into stone — that the pitch dropped a half-step in their childhood, and that this was the great event of their century, and that nobody has heard anything happen since. It is a roar with the temperament of a glacier. Loud past bearing, and patient.

Consequences: nothing here has a voice. All language is incised. Every flat surface within reach of a path is dense with cut marks, and the marks do not weather, so a wall may carry eleven thousand years of conversation with no line lost, arguments and inventories and jokes and grief pressed shoulder to shoulder in a script that runs in whatever direction the stone allowed.

## The green

Water is absent, and the place is nevertheless lush — in patches, with enormous emptiness between them.

The plants do not drink. Their internal fluid is a clear, faintly resinous oil that stays liquid at these temperatures and does not evaporate into this thin cold air. They take their carbon from the heavy gas that seeps up from below, and their energy — this is the part that takes some getting used to — from the drone. Their tissues are piezoelectric. Vibration is food. The great sheaves of bell-grass, three meters tall, dark green going to bronze at the tips, are standing here for the same reason a leaf stands in sunlight.

So the green grows where the sound pools. Amphitheaters, cirque-backs, the concave inside of a curved ridge: these are the gardens. A hanging shelf a thousand meters up a cliff face will be furred solid with listening moss, a foot deep, softly and continuously singing back what it receives. Below and beside it, five kilometers of bare faceted stone with nothing at all. Then another garden, in another fold, out of sight. The land is mostly empty and occasionally overwhelming.

The oil-trees grow in the deep basins where the roar is trapped and doubled. They are low, wide, many-trunked, with leaves like flat grey tongues, and they have no need of insects or birds or wind, since their seeds are moved by the sound itself — shaken loose, walked downslope by vibration across the scree, a few centimeters a year, arriving somewhere in a thousand years. Nothing pollinates. Nothing browses. Nothing burrows.

There are no animals. Not one. No wing, no track, no scat, no shell, no small alarmed thing under a stone. The gardens are silent of animal life in a world where silence is unavailable, and this is a very particular kind of loneliness, the sort you notice in your hands before your mind.

## The light

Dim, always. There is a sun somewhere behind a permanent high haze, and it delivers something like the last twenty minutes of an overcast evening, all day, forever.

The rest of the light is local. Freshly broken rock here luminesces — a pale green-white glow at the new surface, fading over about nine days. So a rockfall is a lamp for a week and a half. So a chipped step glows while you cut it. So the paths, where feet are constantly renewing the stone's surface, are faintly and permanently lit, and from a high place at the dim hour you can see the whole network of them threading the ranges like phosphorescence in a dark sea: every route anyone has ever taken, still shining, because nothing here fades on its own.

The mosses glow too, weakly, in sympathy with the loudest passages of the drone.

## The makers

There are hands here. Not many, and never in numbers — you might cross three basins and find only the marks.

Their tools are stone, bone-that-is-not-bone (the hard core-wood of the oil-trees), and cord twisted from bell-grass fiber. There is a little cold-worked metal, native copper picked out of veins in the fresh rock, hammered into blades that do not hold an edge as well as a flake of the local glassy basalt, and are treasured anyway. They build small: a windbreak, a domed shelter of stacked plates chinked with moss, a cairn, a bridge of two spanning slabs over a fissure. One building per valley, perhaps. Sometimes a valley with only a cairn, and the cairn covered in ten thousand years of writing.

Here is what they are for, as they understand it, and as far as anyone can check:

Nothing in this world wears out. There is no rain to soften it, no frost-and-thaw, no river to carry a grain anywhere, no animal to disturb a slope. The mountains rise and shatter and stay shattered, sharp, unfinished, never resolved into landscape. The only erosion in the world is the erosion that hands and feet cause. A path walked daily for two thousand years goes down into the rock a hand's depth, and the stone at its bottom is the only smooth stone in existence — polished, rounded, dull, *old* — and the makers hold that this is holy, because it is finished, because it has been made real by being worn.

They are wearing the world into existence. Slowly. A basin at a time. They keep a tally of smoothed surfaces, cut into a wall.

## The rules

**Nothing has been worn, so nothing is properly old, so time has poor traction here.** Sequence works locally and stops working at distance. Distances do not sum: three legs of a journey measured honestly, added honestly, do not equal the journey. You will meet the same cairn — same stones, same marks, same chip out of the third stone — twice in one day's walk in one direction, and it is not a copy, and the second time your own newest inscription is already on it.

**The summit is undecided.** Look up at the range's high end and you will see the ridgeline more than once, superimposed like tracings on top of each other, four or five candidate crests at four or five heights, all faintly present, none chosen. On a clear dim day you can watch a whole valley be considered — a drafted trough with drafted gardens in it, hanging translucent over the actual slope — and then be set aside, and be gone. Nobody climbs to the top. There is not yet a top to climb to.

**Roots reach into rock that has not arrived.** In the deep gardens, oil-tree roots run out of the ground into the air and grip firmly onto nothing at a place where, in some centuries, there will be a boulder. Certain shelters are built snug against the shape of a stone that has not yet fallen; the makers fit them to the future contour and wait, and the fit is perfect. This is considered ordinary craft, not prophecy. The stone is already that shape. It simply isn't there yet.

**And silence is the one thing to be afraid of.** There are pockets — small, a few meters across, rare, drifting slower than a plant grows — where the drone is not. Inside them, quiet. Inside them, the vegetation dies within a season, and the rock has no light in its fresh faces, and the ground does not commit to an ending: a slope in a silence-pocket goes down and down past where the slope's bottom is, and the number of joints in your hand becomes a matter of opinion, and a thing dropped does not land, and the writing on any stone dragged inside becomes writing nobody has ever cut, in a script for a language nobody has needed, saying something that has not been decided.

The makers wall these off with cairns when they find them, and inscribe the wall with the date and the word for *unfinished*, and they walk the long way around, and their long way around, over the centuries, wears a smooth path into the raw stone — which is, in the end, the work.
