## The Held Water

At two thousand six hundred metres, in a ring of ridges worn down to the shape of shoulders, a valley is full to its lip.

Not flooded — *filled*, and long since. The water came up so far in the past that the coming is no longer an event in the landscape's memory; the basin has settled into being a lake the way a stone settles into being a stone. Everything that a valley usually keeps above water is below it: the terraces, the paths, the low houses, the pass road, the two moraine hills like knuckles, the beaches of nine older shorelines banded up the slopes like growth rings. The only thing that leaves is the overflow, at a notch in the northeast rim, where a sheet of water two hand-spans deep slides over polished granite and falls out of the world.

You are inside it. That is the condition of the place. There is no shore to stand on.

---

**The light.**

Call it half a light. For four months of the year an ice lid seals the surface, thickening to a metre and going white with wind-packed snow, and beneath it the day is a rumour: a grey that is not quite a colour, strongest at noon, arriving diffuse and directionless so that nothing has a shadow. In the open months the surface admits a cold green shaft that dies at forty metres. Below that the basin makes its own.

The making is done by the plants. Down the walls, in the drowned groves, there is a slow bioluminescence — not the sharp blue of the sea, but a low ochre-green, the colour of a lamp seen through a wet window. It is not steady. It moves through the meadows in unhurried sheets, brightening where the water shears, so that the deep basin looks like a plain under a very distant, very patient weather. You can read the grain of a plank by it if you hold the plank close. You cannot see across a clearing.

---

**The cold and the layers.**

Three and a half degrees at the floor, always. Near the ice, colder and lighter. In spring, when the snowfields on the rim let go, the inflow comes in heavy with rock flour and dives — a grey river running *underwater*, hugging the old streambed, keeping its banks for two kilometres before it frays.

And there are the sills. This is the first strangeness a visitor notices, and it never stops being strange: the water is layered so sharply that the layers have surfaces. At sixty-one metres there is a boundary you can see and touch, a shimmer like heat over a road, where colder and saltier water — the basin leaches salts from an old evaporite bed under the west wall — refuses to mix with what lies above. The detritus of the whole upper valley falls onto it and *stops*. It lies there in drifts. A false floor of pale flocculent snow, metres thick in the hollows, level as a lake within the lake, with the tops of the tallest weed-trees standing up through it like poles through fog.

Below that sill, another at a hundred and forty. Below that, the true floor, which almost nothing reaches.

Things fall for years here. A leaf shed in the upper groves may take three seasons to arrive at the first sill, and then rest there for a century.

---

**Sound.**

It is loud. People expect a drowned place to be quiet, and this one is not, and the loudness has no source you can point at.

The overflow at the notch pours continuously, and the rock of the whole rim carries the note inward, so the basin hums at a pitch below hearing that you take in through the sternum. Layered over it: the ice. In winter the lid works against the shore and against itself, and the cracks propagate for hundreds of metres in an instant, arriving as a *whoop* that slides downward in pitch, then a second one, then a long ricochet of them, all night, all day, a thousand a day in the hard freezes. Gas comes up from the west wall in silver ropes and the ropes chime as they break. Talus, which is nowhere near as settled as it looks, releases a boulder every few weeks; the boulder tumbles down a slope in slow motion, taking half an hour to travel where it would take four seconds in air, and knocks and knocks and knocks the whole way.

And the basin is a resonator. Because the walls are rounded and regular and the layers reflect sound like ceilings, there are standing rooms in the water — places, some the size of a house, where the noise cancels and you are struck deaf in the middle of the din; and places twenty metres away where it doubles and shakes your teeth. These rooms are stable. They have been in the same locations for as long as anyone has kept track. The builders knew about them.

---

**The seiche.**

Every twenty-two minutes the whole lake leans.

It is the basin's slow heartbeat, a standing wave set going by wind on the ice and by the outflow's tug, and it never stops, because there is nothing here to damp it. The surface rises four centimetres at one end and falls four at the other, which is nothing — but underwater, at the sills, the same wave heaves the boundary layers eleven metres up and eleven metres down. So twice an hour the false floor of white flocculence rises through the groves like a tide of fog, swallows them, and withdraws. And the current that comes with it lays the whole meadow over in one direction, holds it, and then lays it the other way.

The forest bows east. The forest bows west. It has done this some eight hundred million times.

---

**What grows.**

Lush, and scattered. Long floors of pale silt lie empty for a hundred metres at a stretch, and then a grove: stonewort meadows crusted with lime, crunching underfoot like frost; beards of moss two metres long streaming off every ledge; and the weed-trees, which are the basin's own invention — lampweed, a single black-red strap of a stem rising thirty metres from a limestone holdfast, branching only near the top, buoyed by a row of gas bladders that pop like knuckles when a bladder is overfilled and gives.

The leaves are nearly black. At this light, a plant cannot afford to reflect anything.

Nothing eats. That is the ecology's central fact and it shows in every direction. Nothing is grazed, nothing is bored, nothing is pruned. So the groves grow until they fail structurally and then collapse in a heap and rot where they lie, and the rot is done by bacteria and by a fungus that makes white lace over everything at a maddeningly slow pace — a fallen weed-tree takes forty years to disappear. There are no borrows in the silt, no tracks, no shells, no bones in the cores, none, not at any depth. No animal has ever lived in the Held Water. The sediment is a clean book.

The absence has a sound too, or rather the absence *of* a sound: no clicks, no scrapes, no small business anywhere. Only geology and weather and the popping of the plants.

---

**What was built.**

Scattered, and old, and dry-stone.

The pass road comes over the rim at the low saddle, well made, cambered, laid with fitted flags, and goes down into the water and keeps going, and at eighteen metres you can still follow it, and at thirty it disappears under a slide. There are terraces on the south-facing slope, twenty-one of them, retaining walls waist-high, holding soil that now holds moss. There are three houses left standing on the second moraine hill: single rooms, corbelled roofs, doorways a person must stoop through, everything cold-preserved and full of clear water. In one, a wooden bowl sits on a stone ledge exactly where it was set down, and a bundle of bast cord, and a bark box with fired clay beads in it, and a chert blade hafted with resin, and nothing made of bone, or leather, or horn, or wool, because there was never any such thing to make it from. They wove and they knapped and they fired clay and they built in stone. They ate seed and root and greens. It was a whole life and it left no fibre of animal in it anywhere.

Down at the first sill, out on the level white, there are the weirs: low stone arcs, forty of them, each opening upstream into the seiche current. They are not fish traps. They cannot be. Nobody has ever settled what they caught.

And there are the cairns — a line of them along what was the summer path, hip-height, well-stacked.

They are still being added to. This is not a matter of speculation. The stones are counted, the counts increase, and the new stones are always the same kind: flat river cobbles of a rock that does not outcrop within the basin at all. They arrive at a rate of perhaps six a year, distributed along the line, and they are placed well — bedded, keyed, chosen for fit. The seiche does it, some say, over centuries. Nobody who has spent a night in a quiet room at the first sill and heard the lake go on roaring around the silence they were sitting in has ever found that explanation sufficient.

---

**The rule of the place.**

Everything here is finished and nothing here is still. The valley has had its shape for a long time and will keep it; the erosion is in balance, the talus at its angle of repose, the shorelines banded and stable, the notch cutting downward at the speed of fingernails. But inside that finished shape there are twenty processes running at once — the ice making and unmaking, the spring plume diving, the gas ropes, the sills breathing eleven metres up and eleven metres down, the groves growing to their limit and toppling, the fungus lacing them away, the fog of white detritus rising and falling twice an hour over a road that goes nowhere and a line of cairns that grows.

Cold, dark, loud, tended, empty of animals, and entirely alive.

It holds its water at the top of the mountains and lets a thin sheet of it go over the lip, and the falling of that sheet is the note the whole basin is tuned to.
