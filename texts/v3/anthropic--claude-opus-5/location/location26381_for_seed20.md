## The Standing Water

Begin with the fact that will not sit still in the mind: there is a lake on top of the mountain, and the lake is falling off it, and the lake never gets any lower.

The mountain is new. You can tell by the edges. Nothing has had time to be rubbed kind. The black glass along the rim still holds the shape of the moment it stopped being liquid — lobes and pillows and ropes of it, piled up like organs, like tripe, like something turned inside out and left to cool in a heap. Set a palm against it and you'll come away bleeding from four places you didn't feel. Under the water it is worse and better: worse because the glass grows in blades there, in fans and blooms and rosettes, and better because the water carries you, so that you drift above the blades like a hand above a bed of scalpels, and only the current decides.

The water is warm. Blood-warm at the surface, warmer as you go down, and at three fathoms it gets into your joints and undoes them, and you understand that this is not a lake fed by snow. Somewhere below, the mountain is still being born. The heat comes up in slow columns you can see because they bend the light, and along the columns the water shivers with a fine white silt that never settles, so that everything is seen through a suspended dust, greenish, dim, aquarium-dim, cathedral-dim. Noon here is the light of a room where someone has drawn the curtains against an illness.

And the sound.

It is not possible to describe the sound as a sound, because sound is a thing that arrives at you and this does not arrive; it is already there, it was there before you, you have walked into the body of it. Say instead what it does. It fills the sinuses like water. It takes the small bones of the ear and holds them still, which is a kind of mercy, and after an hour you stop hearing it and start being inside it, the way a fish is inside the sea and does not remark on wetness. It vibrates the surface of the lake into a permanent gooseflesh so that the water has no mirror-face except in certain places, of which more later. It shakes the fresh glass off the rim in handfuls. It is the reason nothing here has roots and everything here has grip.

The sound is the water going over the edge.

It goes over on all sides at once, or on three sides, or on one — it changes, sometimes hourly. The rim is perhaps four hundred paces across; you can walk the whole circuit in the time it takes to get hungry. Wherever the water is leaving, it leaves in a sheet the colour of a wet flint, thinning as it goes, becoming lace, becoming smoke, becoming a white column of nothing that drops out of sight into a cloud of its own making. From the rim you cannot see the bottom of the fall. There may not be one. What comes back up is the roar and a warm wind and a fine rain that falls upward past your face, so that if you stand at the lip in the wrong weather you are being rained on from below, and your hair lifts, and your tears go up your forehead into your scalp.

Here is the part that will not sit still. That much water leaving, and the level does not drop a finger's width. Not in a day, not in the length of anyone's staying. There is no river feeding in. There is no rain worth the name. The water leaves and the lake is full and both of these are true and the place holds them both without apparent strain, the way a knot holds two ropes.

The people who came here — and people did come here, some time ago, not many, and not for long — decided that the roar was doing it. That the sound of the falling water was what kept the water up. It is a foolish idea and it is the only idea that fits, and after a while in the noise you will find yourself believing it, because you will notice the places where the noise cancels itself.

They are small. A pace across, two paces. The fall throws its sound in every direction, and where two of those directions meet exactly out of step, the roar goes out like a snuffed wick, and there is a pocket of silence standing in the water like a room. You can find them by watching the surface: where the gooseflesh smooths to glass, there is a null. Step into one and the world drops off you and your own blood is suddenly the loudest thing in creation.

Inside the nulls, the water does not hold itself up.

This is where things go wrong, or where they go according to some other rule. In the nulls the lake sags. A hand pushed into a null goes down further than the arm is long. Stones dropped there do not fall — they hover, turn over slowly, and then rise, and go on rising past the surface and up into the spray-cloud and are not seen again. Plants grow in the nulls and only in the nulls: a black whip-weed, three or four blades to a clump, that stands up perfectly straight in defiance of the current and thickens where the silence is deepest. It is the only vegetation of any substance in the entire basin. Everywhere else there is a thin varnish of something red-brown on the glass — not moss, not algae, a film that squeaks under the foot — and once, on the eastern shelf, a single upright tree, drowned to the shoulder, its lower branches bare and its crown still above water and still putting out small tough leaves, year on year, from a trunk that has been dead since before the mountain was.

The nulls move. That is the worst of it. The fall shifts its side, the interference walks across the basin, and a room of silence that was here at dawn is forty paces away by evening, and if you were standing in it you are now standing in the roar again with your ears bleeding a little and no memory of the transition. And things left in a null — a sandal, a cairn, a body — are found afterwards to have been *revised*. Not destroyed. Not moved. The sandal has one more strap than it did. The cairn has always been that many stones and you were wrong about the number. This is checkable and no one wants to check it twice.

There is not much alive. A soft grey eel, arm's length, that comes to the shallows and watches you with its whole flank, and there is only ever one of it, on any shore, at any hour, and it is the same eel. Some kind of pale shrimp working the hot columns, transparent enough to be mostly a suggestion. Birds arrive, blown up out of the weather — always exhausted, always the wrong species for this altitude, gulls, a heron once — and they land on the rim, and they drink, and then they do not leave. They walk the rim. They walk it for days, sober as clerks, and then one morning they are not there and there is no sign of what became of them. It is generally agreed that they went up.

The built things: a flight of nine steps, cut in the glass, going down into the water from a shelf on the north side and continuing down, visible, ten or twelve steps more before the silt takes them. They go nowhere. There is nothing under the water for them to reach. Their treads are worn concave in the centres, which means feet, which means many feet, which means a long time, and this mountain is not old enough for a long time, and that discrepancy is simply left lying there for anyone to trip on. A doorway on the western rim, freestanding, two uprights and a lintel, black glass dressed smooth without any tool-mark at all, framing nothing but the drop and the white column and the cloud. It is not tall. You would duck. Nobody ducks. Three cairns on the southern shelf, or four; a rectangular footing of laid stones half in the water that might have been a bath house, or a tank, or a pen, and which fills and drains with the shifting of the shoreline. And, on the highest lobe of the rim, a hollow boulder the size of a kneeling ox, already ringing. No clapper. No striker. It has been ringing since before the first arrival — a low sustained tone that you can only detect by putting your forehead against the stone, at which point it comes into you through the skull, and is not a bell note, and is the only thing in the basin louder than the fall.

The rules, so far as anyone assembled them:

Down is not toward the centre of the earth. Down is toward the loudest place. In the roar, this is nearly the same thing, and you don't notice. Near the nulls it is not the same thing at all, and you fall sideways.

The mountain is still going. New glass budding at the waterline in lobes that swell and split and cool within the hour; the shoreline never twice in the same place; a shelf you slept on gone entirely by the next night, and a new one, sharper, in the wrong direction. Nothing here has an ending. Everything is in the condition of being still under way.

The surface, seen from beneath, is a mirror — the roar keeps the outside of it in gooseflesh but the underside stays perfect, and if you swim down five fathoms and roll over you will see the whole basin inverted, black rim, white column, dim green sky, and a figure walking the rim upside down, unhurried, at exactly your pace, which is not you, and which is not not you either.

And the noise, if you stay in it long enough, stops being a noise and starts being a sentence. Not words. The shape a sentence makes. Subject, then a long complicating middle, then the sense that a verb is coming. It has been coming for as long as the water has been standing up. You will find yourself, on the ninth or tenth day, sitting at the lip with your legs over the drop and your hair going upward, waiting for it, the way you wait for someone to finish speaking.

That is the reason the birds walk the rim. That is the reason no one who came here left a name behind, only stairs and a doorway and a bath. It is a warm place. It is a small place. You could cross it before lunch. It holds you the way a held note holds a room, and it will not, will not, will not finish.
