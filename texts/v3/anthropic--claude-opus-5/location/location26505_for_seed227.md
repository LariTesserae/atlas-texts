## The Bellows

You find it by the absence of birds.

For half a day's walk the coast is ordinary: peat and heather laid over old basalt, seeps blackening the low places, everything drab and cold and dripping under a sky the colour of unwashed wool. There is no rain and there has been no rain, but every stem carries water; brush a hand through the crowberry and your sleeve is soaked to the elbow. This is a country that never dries and rarely pours. Fog gathers in the hollows at night and by morning has committed itself to the moss.

The birds thin out. Ravens stop working the ridge. Then, underfoot, a sensation before a sound — a slow pressure in the soles, a knock inside the shin bone, arriving in long pulses maybe nine or twelve seconds apart. You mistake it for your own pulse until you notice it is too slow, and does not quicken when you climb.

Two hundred paces further the ground has been worn into a shallow bowl by nothing but weather and time, and in the centre of the bowl the earth simply ends.

---

The opening is not large. Fifteen paces across, oblong, its lip rolled smooth as a lip should be. Grass grows to the very edge and then leans in, permanently combed inward by the draft of the intake. There is no fence and no need for one; the hole announces itself the way a fire does.

The noise comes up out of it.

It is not a roar in any sense you have used the word before. A roar is something a throat does with air it has already got. This is the sound of a great volume of air being *made to leave*, forced up a stone flue two hundred and forty feet long by the weight of the North Atlantic arriving in a tunnel it cannot fit through. It comes in surges timed to the swell, and between the surges it never stops, only drops to a bass mutter that seems to originate in your own sternum. At full surge it is total. It abolishes the concept of other sounds. Shout and you will feel your throat working and hear nothing; you will suspect for a moment that you have never been able to hear at all, that hearing was something you invented to explain the world.

This is the first rule of the place. Sound here is not information. It is weather. You dress for it, you shelter from it, you plan your day around it, and if you are careless with it, it takes something from you that does not come back.

---

The descent is on the seaward wall, where the rock has fractured into a natural stair, and where — perhaps three hundred years ago, perhaps eight hundred — someone improved it.

The improvements are modest and everywhere. Handholds pecked into the basalt with a harder stone, each one now dished and softened by generations of palms and by the endless condensation, so that they read less as tool marks than as the fossil prints of hands. A line of iron pegs, four of them, hammered into a crack — cheap bog iron, bloom-forged, more rust than metal now, swollen to twice their thickness and locked immovably into the stone they are destroying. A rope of braided nettle fibre and horsehair still hanging from the third peg, gone stiff and black, holding together out of habit. At the sixth turning, a lamp niche, cut square, blackened at the back, containing a scrap of moss wick and a rime of tallow that the cold has kept from spoiling.

Nobody has come down for a long time. The marks are not abandoned so much as finished. The place and the marks have arrived at an arrangement.

Halfway down there is a ledge, and on the ledge a hut.

Calling it a hut is generous. Four courses of dry-laid stone in a horseshoe against the wall, roofed once with driftwood and turf, the turf long since slid off and the driftwood collapsed inward into a grey basket of ribs. Inside: a hearth ring that has not held fire in living memory; a whalebone scraper; three shallow stone bowls, one still holding an inch of water that is not rain but the wall's own sweat, arriving one bead at a time. And in a fold of the rock behind, protected from the draft, a fist-sized lump of beeswax, kneaded, dark with handling, with the hollows of two thumbs still in it.

That is the whole technology of the Bellows: a stair, a rope, a lamp, a bowl, and wax for the ears. It is exactly sufficient. Nothing further was needed and so nothing further was made.

---

Below the ledge the light gives out.

There was never much. At the top, on the best day of the year, the sky is a pale slot; in this latitude, in this season, the sky is a slightly less dark thing than the rock, a suggestion of grey rumour overhead. Fifty feet down, that rumour is all there is. A hundred feet down you navigate by the temperature of the stone and the direction of the draft.

And in that dark, the walls are green.

This is the strangeness that isn't strange — the thing you understand at once and are still unprepared for. The Bellows is perpetually damp, sheltered from wind that is not its own wind, frost-free because the sea's breath is never colder than four degrees, and lit, barely, by a slot of northern sky for a few hours a day. Mosses are patient about light. Liverworts ask for almost nothing. The whole shaft from lip to floor is upholstered: sheet moss in overlapping shingles, cushions of it swollen and dripping, filmy ferns with fronds one cell thick fanning out where the seep runs steadiest, and in the deepest reaches a pale flat crust of liverwort that has given up on green and settled for the colour of a drowned thumbnail. In summer the surge-spray pushes a faint salt haze up the flue and the plants nearest the floor take it and endure it and grow a little tougher for it.

It is lush. It is also, and this is the point, only a skin.

Because the Bellows itself is empty. Two hundred and forty feet of vertical air, and nothing in it. No trees to fill it, no birds crossing it, no falling water — the water here does not fall, it only weeps, one bead per stone per minute, and the beads are taken by the moss before they can gather into anything with a voice. All that green is spread out flat against the walls, three inches deep, a wet tapestry on the inside of an enormous hollow bottle. Stand at the bottom and look up and you are standing in a room whose volume is entirely unoccupied, whose every surface is crowded with slow life, and whose contents are one substance only: noise, under pressure, going up.

---

The floor is a rubble cone of boulders, worn round, with gaps between them through which the air screams. Off to one side, out of the draft, a pool the size of a cart wheel, three feet deep, black, and so still that the surge does not trouble it — only a fine shiver crossing it with each pulse, like a struck drumskin.

In the pool: shrimp. Nine of them, or eleven. Translucent, eyeless, moving with the unhurried authority of things without competitors. They have been here as a population longer than the stair has been a stair.

Elsewhere in the shaft, if you have the patience to hold a lamp against the moss for a long time: white springtails in the thousands, so small they read as a dusting of flour that flinches. One species of moth, grey, small, and effectively flightless here — it walks on the moss and mates on the moss and will not commit itself to open air that is moving that fast. A single spider per ten square feet of wall, each one holding a territory it will never leave, in a web the size of a coin, spun in the lee of a stone.

And nothing that sings. Nothing that calls, or listens, or needs to. Every animal in the Bellows has solved the problem of sound by not participating in it. That is the second rule: this is a country where hearing has no use, and so the whole ecology has quietly set hearing down.

---

Three things change here, and all three change slowly.

The tide, over six hours, walks the roar up and down through its register; at the lowest ebb of the smallest neap, the Bellows goes almost quiet — a wet hollow breathing, twenty minutes of something a person could speak in, once or twice a month, and that is presumably when the bowls were filled and the words were said. The season, over a year, thickens and thins the moss and sends the salt haze higher or lower. And the shaft itself, over ten thousand years, deepens by the width of a fingernail, drinks its iron pegs, rounds its own edges, and holds precisely to what it already is.

Nothing here is going anywhere. The place attained its shape a long time ago and has been maintaining it ever since with the enormous, uninterrupted, indifferent labour of a sea breathing through a hole in a cold country, in the dark, forever, to an audience of eleven blind shrimp and a lump of wax with somebody's thumbprints in it.
