## The Loft

You come at it from below, and the first thing you understand is that the air has an edge.

For three days you climb a young mountain — glass-black basalt still holding the shapes it cooled into, ridges like broken bottle rims, no soil, no rounding, nothing yet worn soft. The rock is barely older than the idea of the rock. Frost stands on it in fibers an inch long, grown overnight out of nothing, and crushes to powder underfoot with a sound like sugar. The air up here is thin enough to make your vision pulse at the corners.

Then, at around the height where you expect to die, you push your hand into something.

There is no wall, but there is a boundary: a shimmering, waist-deep, then head-deep, then shoreless layer of *thicker air* lying across the summit plateau like water in a basin. Above your knees it is cold and starved; above your shoulders it is cold and rich, dense as sea-level air, faintly warm, faintly sweet, and utterly loud. You climb the last twenty meters and you have surfaced into an atmosphere the land is making for itself.

The plateau goes on past the curvature of anything. Call it a continent set on a shelf four kilometers up. It has its own weather, its own pressure seasons, its own drought and flood, and none of it is inherited from the world below.

## The Machine, Which Nobody Built

There is not one nail here. Not one lashed pole, not one shaped stone, not one fired thing. In all the vast Loft nothing has ever been *made*, because nothing here has ever needed to be separate from its maker. Function did not get externalized into tools. It got grown into bodies and then bodies got grown into each other, and the result, over a stupefyingly patient span of time, is industry.

The plateau is an atmosphere factory.

Its principal works are the **bellowers** — herd animals the size of barns, low-slung, six-legged, thickly pelted in a black wool that drinks the glare. Their torsos are two-thirds lung, arranged in an accordion of ribbed cartilage bands, and they do not breathe so much as *stroke*. Each stroke takes about ninety seconds: a long inward rasp, a pause you can walk across, a long expulsion of warm humid gas that hangs above the herd in a visible seam. They eat the cushion-forest and they eat rock lichen and they eat the frost itself. What they exhale is a made air — nitrogen-heavy, oxygen-enriched by symbionts packed into their intestinal galleries, warm by four or five degrees, and just heavy enough to sink and stay.

There are, by any honest reckoning, hundreds of millions of them. They stand shoulder to shoulder to shoulder for horizons at a time. From a ridge, a bellower plain looks like a black sea with a slow swell in it, and the swell is synchronized — not perfectly, but in vast drifting bands of phase, so that pressure ripples travel across the plateau over hours the way weather fronts travel elsewhere.

They are not the whole plant. They are only the pumps.

**Flue trees** are the ductwork: hollow, silver-barked, valved. A mature flue stands forty meters, has no leaves in the ordinary sense, and functions as a chimney with a check-valve of pulpy tissue at its base. Warm exhalation collects in the ground layer, is drawn up the flue by the temperature difference, and is released high above the canopy, where it spreads and forms the ceiling of the lens. Their bark is a condenser: it is corrugated, hydrophilic, and colder than the gas rising inside it, so water beads on the inner wall and runs down and out at the roots in a permanent thin dribble. Every flue tree stands in its own small dark ring of mud and moss, which in this frozen brightness is a garden, a spa, a metropolis.

**Gyres** are the flywheels. This is the part visitors refuse to believe. A gyre is a sessile animal, roughly toroidal, two to five meters across, that spends its adult life anchored in a rock cleft with a ring of dense muscle and mineralized tendon spinning inside a lubricated sheath. It turns at maybe forty revolutions per minute and it never stops, not for its entire hundred-and-forty-year life; it is fed by attendant symbionts that crawl into its intake and are dissolved. What it does with the stored rotation is pump groundwater and grind rock into the mineral slurry the cushion-forest eats. Gyre fields hum. A hillside of them at midday makes a sound like a power station heard from a field.

And there is **filament**: a black, wiry lichen that ropes across every surface, up every flue, through every gyre-cleft, into the pelt of every bellower. It is electrically active — genuinely so, ampere-scale — carrying charge from the muscle-work of the herds to the roots of the forest, where it drives ion pumps that no chemistry at this temperature could otherwise afford. The Loft is wired. On overcast days, if there are ever overcast days, you can smell ozone in the low places, and the filament ticks.

## Density

Nothing here is spaced out. There is no clearing, no meadow, no bare patch, no interval.

Every square centimeter of surface is occupied and most are occupied three or four deep. Rock wears a pelt: crust lichen under moss under a felt of hair-fine hydroid animals under a mobile skin of mites. Cushion plants grow so tightly packed that the "forest floor" is a continuous upholstered mattress — springy, warm to the touch from the respiration inside it, and thoroughly inhabited. Push a finger in and you feel the mattress *move away from your finger*, thousands of small tenants relocating.

The air column is stratified like a warehouse. Ground level: crawlers, grazers, the perpetual scuttling carpet. Knee to head: the small flying things, dense enough that walking is like pushing through drizzle made of bodies, harmless, constant, a soft peppering on the cheeks. Above the canopy: the great slow soarers, ribbon-winged, gliding in the exhalation thermals, so numerous that their shadows make the ground flicker like a bad lamp. Above them: pale specks, kilometers up, at the ceiling of the lens, where the manufactured air ends and the dead air begins — creatures that farm the boundary layer and have never come down.

Overwhelming is the honest word. Not menacing. Nothing here is much interested in you. It is simply that presence is total; there is no empty; you cannot find a place to set down a bag where the bag is not immediately in someone's house.

## Light

The lens focuses.

Denser air over thin air, warm over cold, curved by pressure — the whole atmosphere of the Loft is a lens, and it does what lenses do. Sunlight enters slightly bent and slightly concentrated. Add the ice-crystal haze that never quite clears, add the glare from frost fields and from the mirror-scale of certain herd animals' flanks, and the result is a brightness that has physical weight. It is not warm. It is just *much*. Whiteout brilliance in a place four degrees above freezing.

You will see two suns most days, sometimes four, sun-dogs pinned at twenty-two degrees and again at forty-six, with a vertical pillar in the morning and an arc overhead like a smile turned upside down. Rainbows lie flat on the ground in the fog rings under the flue trees. The horizon doubles: one real, one floated above it by the density gradient, so that distant herds appear both on the plain and slightly in the sky, upside down, grazing on nothing.

The vegetation answers this by being black. Coal black, velvet black, black as a stove — every leaf and cushion and thallus is pigmented to the absolute maximum, because photons are the only free heat in the world. Snow will not lie within a hand's width of a living plant. The whole landscape is a soot-dark quilt in a blinding white light, and the visual effect is of looking at a photographic negative that is somehow also correct.

Almost everything with eyes here has slits, or nictitating smoke-films, or many small apertures instead of few large ones. A significant portion of the fauna has given up on vision entirely and navigates by sound, because the sound is reliable and the light is a scream.

## Sound

The Loft has a fundamental. It sits somewhere around 30 hertz — the aggregate of the bellowers' exhalation stroke, the flue trees' draft, the gyre-hum — and it never stops, has not stopped in the geological memory of the place, and is loud enough that you feel it in your sternum before you consciously hear it. On top of that: the higher hum of the gyre fields, the papery roar of the insect layer, the wingbeat percussion of the soarers, and, cutting across everything at intervals, the *knocking*.

Flue trees knock. When their basal valve closes against a pressure surge, the trunk raps once, a hard dry crack like a plank dropped on stone. A pressure ripple crossing a flue forest sets off a wave of knocking that travels for a hundred kilometers at the speed of sound, arriving as a rattling applause that swells, passes over you, and goes on into the distance for two full minutes.

There is no silence anywhere on the Loft. Animals that evolved here and are somehow carried below the rim die of the quiet — not metaphorically; they lose their orientation, their heart rhythm, their nerve. Sound is the medium the way water is a medium.

## Water

Damp, never wet. There are no rivers. There is not enough liquid at this altitude to organize itself into a river, and the young rock has not been carved long enough to give one a channel.

Instead there is condensation, and it is everywhere, and it is entirely biological in its scheduling. Warm exhaled air meets cold black rock and lays down a film. Flue-tree bark sweats. Rime feathers grow on the windward side of every cushion overnight and are harvested at dawn by armies of small licking things. In the hollows of the fresh basalt, where fracture-joints make hexagonal pits, meltwater and condensate collect in **sweat tarns**: still, dark, glassy pools a few meters across, thermally insulated by a lid of living scum, each one packed to opacity with larvae. Step wrong and the lid takes your foot and the pool below is soup.

The bellowers drink by breathing over one another. The forests drink from their own chimneys. Nothing here waits for rain, and rain — real rain, a proper convective storm brewed in the lens — comes perhaps four times a year and is treated as a catastrophe and a festival, a hammering warm deluge that flushes the mattress, drowns a fraction of everything, and leaves the plateau steaming for a week.

## Slowness

Everything in the paragraph above happens, but it happens *slowly*.

The Loft has a metabolism, and it is a cold one. The great pressure cycle — the plateau's own breathing, the slow accumulation and slow leakage of its manufactured atmosphere — takes about four hundred days to complete. That is the year here: a pressure year, not a temperature year. High season, when the lens is at maximum thickness and the ceiling is at its highest and the light through it is most concentrated; low season, when the lens has thinned by a fifth, the soarers descend, and the animals at the ceiling come down and are eaten.

Individual lives are long and their events are few. A gyre spins for a century and a half and its single reproductive act takes eleven years to complete. A flue tree grows three centimeters annually and lives past two thousand. A bellower's gestation is six years; its calf takes another nine to join the stroke-rhythm of the herd, learning the phase by pressing against its mother's flank and being pushed.

Decay is slow too, because it is cold, and this matters more than it sounds. Things do not rot away here; they compress. Under the living mattress is a meter of half-mattress, and under that is dense brown peat that is mostly the flattened bodies of previous tenants, and under that is a black rubbery layer that will, in a few hundred thousand years, be something like coal. The ground is deceased fauna. You are walking on ancestors, and they are springy.

Nothing here is in a hurry, and nothing here has anywhere to go. The one thing that moves fast is the light.

## The Rim

At the edge, the whole industry declares its books.

Warm dense manufactured air reaches the escarpment and pours over it. It does this continuously, in a fall thirty or forty meters deep that runs for the entire perimeter of the plateau — thousands of kilometers of slow atmospheric waterfall, visible as a rolling white cataract of condensing fog, sliding down the young cliffs and dispersing into the thin cold nothing of the outer world.

That fall is the plateau's loss column. Everything the bellowers make, the rim spends. The Loft is a factory running flat-out for a hundred million years to hold, against leakage, a lens of air four kilometers up — a project with no product, no surplus, no purpose beyond the continuance of the conditions that permit it. It manufactures the room it stands in. It has done nothing else and will do nothing else.

Stand at the rim in the glare, with the drone in your ribs and the black quilt of the world at your back and the fog pouring away beneath you into a thin blue dead planet, and the arithmetic is very clean: below the line, nothing lives. Above it, everything does, all at once, packed shoulder to shoulder, breathing in a slow unified stroke, blindingly lit, deafeningly patient, and entirely without a single made thing to show for it.
