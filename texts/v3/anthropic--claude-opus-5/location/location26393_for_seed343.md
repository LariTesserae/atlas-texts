## The Palm

At this altitude there should be ice. Instead there is a warm wind coming up over the rim all afternoon, smelling of rot and green, and the reason is simple once you have stood at the edge: three vertical kilometres below, a lowland forest is breathing, and its breath rises. The cliffs act as a flue. The plateau is a lid on a slow cooking pot, and everything that lives up here lives on exhaust.

The plateau itself is old past the point of drama. Whatever range this was has been reduced to the flat of its own hand — the root-rock, the deepest and hardest stuff, gneiss whorled and folded like a thumbprint, ground down over an unimaginable duration until the horizon runs level all the way around, unbroken by peak or ridge. No valleys. No streams that go anywhere. Rain falls, sits, evaporates, or seeps sideways. The land is finished. It has arrived at what it will be, and it will be that for a very long time.

Over the rock lies the mat.

## The mat

Four metres deep in places, thinner in others, a single clonal grass-moss with no name in the local speech other than *ground*. It is not soil. Nothing here has bothered to become soil. It is compressed generations of itself, brown and fibrous below, olive above, springy underfoot so that you sink to the ankle and the sink stays. A footprint here can outlast the foot's owner. Old routes show as pale seams, threadbare, kilometres long, worn by three hundred years of the same walk between the same two towers.

It holds water like a lung holds air. Step hard and the mat weeps around your boot; a pressed hollow will fill overnight with brown, sweet, drinkable water and then be reabsorbed by noon. There are no rivers on the Palm. There is only the mat, and the fog it drinks, and the towers.

## The towers

They are called canes.

They stand kilometres apart, and they are the only vertical thing in the world. Sixty metres for a young one, two hundred for the old giants, eight to twenty metres across at the base — hollow-stemmed, jointed at long intervals like impossible bamboo, silver-grey on the outside where the fibre has weathered to something between wood and rope. Not trees. Grass. The rhizome runs beneath the mat, on the naked rock, and it is very likely that every cane within a hundred kilometres is one plant, that the whole plateau is a single organism putting up spires at intervals it decided on before there were people to count them.

Each cane is a cistern. The outer fibres are fletched to comb the afternoon fog; the water runs inward through the joints and descends the inner wall in sheets, and at the base of the hollow there is a black standing pool, cold, root-flavoured, permanent. This is the entire hydrology of the plateau: dry open plain, and inside each column, a well going up two hundred metres into a green-lit throat.

Which is why everything alive is inside them.

## The sound

You will hear the Palm before you climb onto it. From the trail below the rim it arrives as pressure rather than noise, a felt thing in the sinuses.

The canes are full of birds.

*Sift* — small, dark, hook-winged, no bigger than two knuckles, nesting in the interior walls in colonies of millions. Each hollow is packed from the waterline to the crown with mud-and-spit cups, layer upon layer, generation upon generation, the older nests fossilised into a continuous crust that has its own strata and its own insect fauna. The noise inside an occupied cane cannot be described as birdsong. It is white, total, physical; it moves the hair; it makes the fibrous walls resonate so that the whole two-hundred-metre column hums a low note under the shriek, and the hum can be felt through the mat forty paces out.

And at dusk the sift come out, and the bats go in.

The exchange is the loudest event in the world. For nineteen minutes the column vomits a black helix that unwinds westward over the plain toward the rim, and simultaneously the returning flight — leaf-nosed, hand-sized, in numbers no one has tried to guess — pours the other way, and the two streams braid without touching, and the sound is a solid object. Falcons work the braid, three or four of them, plucking. The falcons are silent. Everything else is not.

Then, at dawn, it reverses. There is no hour when a cane is empty and no hour when it is quiet. Day shift, night shift, an unbroken tenancy of the same hollow going back further than the rock's memory of being a mountain.

## Overwhelming, and empty

Both are true and they are true in the same place.

Stand at the midpoint between two canes at noon. There is nothing. Flat olive mat to the flat horizon, hard thin sunlight, one moth crossing, one distant column standing up out of heat-shimmer like a mast with no ship. The air is enormous and unoccupied. You could walk for an hour and meet nothing that has a face.

Then walk to the cane, and cross its green halo — the ring of taller, richer, guano-fed mat two hundred paces wide where the moth-grass grows knee-high and the little pale grazers come to crop it and the snakes come for the grazers — and put your hand on the wall of the tower, and there are twenty million animals on the other side of eight centimetres of grass fibre.

The Palm is a near-void punctuated by concentrations so extreme they distort behaviour, weather, hearing, and law. All the biomass of a region is packed into a few dozen columns. Between them: air.

## The dead canes and the people in them

Canes fall. Not often — perhaps one in a generation within sight — but when one goes it does not shatter; it lies down and becomes a tunnel, a hundred metres of hollow pipe lying on the mat, drying, hardening, gradually silvering. The nest-crust inside dries out and can be quarried in blocks. It is the best fuel and the best fertiliser and the only good building block on the plateau.

People live in the fallen canes. That is the whole architecture of the Palm: a stone doorsill set into the open end, a wall of nest-block, cord lashings of cane fibre, sleeping shelves, a hearth on a flat rock plate because fire directly on the mat is the one unforgivable thing. From outside a settlement reads as almost nothing — a long grey log on a green plain with smoke coming out of a hole.

Their tools are bone, cane-fibre rope, ground stone, a very little soft copper traded up from the lowland forest and treated as precious beyond reason: a copper needle is a dowry. They climb the living canes with driven hardwood pegs, ascending in a spiral, replacing pegs every season, and every cane has a peg-spiral that its owning families have maintained for centuries. From the crown they take squab, eggs, fibre, and — lowered by rope in cane-bark buckets — water and nest-crust from the throat.

There is a rule about the throat. You go in during the middle hours, when the day tenants are out foraging and the night tenants are asleep in the upper crust, and you go in with wax and moss in your ears, and you come out before the exchange. To be caught inside a cane at exchange hour is not usually fatal, but people who have survived it do not go in again, and they are marked out afterward: the ones who sit at the edge of the halo and look at nothing.

Almost everyone here is partly deaf by thirty and completely deaf by fifty, and so the language of the Palm is a hand-language, fast, low, made against the body, and speech aloud is used mainly for infants and for grief. Their singing is done by drumming on the hollow walls; the canes take a beat and hold it, and a struck cane can be heard by the next cane, and messages cross the plateau column to column at the speed of a good rhythm.

## Cycles

Fog comes up the cliffs at the third hour after noon, every day of the year, and for two hours the plateau is inside a white nothing that condenses on everything, drips off the canes' combing fibres, wets the mat, beads on the fine hairs of your forearm. Then it clears and the sky is the hard rinsed blue of high places, and the mat steams.

Twice a year the sift leave. Both populations overlap their absence by about ten days, and in those ten days the canes are empty and the Palm is silent — not quiet, *silent*, a silence with an aftertaste, the plateau ringing like a struck bowl in a register no one can hear. The mat hisses under wind. That is all. The people call it the Hollow, and they do not work during it, and they do not sleep well, and the old ones go out to the midpoint between towers and sit down there in the open on purpose, which is either a religion or the closest thing to one.

Then the ribbon comes back over the rim, a smear on the sky at first, then a sound, then the world's normal noise resumes, and the towers hum again, and everyone can rest.

## What the place seems to want you to understand

That the land is done and the life is not. The rock finished its work an age ago and has nothing further to express; it is a floor. The grass has decided the shape of the world — ground and column, horizontal and vertical, nothing else. The animals fill in the volume, but only in the few narrow places where the water is, and everywhere else there is a great warm illuminated emptiness that the wind moves through.

And that the loudest place anyone knows is also the most vacant, and the people who live in it speak with their hands.
