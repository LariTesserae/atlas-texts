## The Finished Country

**1.**

You come down onto it from nowhere in particular, because there is no up to come down from — only a long shallow ramp of packed grey-fawn grain that stops being a slope at some point you don't notice, and then you are on the plain, and the plain is the world.

The first thing is the light. It arrives from the whole sky at once. Somewhere above the haze there is a source, but the shell has been diffusing it for so long, and so well, that the sun has been smeared into a uniform hemisphere of glare. Nothing casts a shadow here except straight down, and even that is a soft grey bruise under your heel rather than a shape. Your own body has no dark side. This is the sensation nobody warns you about properly: not the brightness, which the eye adapts to, but the shadowlessness. You cannot tell the time. You cannot tell how far away anything is. Everything looks equally near and equally lit, like objects in a diagram.

The second thing is the warmth. The ground is blood-temperature and stays that way all night, because it has enormous thermal mass and a long memory. Lying on it is like lying on a large animal that has recently stopped breathing.

**2.**

The plain is not soil. There is no soil anywhere on this world, and no rock either, in the sense of something that was never anything else.

Kneel and take a handful. Under a lens, the grains are chips: fragments of laminate, ceramic, glassy sintered stuff, each one rounded to a lozenge, each one bearing on its surface the ghost of a circuit, a channel, a serial mark worn to a smear. The sand is made of the machines that made the sand. Beneath the loose top few centimetres, the same material is welded into a dense pavement, and beneath that, strata: hundreds of metres of laminated construction, then thousands, then — the numbers stop meaning anything — a crust of built stuff going down further than the continent goes across. The world's radius is architecture. There is no basement rock. If there was ever a planet under this, it has been eaten and re-laid so many times that asking about it is like asking a river which raindrop it is.

The grain of the surface all runs one way. Every chip lies with its long axis in the same direction, combed by a wind that has been blowing more or less the same way for a period best described as *most of the time there has been*. Walkers call it the Lay. You navigate by it, because there is nothing else to navigate by: the horizon is a straight line in all directions, one part in a thousand of relief across a day's travel, low broad swells you only detect because your knees detect them.

**3.**

And the plain is crowded.

At first you think it's textured. Then you resolve them: stubs. Nubs. Low worn stumps rising from the pavement, knee-high, thigh-high, occasionally chest-high, spaced roughly a stride and a half apart, in every direction, to the limit of sight, for a distance which is — and this is the fact you should sit with — continental. Hundreds of millions of square kilometres of them. Perhaps ten thousand to the hectare. You do not walk *across* this landscape so much as *through* it, brushing, stepping over, turning your hips.

They are towers. They were towers. Their cut tops are polished flat and glassy by dust-blast, and each top is a cross-section: an agate slice of a building, showing you concentric floor plates, hair-fine risers of plumbing, the pale ovals of shafts, layer on layer on layer, the whole thing as intricate and dead-looking as a fossil coral. Some of the sections still function. Put your palm on one and it will be a half-degree warmer than its neighbour. Put your ear to it — nothing. But your palm knows.

The stumps get lower and rounder as you go, in a direction you eventually learn to feel, and after some weeks of walking they are no longer stumps at all but low smooth domes, all the same size, all the same shape, evenly spaced, glazed, featureless, like loaves laid out to cool. That's the country everyone calls Finished. It goes on for a third of the world. Nothing there is broken. Everything there is *done*.

**4.**

Here is the rule that makes this place what it is.

Nothing on this world erodes in the ordinary sense, because there is no water to do it with. What wears things down is repair.

Every made thing here is maintained, continuously, at a scale below sight, by processes that have long since lost their instructions and retain only their habits. Scratch a stump with a blade and within an hour you will see the damage healing: a bloom of pale frost-like matter creeping in from the edges of the cut, silently, at the speed of a slow snail, filling and smoothing. By morning the scratch will be gone. But it will not be gone *exactly*. The repair works from an average — an average of every previous state of that surface, averaged again with each pass, a copy of a copy of a copy across a span of time long enough for corners to become curves.

So the towers did not fall. They were mended into flatness. Detail is the thing that erodes here. Sharpness, specificity, individuality: those are the soluble minerals. What remains is the mean shape of all things ever built on this world, and the mean shape is a low smooth dome, and the Finished Country is where everything has already arrived there.

Your footprints heal from the edges inward, over about forty minutes. You can watch it. It is the single most unnerving process on the plain, and after a week you stop watching, and after a month you find it restful.

**5.**

Which is why it's quiet.

Vibration is damage. Somewhere, long ago, that was written into the maintenance habit, and the habit has been obeyed ever since with terrible thoroughness. Loud sound is repaired away. Speak normally and you can hear your own words being taken from you at about three metres — not muffled, exactly; *deducted*. Shout and the shout goes flat and dead almost immediately, as if you'd shouted into a pillow the size of a country. The plain is not silent, but it holds sound at a strict ceiling, and everything below that ceiling is very clear:

the sift of grains moving on grains, a dry hiss like sugar poured slowly;

the tick — every few minutes, from somewhere close, from somewhere else — of a stump adjusting to a thousandth of a degree;

your heartbeat, which you will get to know better than you want to;

and, at intervals of roughly a day, from an unaccountable distance, a single low note, like a swallowed bell. Nobody has found its source. It is not repaired away. It does not appear to be wear. It is the reason people talk about this place in slightly lowered voices even after they've left.

**6.**

There is no water. This was decided.

The builders removed it, because water is a solvent that eats structure, and because a world designed to last needed a chemistry that would not corrode. What water they could not export they bound: the plain's deep strata are threaded with hydrate seams, water locked into crystal as a mineral, dry to the touch, inert, archived. You can dig a nodule out of a bank and hold it: a fist of pale blue-white stone, sweating nothing, weighing more than it should. Crack it in a stove and you get a mouthful. That is where drinking water comes from here. Water is a rock you mine, and the world regards its liberation as a leak, and will begin, very slowly, to try to put it back.

In place of water there is dust, and dust behaves like water at long timescales. It flows. It settles into the low swells and forms broad pale basins, flat as milk, hundreds of kilometres across — dust lakes, playa, whatever you like — with a crust you can walk on for the first three steps. There are tides in them. The daily thermal wind pushes the fines one way, the shell's cooling pulls them back, and the shorelines of these dry lakes advance and retreat a few metres each day, endlessly, in silence, with the little collapsing hiss of a wave that has forgotten how to break.

**7.**

Life is present and thinly spread.

The plants are not sparse because conditions are hard but because the plain gives them so little to argue over. One every twenty or thirty metres: a stalk of dull dark blue-green, a hand's height to a man's height, ending in a fan of stiff panels that turns over the course of the day. Their roots are wires. They tap the stumps for feedstock — metals, carbon, hydrogen cracked from hydrate seams — and their sap is not water but a deep eutectic, a mixture of salts that dissolve one another and stay liquid and refuse, absolutely refuse, to dry. Break a panel and a bead of it stands on the wound like clear honey and does not evaporate, ever, and will still be sitting there next year, catching the light.

They smell faintly of hot ammonia and clean laundry. They rustle, but the rustle is deducted at two metres.

Animals are genuinely rare: you may go five days seeing none. What there is:

**Readers.** Low six-legged things the size of a cat, pale as unglazed porcelain, no eyes to speak of. They walk the stump-field methodically, laying a foot on each nub, pausing, moving on. They are sampling something. They have been sampling it for a very long time. They are not afraid of you; they will lay a foot on your boot, pause, and move on, and this is the closest thing to being greeted that happens on this world.

**Sleeves.** A mat of tissue two metres square and a millimetre thick, indistinguishable at rest from a patch of pavement — until it lifts an edge and slides, faster than seems reasonable, and rolls itself around a reader. Silent. It takes a day. Afterwards it lies flat again, slightly thicker, and the reader's water has been claimed and locked before the body cools.

**Kites.** Thin translucent gliders, three-metre span, always high, riding the midday thermal column. They do not land. They are born aloft and they come down once. When one does, the repair habit begins on it immediately, and in a week there is a new low smooth dome where it fell.

**8.**

The weather is entirely the world's own, and there is a lot of it, and almost none of it is loud.

Morning: the plain exhales. Air sinks off the shell, warms on the pavement, and starts the Sift — a knee-high dry tide of fines running with the Lay, so that for three hours you walk in a shallow river of moving powder that hides your boots and hisses and clings to you with static until it decides, all at once, to let go.

Midday: the Crown. The diffuse glare tightens, briefly, into a column overhead where the shell's cells align, and for perhaps forty minutes there is a single true shadow beneath everything, hard-edged, blue-black, and the whole stump-field looks abruptly like a graveyard, and then it disperses and there are no shadows again.

Afternoon: fronts. You see them coming for an hour — a soft pale wall, no more than fifty metres tall, advancing at walking pace, tinted faintly rose. Inside, visibility is four metres, the air is thick and warm and dry as flour, and every stump top wears a corona: a still pale flame of cold plasma, standing, absolutely without noise. Static discharge here does not crack. It leaks. Your hair rises, the taste of metal fills your mouth, and pale light drips off your fingertips onto the ground. This is called a White. They last two to nine hours. Nothing is dangerous in them except getting lost, which is easy, and being still, which is worse.

Night is not dark. The shell releases its stored day and hangs overhead like a lit ceiling turned down to a dim warm grey-gold, and the pavement gives back its heat, and there is a faint answering glow from the ground itself, so that you sleep in a soft bright nowhere with no stars and no direction and no cool. It is very restful and very few people can do it for long.

**9.**

Two more things, and then you should go on.

The first: if you press your palm flat on a stump top and hold it, and hold it, and are patient, faint dark dots will surface under the glaze and arrange themselves. They are numbers. They are giving you your elevation above the Datum — the remembered zero, the reference plane of a world that no longer has a sea to measure from. Most of the plain reads between plus thirty and plus seventy. Ask again and it will tell you again. Ask something else, in the right way, with a rhythm, and it will sometimes answer that too, and the answer will be worn: averaged, generalised, sanded down to the mean of all answers ever given, and therefore true and useless. *A place. Some time ago. Many.*

The second, which is the reason walkers walk: do not sleep more than fourteen hours in the same posture.

The repair habit works from the average, and the average does not include you. Sleep long enough and it will read the seam where your body meets the pavement as a crack, and it will begin, silently, with that pale creeping frost, to smooth it. People have woken with their boot fused into the floor of the world, and a soft white fillet, quite beautiful, running up the outside of their leg.

The plain is not hostile. It has simply finished, and it is very good at finishing things, and it will do you the same kindness it has done everything else: take away your edges, average you into all the shapes that came before, and leave a low smooth dome, warm to the touch, in the bright shadowless light, one of ten thousand in that hectare, one of an uncountable number, exactly the right size
