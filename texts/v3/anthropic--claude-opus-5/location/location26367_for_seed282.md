## The Understratum, Bay 14

You come down into it by a ladder that is warmer than the room. That is the first fact of the place: the ladder retains the heat of the corridor above, and by the fourth rung you have left that heat behind, and by the eighth you are in the cold — a dry-edged, mineral cold, about two degrees above freezing, held there deliberately, the way a hand holds a bird.

The chamber is small. Eleven paces by seven, and low enough that a tall person walks with a slight apology in the neck. It does not feel small. It feels *packed*, the way a workshop feels packed, or an organ's interior — so many things per cubic meter that the air seems structured. Pipes cross the ceiling in three tiers. Cable trunks run the walls in bundles thick as thighs, lashed with copper wire at intervals of a hand's width. Racks stand in the middle of the floor, and you edge past them sideways, and something cold and flat brushes your coat, and you turn and see that it is a sheet of stone.

Not a slab quarried. A sheet *made*: eight millimeters thick, a meter square, laid horizontally on a rack of thirty others, each separated by felt spacers. Grey-green, with a fine banding in it. If you were shown it in daylight in a hundred million years you would call it siltstone and think nothing of it at all.

---

There is no natural substrate anywhere in this room. That should be said plainly. The floor is riveted deck-plate over a lattice; below the lattice, a void, and below the void, another deck. The walls are cast panels bolted in a running bond, and where the panels are already faced, they are faced with the product: thin strata glued up in sequence, joints staggered, the seam-lines painted out with a matched slurry so that a section three meters wide reads as a continuous outcrop. On the north wall the facing stops mid-course, and you can see the raw panel behind it, stencilled with a lot number and the words **DO NOT AGE BEFORE CLADDING**.

The stone is manufactured here, in this bay, at a rate of roughly four millimeters a shift.

The machines that do it are called extruders in the paperwork and *layers* by whoever writes on the walls in chalk. Each is a low gantry on a rail, carrying a hopper and a blade, and each one travels the length of its bed at a speed that looks, at first, like stillness. You have to watch a fixed point — a rivet head, a scratch — and count to sixty, and then you see that the blade has moved, and that behind it a new film of mineral paste lies down in a matte band that is a shade paler than the band beneath it. Then the mist-heads over the bed open, and the film darkens as it takes the water, and the crystals begin their slow interlock, and this is where the cold matters: warm, the paste sets coarse and cloudy and reads as artificial to anyone who knows how to look. Cold, it sets fine. Cold, it sets *believable*.

That is the whole reason for the temperature of this room. Not preservation, not comfort. Verisimilitude.

---

Water arrives on a cycle of thirty-eight minutes.

You hear it coming: a rising hum in the ceiling as the condensation combs are charged, then a change in the quality of the air, a thickening, and then the drip begins. Not rain, not really — rain implies a sky, and there is no sky within four hundred meters of here. It is the harvest of the room's own breath, driven up into the combs, chilled, and released. It falls unevenly, in the places where the combs have been aimed and in a few places where they have not, and the floor is dark and glossy along those lines and dry as chalk between them. In one corner, where two panels meet imperfectly, there is a permanent pocket of water four centimeters deep with a rust bloom around its rim, and something has begun growing in it without being asked to, and nobody has decided yet whether that constitutes contamination or progress.

For eleven minutes it drips. Then the drains open, low in the deck, and take the excess with a sound like a throat clearing, and there is a silence with a slight ringing in it, and then the extruders resume.

Between cycles the room is nearly quiet. This surprises people who expect industry to be loud. But haste ruins strata, and the machinery here has been geared down past the point where gears complain. What you hear instead: the pumps, deep in the floor, at the tempo of a sleeping heartbeat; the tick of a cable-tray contracting somewhere in the ceiling; the whisper of the mist-heads at low idle; and, if you hold still long enough, a very faint granular *shhh* which is either the paste setting or your own blood, and which nobody has ever settled.

---

Light is rationed, because photons bleach the unset minerals and because the room does not need much. There are amber filament strings clipped along the pipe runs — dim, unblinking, the colour of weak tea — and one caged work-lamp on a magnetic base that can be carried to whatever is being worked on. Above the trays, four narrow grow-bars burn a cold violet on a twelve-hour cycle that is out of phase with everything else, so that twice a day the room is briefly lit in two irreconcilable colours and every object has two shadows going different ways.

The trays are the only living things.

There are nine of them, stacked on a rack near the ladder in the least cold corner. Tray one through five: a black moss cultivar, low as felt, tough, grown on discs of the manufactured siltstone. It is not here to be beautiful. It is here to be *taught to the rock* — to establish, in the pore structure of the product, the faint chemical grammar that a moss leaves behind, so that later analysis will find the right traces in the right sequence. Tray six: pale filament-fungus in a covered dish, thriving, ignored. Trays seven and eight: something fernish, three fronds, in a sealed jar with a hand-written note reading *ahead of schedule — do not log*. Tray nine is empty, swept, labelled with a name in a taxonomy that does not exist yet.

That is all the life there is. Nothing here moves on its own behalf. No animal is permitted below the seal — animals eat, and excrete, and wander, and the strata of Bay 14 must contain only what has been authorized to be found in them. There isn't so much as a mite. The absence has a texture; you notice it in the way that dust settles perfectly evenly, undisturbed, in the way nothing in the room is ever startled.

And yet, on the bench under the work-lamp, in a shallow form of grey clay, someone has been pressing tracks.

Three-toed, about the size of a child's hand, in a walking gait with a slight drag on the left. There is a wooden stamp for making them, well-worn at the handle, and a chart on the wall showing correct stride-to-depth ratios for various substrate moistures. The tracks will go into the bed tomorrow, between courses, and in due course they will be found, and the finding will be true. The animal is scheduled for a much later phase. The evidence of it goes in now, because evidence must be beneath, and beneath takes time to build.

---

The rules of the room, as posted and as understood:

Nothing here is older than its stamp says. Age is applied, not accrued — there is a press in the far bay that does it, and a log book, and the entries are neat.

You may not hurry the cure.

Erosion is a different contract. Bay 14 makes rock; it does not weather it. The weathering crews come after, with their acid and their grit-blast and their patient wheels, and they are famously slow, and they have not been down here yet, and every surface in this chamber is therefore *raw* in a way that is difficult to look at for long — every edge sharp, every face unpitted, every fracture fresh and clean and without any of the softening that makes a stone look like it belongs to a world. The room is pre-history in the most literal sense. It has no past yet. Only inventory.

And: leave the lights as you found them.

---

There was a shift here recently, or there always is. A pair of gloves lies on the bench, fingers curled, and if you put your palm near them there is a suggestion of warmth that may be honest. A tally in chalk on the north panel counts something in fives up to two hundred and sixteen. A tin cup half full of water that has taken on the taste of the room. No one is here. No one is ever quite not here.

Stand in the middle, between the racks, and let the thirty-eight minutes come around. The hum climbs. The air thickens against your face. The drip begins, and the fresh band on bed three darkens as it drinks, and somewhere below the deck the pumps keep their long slow rhythm, and the two shadows of the ladder cross each other on the floor.

In the topmost rack, sheet by sheet, separated by felt, lies a bed of siltstone eight courses thick with a ripple-mark on the fourth, which is destined for the third tier of a cliff above a river in a valley that has not been surveyed. Somebody will stand at the base of it, one day, and put a hand on the fourth course, and feel the ripple, and understand — correctly, completely — that water once ran here, long ago, in the quiet, in the cold.
