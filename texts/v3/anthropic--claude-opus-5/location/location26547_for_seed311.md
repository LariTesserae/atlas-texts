## The Cistern at Grieve's Hollow Stair

**First, the container.**

You find it by the sound, which arrives through stone as a pressure in the jaw rather than anything the ear accepts. Behind the disused stair, under the third landing, there is a hatch of grey composite with a rime of salt crystal around its lip. Lift it. The hinge is new. Everything here is new.

Below is a room roughly four metres by five, filled to within a hand's breadth of the ceiling with water so clear and so brightly lit that for the first several seconds you misread the surface as absent, and the city below as something happening in air.

That is the whole place. There is nothing else. Whatever is outside the hatch is outside this description.

**The water.**

Cold — nine degrees, maybe ten near the outflow — and doped. Something in it fluoresces under the ultraviolet lattice set into the ceiling coffers, so that the light is not a beam from above but a general daylight emitted by the medium itself, from everywhere, casting no shadows worth the name. The effect is disconcerting: the buildings below have no undersides. They are lit from beneath as evenly as from above, and so they seem to float even where you can see them fused to the floor.

The water holds shape. Not much, not for long, but where the resonance drivers cross, the water stiffens into faint standing surfaces — planes and lenses and long taut membranes you can see by the way the fluorescence bends across them. Drop a grain of grit and it will slide down one of these invisible ramps before it falls. The engineers call them *floors*; the word is used without irony.

**The terrain.**

The bottom of the cistern is not a floor but a *druse*: a bed of freshly grown mineral, terraced in three low steps, total relief perhaps twenty centimetres across the whole room. Low country. Nothing here is high. But the terraces are edged so sharply that the light catches along them in threads, and the edges are still growing, still adding to themselves at a rate you can watch if you are patient — a hair's width an hour, laid down in translucent aragonite and something denser, bluer, that current physics has no settled name for and that the builders call *hard water* without further explanation.

Nothing has had time to wear down. This is a landscape that has never been rained on, never frozen, never walked. Its youth is the first thing you notice after the light. Corners are corners. Facets are facets. The only softening is in the oldest quarter — the low mound in the northeast, six weeks old, where the currents have begun to round the parapets and lay down a first grey film. Six weeks is antiquity here. There is a word for the mound in the operators' logs, and the word means *ruin*.

**The city.**

Packed. That is the parameter and there is no better word. From terrace edge to terrace edge, the floor is fully occupied — towers of the height of your index finger crowded shoulder to shoulder, no street wider than a pencil, no plaza, no gap that is not itself a shaft or a flue or a resonance channel doing work. Density here is not congestion; it is the design. The whole city is one continuous solid perforated by the necessary voids, in the way a lung is one continuous solid perforated by the necessary voids.

The construction is layered because it is grown: each night's session of standing waves precipitates a new skin over the last, so that a tower is a stack of nightly deposits, forty or fifty laminations thick, each lamination a slightly different translucency. Break one (you should not) and it flakes like mica, and each flake is a record of one night's frequency map. The city is its own archive. It cannot help it.

Forms: the towers are not towers exactly. They are antinodes. Where two or three drivers agree, mineral falls out of solution and stays, and the resulting shape is whatever the interference pattern was — so the district under the northern driver is all hyperbolic sheets and cusps, elegant, cruel-edged, indefensible; the district under the failing eastern driver is a slumped mess of blebs and drips, growing crookedly, which the operators have decided to keep. There are arcades that are simply the shadows of nodes. There are spires that terminate in a fringe of needles finer than any tool could make, because at that scale the water was doing the making.

Infrastructure is visible and everywhere: conduit runs along the terrace faces in ribbed bundles; heat-exchange fins stand out from the mound like fish gills, silver, sharp; a spillway of engineered stone carries the warm outflow down the second terrace in a shimmering thermocline you can see as a wobble in the light. It looks like a town, at settlement scale, with all the fussy competence of a town — drains, jetties, a lit boulevard, a district plainly given over to plant. It is thirty centimetres across.

**The sound.**

Loud. Underwater, loud does not mean what it means in air; it means the medium itself is being shaken hard enough that your bones participate. The drivers run at four fundamental frequencies with a shifting fifth, and their beats produce a slow pulse — roughly one every one and a half seconds — that you feel first in the sternum and then in the teeth and then, unpleasantly, behind the eyes. Above the water it is only a hum and a thin whistle and the clatter of the surface, which is never still: the whole face of the water is standing in fixed peaks like a field of small grey pyramids, quivering, occasionally cavitating with a crack.

Put your hand in and the water buzzes. Put your head in and you will be unable to think for as long as you keep it there.

The sound is not ambience. The sound is the mason. Nothing in this city was placed. Every centimetre of it was *sung out of solution*, and the volume is the price.

**The rules.**

*One:* nothing may be still. Stillness lets mineral fall out of solution indiscriminately — a stagnant hour produces a grey sludge that ruins geometry for a week. The drivers therefore never stop, and the pumps never stop, and the tide (four hours flood, four hours ebb, driven from below) never stops. Activity here is not liveliness; it is hygiene.

*Two:* the city grows only where sound crosses. Planning is done by tuning. To demolish, you retune and let dissolution take the block back — three days for a tower, and the water goes faintly milky while it happens.

*Three:* no animals. Not a shrimp, not a copepod, not a rotifer. This is enforced, and it is enforced for a technical reason rather than a moral one: a swimming body is an unpredictable scatterer, and one animal the size of a rice grain crossing the northern driver's field will introduce a defect that propagates through six nights of construction. Filtration is aggressive and continuous. The absence is total and it is the strangest thing about the room — a whole functioning world with no eyes in it.

Except, in the third pump gallery, in the dead water behind the second baffle, there is something the length of a fingernail paring that has been logged eleven times and identified never. It does not swim. It flexes. The operators have not removed it. There is a note in the log, in a different hand: *leave it, it's the only one*.

**The living part.**

Three species, no more, and only where the engineering permits.

In the warm spillway, a filamentous alga grows in ropes the colour of old brass, combed flat by the outflow, the only soft thing for metres. It is tolerated because it scavenges phosphate. Its ropes shiver in the beat pulse at one and a half seconds, all together, the whole colony nodding.

On the second terrace, in the lee of the heat fins where the sound field has a quiet hole, a stonewort-analogue stands up in tiny grey-green candelabra, calcifying as it grows, so that its lower stems are already part of the city — architecture by accident, the only structure here that was not designed. The operators sweep around it.

And in the air gap under the ceiling, where spray keeps the composite permanently damp, a lichen-thing has arrived from somewhere and made a black stipple on the coffers. Nobody knows how it got in. It is the only genuine trespasser and it is winning slowly.

**The processes, all at once.**

Stand at the hatch for an hour and you will watch: the tide flood and the thermocline flatten; a fine snow of unsanctioned mineral drift down and settle grey across the eastern district; a cleaning pulse sweep it off in a visible bloom; the northern driver step up a half-tone at the hour and a new lamination begin to whiten across four hundred rooftops at once; the surface pyramids reorganise into a coarser lattice; a dry pocket — a bubble held in a lattice of stiffened water, roughly the size of a plum, edges faceted — detach from the ceiling and *walk* down the invisible floors, step by step, without popping, until it reaches the spillway and is torn into a hundred smaller pockets that scatter like beads; the alga nodding, nodding; and the whole city, in the flat shadowless daylight of its own water, growing at a hair's width an hour, all of it, everywhere, with no one living inside it and no room left to put anything.

Then close the hatch. The hum drops into the stone and follows you up the stair for a while, and stops.
