## The Stanchion

Above four thousand metres the air gives up its water grudgingly, and it is exactly this grudging that the Stanchion was built to exploit.

You come to it along the spine of a range that has been dead a long time — dead in the sense that nothing there is being made or unmade quickly, the frost having already taken everything it wanted from the rock and settled into a slow, chewing patience. The ridge is dry. Not desert-dry, but dry the way old paper is dry: it has moisture somewhere in it, held deep, unwilling. Below, on both sides, the cloud sea lies flat and blinding at midday and the colour of scorched milk at evening. Below the cloud is a plain nobody sees from here.

The summit the Stanchion stands on is not a summit. It was one. It was quarried off, flat, and replaced with a plug of grey pour four hundred metres across, ring-poured in seventy lifts whose seams you can count like tree rings if you crouch. Everything above that plug is made. Everything within it is made. There is no natural ground here anymore; there is floor.

---

### The Drum

The structure is a single cylinder. Ninety metres in diameter, three hundred and eighty metres tall, riveted iron plate over a concrete core, and the concrete has been shot through so long by cold and condensate that it has a skin — a hard, blistered, mineral rind like the inside of a kettle, pale green where copper has wept down from the fittings.

Nothing else stands here. No sheds, no yard, no second tower. The scale of the place is the scale of one object: you can walk around its outside in eleven minutes. That is the whole world of it. And yet it is not small, because the world of it goes upward and inward, and because inside it there is almost nothing at all.

The door is a pressure door at the base of the plug, and there is a sign beside it, iron letters cast proud, that reads in a language no one speaks: **THE DRUM IS SPEAKING. DO NOT ANSWER.**

---

### The Ox

At the bottom of the cylinder, seated in the plug, is one turbine.

It has run without stopping for somewhere between two and four hundred years. It runs on nothing but difference: cold thin air outside, warm wet air inside, and a throat between them. It made the warmth itself, originally, from friction and from a coal-fired preheat that has been cold and rusted shut for two centuries — but the warmth stayed, because the machine's own labour keeps making it, and because the garden inside now breathes out heat of its own. The thing feeds itself. It has become a weather rather than an engine.

Its fundamental is around thirty-one cycles per second. That is too low to hear properly and too strong to do anything but hear. It arrives in the sternum and the sinuses and the loose water of the eye; it makes the jaw itch. Riding on it are the harmonics — a hundred and sixty, a rasping four-forty from a bearing that has been failing since before anyone's grandmother, and above all that a thin white shriek from the throat vents that is the only part your ears will admit is a sound.

The composite is a hundred and thirty decibels at the floor. It is not noise. It is a material. It fills the cylinder the way water fills a cistern, and like water it has a surface, layers, currents, and places where it does not move.

---

### The Pattern

Because the cylinder is a cylinder, the sound standing in it makes a shape, and the shape is stable, and the shape has been stable for centuries, and so the shape has been *colonised*.

Vegetation grows inside the Stanchion in horizontal bands. Nine of them, spaced up the height of the drum where the pressure nodes sit, each band five or six metres deep, each band gorged with green. Between them: bare rind. Blistered pale concrete, scoured clean and dry, where nothing has ever taken hold and nothing ever will, because in the antinodes the air itself is being hammered a thousand times a heartbeat and no root, no spore, no film of moss can hold on.

So: nine hanging jungles, stacked in a hollow tower, and between them nine bands of bright sterile nothing. From the floor, looking up, you see the sound.

The bands are not neat. Each has a lip of drip-ledge cast into the wall — the builders knew what they were doing, they put shelves at the nodes on purpose — and out of those ledges pours a lush, warm, disorderly matting: fern that grows in flat overlapping plates like fungus; a thick-stemmed vine with leaves the size of a hand and a habit of sending down aerial roots eighty metres before it finds another band; something like a fig, gone woody, that has cracked its ledge and rebuilt it out of its own root mass; liverworts in mats you could lie on. There are orchids in the fifth band that exist nowhere else and are pollinated by touch. In the second band the vine has fruited so heavily for so long that the ledge below it is knee-deep in seed pulp fermenting quietly in the warm.

The green obeys the sound absolutely. Where the pattern moves — and it does move, by centimetres, over decades, as the bearing wears and the fundamental sags — the bands migrate with it, dying off along their upper lip and creeping down their lower, so slowly that only the sterile scars record it. Above the third band there is a dry stripe two metres wide that was jungle within living memory. The whole garden is a needle dragging across the wall, one hair's width per year.

---

### Water

There is not much water and it never stops moving.

Cold outside, warm and wet inside: the plate sweats. Every square metre of the Stanchion's inner shell weeps continuously, and the weeping is gathered by the cast ledges, run along channels, tipped through the beds, drunk, transpired, risen, and condensed again higher up. The tower is a closed loop with a slow leak, and the leak is what it was built for: at the plug, one pipe leaves, forearm-thick, and goes down the mountain to the plain. A forearm of water, forever. In a dry country that is a city.

So the whole thing is a still. A fog-still four hundred metres tall, powered by its own exhaust, kept alive by a garden that lives on its condensation, screaming.

The damp inside is not rain. It is worse than rain and gentler: it never falls, it just *is*, on every surface, in the lungs, on the back of the neck, warm as breath. Metal here doesn't rust so much as furry. Every rivet head wears a bead.

---

### The Hollow

Now the important part.

The garden is on the wall. The turbine is in the floor. The middle of the Stanchion — ninety metres across, three hundred and eighty tall — is *empty*.

Not empty of sound. Empty of things. A column of warm wet lit air with nothing in it. You can stand at the rail of the fourth gallery and look across and see the far wall's greenery in perfect focus, and look down and see the turbine housing small as a coin, and there is nothing at all between. No cables. No struts — the builders were showing off, the load is all in the shell. Dust doesn't hang; the sound sorts it, driving it out of the antinodes into thin visible discs that lie in the nodes like standing smoke. Nine plates of dust, edge-on, perfectly level, hovering at each green band's height.

Light comes in through the crown. The top of the cylinder is a grille, not a roof — a cast iron lattice, half-choked with vine, and through it comes ordinary daylight, no more and no less than a day outside. It falls the length of the hollow and arrives at the turbine floor as a soft, unremarkable, entirely sufficient grey-gold. Shafts, when the crown vine moves. Nothing theatrical. Just light that has fallen a long way.

At the geometric centre of that emptiness — one hundred and ninety metres up, forty-five metres from any wall — is the node of nodes. A pocket of quiet perhaps a metre and a half across. The builders put a cage there and a bosun's line to reach it. Inside the cage, you can hear a pin drop. You can hear your own voice, which by then you will not have heard in days. People have gone out to it and come back changed in ways they don't explain well. The cage has a lot of chalk writing in it and much of the chalk writing is apologies.

---

### Fauna

It is loud and warm and wet and full of fruit and utterly free of predators larger than a shrew, which is to say it is paradise, and the animals have accordingly arrived.

Swifts: thousands, of a race that has forgotten how to call. They nest in the antinodes — the bare bands, where no plant can hold but a mud cup, once dry, holds fine, and where nothing can climb up to them. They cross the hollow in long slow arcs and use the dust discs as reference planes.

Moths, enormous, in the fifth and sixth bands, wingspan of a spread hand, with no ears at all and no need for them; they signal by pressing their abdomens together in patterns. Beetles that ride the vibration of a leaf to find each other. A frog in the drip channels, translucent, that mates by drumming its foot against the metal and reading the metal's answer, which means it courts the whole tower at once and gets replies from a hundred metres away.

Rats, of course. Deaf, fat, and civil.

And in the second band's fermenting pulp, something has been living for a long time and has arranged the seed husks into a low spiral wall that it repairs. Nobody has seen what it is. Nobody has changed the arrangement.

---

### Rules

Speech is not possible and so speech is not used. Chalk on the rail, chalk on the plate, chalk on your own forearm. Everyone who works here loses their hearing in the first three years, and this is understood as the entry fee rather than an injury; the third-generation tenders are born deaf and are considered lucky. Hands do everything. There is a full grammar in it, and the grammar has words for things that only exist here: *the pattern has slipped*; *the bearing is telling a new story*; *go to the cage*.

Nothing is ever repaired quickly. The Ox cannot be stopped — stopping it would kill the garden, dry the shell, and end the pipe, and the plain below drinks from the pipe. So maintenance is done alongside a running turbine, in the sixty-second windows when the seasonal valve cycles and the pressure eases. The valve cycles once. Per year. A single slow breath, in autumn, when the fundamental drops a whole tone for about a minute and every swift in the tower takes off at once because the light through the crown flickers with them.

That is the fastest thing that happens here.

---

### Balance

Nothing about the Stanchion is getting better or worse. The rust reached its own depth long ago and stopped; the patina is a finished thing; the concrete's blisters are stable; the garden has found its arrangement and holds it, and the arrangement drifts a hair a year and will keep drifting until the bearing finally goes, in a century or three, and the pattern collapses, and the nine jungles come down through the empty middle in one green afternoon that no one will hear.

Until then: a made mountain-top, warm and wet and screaming, nine hanging forests on the inside of an iron drum, nine plates of motionless dust, a column of ordinary daylight with nothing in it, a small silence hung at the middle where people go to say the thing they can't say anywhere else, a forearm of water going quietly down to the plain, and swifts.
