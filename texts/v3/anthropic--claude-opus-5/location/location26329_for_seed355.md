## The Sill

### Approach

The land comes down for a long time before it arrives here, and then it stops coming down and simply lies there, three or four handspans above the water it is made of. You do not descend into the Sill so much as notice that the descending has ended. The horizon is a low grey seam in every direction, and the ground between your feet and that seam is mud the colour of wet ash, cracked into plates the size of doors, and every crack is full of water, and every plate is rimed white at the edges like a lip that has been talking too long.

The heat is the first fact and it never stops being the first fact. It is blood-temperature, or a little above — the exact temperature at which you cease to be able to tell where your skin ends. The air holds all the water it can hold and then holds more, so that walking is a kind of slow swimming, and every surface you touch is already wet with your own breath returned to you. Nothing here dries. Nothing here is ever quite wet, either. Everything is at the balance point, the long-established compromise between the water below and the warmth above, and it has been at that balance point for so long that the compromise has become the character of the place.

### Light

There is almost none, and yet you see.

The sky is a lid: unbroken vapour, hot and low, close enough that towers vanish into it at eight or nine storeys. No sun crosses it. No stars pass behind it. Whatever light the sky has is a diffuse charcoal glow, the light of a room at the wrong end of night, enough to tell wall from gap and no more.

The real light comes from underfoot. The oldest water shines. Not brightly — a milk-and-mineral phosphorescence, the pale green of a moth's underwing — and only where water has stood long enough to forget it was ever rain. So the puddles glow, and the flooded streets glow, and the seeps at the base of every wall glow, and the whole city is lit from below.

This inverts everything. Shadows climb. Faces are lit at the jaw and dark at the brow. Doorways are bright at the threshold and black at the lintel, so that entering a building looks like walking into a mouth. Ceilings do not exist; there is only the place where the upward light gives out. When you look down a long avenue you see two hundred metres of shining floor and, above it, nothing at all — a city known entirely by its soles.

### The Building

There is a city here. That is the difficulty of the place, and its whole argument.

Streets, kerbs, thresholds, courtyards, cisterns, stairs with treads worn into shallow bowls by feet that were never here. Districts with characters: a quarter of narrow arcades, a quarter of low broad halls with piers like the legs of something kneeling, a quarter that is nothing but wells. Culverts run under the roads and carry water intelligently, sorting it, delivering it. There are drains. There are gutters cut with a channel-profile so exactly correct for the local rainfall that engineers, if any came, would weep.

And no one made it.

The Sill is precipitated. Groundwater rises through hot mud saturated with carbonate and gypsum and salt; at the surface it gives up its cargo; and the cargo comes out of solution *in the shape of a city*. Wall by wall, course by course, at about the thickness of a fingernail per year. The material is a fine pale travertine, banded like the inside of a shell, warm to the touch, faintly translucent at the edges where it is thinnest, so that a doorjamb glows amber when there is glowing water behind it. In places the stone is still soft and squeaks like new cheese. In places it has hardened to a substance that rings.

Nothing here was cut. Nothing was lifted. There are no tool marks, no mortar joints, no fixings, no seams of any kind — the arches are of a piece with their piers, the stairs of a piece with their walls, the whole quarter one continuous secretion, as if a coral had dreamed of masonry. And equally: no doors, no glass, no metal, no wheel-ruts, no rope-grooves at the well-mouths. The infrastructure of a town without a single artefact in it. Not one broken pot. Not one nail.

### The Balance

It is also dissolving. The same water that builds it takes it away. Rain — and it rains often, hot fat rain out of the low lid — dissolves the travertine at very nearly the rate the seeps lay it down. So the Sill is not ruined and not new. It is *established*: settled into its own form the way a river is settled into meander, endlessly replaced and unchanging.

The effect is a city that has been weathered into its own best version. Every corner is rounded. Every step is dished. Every wall has run and re-set into soft flutes and drapes, so the arcades look like curtains and the piers like melted candles that have decided to hold the roof up anyway. There is no rubble. Collapse is not the failure mode here; slow re-absorption is. When a tower goes, it does not fall — it *sags*, over decades, into a broad white mound, and the mound seeps, and the seep begins the outline of a new tower, thirty metres east, for no reason.

### What Grows

Very little, and it matters enormously.

**Blacksalt.** Mats of a leathery, tar-dark algal crust on the flooded pavements, which is the only unambiguously living thing in the Sill. It squeaks when trodden and smells of hot iron. It respires in slow visible pulses, hourly, releasing threads of bubbles.

**Glasswort.** Fleshy jointed stems, the colour of a bruise, standing in the shallow flooded courtyards. They stand very upright and very far apart, one every ten or twelve metres, like something posted.

**Well-hair.** Fine pale filaments in the crack of every kerb, hanging down, wet, moving slightly. Not in the wind. There is no wind.

**And in the fourth quarter, in a bowl-shaped court of dished white stone, one tree.** A fig-shaped thing, black-barked, leafless, alive. It fruits. The fruit is warm and cannot be eaten and cannot be carried out of the court; halfway up the stairs it is a stone in your hand, then a wet place on your palm, then nothing.

That is the whole ecology. There is no other. No insect walks the blacksalt. No worm turns the mud. No bird crosses the lid of the sky, and no fish moves in all that shining water — you can put your face into a canal and see forty metres down through pale green light to a flooded street below the street, and there is nothing there but more street. The absence is not eerie in the way a battlefield is eerie. It is older than that. The Sill is not a place where the animals died. It is a place where they were never part of the plan.

### Sound

Water, and only water, and not much of it.

A drip somewhere behind you, at the interval of a slow pulse. The tick and creak of salt crystallising in a crack. The exhalation of blacksalt. Very occasionally, from a long way off, the soft *whump* of a saturated wall giving up a slab of itself into a canal — and then the long circles of the sound not arriving, because sound here is poorly conducted by the thick hot air and dies within fifty metres. This is the practical reason the Sill is quiet: you are always standing in a small sphere of audibility inside an enormous silence, and the city continues to happen inaudibly all around it.

Your own footsteps are louder than they should be, and arrive slightly late.

### The Rules

Here is where the place stops behaving.

**The city does not have a number of towers.** Count them from a rooftop: eleven. Count again: eleven. Walk to them, and there are fourteen, and none of the extra three are new. This is not illusion or fatigue. The Sill is genuinely indefinite in certain of its quantities. Towers, wells, and the steps in the long stair from the mud flats to the arcade quarter are indefinite. Doors, thresholds, and the tree are exactly definite. There seems to be a rule about which is which, and no one has stated it.

**Geometry does not close.** Walk a rectangular block and return to your starting point having turned four right angles, and you will be standing in the same place facing a different way, with the whole city rotated about you. Nobody minds. You get where you are going by intending to, and by walking, and the two do not have to agree.

**Scale is a local variable.** The same doorway occurs in the arcade quarter at a height of two metres, in the hall quarter at a height of eleven, and in one particular culvert at a height of nine centimetres — the same doorway, with the same three flutes worn into the same left-hand jamb by the same absent shoulder. It is not a motif. It is one object, encountered at three magnifications.

**Rain, in four courtyards, falls upward.** It gathers on the floor, hesitates, and goes up in fat slow drops to be re-absorbed by the lid. These courtyards are cooler. They are the closest thing the Sill has to relief.

**Time pools where water pools.** In the deep old shining cisterns, an hour is a long thing; you can sit at the rim and watch a drop of condensation take most of a morning to gather and let go. On the dry plates of the mud flats, hours pass in the interval between two steps. Nothing here is broken by this. The processes of the place — the seep, the bloom, the dissolution, the twice-daily breathing of the water table that raises and lowers every canal by a handspan — all run at their own rates through pooled and rushing time, and stay in balance, because balance is what this landscape does.

**Your footprints are already there.** Wet, glowing faintly, in the blacksalt, ahead of you, going where you are about to go. If you deliberately go elsewhere, the prints ahead of you are the ones you did not make, and they persist for some hours before dissolving, and are exactly your size.

### At Last

Stand at the rim of a cistern in the third hour of a hot black afternoon that has no sun in it. Below you, forty metres of green-glowing water and, under the water, more city. Above you, the low lid, breathing. Around you, twelve or fourteen or eleven towers going up into nothing, each one softly weeping, each weeping precisely as fast as it is being built.

There is a great deal of space here. That is the last thing to say about the Sill: for a city, it is astonishingly empty. Wide flooded plazas with a single glasswort standing in the middle. Whole arcades with nothing in them but the smell of hot stone and the light coming up off the floor. The Sill is settlement-scaled and organised and drained and paved and utterly unoccupied, and it is not waiting for anyone. It has reached its own state. It will keep reaching it, wall by wall, fingernail by fingernail, in the dark, in the heat, in the quiet, for as long as the water keeps coming up.
