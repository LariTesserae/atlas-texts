## The Dry Uplands

### 1. The Shelf

Begin with the fact that dominates everything: it is high, and it is enormous, and it is dry in a way that is not an absence but a policy.

The plateau begins somewhere below the horizon and does not end. Fly a straight line across it for a week and the character of the ground will change four or five times — pale terraces, then a province of rounded ridges like the backs of buried animals, then a pan so flat and white that the eye reads it as sky laid down flat — but you will not leave. It is continental. It makes its own weather, keeps it, and does not share it with the lowlands. From the rim, looking outward and down, there is a haze ocean, an amber-grey blanket lying five kilometers below where the thick air pools with everything the thick air carries. Nothing from down there comes up. The plateau drinks a different sky.

The rock is a fine-grained pale stone, feldspathic, faintly pink in the mornings and the color of bone at noon. It rings under a boot heel. Everywhere it has been worked to its final shape: the ridges are rounded because they have been rounded for a very long time; the escarpments have shed their loose material and stand at the exact angle where nothing more will fall; the pans are floored with a hard crust of salts pressed into hexagons a meter across, each hexagon rimmed with a lip a finger high, the whole floor looking like an unfired tile-work laid by no one. That is the general condition here. Everything has already found the shape it is going to keep. There is no rawness anywhere. The land is not eroding so much as maintaining a very old agreement with the wind.

### 2. Light and Air

The air is thin, and cool, and extraordinarily clear.

Because the air is thin, the light arrives with almost nothing taken out of it. The sun is a small hot white coin and it makes shadows with cut edges. Sunward, every surface is glaring; shadeward, three feet away, it is cold enough that frost would form if there were anything to freeze. Objects here have two climates: a noon side and a midnight side, separated by a hand's width. Walk a ridgeline and you can feel the seam of it move across your body.

The sky is not blue. It is a deep flat indigo at the zenith, paling to white grey at the rim, and at midday you can see the brighter stars if you shade your eyes and are patient. There is no haze, no aerial perspective. A cliff sixty kilometers off is as sharply drawn as your own hand, only smaller, and the mind refuses this and keeps insisting the cliff is close. Distance has to be learned here as a fact rather than seen as an impression.

### 3. The Groves

And now the strange thing: it is green.

Not everywhere. That's the point. From altitude the plateau reads as pale ground with dark islands on it, and the islands are far apart — twenty, forty, sometimes ninety kilometers of empty crust between one and the next. But each island is dense. Each is a grove of enormous closed-bodied plants, and inside a grove the canopy is so thick that at noon the floor is in true night.

Call them casks, or towers, or by the sound they make. They stand ten to thirty meters, thick-trunked, and their skin is a hard translucent rind like green glass held up to a lamp — very dark, an ink-green so deep it is nearly black at the base, lightening toward the crown to the color of a bottle. There are no leaves in the ordinary sense. There are fronds, but they are rigid: flattened blades of the same glassy material, arranged in fixed spirals, angled with the precision of something solved long ago. They do not flutter. Wind passes through a grove and nothing moves.

They are sealed. That is the whole architecture of them. No stomata, no pores, no transpiration, no exhalation. Gas exchange happens by slow diffusion through the rind, which is why the rind is thin and vast in area, why the fronds are broad and countless, why growth is measured in centuries. A closed body loses nothing. A closed body is a vault.

And because they are so dark, they are warm. This is the second fact you learn about a grove, after the darkness: you walk in out of the cutting white glare and it is *warmer*. The trunks are warm to the palm, blood-warm, having spent all day absorbing that undiluted sun and having no way to sweat it off. At night a grove is a lantern of heat with no light in it. Everything alive on this plateau, at nightfall, is walking toward one.

### 4. The Water Law

There is no free water on the Dry Uplands. Not a drop, not a film, not a frost. No rivers, no lakes, no springs, no rain, ever, in any season, in any century.

There *is* water. All of it is inside something alive.

The plateau's entire inventory — some unimaginable but finite tonnage, the residue of an ocean that left before the ridges were round — has been drawn up into tissue and it circulates there and only there. A cask tower holds perhaps forty tonnes of it in a sealed internal hydrology, moved by osmotic pumping, never released. When a tower dies, it does not rot; nothing here rots, there is no medium for rot. It stands. It stands for a thousand years as a hollowing shell while its water is withdrawn, molecule by molecule, through root-grafts into its neighbors and its children. Death on this plateau is a transfer of custody.

This is the law that shapes everything: **water is inherited, never found.**

So the groves are not communities. They are lineages, and their sparseness is not scarcity of soil but the arithmetic of a fixed estate. Every grove is exactly as large as the water it holds allows, and no larger, and has been for a very long time. A grove can only expand by shipping a portion of its inheritance out into a daughter — a seed the size of a barrel, a sealed dark sphere with a two-liter dowry inside it, rolled downwind across the pans by the dust fronts, coming to rest in a crack somewhere and spending the next two hundred years turning two liters into a sapling. Nearly all of them fail and dry to husks. The husks litter the pans, dark spheres on white ground, spaced like a scattering thrown by a hand.

Over the millennia the groves migrate this way, one successful daughter per century, drifting slowly downwind across the plateau like an extremely patient herd.

### 5. The Rare Ones

Animals are the water thieves, and there are very few of them, because a thief can only live where there is enough stored to steal.

Perhaps one grove in five holds a browser. They are low, broad, slow things with hide like grey suede and a rasping mouthpart made for the single task of scoring a cask tower's rind and drinking what wells up before the wound seals. A tower can afford this a few times a decade. The browsers know the ledger better than the towers do; they take little, they move on, they walk sixty kilometers of open pan under that blazing sun to the next grove, arriving with their hides gone slack and their eyes recessed. They never hurry. Hurrying costs.

They do not drink from the ground because there is no ground to drink from. They do not urinate. They exhale nothing. They are as sealed as the plants; convergent solutions to the same law.

Higher up there is a flier, rare enough that you might cross the plateau twice and see one — a long thin sailing thing with a wing membrane like scraped horn, riding the thermals that stand permanently over the dark groves. It lives on the seeds. It cracks a barrel-sphere, drinks the dowry, and in doing so kills a possible forest. It is also the only reason the groves move at all, since it carries the ones it doesn't crack and drops them further than the wind ever would. Nobody arranged this. It simply settled into balance a long time ago and stayed there.

### 6. Weather Without Water

The plateau has weather; it just has no clouds.

**Dust fronts.** Once or twice a season a wall of pale fines comes across the pans, five hundred meters high, moving at a walking pace, absolutely silent because the air is too thin to carry its roar. You see it for six hours before it reaches you. It goes over like a dimming of the world, and then it's past, and the hexagon rims have grown a little, and the seeds have rolled a little further downwind.

**Static.** Dry dust plus dry air makes charge. During a front the groves stand and glow faintly at their crown-tips, blue and continuous, and small sparks tick between frond and frond. This is where the plateau does most of its nitrogen chemistry. The towers have evolved to *want* the front; they turn their upper spirals into it.

**Thermal tide.** Every day the whole plateau breathes. The ground heats, the air above it rises, and the cool dense air of the rim slides inward to replace it — a slow radial inhalation, all day. At sunset it reverses and the plateau exhales over the escarpment into the haze ocean. Stand on the rim at dusk and feel the entire continent breathe past you and down.

**Dawn sweat.** In the last hour before sunrise, at the coldest, the groves permit themselves a single indulgence: a thin exudate on the trunks, a film that is not quite water but carries some, released to catch the trace of humidity the night air has hoarded, and reabsorbed before the sun clears the ridges. For that one hour the trunks glisten. It is the only wet thing on a continent. The browsers know the hour and stand pressed against the trunks with their mouthparts flat to the rind, and this is tolerated, and no one knows why.

### 7. Sound

The plateau is quiet in a way that is medically strange to a visitor. Thin air is a poor conductor; sound goes a short distance and then simply stops, rather than fading. Shout, and it is as if the shout has been taken out of the air ten meters away and put in a box.

So what you hear is nearly all close and nearly all your own: the crunch of crust, the creak of your joints, your pulse in your ears, which becomes loud enough to be unpleasant.

Beyond that, three sounds exist.

The first is the **tick**. Rock and rind expand at dawn and contract at dusk, and both events produce hours of small irregular clicking across the whole landscape, like a very sparse rain on a tin roof, which is a comparison the plateau would not understand.

The second is the **grove tone**. Inside a dense grove, if you are still, there is a low continuous note — under a hertz or so, felt more than heard, the sound of forty tonnes of fluid being pumped up thirty meters of sealed column by a mechanism with no valves. It is the closest thing here to a heartbeat and it is not rhythmic; it is steady.

The third is the **silence of the pan**, which is not really a sound but is not really an absence either, and everyone who has stood in the middle of one reports it as a presence.

### 8. What It Is Like to Be There

Nothing has ever been built here. There are no ruins, no cairns, no trails except the browsers' faint pressure-lines in the crust, which the dust fronts erase and which the browsers redraw by walking the same reasoning. There are no tools, because there are no hands, because a hand is a device for changing things and nothing on this plateau has ever had a use for that.

And yet it reads as *made*. That's the persistent illusion of the place. The hexagon floors look tiled. The spirals of the fronds look solved. The husk-spheres on the pans look placed. The spacing of the groves looks surveyed. It is all just what a system looks like after it has had long enough — every arbitrary thing has been worn off, and what remains is only what works, and what works always looks like it was intended.

Understand: this landscape is not dying and never was. Dryness here is not a wound. It is the finished condition of a world that took its water indoors a hundred million years ago and has run it, since then, with no losses, through a slow closed circuit of dark glass towers standing far apart on a white and shining continent under a small white sun.

It is not a hard place to be. It is cool, it is bright, it is silent, and every forty kilometers there is a black grove full of warmth and a low note that you can feel in your sternum.

It only asks that you bring your own water, and take it with you when you go.
