## The Flat Above the Weather

Begin with the fact that there is no rock here.

You will want rock. Everything about the place suggests it — the altitude (your ears keep asking about it, your blood keeps complaining), the cold that never quite becomes frost, the way the horizon runs off unbroken in every direction like a table set for nobody. Uplands are supposed to be bone. But this upland has been worn past bone. The mountains that stood here spent themselves so long ago that even their gravel is gone, dissolved, carried off by rivers that themselves dried and were forgotten and were reinvented. What is left is a plateau three thousand meters up and roughly two hundred kilometers across, and it is made entirely of plants: living above, dying in the middle, dead and compressed and still faintly warm below, down and down for a depth nobody has ever measured because the boring never stops being vegetable.

The ground gives underfoot like the flank of a large sleeping animal. It is the color of old bronze and wet moss and the inside of a walnut. Step and water rises around your boot in a ring; lift and the ring closes behind you with a small sucking apology. Everything you walk on is a body. There is no soil in the sense of mineral grit. There is only the accumulated patience of moss, kilometers of it, layer on layer, the oldest continuous project in the world.

---

**The Air**

The plateau makes its own weather and then lives inside it. All that living mass breathes out water; the cold catches the water and holds it as a low ceiling of cloud a hundred meters up, so the light arrives already used — sieved, greened, going the color of a bottle held to a window at dusk. There is never full dark and never anything you'd call day. The illumination simply thickens and thins on some schedule the eye can't hold.

The air is at eighty, ninety, ninety-six percent water and cold enough that it condenses on you personally. Ten minutes out and your eyebrows bead. Your sleeves get heavy. You are being gently rained on by nothing, from all directions at once, forever. Breathing feels like drinking. Where the ground bulges into low domes — never more than four or five meters, the plateau's entire vocabulary of relief — the domes weep. Not springs. Just sweating, everywhere, a sheen that runs off the moss in threads too small to hear, joining and joining until somewhere out of sight there is a sound like a crowd murmuring, which is the whole surface draining into the whole surface.

---

**The Engines**

And then, very far apart, the boilers.

You see the first one from perhaps eight kilometers off and take it for a hill, because your mind has been starved for verticals. It is not a hill. It is a plant. It stands sixty meters and it is shaped something like a pear, something like a lung, something like a foundry, and it is shuddering.

Nobody built these. That's the thing to hold on to. There is not a single made object on the plateau, no wall, no path, no rusting relic, no scratched initial. The industry is native. Somewhere down the long ages the moss-mass discovered that fermentation makes heat, that heat in a cold wet world is capital, and it began building organs to exploit this the way other lineages built flowers. A boiler is a vegetable pressure vessel. Its walls are laminated heartwood forty centimeters thick, banded in living hoops that tighten as they grow. Inside, a sealed chamber packed with sugar-slurry and yeasts goes hot — you can feel it from thirty meters, an animal warmth, the only warmth for a hundred kilometers — and the steam it raises drives things.

Drives *what,* exactly, is the question you'll spend your time on. Pistons of oiled resin-wood rise and fall in sleeves, slow as a heartbeat, pumping sap up through the whole structure and out along runners that go anywhere, kilometers, drilling roots down into the peat to fetch more sugar for the fire. Turbine wheels of stiff varnished leaf spin in vent shafts with a whistling that carries so far you hear four boilers at once from any point on the plateau. Valves — actual valves, cartilaginous, self-seating — cough off overpressure at irregular intervals with a *whump* that you feel in your sternum from a kilometer away, and a white column goes up two hundred meters and flattens against the cloud ceiling and joins it.

This is why the place is loud. Not busy-loud. Loud like a mill valley at night, the sound of enormous slow work being done in the middle distance by something with no interest in you. Piston knock. Turbine whistle. The long groan of a runner-root taking up strain across wet ground. And under all of it the murmur of the draining surface, and above all of it, wind in nothing, because there is nothing for wind to be in.

The boilers stand three to eight kilometers apart. Between them: emptiness, flatness, moss, standing water in sheets you can see clouds in. That's the plateau's proportion — a vast sparse machine-savanna, each engine alone with its own horizon, connected only underneath.

---

**The Fauna**

Wet, cold, and warm at intervals — so of course it teems.

Amphibians own the flats. Not frogs exactly; long-bodied, dark-backed things with skin that carries its own photosynthetic film, so they graze partly on the light they lie in. They congregate at the boilers for the heat in numbers that turn the lower walls into a shifting hide. When a valve blows they all go silent at once and then all resume, and the resumption is the loudest thing on the plateau — ten thousand throats at once, a wall of sound you can lean on.

There are birds with an odd habit: they nest *in* the turbine shafts, in the moving parts, and the plant tolerates it, and they leave when the wheel is about to seize, minutes before, reliably. Nobody has a good account of how.

There are grazers, waist-high, six-legged, with wide splayed pads for the soft ground and dense wool going grey-green with epiphytes, so that a herd at distance looks like the plateau's own surface loosely detached and walking. They eat the moss top and their trampling opens water, and the water lets the moss breathe, so the moss grows them the way it grows anything else it needs.

And there are the pilgrims. Small dun animals, unremarkable, moving always in one direction — the same direction, all of them, always, across the whole two hundred kilometers. They do not arrive anywhere. They do not come back around. They have been doing this since before the first observer and there is no plausible destination.

---

**The Thinning**

Here is where the place stops behaving.

Erosion, on this plateau, went further than rock. It got into the mechanism. When a landscape holds still long enough — really still, tens of millions of years of the same wet cold and the same slow breathing — the local laws wear at their edges like a stair tread. They still work. They just don't work *sharply* anymore.

So: distances do not sum. Walk from boiler to boiler and it is four kilometers. Walk back, four kilometers. Walk the triangle to a third and home again and you have covered eleven and the numbers will not be argued with; the plateau simply has more inside it than around it, and has for a long time.

So: rain sometimes falls upward. Not violently. A sheet of standing water will begin to unbead itself, drops rising in a slow silver drift into the cloud, and the amphibians go on grazing through it, unbothered, because to them this is just weather.

So: things happen twice, or happen and then get ready to. A valve blows and thirty seconds later the pressure rises to the point of blowing and there is a moment where it is unclear which of the two events is the memory. You will see a grazer step into a pool and see the same grazer, unmistakably, still approaching the pool from four hundred meters back, and both of them are real, and both of them are the only one, and in an hour there is one grazer and no discrepancy and no wound in the world where the extra was.

The boilers are worst near their throats. Stand under a vent stack and the steam coming out has been out for a while — it hangs in the shape it will make, faintly, before it makes it. Your own breath does the same. Speak and hear the sentence begin a half-beat before your mouth commits, and lose your nerve, and stop, and hear the rest of it anyway, in your voice, saying what you had decided against.

The pilgrim animals, I think, are a symptom rather than a species. Something in the plateau's causality leaks in one direction and they are what that current looks like when it picks up flesh.

---

**Rules, as far as anyone can state them**

The place is turbulent under all its slowness. This is the contradiction you have to hold. Individually, every process is glacial — moss adding a millimeter a decade, a piston at twelve strokes a minute. Collectively, the plateau is a storm. Boilers overheat and rupture, spectacularly, throwing a hectare of hot pulp across the flats, and the flats are green over the wound within a season and a new bud is rising from the scar within three. There are always four or five burning somewhere on the horizon, orange under the cloud, and always a dozen new ones coming up like slow fists. The whole system is rebuilding itself at all times at every scale, and the rate of destruction exactly equals the rate of creation, and has for so long that the equality has become one of the local laws, another worn stair.

Nothing here is preserved and nothing here is lost. Drop something and it is taken into the substrate within a year, and it does not decay so much as get *incorporated* — the plateau makes tissue of it. There is no archaeology because there is no separateness. Anything that arrives becomes moss eventually, including the mountains, including the weather, including you if you stay through more than one thinning.

The final thing to say is that it is beautiful in a way that has nothing to do with you, and it is cold, and it is not hostile. It simply completed itself a very long time ago and has been idling at full capacity ever since — breathing out its own sky, drilling its own fuel, burning and regrowing, hammering away in the dim green at eight-kilometer intervals across a table of living peat, with ten thousand throats going and the water rising and falling both ways, having long since worn smooth every distinction that would have let it be one thing rather than the whole of what there is.
