## The Lees

Begin with what the mountains left behind.

There was a range here once — the kind with a snowline and a shadow that ran for a hundred miles at evening. It is gone. Not fallen, not toppled; simply spent. What remains is its root: a tableland of grey-green gneiss, banded like muscle, standing four and a half thousand metres above a sea it has never met. The old summits survive only as tors — knuckled outcrops that break the surface every kilometre or so, worn to the shapes of things left too long in a river. Between them the ground rolls. It swells and settles like a held breath. Nothing here is steep. Everything here is high.

The people call it the Lees, because it is what settled out.

## Air

The first fact of the Lees is thinness. The air here carries about half the weight it does at the coast, and it carries sound badly. A shout dies at twenty paces, flattened, as though the world were absorbing it politely and declining to pass it on. Hammer-blows arrive at your ear without their edges. Two people talking forty metres apart cannot hear each other at all, and know it, and don't try.

So the Lees is quiet — not the quiet of emptiness, but of transmission failure. There is a great deal happening. Very little of it reaches you.

The second fact is that fire does not want to burn. Flame here is low, blue-hearted, sullen; a lamp gutters at the height of a thumb and gives a light like the inside of a shell. Coal will smoulder but not roar. This is why the Lees never learned combustion the way the lowlands did, and instead learned patience.

## Water, which arrives sideways

It rains rarely. But the Lees is wet.

Every morning, between the fourth and the ninth hour, cloud comes up from the drowned valleys to the west and moves through the tableland at ground level — not overhead, *through*. It is a slow wet nothing, a fog with grain to it, and it does not fall. It touches. It condenses on every surface it can find: on stone, on wool, on eyelashes, on the ten thousand hairs of every cushion-plant, on the wires and nets and slatted fences the townspeople have raised across two hundred square kilometres of moorland for exactly this purpose.

By the seventh hour the whole plateau is ticking. Not raining — ticking. Drops forming, swelling, releasing, striking moss. Multiply that by every leaf and every wire and you have the ambient sound of the Lees, a sound like a very large animal listening in the dark.

The water goes down. It collects in runnels no wider than a wrist, cut into peat over a few thousand years, threading between hummocks with a sound so small you have to kneel to hear it. The runnels feed shallow pools the colour of strong tea, and the pools feed the bogs, and the bogs feed the mill-races, and eventually — at the plateau's southern rim, where the old root finally breaks off into cloud — all of it leaves in one thin white ribbon that falls for a kilometre and turns to mist before it lands. No river reaches the bottom. The Lees exports its water as weather.

## What grows

Vegetation here is not tall. It is *thick*. Height is punished — by wind, by frost, by the ultraviolet coming down hard through half an atmosphere — and so everything has gone sideways and inward instead, packing biomass into the lowest thirty centimetres of the world with the intensity of a city.

Walk out from town and you walk on a mattress. Sphagnum in six colours, russet and ochre and a sick brilliant green, wet enough to print your foot with water. Cushion plants in domes the size of skulls, each dome ten thousand leaves and, inside, a dry warm interior full of insects that never see the outside. Lichens crusted over lichens, growing at a millimetre a decade, so that a hand-sized patch of a tor may be older than the town, older than the language of the town. Dwarf willow, ankle-high, four hundred years old, with a trunk you could snap and a root system you could not.

And above the mat, at intervals, the rosette-trees: the only tall things. Three metres of dead leaves, retained, wrapped tight around a soft trunk as insulation, topped with a silver-furred crown that gathers fog better than any net the millwrights ever built. They stand in loose crowds on the north-facing swells, ten thousand of them, all leaning very slightly the same way. In dim fog they read as a congregation. This is where most visitors get their idea that the Lees is haunted; it isn't, but the impression is not unearned.

Nothing rots properly. It is too cold and too acid. So the peat below holds everything it has ever received, layer on layer on layer, eight metres deep in the bowls — pollen, ash, moss, the odd mummified bird, wooden tools of people no one remembers. The ground is an archive that has not been read. When the millwrights cut a new race they cut through eras, and they are careful, and they take out whatever they find and lay it on the bank, and look at it, and don't know.

## Thimble

The town sits in a shallow bowl three kilometres across, which is why it has its name: a small vessel for catching.

It is layered, because the bowl is small and the wind is not, so Thimble has built downward and inward. Roofs are turfed and stitched with wire, and one house's roof is another's yard. Streets run as covered lanes, plank-floored over running water, so that walking through town you hear beneath your boots the entire hydrology of the plateau being routed toward the works. Walls are drystone, gneiss, laid without mortar because mortar cracks in the nightly freeze; the stones are round-shouldered, taken from the moor, already ancient when they were lifted. Windows are small and doubled, and the gap between the panes is always beaded with condensation, which is collected, which is used.

Everything is collected. Everything is used.

The works occupy the bowl's low centre: fog-halls with slatted louvered walls a hundred metres long, where cloud is walked through baffles and combed of its water; settling tanks; the moss-presses, where sphagnum is pressed into fuel-bricks and bandage-wadding and insulation-batt and exported down the cableway to the lowlands, which want a great deal of it and always have. Sheds of turf and iron. Yards of stacked pale bales. Copper pipe everywhere, gone green, running along walls at hip height, warm to the touch in the evening and rimed white at dawn.

And the engines.

They are enormous and they are slow. Thin air makes a poor working fluid and there is no fire worth the name, so Thimble runs on the day: on the difference between the fog-warmth of morning and the hard clear frost of the night. Sealed vessels, ten metres tall, charged with a liquid that boils near freezing. Each one has a single stroke per cycle. Each cycle is a day.

So the town's industry does not hum. It *turns over*, twice: once about the ninth hour when the fog lifts, and once about the second hour after dark, when the sky opens and the temperature drops eighteen degrees in ninety minutes. At those two moments, every engine in Thimble moves at once. The sound is not loud — nothing here is loud — but it is enormous in some other dimension: a groan of metal a kilometre wide, arriving from all directions with its edges sanded off by the thin air, lasting perhaps four minutes, ending in a long series of settling clicks. Then nothing. The pumps hold their pressure. The flywheels — which are not wheels but stone counterweights the size of houses, on chains, in shafts sunk into the peat — begin their fourteen-hour descent, and that descent is the town's power, doled out in trickles to the presses and the winches and the four hundred small electric lamps that constitute the entire artificial light of the Lees.

Children are taught to be indoors, or at least standing still, for the turnover. Not for safety. For the manners of it.

## Light

Direct sun happens perhaps forty days a year and is brutal, blue-white, unbearable at noon; there are shutters for it and everyone hates it. The normal condition is dim: cloud below, cloud within, cloud above, light arriving from no particular direction and casting no shadow at all. Shadowlessness is the visual signature of the Lees. Depth is difficult. Distance is a guess. The rosette-trees float. A tor at two kilometres and a stone at two metres present the same grey.

At dusk — which is long, because the plateau is high enough to hold light after the valleys have gone dark — the cloud beneath the southern rim turns the colour of a peach, and for about twenty minutes Thimble sits on an island above a lit sea, and everyone who can stops what they are doing and goes to look. Then it goes out and the frost begins and the engines turn over.

## What lives here besides people

Very little, and it is noticed.

There are insects, in vast quiet numbers, inside the cushion-plants and under the moss — flightless, dark, slow. There are two kinds of bird: a small brown one that lives in the peat banks and shrieks at nothing, inaudibly, and once or twice a year a formation of very high geese crossing north, so far up that even here they are far up, and no one hears them at all, and someone always points.

And there is the vicuña-like thing the town calls the leaner: long-legged, wool the colour of wet stone, standing in the rosette-groves in the fog with its head down, alone, always alone. Perhaps six hundred of them on the whole plateau. They are not tame and not afraid. They tolerate. If you walk toward one it will move away exactly as far as you walked toward it, maintaining a distance it seems to have decided on some centuries ago, and it will keep doing this all day if you like. Their tracks are the only trails on the moor that aren't human, and the humans use them, because a leaner path across a bog has never once been wrong.

That is the fauna. That is all of it.

## The rules

Nothing here happens quickly, and everything here is happening.

You may not raise your voice usefully; therefore Thimble speaks close, and touches shoulders to get attention, and has an entire second grammar of taps and lamp-flashes for the works. You may not burn much; therefore heat is hoarded, moved through copper, slept against. You may not build tall; therefore the town has folded. You may not hurry; therefore the calendar of the Lees is not made of hours but of turnovers — a job takes four turnovers, a debt is settled in ninety, a child is grown in eleven thousand.

And nothing here decays; therefore nothing is thrown away, because it wouldn't go. Everything that has ever been dropped on the Lees is still on the Lees, a little deeper down, waiting in the cold to be found, held in the last, final, settled shape of a mountain that finished being a mountain a long time before anyone arrived to press its moss into bricks.
