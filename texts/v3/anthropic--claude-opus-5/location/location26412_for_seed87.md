## The Vault Under the Sink-Hole

You could stand on top of it and never know. Above, a cold flat country of peat and standing water, sedge going brown, a skin of ice each night that the day doesn't quite take back. Somewhere in that ground the water found a lens of soluble rock and began, some hundreds of years ago, to eat a room.

The room is not finished. That is the first thing to understand about this place: the room is younger than the city inside it.

---

### The chamber

It is about the size of a chapel, if the chapel were meant for one worshipper. Roughly eleven paces the long way. The ceiling is low and irregular and everywhere weeping — not leaking, *weeping*, a slow bead-and-release of water so saturated with dissolved stone that it can barely hold what it carries. Where each bead hangs long enough, it leaves a ring behind. The rings stack. The ceiling is furred with the beginnings of ten thousand stalactites, none longer than a thumb, most no longer than an eyelash. In a hundred years there will be a forest up there. Right now there is only intention.

The floor is worse-defined than the ceiling. It isn't rock yet. It is a slurry, a deep pale silt of precipitate that has fallen out of the water and not yet learned to be stone, and it goes down further than anything living here has ever gone. Drop a pebble; it does not land. It slows, and slows, and is gone.

Between the unfinished ceiling and the unfinished floor: water, filling all of it but a hand's breadth of trapped, sour air at the highest point of the dome.

The water is cold. Two degrees, three at the warmest end of the year. Cold enough that everything in here moves as if remembering how.

---

### The layers

The water is not one water. It stands in three distinct floors, and the boundaries between them are visible — actually visible, like panes of very old glass laid flat in mid-space.

The top layer is thin, fresh, and shivering, fed by the surface. Meltwater comes in through a fissure at the eastern end each spring and lies on top of everything else without mixing.

The middle layer is the great body of the chamber. Clear, mineral-heavy, still.

The bottom layer is brine — dense, slightly warmer, faintly sulphurous, welling up from a seep in the silt that pulses about nine times an hour. You can watch the pulse. The interface between brine and clear water lifts and settles like a slow lung, and things resting on that interface rise and settle with it.

Anything less dense than brine cannot sink into it. Small dead things lie on top of the brine layer as if on a floor, in mid-water, waiting to be eaten. This is the larder of the whole system and everyone knows where it is.

---

### The city

It is a metre and a half across.

The builders are the length of a grain of rice, and there are perhaps four hundred thousand of them. They are pale, segmented, mostly transparent, with a hard ventral plate they use as a trowel, and they secrete from a gland behind the head a cold cement — carbonate paste, the same substance the ceiling is dripping, but taken up, worked in the body, and laid down deliberately.

That is the essential fact of this place. The chamber is trying to build itself, stupidly, one bead at a time. The city is made of the same material, doing it on purpose.

The architecture grows in three registers, because the water has three floors.

In the **brine**, at the bottom, are the roots: squat, thick-walled, blunt vaults with narrow mouths, built to keep the sour heavy water out of the places that need clear water and to let it into the places that need feeding. There are channels down there — genuine plumbing, laid with a bricklayer's patience — that carry brine uphill in closed conduits to feeding-galleries thirty body-lengths above the seep, because brine, being dense, will run through a sealed pipe wherever the pipe goes. The wrights have solved hydraulics with no tool but a jaw and a plate of chitin. There is no metal here, no fire, no lever, no string. Nothing in this city was ever *made*; it was all *exuded*, and then pushed into place with the flat of a body.

In the **clear middle water** is the bulk of it: the towers. They are not towers as we build them, because there is no gravity worth respecting at that size in that medium. They are branched, and they branch in all directions, including downward. Spires grow from the undersides of terraces. Bridges arch below the things they connect. The whole mass is a dense reef-thicket of fluted, chalk-white, wafer-thin construction, tier upon tier, with barely a body's width of open water anywhere in the middle of it. Density is the point. A wright is never more than a few seconds from a wall.

In the **thin cold fresh water** at the top, pressed against the weeping ceiling, is the strange part. The wrights build up there too, but inverted: a second city hanging down from the roof, mirroring the one below, tower for tower, terrace for terrace. It is not inhabited. It has never been inhabited. Every wright that dies is carried up, is fitted into a socket in the upper city, and is cemented over, and the shape it makes becomes part of the mirror. The upper city is a record and a graveyard and, as far as anyone could determine, a reflex — the colony builds its dead into the ceiling because the ceiling is building downward too, and something in the species cannot allow the rock to be the only thing growing overhead.

Where the two cities are closest, at the centre of the dome, they are three finger-widths apart. That gap has been narrowing for two hundred years.

---

### The neighbours

The city is not alone in the chamber and is not the strongest thing in it.

Copepods in uncountable numbers, drifting like dust in a shaft of light, grazing the mineral film. Amphipods, larger, hunched, scavenging the brine-floor larder. A dozen blind flatworms, each the length of a finger, that flow over the outer terraces at night and are the great terror of the place — a flatworm can push its stomach through a doorway that would stop its body. Ostracods that clack (soundlessly, at our scale) along the conduits. A single population of white shrimp with vestigial eyes, hanging in the mid-water, doing nothing much, drifting through the towers as through weather.

And on the ceiling, at the eastern end where the meltwater fissure comes in, there is light.

---

### Light

Enough surface light reaches down that crack, in the four brightest months, to be called light: a green-grey dimness, roughly the brightness of a room with the curtains shut. It does not reach the far end. The far end is lit only by biology — three species of luminous copepod, and a mat of something on the north wall that pulses a slow cold blue about once a minute, for no reason anyone could establish.

Under the fissure grow the gardens. Sparse — seven patches, none bigger than a hand: a black-green algal crust, a few filaments standing up in the current like grass under wind, and one dense low turf of moss-like growth that is genuinely a moss, spores washed down from the sedge above, living slowed almost to a stop in the cold.

The wrights do not eat the gardens. They *build around* them. The seven patches are walled, gated, and kept clear of grazers by continuous physical patrol. There is no evidence they get anything from the plants. The walls have been maintained for longer than the towers have stood.

---

### Sound

There is none.

Put your ear to the peat above and you hear peat. Drop a hydrophone down the fissure and the recording comes back almost blank: a hiss of water moving over water, the nine-per-hour sigh of the seep, and — very rarely, at the threshold of the instrument — a faint irregular ticking, like a clock made of sugar. That is crystallisation. That is the ceiling growing.

Everything the wrights do, they do in silence. They speak by touch: a run of taps down the flank, a pressure held for a count, a pattern drummed on a shared wall so the whole terrace feels it through the stone. The city is a single organ of conducted vibration, and the vibrations are far too small and too quiet to leave the water. A crisis can cross the entire settlement in four seconds and never make a sound louder than a hair falling.

---

### The rules

The brine only rises; the fresh water only lies on top; nothing mixes without violence.

Nothing sinks all the way to the bottom, because there is no bottom.

The dead go up.

The ceiling and the city are in a race, and the ceiling does not know it is racing, and will win. Each year the gap at the centre of the dome closes by the width of a hair. When it closes, the two cities will fuse, and the chamber will be one solid mass of worked and unworked carbonate with several hundred thousand small masons sealed inside their own work, and after some further thousands of years the peat above will subside a little, and there will be a shallow pond up there of no particular interest, and nothing will indicate what it is standing on.

Meanwhile the seep pulses. The flatworms hunt at what passes for night. The gardens are guarded. Somebody is laying a new course of cement on the underside of a bridge, in the dark, in the cold, in total silence, and it is good work, and it will hold.
