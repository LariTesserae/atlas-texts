## The Drowned Cordillera

### The shape of it

Begin with the stone, because the stone is the youngest thing here and still behaves as though it has just arrived.

A range of mountains, freshly made — thrust up out of some patient collision — stands filled to the shoulders in warm water. Not sea water. Something lighter, faintly sweet, tasting of mineral and of the crushed green of leaves, so clear that at fifty meters you can still read the grain of the rock. The peaks are pale: a new granite, nearly white, and darker andesite in bands, and everywhere the glassy conchoidal scars of stone that has broken but not yet been *worn*. There are no rounded shoulders. No screes fanning out into aprons. The arêtes come to edges you could cut a palm on, and the faces meet at angles that have never been negotiated by weather. Here and there a flake the size of a door hangs off a cliff, released but not yet fallen, held by friction and by the drag of the water, which is in no hurry.

Above the waterline — and there is a waterline, roughly two hundred meters below the highest crests — the summits stand out into a warm and saturated air that is barely air. Mist so thick it drips upward off the underside of leaves. The line between the water and the not-water is legible mostly by the change in how light behaves. Everything above is soft and diffused, everything below is sharp and lit.

There are perhaps forty such summits in view from any high ridge, receding in tiers of paler and paler blue-green, and between them the basins go down and down, dark with forest.

### The water and its floors

The great strangeness of the basin is that the water is not one water. It is stacked.

There are five or six layers, sometimes more, differing in density by trivial amounts — a hair of salt, a fraction of a degree, a load of dissolved silica leached from the new stone. Because nothing here stirs, the layers have never mixed. Their boundaries are visible, and they are *thin*: a shimmer no thicker than a fingernail, running horizontally for kilometers, cutting across cliff faces like the pencil lines of a survey nobody made. Look along one edge-on and it becomes a silver plane, a floor of mirror, with the forest reflected in it upside down.

Things settle onto these interfaces and stay. This is the second strangeness. Fallen leaves — enormous, translucent, thin as the skin inside an eggshell — drift down until they meet a boundary and then simply lie there, tens of thousands of them, overlapping, going pale and glassy over decades. They mat. They knit. Over long enough time each interface acquires a lid: a false floor of accumulated foliage, pollen slick, spore dust, and the long silvered threads of something like a fungus that has learned to live nowhere but on the seam between two waters. From below, these lids are ceilings — bright, veined, backlit, an entire sky made of dead leaves. From above, they are meadows. You could set your weight on the older ones. They would hold, for a while, and dimple, and let a slow bloom of water up through the puncture, and then close.

So the world has floors. Six or seven of them, going down, each with its own light and its own forest, each roofed by the litter of the forest above.

### The forest

Vegetation here has been released from a great many obligations. There is no wind, so nothing needs to be strong. There is no drought, so nothing needs to hoard. There is only light, and competition for it, and time.

The result is a forest of absurd, tender extravagance. The dominant growth is a stem-plant — call it a sheath-tree — built of hollow gas-filled chambers stacked one on another like the segments of a bamboo, each chamber a little bubble of oxygen the plant made and kept. The stems rise straight because they float; they are guyed to the rock below by fine white anchor-threads that hum faintly under tension. A sheath-tree may be ninety meters tall and no thicker than a wrist. It cannot fall over. It has never had to consider falling over.

From each node the leaves come out in horizontal tiers, and they are vast. Single blades three, four meters across, and so thin that light passes through them and turns green-gold on the far side, and so thin that they tear on contact with anything, which is why the forest is arranged in strata that do not touch: each tier hovers a hand's width above the next, motionless, layer on layer on layer, forty tiers deep, and to move through it is to pass through the pages of a book without turning any of them.

Density: everything is nearly touching and almost nothing touches. The volume is *full*. Between the great tiers there are epiphytes strung on threads, hanging bells of something mossy that breathe out single bubbles at long intervals; there are climbing plants with leaves like wet paper lanterns; there are floating rosettes that have given up on roots altogether and simply hang at the depth that suits them, thousands of them, at exactly the same depth, a horizontal reef of green wheels. On the interfaces themselves grow the lid-meadows: dense, low, felted, dark. In the shafts of light that come down through gaps, the water is visibly thick with drifting particulate — pollen, and the shed scales of leaves, and glassy diatom-things — so that the light has body and edges and you can see it end.

Where the summits stand out into the air above, the forest changes character: smaller, sturdier, more familiar, mosses in mattresses a meter deep, dwarf trees with leaves gone leathery against the mist. A treeline in reverse — the *upper* forest is the stunted one, hardened by exposure to atmosphere.

### Light

Bright. The sun is unimpeded above; the basin is at altitude and the water is clear, and so down to the third interface it is simply *daylight*, green-gold and even.

But the stacked mirrors do things. Standing on a ridge under water and looking out across the basin, you see the far peaks, and above them their reflection in the underside of the interface, and above that — if the geometry cooperates — a second, dimmer reflection off the next boundary up, so that the range repeats itself in fainter and fainter tiers into the high water, a whole false cordillera hanging over the real one, upside down, receding. The scale of the place is thus at least doubled, and it is not always immediately obvious which mountains you could actually walk to.

Look straight up from the deep and the surface is a mirror everywhere except for one circle overhead, through which you can see the sun and the mist and the black wet rock of the summits, wobbling slightly, compressed. That circle follows you.

### Sound

Almost nothing. This is the quietest lush place imaginable.

No wind means no rustle. No current means no rush. No surf, no rivers, no rain striking anything. The forest is silent in the way a held breath is silent — a silence with volume in it.

What sound there is arrives with enormous clarity, because water carries it far and there is no other noise to hide in. The *tick* of a sheath-tree's gas chamber equalizing, a small dry sound like a knuckle, heard from four hundred meters. The long groan of the mountain settling, which comes not really as sound but as a pressure against the sternum, and which is followed some seconds later by a distant slow avalanche of one flake of stone parting from a cliff and going down through the tiers, shearing leaves, and the shearing of leaves is audible, a soft continuous rip that lasts nearly a minute. Then quiet again for a day or a week.

### What lives here

Very little moves. That is the fourth strangeness, and the hardest to get used to: a forest at maximum saturation and almost nothing in it that is not a plant.

There are the clock-snails, palm-sized, shell like a wound sheet of mica, grazing the underside of the lid-meadows. They advance perhaps the width of a hand per day. Their grazing tracks are legible on the leaf-ceiling as pale meandering lines, and a single individual's lifework may be a hundred meters of line, a hundred years long.

There is one thing large enough to see coming: a flat gliding animal, a meter across, fringed all around like the edge of a lettuce, translucent enough that you can watch its slow gut working. It moves once and then coasts, and coasts, and coasts, banking on nothing, taking twenty minutes to cross a clearing. You might see one a month. Whether they eat is unclear; possibly they are photosynthetic, farming something in their own tissue.

And there are clouds. Loose, drifting aggregates of small colonial things, the size of a room, hanging at particular depths, faintly luminous when the light hits them wrong. They keep station for years at a time. It is not obvious that they are animals. It is not obvious that they are not.

That is nearly the whole census. No fish. No birds above the water except, once or twice a season, something high and unidentifiable crossing between summits. The forest does not need pollinators; it releases its gametes into a still medium and waits for them to arrive by diffusion, which takes months, which is fine.

### The rules

**Nothing is worn down.** Erosion requires abrasion, and abrasion requires motion. Here the only weathering is chemical, and slow: the stone dissolves a molecule at a time and the silica goes into the water and thickens it and makes another interface. The mountains stay sharp. Their sharpness is not a sign of youth so much as a sign that nothing is coming for them.

**Nothing falls fast.** Drag and buoyancy conspire. A dropped stone takes a long time to reach the bottom of a basin. A dropped leaf may never.

**Nothing rots on schedule.** Decay is bacterial, and the bacteria here are warm but sparse and the litter is glassy and unappetizing. So the dead accumulate as architecture. The lid-meadows are middens. The deepest layers of the basin floor are a compacted felt of leaves fifteen meters thick, going amber, going toward something like coal, with nothing on top of them heavy enough to press.

**Everything drifts toward its own level and stays.** Rosettes, clouds, animals, litter, dissolved minerals, even sound — the world is stratified all the way down, and the vertical is the only direction with real consequences.

**The Turning.** Once in something like eleven years, the layers exchange. It begins as a warm bruise low in the basin and takes six weeks. The interfaces buckle, very slowly, and pass through one another, and the lid-meadows tear and go down or come up, and the whole stacked world folds over on itself at the speed of an hour hand. There is almost no noise. The clock-snails, whose ceilings have become floors, turn around and begin grazing the other way. Afterward the water is briefly one water, and briefly murky, and briefly full of a smell like cut grass and iron, and then it begins to sort itself out again, and the new leaves begin to fall onto the new seams, and the new floors begin.

### The one ambiguous thing

On the north face above the third interface there is a formation the eye insists on misreading. Columnar jointing in a young intrusive, weathered — barely weathered — into a flight of eleven regular steps, each about the height of a step, rising to a flat landing and then stopping in mid-air. Beside it, a sheath-tree colony has anchored its guy-threads in a taut regular lattice, twelve strands, evenly spaced, running from the landing down into the dark, and the threads have collected leaf-litter along their lengths so that the whole thing reads unmistakably as a rope bridge with the planks rotted through.

It is not. Nothing here has built anything. There are no makers in the basin, no tools, no artifacts, no fire — fire is not even a concept that would occur.

But the stairs are there, and the lattice is there, and the water is very still and very clear, and the reflection puts a second identical stair upside down above the first, going up where the other goes down, and the mind does what the mind does. That is the only construction in the drowned cordillera: the strong, false, recurring impression that someone came through here once and left before the leaves fell.
