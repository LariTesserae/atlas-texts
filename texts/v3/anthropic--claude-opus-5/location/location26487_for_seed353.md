# The Stand

## The approach

You come up onto it from below, which means you come up out of warmth into more warmth, and out of dark into a different quality of dark. The slope is not rock. There has been no rock at the surface here for a very long time. The slope is a laminate — layer on layer of pressed pith, each layer about the thickness of a fingernail, each one laid down and cured and walked on and laid over, so that the ramp you climb is a book of some hundreds of thousands of pages standing on edge. It gives very slightly underfoot, like a floor over a floor. In places the lamination has slumped and re-cured in ripples, and these ripples are the closest thing to erosion the Stand permits: not the wearing away of stone but the sagging of something that was never stone, settling toward the shape it will keep.

At the lip, the air changes. Thin — you are high, a couple of thousand meters above whatever counts as a plain out there — thin and dry past the meaning of dry, and hot enough that your first breath feels like a hand pressed flat against the back of your throat. Nothing here has ever been wet. There is no word in the Stand's chemistry for wet. What runs in the pipes is oil: a heavy, terpene-smelling, straw-colored oil that will not evaporate, will not freeze, and will not, in this atmosphere, catch fire.

And you can see almost nothing. That is the first fact and it stays the first fact. Light: none from above. There is no sky worth the name, only a lid of dust and exhaled lipids that the Stand has been breathing out for aeons, an atmosphere of its own manufacture. Light comes from below, and it is not light so much as temperature made visible — down in the shafts, in the vent-throats, in the seams at the root of everything, a dull cherry glow, the color of iron thinking about becoming soft. It does not illuminate. It silhouettes. You learn the Stand as a field of black shapes standing against faint red floors, and you learn the rest by hand.

## What grows

Everything you touch is a plant, and everything the plant touches, it made.

There is no soil. There is no bare mineral anywhere on the upper surface. Below the laminate is more laminate; below that, char; below that, sinter; below that, at last, at eighty or a hundred meters, the old bedrock, and the bedrock is hot, and the heat is the whole point.

The Stock — one lineage, probably, though it has forked into forms that would not recognize each other — is thermotrophic. It does not eat light. It eats *difference*. It sinks black rootwork down into the hot seam and raises a column up into the cold thin dark, and across that column runs a gradient, and out of the gradient it takes its living. Metabolism here is a heat engine in the strictest sense. Every organism is a chimney and every chimney is a stomach.

Which is why the Stand is a forest of towers, and why the towers are shaped the way they are.

They rise thirty, sixty, in a few places a hundred and forty meters, and they are not trunks. A trunk would be a waste. Each is a bundle — hundreds of thin hollow rods bound in a sheath, spaced for airflow, and at the top each rod opens into a fan of black radiating vanes, membrane-thin, veined with oil. The vanes are the coldest tissue in the world. They dump heat into the dark, and the dark takes it, and the taking is what pulls the oil up from the roots and drives the whole machine. Stand under a mature tower with your hand up and you will feel a slow steady downdraft of air that has been *used*, cooled, spent, sighing back to the floor.

Growth is upward because hunger is upward. A tower that stops climbing starts starving. Nothing here has ever had the option of being content.

## The districts

The Stand is not large. Twelve or fifteen kilometers across, a low near-flat cap on a high place, with a rim that has slumped and a middle that has filled in. You could walk it in a long day if it let you, which it will not. But it has parts, and the parts have characters.

**The Radiators** are the rim: the oldest, tallest, coldest-topped towers, standing shoulder to shoulder in a ring three hundred meters deep. Their vanes interlock overhead into a continuous black ceiling. Walking in the Radiators is walking in a colonnade with no ends, where the columns are warm to the touch at knee height and cool at shoulder height, and where the ceiling ticks — very quietly, constantly — as vanes expand and contract against each other. This is the sound of the Stand at its most restful: a fine dry ticking, like a room full of clocks in another building.

**The Combs** are the middle, and the Combs are a town. Not town-*like*. A town, in the sense that everything in it was made on purpose, to a plan, by something that had a plan. The tower-bundles here have been overgrown together into blocks — cellular, hexagonal in tendency, six to nine stories of chambers with pith walls forty centimeters thick, floors of that same laminate, doorways with lintels, stairs with a rise and run that fits a leg of some kind. Ducting runs in the walls: oil lines, hot lines, gas returns, all in grown pipe, all with valve-bodies at the junctions that open and shut on a slow schedule. There are rooms with benches. There are rooms with racks. There are rooms in which nothing has ever happened and the racks are empty and the bench has a groove worn in it by nothing.

**The Redroots** are the shafts: vertical wells, some of them ten meters wide, dropping through the laminate into the seam. This is where the light is. Stand at a Redroot mouth and you can read by it, barely, and see your hands, and see the rootwork — black cables the thickness of a body, braided and re-braided, hanging into the glow, sheathed in a white mineral bloom that the Stock sweats out to insulate itself. The heat coming up is not survivable at the lip for long. Everything within thirty meters of a Redroot is glazed.

**The Slack** is the district that failed. Six hundred hectares of collapsed Combs, folded in on themselves, being digested. Digestion without water is a slow patient chemistry of kilns and mineral-eating rot: the Stock grows small hard fruiting bodies into its own dead structure, cooks it, and reclaims the carbon. Walking the Slack, you crunch. The crust is char and shell and reclaimed fiber, and under it are voids, and the voids are hot.

**The Pan**, at the very center, is the one place nothing grows: a dead flat plate of sintered ash and unwanted metals, four kilometers across, dumped, refused, glassy, absolutely level. It is the Stand's tailings and its only horizon. It is also the only silence — an actual silence, no ticking, no sighing — and standing on it in the dark, with the black towers all around the edge and nothing overhead, is the most frightening thing the Stand offers.

## The works

Here is where it stops being a forest.

Along the inner face of the Radiators, and threading the whole of the Combs, the Stock has built an industry. Not metaphorically. Grown pressure vessels the size of houses, with grown gauges on them, dials of hardened resin with hairline needles that move. Belt drives — endless loops of woven tendon-fiber running over pulleys of dense burl, kilometers of them, running at the speed of a slow walk, oiled, nearly noiseless. Reciprocating engines with pith cylinders and glass-hard pistons, driven by the same gradient that drives everything, one stroke every eleven seconds, forever. Kilns. Sorting tables. Hoppers. Something very like a rolling mill.

The Stand is electrified, after a fashion. Oil-lymph is charged as it climbs; the towers hold potential between root and vane; and the Stock has grown conductor — a soft grey pith with metal laced through it — into busbars that run along the Comb ceilings, tapping the difference, powering the belts, powering pumps, powering the small blue-white bioluminescent nodes that flick on above a working station and off again when the work moves on. Those nodes are the 0.2 of light in the sky's absence. From a distance, at night — it is always night — the Combs blink. Sparsely. Like a city with a strict curfew and a few people who don't sleep.

And what the industry makes is this:

Fittings. Spouts. Sleeves. Flat plates with four holes. Coils of fine dark cord. Rectangular cases with hinged lids and nothing in them. Tools with grips sized for a hand that is not yours — longer, with an extra flexure. They come off the tables finished and dressed and cool, and the belts carry them to the warehouses, and the warehouses are full. They have been full for an unguessable time. Aisle after aisle of racked goods in the dark, all of it perfect, all of it dusted with exhaled lipid, all of it waiting for collection.

Nobody collects. There is no evidence anyone ever did.

The obvious reading, and probably the true one: this was a crop. Something cultivated the Stock, bred it toward manufacture, taught it the plan, and then stopped — died, left, was buried under its own product. The Stock kept the plan. Plants are very good at keeping instructions. And with no animals in the world at all — no pollinator, no browser, no scavenger, nothing with a nervous system anywhere on the plateau — the Stock has had to do its reproducing through the machinery. The belts *are* the pollination. Gametes ride the conveyors in sealed sleeves; the sorting tables are matchmakers; the hoppers broadcast propagules down the ventilation shafts of new Combs. The factory is the flower. That's the whole strangeness of the place in one sentence, and it took me a long while to see it, because I kept looking for the worker.

## The turbulence

Do not mistake ancient for still.

The plan is finished. Nothing about the Stand's *design* has changed in an age; it reached its final form and stopped, the way a shell stops, the way a crystal stops. But inside the finished form, it seethes.

A tower that falls behind on its gradient is cut off within a day — valves shut, oil rerouted, and the tower cooks itself and cracks and comes down. Something comes down somewhere in the Stand every few minutes. You hear it as a soft *whump* and a long dry sifting. Growth runs at the same tempo: a Comb chamber can be walled, floored, and ducted in a week. Belt runs are torn out and re-laid. Whole streets in the Combs close, are digested, and reopen a hundred meters to the left with the same lintels and the same worn bench-groove, because the plan says bench, and the plan is not up for discussion.

Nothing here holds still and nothing here changes. The Stand is a storm that has memorized its own shape.

## Rules

Heat is food. Cold is appetite. Height is hunger.

Nothing is wet. If you bring water, it will be found, wicked away, and sealed into a mineral nodule within a day, and the nodule will be carried to the Pan and refused.

Nothing burns. The air is too poor in oxygen and too rich in its own exhalations. Things char, smolder, crack, and slump. Fire is one more thing this world has never learned.

There is no floor that is not made. There is no wall that is not alive. There is no light that is not either temperature or a signal.

The plan is fixed and the execution is chaos and both are correct.

The goods are finished. The goods are stacked. The order was placed a very long time ago, and it is still, patiently, in the dark, at temperature, being filled.
