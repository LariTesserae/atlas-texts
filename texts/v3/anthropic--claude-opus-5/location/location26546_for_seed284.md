## The Vug

Nothing about the mountain suggests it. From outside — if there were an outside worth speaking of, if anyone stood on that ridge — you would see only a young summit, black and sharp as a broken tooth, rising through air too thin to hold a cloud. The rock is fresh. It has not had time to be softened. Its edges still cut; its faces are the faces of fracture, not of weather. Frost never forms here because there is no water to form it. The wind takes what little moisture the stone once held and does not give it back.

Somewhere below the summit, embedded in the mass of it, is a cavity the size of a modest warehouse. A vug: a bubble in the rock, lined with crystal, the kind of pocket that forms when a vein opens and does not quite close. Its walls are quartz and heavy spar, grown inward in a bristling geometry, each face a plane the length of a door, each termination a point.

The city is inside it.

---

Nothing rests on the floor. The floor is not to be trusted — it is a scree of fallen prisms and shattered growth, and it changes. Everything is hung. The builders drive pins into the seams between crystal faces and sling their structures from those pins: platforms of hammered plate, catwalks of drawn wire twisted into cable, whole tenement blocks cantilevered off a single spar and counterweighted with sacks of ore. The construction goes up the walls and over the roof, so that when you look toward the vug's apex you are looking down into another district, its chimneys pointing at you, its inhabitants going about their work upside down with no evident concern for the arrangement.

The buildings are made from what the mountain gives. Sheet copper beaten thin from vein-metal. Fired clay from the vug's own decomposed lining. Plates of muscovite mica, split into panes, glazing every window with a bronze translucency — the city has no glass but has an inexhaustible supply of the next best thing. Nothing is painted. Everything is polished, because polishing is the only cleaning available in a place without water. Surfaces are burnished with graphite until they shine like gun barrels, and the graphite comes off on everything, so that the whole population has dark hands and the whole city has the smell of a pencil.

---

The light is dim and comes from three sources, and the citizens are attentive to all three.

The first is industrial: the arc-glow of the smelting galleries, blue-white, leaking through mica shutters. There is not much air here to feed a fire, so combustion is a managed, expensive thing. The foundries burn stone to breathe — they crack oxides in retorts to liberate oxygen, and they feed that oxygen carefully into their furnaces through leather chains of bellows worked by treadmill. A single furnace consumes the labor of forty. This is why there is smoke, and why there is so little of it.

The second is thermoelectric. The mountain's interior is warm and the vug's upper reaches are cold, and the difference is harvested by junction-piles: stacked plates of bismuth and antimony wired in series, bolted into the deep seams, drawing a thin steady current that runs the filament lamps along the catwalks. These lamps are the color of a dying ember and are never bright. Eyes here are enormous and adapted; the citizens find the foundry glare painful and shutter it away.

The third is the mountain itself. Quartz under strain emits light. When the rock is loading toward a fracture, the crystal faces flicker — faint, cool, greenish, running along a seam like a fingertip. This is the city's weather forecast and its alarm system. Whole professions are built on reading it: the *seam-watchers*, who sit in unlit booths for eight-hour shifts doing nothing but looking at a wall; the *pin-callers*, who decide within seconds which platform to cut loose and which to reinforce. A district can be evacuated and dismantled in an hour. It happens perhaps twice a week.

---

Because it is quiet, you would think it slow. It is neither.

The air is too thin to carry sound well. Speech at three meters is a whisper; a hammer blow across the vug is a distant tap, absurdly small for the force behind it. The city runs at a tremendous pitch of activity and makes very little noise about it, and this discrepancy is the first thing that unsettles a newcomer: to watch a thousand figures swarming a collapsing scaffold, hauling, cutting, shouting, and to hear almost nothing.

But the *stone* is loud. Solid rock carries sound magnificently, and the citizens exploit this without romance. They talk by tapping. Every structural member is a telephone line. Every worker carries a striker on a thong and knows the codes. Press your jaw to a stanchion and the whole city floods in at once — the tick and clatter of a hundred conversations, the rhythmic thud of the treadmills, the long groan of the mountain settling, all conducted through your teeth. Newcomers are told not to do this for more than a minute at a time. It is said to be habit-forming.

---

There is life here that predates the city and will outlast it. Nothing green — there was never any question of green. The base of the ecology is a film: chemolithotrophic mats, iron-oxidizers and sulfur-reducers, that grow on the crystal faces in rusty and slate-blue washes, feeding directly on the rock. They are the reason the walls are stained. They are also the reason the walls are edible.

On the film graze the *scale-lice*: flat, armored, palm-sized, moving in slow tides across the quartz, rasping the mats away. They are farmed. They are also constantly escaping into the machinery. Above them: the *needlers*, long-legged and blind, that hunt scale-lice by vibration and can be trained, poorly, to hunt them in a specified direction. In the deep seams live the *drills*, bigger than a person's forearm, boring through fresh rock with rotary mouthparts, excreting a fine tailings-flour that the city collects and fires into brick. The drills are not domesticated. They are followed. Prospectors track them the way other people track rivers, because a drill will find a vein faster than any survey, and where it exits the rock there is often metal.

The density of animal life in the vug is remarkable. Something is always moving on any surface you look at. Louse-tides drift across the ceiling districts; needlers pick their way along the power cables; small pale mites live in the graphite dust and in the seams of clothing and are considered unworthy of comment. Every structure is a habitat. Every habitat is somebody's crop or somebody's pest, depending on the hour.

---

The rules, such as they are:

Nothing is built to last, because nothing lasts. Buildings are designed to be disassembled in units one person can carry. Bolts are preferred to rivets, pins to welds. A structure that cannot be taken down is called a *grave* and its builder is fined.

Water is not a substance but a rumor. There is a sealed vessel in the assembly hall containing perhaps two liters, recovered generations ago from a hydrated mineral pocket, and it is looked at on certain occasions. Bodies here manage on the moisture in their food. Cooking is dry-roasting. Nobody sweats.

The Cold Face is the one place the vug opens to the outside: a fissure, hand-wide, on the high northern wall, where the pressure differential howls and the temperature drops fast enough to crack copper. It is barred, and beyond the bars there is nothing to see but a slot of black and, occasionally, a star. Children are taken to look at it once. Most decide that they have now seen the outside and require no more of it.

And the mountain is still rising. That is the fact under every other fact. It gained four centimeters last year. Twice in living memory it gained a meter in a single night, and both times a third of the city came down, and both times a new vein opened in the fresh break, and the metal from that vein paid for the rebuilding. The citizens do not describe this as tragedy or as fortune. They have a word that covers both, and they use it the way other people use *harvest*.

---

If you were to hover, impossibly, in the center of the vug, you would see it whole: a room, a single closed feature, dimly lit in ember-orange and flickering green, entirely upholstered in scaffold and tenement and treadmill and hanging cable, crystal points pushing through the construction wherever the construction has not yet worked around them, and everywhere the small dark deliberate motion of hands and legs and lice and machines. And you would hear, through the thin cold air, almost nothing at all: a faint high whine of bellows, a tapping like rain, and the mountain around you, taking its time, getting bigger.
