## The Unworn Room

You are inside it before you can be outside it. That is the first rule, and the room enforces it gently, by being the only place.

Four paces by five, and low — the ceiling meets your knuckles if you raise a hand without thinking. You cannot stand up straight in the middle because a bundle of drying cord hangs there, and you cannot step aside from the cord because the corner is full of ferns, and you cannot press back from the ferns because behind you the wall weeps and you would rather not be wet through, though you already are. There is no unoccupied space here. Not "not much" — none. The air itself is an occupant: warm steam so thick it has grain and direction, moving in slow ropes, brushing the backs of your hands like something with a body.

It is hot. Nine-tenths hot. The heat comes up through the soles of your feet, through the fitted floor tiles, which are the temperature of a loaf half an hour out of the oven. Under the floor is the kiln, and under the kiln is more kiln, and the whole mountain below you — all nine unmeasured kilometres of it — is one continuous firing that has never once been let go out. You are standing on the lid of it. The mountain is not a mountain. It is a made thing, being made, and being fired as it is made, and this room is the topmost course of the work, set here to cure.

---

**Light.** There is no sun in the room and no lamp. The light comes out of the bricks.

Fire a brick hot enough and long enough and something in it keeps a coal at its heart for a while, a dull red-amber that leaks through the clay like light through the shell of an ear. Newly laid bricks glow. Older ones glow less. Courses laid a long while ago are simply dark, the colour of a wet dog.

So the walls of the room are a map of when. The east wall is bright — a fresh, uneven, orange-honey wash of light that shows every thumbprint, every grass-blade pressed into the mould, every place where the mason's palm smoothed the face. The north wall is banded: bright at the top, cooling down through brown-red, black at the base. The west wall is nearly all dark, and to see what grows there you have to lean close and let your own dim shine off the far bright wall do the work. Dim, everywhere. A light you read by touch as much as by eye.

This is also the only clock. Nothing here is old enough to be old, so the fading of the bricks is the whole of history.

---

**Sound.** It is loud in here, and the loudness is layered.

Underneath everything, the kiln: a sound like a held breath in a large throat, the low roar of draught going up through the flues of the mountain, so constant you stop hearing it and then hear it again with a lurch whenever it changes pitch, which it does, every so often, for reasons you would need to be lower down to know.

Above that: the water. The walls weep. The ceiling weeps. Steam driven out of the curing clay condenses on every surface that is cooler than the surface next to it, which is all of them in rotation, so the room is always raining somewhere. And because the whole room is made of fired ceramic of different thicknesses, every drop that lands makes a note. The floor tiles are pitched — the mason cut them that way, deliberately, tapping and shaving each one before setting it — so the drip from the ceiling boss rings a low E, and the run off the lintel patters a high broken sixth, and the seep at the west corner falls into a bowl-shaped depression and knocks out something almost like a rhythm. You are inside a struck instrument that is being played by its own sweating. It never stops and it is never quite in time.

And above *that*: the hammering. Someone is working on the other side of the west wall. Wooden mallet on wooden float, the double *tup-tup* of a brick being tapped down into its bed, the scrape of a bone-edged scraper drawing off surplus slip. It comes from outside. There *is* no outside. You will come back to this.

---

**Water.** It is everywhere and it is strange, because of the height.

Up here the air is so thin that water gives up early. The seeps in the north wall boil at the temperature of a warm bath. You can put your finger into a rolling, spitting, furiously steaming pot and find it merely pleasant. Everything in the room is therefore veiled in a violent-looking, harmless, lukewarm fog that comes and goes on a cycle of about two hundred breaths: thickening until you cannot see the far wall, until the ferns are grey silhouettes, until you are alone in a hot white nothing three feet wide — and then some shift in the kiln's draught pulls the air up and out through the ceiling-gap and the room clears all at once, streaming, every leaf beaded, every brick face polished and shining, and you can see the whole four paces by five of the world at once.

The water is perfectly clear. Not filtered-clear; *empty*-clear. It has nothing to carry. There is no silt in this world, no dust, no grit, no soil, no sand in the corners, not one flake of anything spalled off anything. Run your hand along a brick edge laid — how long ago? — and it is as sharp as the day it left the mould. Nothing has ever worn down. There is no erosion here at all, none, zero, and the absence is uncanny once you notice it: the room has no crumbs.

---

**What grows.** Lush, and obedient.

Because there is no soil, nothing roots in the ground. Plants here live in the joints. The mason lays dry, no mortar, brick tapped to brick, and every seam in the room holds a hairline of standing condensate, and in that hairline everything lives.

So the vegetation draws the architecture. Green lines the herringbone. Green outlines every course. Ferns — pale, translucent, fronds like fish-fins — hang in tiers from the horizontal joints of the north wall in perfect ranks, a hanging garden that is also a diagram of the masonry. A fig-thing has got into the lintel joint and sent its roots along the seam for two metres in both directions, flat as ribbon, and because nothing here can be split or wedged or broken open, the root simply slides and slides and never does the fig any good; it will never bring a wall down; it has nowhere to go but sideways forever. There is a sweat-lichen on the east wall, a grey-lilac bloom, that grows only where hands have touched, so the wall carries the ghost of every reach and steady and lean-against, mapped in pale fur.

Everything smells of hot wet clay and crushed fern and the faint iron ghost of copper — there is a little copper here, in the mason's tools, and the steam finds it.

---

**Who is here.** Almost nobody.

Three gutterlings in the west joint: eyeless, thumb-length, skin like wet paper, mouths permanently open under the drip. They do not move for hours and then move all at once. One has arranged itself in the low-E drip and swallows the note.

A cricket somewhere in the north wall, in a flue-gap, that keeps a beat. The mason works to it. When it stops the hammering stops, and when it starts again the hammering picks up on the second stroke, every time.

And the mason, who is not in the room. You never see more than the hands: at the sill, a wrist and a palm coming in to feel for the plumb-bob (unfired clay on a cord, hanging, and it hangs *true*, and how, at this angle, on this mountain, nobody says). The hands are broad and pale-scalded and the nails are worn to nothing — the only worn thing in the world.

---

**The sill.** The room has a doorway in the west wall, and the doorway has a threshold, and beyond the threshold there is one step down, made, fitted, swept.

And beyond that step: no step.

Not a cliff — a cliff would be a decision. What's out there is grey and it is not fog, because fog is somewhere. It has no distance in it. The eye tries to focus and finds nothing to focus on, no near, no far, no down, no scale, and after a few seconds looking at it your ears fill with a soft roar and your hands start to feel like they're the wrong size. Reach out over the step and your arm goes into it and comes back fine, unchanged, wet, and you cannot afterwards remember what your arm felt.

To go out, you must first build out. This is how the mountain gets bigger. The mason lays a brick beyond the sill, and where the brick is, there is somewhere, and where the brick isn't, there isn't. Height is not accumulated here; it is asserted, course by course, with a mallet and a cord and a plumb-bob that hangs true because the room has agreed that it should. There is no base to this mountain. Nobody has ever needed one.

---

**The model.** In the corner where the ferns are thickest, on a shelf of set brick, there is a model of the room.

It is the length of a forearm. Same herringbone. Same courses, banded bright to dark, the east wall of it glowing honey. Tiny ferns in the joints. A thread of cord hanging in the middle where you cannot stand upright. A pinhead of standing water in the west corner, boiling.

And in *its* corner, on a shelf the size of a fingernail, there is a model of the model.

If you take the mallet — the real one, the heavy one, wet-handled — and press a thumbprint into a soft brick on the model's east wall, you will hear, from somewhere above the ceiling and to the left, a single *tup*, and a warm draught, and the room's own east wall will have a thumbprint in it that you did not make and that is exactly the shape of your thumb.

The hammering behind the west wall is not the mason of *this* room.

---

**The rules, as far as they can be stated:**

Nothing here wears. Therefore nothing here has a past — only a temperature. Therefore memory does not stick to the place; it evaporates like everything else and goes up through the ceiling-gap. You will not remember this room afterwards. You will remember that there was a room.

The only record is impression: what has been pressed into soft clay and then fired. Thumbprints. Grass-blades. The whorl of a palm. That is the entire archive of the world, and it is on the walls, and it is legible, and it is dim.

Everything is made. There is no ground under the made thing. There is no stone that was not fired, no water that was not driven out of clay, no plant that does not live in a joint cut by a hand. When you look for the natural substrate you find another course of brick, and under that, the glow, and under the glow, the draught, and under the draught, the *tup-tup* of somebody still working, downward, into the grey, laying the mountain that this room is standing on top of, from the top down, which is the wrong way, and which is the only way it has ever been done.

The fog thickens again. The whole world narrows to three feet of hot white. Somewhere in it a gutterling swallows a low E. The cricket keeps time.

Press your thumb into the soft brick by the door. It will be there for a while.
