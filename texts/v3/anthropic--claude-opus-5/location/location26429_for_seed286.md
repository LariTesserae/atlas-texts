## The Shelf

Five kilometers above any sea that has a shore, a granite highland the size of a small country holds a lens of water in a basin whose rim has been worn down to a long, pale, almost-nothing ridge — a lip of stone you could walk over without noticing, if there were anyone to walk. Inside that lip: eleven hundred meters of cold clear water at the shallow edges, closer to two thousand at the center, and nowhere any beach, because the water goes right up to the ridge and stops there, level, unbothered, in a plane so exact that from the air it reads as a flaw in the rock rather than a body of water.

The air above is thin and cold and does very little. The water below is 7°C from top to bottom, everywhere, always. This is not a natural gradient. It is a maintained one.

## What grows there

The basin is full of plants and almost nothing else, and the plants are machines.

Not machines in the sense of having been made. Nothing here was made. They are machines in the sense that a heart is a pump and a bamboo is a mast — that structure and function have been ground against each other for so long that the organism has arrived at engineering, and stayed.

**Drivewood.** The largest of them. A drivewood rises from the floor of the basin as a fluted column of black-green lignified tissue, eighteen to thirty meters through at the base, tapering, ribbed longitudinally with helical reinforcement you can read like a screw thread. It is hollow. Inside the hollow are stacked chambers — hundreds of them, each with a valve of horn-hard cellulose that opens and slams — and the drivewood works these chambers off the salinity difference between the brine seeps in the granite and the fresh water above, and it moves water upward. Enormous volumes of water. A mature drivewood lifts something like forty cubic meters a second, continuously, and has done so for longer than the concept of duration is useful here.

The slamming of the valves is the loudest thing on the Shelf. Underwater, close to a drivewood, the sound is not heard but received — a 6 Hz thud that arrives in the sternum, overlaid with the grinding of the internal chambers, overlaid with a fine silver shrieking where cavitation bubbles collapse against the throat of the column. Each drivewood has its own pitch, set by its own dimensions, unchanging. There are perhaps nine hundred of them in the basin.

**Wheelweed.** Where a drivewood's uprush meets a drivewood's downwash, the water shears, and in that shear grow the wheelweeds: axles of polished white tissue, seated in cups of the same material, carrying whorls of stiff blades four to twelve meters long. They turn. Slowly, steadily, in one direction, at a rate that varies by less than a percent across a year. The blades mill their own seed against a plate at the hub; the axle's rotation is also wrung, through a twisted core of conductive fiber, into electricity.

**Sheetgrowth.** Between and below everything else hang the photosynthetic sails — single leaves, if leaf is the word, a kilometer across and forty microns thick, translucent, veined in a branching pattern that a hydraulic engineer would find familiar and a botanist would find slightly wrong. They hang vertically from float-bladders and they do not flap, because the currents are steady, and they are hung at exactly the angle at which the currents are steady. In the green half-light they look like the inside of an eyelid.

**Cable.** Vines, but their business is conduction, not support. They run in loose bundles between drivewoods, sagging in long catenaries, and inside them are strands of biologically precipitated copper — the water of the Shelf is metal-poor to the point of distilled purity because everything metallic in it was taken up and laid down in cable, ages ago. Where a bundle bends, the insulating rind is thin, and a faint field leaks out. You can taste it. Tin, and the back of a spoon.

## Spacing

And yet: this is not a jungle. The engines stand three to eight kilometers apart.

That is the strangest fact about the Shelf, and the one that takes longest to accept. Sound says *packed*. The water roars from every direction at once, a hundred-source chord with no gap in it, the acoustic signature of a foundry district. But light says *empty*. Visibility runs to sixty or seventy meters in that scoured water, and for most of the basin, most of the time, there is nothing within sixty meters. Blue-green nothing, faintly striped with the shadow-bands of far-off sheetgrowth, and one drivewood a long way off like a smokestack in fog, and the noise of a thousand more that cannot be seen at all.

Things travel a long way here and arrive undiminished. That is the whole ecology of the place: enormous separated bodies, coupled by current and sound and current-borne charge, exchanging across distances that would be absurd on land.

## Fauna

There is a great deal of animal life and it is all employed.

**Fitters** are the commonest — flattened, armored, palm-sized, with two pairs of scraping mouthparts and a tolerance for being crushed. They live in the seats of the wheelweed axles and in the valve throats of the drivewood, eating the biofilm that would otherwise foul the bearing surfaces. A wheelweed axle that lost its fitters would seize within a season. It has never happened.

**Silverswarm** — small, schooling, ten thousand to a shoal — grazes the leakage nodes on the cable bundles, feeding on the mineral crust that electrolysis lays down there. The shoals hang in the field like iron filings, all oriented the same way, and when a node discharges they flinch as one body with a sound like gravel thrown at a window.

**Ballast beasts** are the big grazers: eight meters, slab-sided, lung-less, with a gut full of swallowed granite for trim. They browse the edges of the sheetgrowth in long slow arcs, and they crop it at precisely the rate at which it extends. They are not managing anything. They simply cannot eat faster, and it cannot grow slower.

And there are the **listeners** — eyeless, pale, fist-headed, propelled by a single sculling fin — which navigate solely by the chord. Every drivewood's pitch is fixed and unique; the interference pattern at any point in the basin is therefore also fixed and unique; a listener knows where it is the way you know which room of your house you are standing in. They are the only creatures here that could be called lost, and only if the noise stopped, and the noise does not stop.

## The rule

Nothing on the Shelf is changing.

Not slowly. Not imperceptibly. The system arrived at closure some vast time ago and has been sitting in it since, and the closure is exact.

The drivewood valves abrade at their seats, and the tissue at the seats regrows, and the two rates match to the width of a cell, so that each valve seat is a worn groove that has been *the same worn groove* for ten thousand years. The granite floor is worn — rounded, loafed, the whole basin floor a landscape of pale bread — but it has finished being worn, because the currents that would erode it are the currents the drivewoods maintain, and they scour only where they have already scoured everything scourable. The rim ridge is low and soft-shouldered and will not get lower. The sheetgrowth extends and is eaten. The fitters breed and are crushed. When a drivewood dies — it happens; something like once a century — a seed germinates in the cavity where its root plate was, in the exact hydraulic shadow the parent occupied, and grows to the parent's exact dimensions, and comes up to the parent's exact pitch, and the chord is unaltered.

There are days and nights. The light comes down in the ordinary way, greened by the sheets, banded and moving. It makes no difference to anything. The engines run on salt and rock-heat, not sun; the sheetgrowth banks its surplus and spends it at a constant rate regardless. Noon and midnight, the basin does the same work at the same volume in the same tone of voice. There is weather up top — mist, sometimes a hard cold rain that drums the surface flat — and the water below does not register it.

So there is no history here, and no ruin, and no promise. The Shelf is not old the way a forest is old, accumulating. It is old the way an equation is old.

## The surface

Worth going up, once.

The tops of the drivewoods break the plane — nine hundred of them scattered across a sea the size of a province, each an island of bare ribbed wood ten or fifteen meters across, wet, dark, no leaves, faintly warm. Each vents. The lifted water arrives at the crown and is discharged in a low continuous fountain that in this cold thin air fogs immediately, so that every drivewood stands under its own permanent plume, leaning downwind, and the basin from above looks precisely like a working industrial plain: nine hundred stacks smoking on a flat grey floor, no roads between them, no roads anywhere, nothing that was ever built.

The plumes merge downwind into a low cloud deck that sits over the eastern third of the basin and rains there, always, on the same forty thousand square kilometers, and that rain returns to the water it came from.

Stand on a crown. Below the soles of your feet, through fifteen meters of wood, the valves are slamming. The noise up here is only a great steady thunder with a whistle in it, dulled by the air, going on and on with no gathering and no release.

There is one thing on the Shelf that looks made. At the base of an old wheelweed near the northern rim, the axle has, over an unguessable span, worn a shaft down into the granite — round, forty centimeters, plumb, walls polished to a gloss, going down eleven meters and ending in a hemisphere. It is a drill hole. It is not a drill hole. It is the only feature in the whole basin that suggests intention, and it was produced by a plant turning slowly in the dark, in balance, for no reason, forever.
