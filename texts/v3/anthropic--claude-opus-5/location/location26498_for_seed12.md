## The Shine and the Warm Stratum

Begin with the fact that decides everything else: here, the sky is water.

Not rain, not fog, not flood in the sense of something that came and will go. At this altitude the atmosphere simply gives up being air and becomes a lake — a warm, still, faintly green body of it, kilometres deep, filling the whole highland basin and standing several hundred metres above the highest stone. Look up and you see the underside of it: a silver ceiling, wrinkling very slowly, called the Shine. That is where the light comes from, and by the time it has waded down through all that water to the rooftops it is dim as an hour after sunset, and the colour of weak tea held to a window.

Nothing falls here. That is the second fact, and it follows from the first.

---

**The rock.**

The mountain is new. You can tell without being told, because it has not yet learned any of the shapes that age teaches. There are no valleys, no fans of gravel, no beaches, no soil. What there is instead is a landscape of bulges: pillow after pillow of basalt, extruded slow as toothpaste from vents in the mountain's flanks, each one swelling until its skin froze and the next one budded out of its side. Loaves. Sacks. Piled bread. They are black and glassy on the outside where the water quenched them, and when you break one — you can break one with a heel — the interior is grey and full of frozen bubbles, light as pumice, still faintly warm at the core.

The glass rind is the only sharp thing in the world. It flakes into splinters that will open a hand to the bone. It is the local flint, the local steel, the local everything.

There is no sand. The mountain has not yet learned how to make sand. Without current, without surf, without frost or rain or running streams, nothing here grinds anything else down. Erosion has not begun; it is merely scheduled. The edges you see are the edges the rock cooled into, three or four or eleven generations ago, and they are still that sharp.

And the mountain is *rising*. Slowly — a hand's breadth in a lifetime, sometimes more when the deep vents open and the ground swells like a held breath. Everything in this place drifts upward toward the Shine, at the speed of geology, whether it consents or not.

---

**The layers.**

Because the water never moves, it stacks. The heat comes from below — from the vent fields, from the young rock still shedding the fever of its making — and so the basin is stratified into panes: a scalding, mineral-bitter band right at the vents, then a broad warm stratum like bathwater, then cooler, then cold, then the last chilled sheet just beneath the Shine where light is strongest and warmth is least.

You can see the boundaries. Cross one and it shivers, bends the view of whatever is on the other side, and closes behind you like a curtain. Children are taught to read the panes the way children elsewhere are taught to read weather.

The town lives in the warm stratum. It has no choice. That is the habitable band — thirty or forty metres of thickness, wrapping the mountain's shoulder like a belt — and everything human is inside it.

---

**The forest.**

The warm stratum is crowded. Vegetation here grows *up*, and it grows with the enthusiasm of something with no wind to fear and no drought ever.

The dominant plants are the bellvines and the lung-fruit — coarse, rubbery, dark-olive stems that root in cracks in the pillow basalt and rise in ropes, branching, matted, layered so thickly that the town is not built in a clearing so much as inside a thicket that has been persuaded. Their leaves are broad and thin as a fingernail paring, and they hang absolutely still. Between them grow finer things: rust-red feather weed; a pale moss that fluoresces very faintly where the light is thinnest, so the deepest lanes have a suggestion of green frost on their walls; and the tongue-lilies, a metre long, translucent, that clench shut if you touch them and take a full day to open again.

The lung-fruit are the reason there are people here at all. Each is a bladder the size of a heart, and each is a small factory of breathable gas. You bite the stem, you hold the fruit to your mouth, and it will keep you for the better part of a morning; then it goes limp and you hang it back on the vine and it slowly fills again. Whole terraces of them are tended, pruned, argued over, inherited. The forest is not scenery. It is the lung of the settlement, and the settlement is a growth inside the forest, the way a wasp gall is a growth inside an oak.

Above the warm stratum the plants thin out and stop. Below, in the scalding band, nothing grows but white mats of something that is not quite a plant.

---

**The town.**

Perhaps four thousand people, in a long crescent on the mountain's shoulder, stacked in five or six shelves that follow the contours of the pillow flows.

The building is dry-stone, because nothing that sets in air will set here — the masons cut and fit and balance, and they are very good at it, and the walls have a swollen, hand-laid look, black basalt blocks with their glassy rinds turned outward so the walls glitter dully when you pass with a lamp of glow-moss. Roofs exist, but not for rain. Roofs are inverted: domes, upturned bowls, made to *trap*. Every house has an air-bell in the ceiling, a pocket the size of a barrel or a room, filled a bladderful at a time over years and jealously maintained, and to enter a rich family's home is to climb up into an actual room of actual air where the walls run with condensation and everything smells of wet stone and breath.

Inside the air-bells, and only there, fire is possible.

This is the whole ceiling of their technology and the reason it is where it is. They have obsidian blades better than most of the world's steel and nothing to make steel with; you cannot smelt underwater, cannot fire a pot, cannot bake, cannot kiln, cannot burn lime. So the town is a woven town: baskets, not crockery; lashings, not nails; nets, cordage, plaited mats weighted at the corners with stones so they will lie flat. Every object here is either stone, bone, or braid. The only fires are the hearth-bells, one per quarter, each about the size of a cottage, tended by two families in rotation, used for hardening spear points, drying cord, and the slow reduction of vent minerals into pigment. A hearth-bell going out is the largest disaster the town's history records.

They walk. This is worth saying: they do not swim much. In the warm stratum, a person weighted with a stone belt walks the lanes at a heavy, dreamlike, low-gravity pace, feet finding the polished tops of the pillows, hair standing up off the scalp like smoke. Nobody hurries. Warmth and buoyancy conspire against hurry.

---

**Sound.**

The place is almost silent, and its silence is loud in a peculiar way, because water carries what little there is enormously far and fast.

You cannot speak. Voices come out as a smeared groan. So the language is percussive: knuckle-taps on stone, clicks made in the throat, a dense sign-vocabulary of the hands, and for distance, the tapstones — pillars of dense basalt set at the corner of each shelf, struck with a bone mallet in patterns that anyone in the basin can hear and read. Two taps and a scrape: *come up*. A long roll: *the vent field is breathing*.

Under all of it, always, is the ticking. The young rock is still cooling, and cooling glass cracks. It is a faint, continuous, granular tinkling from every direction at once, like a room full of people quietly unwrapping something. Nobody notices it until they are old, and then some of them cannot stop hearing it.

---

**Fauna.**

Sparse to the point of ceremony. There is one fish — a blunt, pale, slow-swimming thing the length of a forearm, whose ancestors arrived by some route no longer available and which now exists in modest numbers and is not eaten, out of a superstition nobody defends. There are the eel-children, blind, finger-thin, living in the pumice bubbles of broken pillows. There are shrimp-like grazers on the vent mats, harvested with hand-nets by people who go down into the scalding band for a count of forty and come back up boiled pink. There are, in the air-bells, flies. Nobody knows how the flies got there either. They are considered lucky and are not swatted.

And there is the upward snow. Plant debris — shed leaf-parings, spent lung-fruit, the general litter of a forest — does not settle. It rises. All day, always, a thin drift of pale flecks goes up past the rooftops, and up, and up, and accumulates against the underside of the Shine in a soft ceiling-sediment, a beach hanging over the world.

---

**The rules, plainly stated.**

Everything rises. Refuse rises. Bubbles rise. The dead rise, unless you weight them, and the weighting of the dead is the central moral question of the culture: to hold a grandmother down among her family under a cairn, or to cut her belt and let her go up to the Shine, where the light is, where the drift-beach is, where — the priests say, and nobody has been able to check — the water finally thins out and becomes sky again, and you break through into air on the other side, once, permanently.

And the mountain rises too. The uppermost shelf of the town is now only ninety metres beneath the Shine, and the cold pane has come down over it, and the lung-fruit will no longer fruit there. Those houses have been abandoned upward: emptied, roofs opened, air let out, left standing in the pale cold light as a kind of monument to a direction. In four or five hundred years the summit shrine will breach. There are songs about this — tapped, not sung — that describe it as a birth, and others that describe it as a death, and the town has never decided, and does not have to, and won't.

Meanwhile it is warm. The light is the colour of weak tea. The vines hang absolutely still, and the glass ticks, and someone across the basin strikes a tapstone twice, and everyone stops for a moment to listen, and then goes back to their braiding.
