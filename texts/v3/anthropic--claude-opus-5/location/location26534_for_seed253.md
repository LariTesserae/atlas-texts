## The Roofed Sea

Begin with the mountains, because they are the reason for everything, and they are almost gone.

They were a range once, the kind that makes weather and breaks continents. Time took them the way water takes a sugar cube: not violently, just continuously. What remains is a shield of old rock six thousand metres above any sea that has a shore, a plateau some sixty kilometres across, worn so flat that a marble set down at its centre would take a week to decide which way to roll. Around its edge stand the stumps of the summits, none higher now than a hill, rounded, patient, done.

The plateau holds water. Nine hundred metres of it at the middle, thinning to shallows at the rim. Cold water, four or five degrees, the temperature at which water is heaviest and least interested in moving. It has been here long enough that the word *lake* has stopped applying. It is a sea by behaviour if not by size: it has currents, a deep, a benthic province, an accent of its own.

And it has a lid.

---

## The Ceiling

The lid is alive. Seen from above, if anything ever saw it from above, it would be a plain of dark felted green, unbroken, faintly domed, stretching from stump to stump. Standing on it you would not sink. You would feel it flex, a few centimetres, like the skin over a shoulder.

Underneath, it is the ceiling of the world. Two to four metres thick, laminated: the top handspan photosynthetic and busy, the rest a compressed history of itself, dead tissue pressed into a felt so dense that water moves through it only by argument. It is the floor of a plant and the sky of a sea.

There is no sky above it either, not really. The plateau sits in a standing wave of weather, an orographic storm that has been parked here since the mountains were tall enough to make it. Cloud all the way down to the felt. Rain that falls as sleet and lands as slush and is worked into the mat within a day. The storm has no season and no direction and no end. It is not an event. It is a condition, like the temperature.

So there is no light. Not none-none; a thin grey bleeds through the thinnest patches of ceiling at the rim, enough to keep a stripe of green algae going along the shallows, enough that the water there is black-with-a-hint rather than black. Everywhere else, past the first kilometre inward, the darkness is complete and has been complete for longer than any lineage of eyes could hold out. Eyes here are vestigial or absent or repurposed into pressure organs. The dark is not a lack. It is simply the medium, like the cold.

---

## The Columns

The ceiling would sag and drown itself under its own weight. It does not, because it is held up.

Every few hundred metres, a column rises from the floor to the roof. They are the stems. Each is between two and nine metres thick at the base, tapering hardly at all, running the full nine hundred metres up before flaring into a bell that fuses seamlessly with the underside of the mat. They are made of woven fibre laid down in helices, sheathed in a bark that a hundred centuries of slow current have polished to the smoothness of a river stone. Old columns have grazing bands scoured into them at the heights where the grazers prefer to work, so each one carries a striped record of appetite.

Inside, they are hollow. The lumen carries gas upward, generated at the roots in the anoxic felt below, and this is what makes the ceiling buoyant: a plant that inflates its own sky. Sap comes back down the walls, dark and sweet, feeding the floor.

They are sparse. That is the first thing you would understand about this place if you were in it. From the base of one column you cannot detect the next by any means but hearing. The space between them is enormous, black, cool, and almost empty of solid matter. Ninety-nine hundredths of this world is open water with nothing in it but the fall.

---

## The Fall

The ceiling sheds. Continuously, everywhere, at a rate that has not measurably changed in geologic time: fragments of dead upper tissue, spent cells, the sloughed-off bottom laminate of the mat. It drifts down through nine hundred metres of still cold water and takes about a day and a half to arrive.

This is the snow, and it is the entire economy. Nothing below the ceiling photosynthesises. Everything below the ceiling is waiting for the fall or eating something that waited.

It lands on the floor, which is not rock and has not been rock for a very long time. The floor is felt: the same material as the ceiling, deposited from underneath, compressed by its own accumulation into a peat kilometres deep that has swallowed the old topography of the plateau entirely. Ridges, cirques, moraines, all of it drowned in plant. The top few centimetres are soft and dark and crawling. Below that it goes anoxic and preservative and holds everything that ever fell into it, in order, unrotted, forever.

The whole system balances. Growth at the top equals shedding equals burial at the bottom. It has balanced for so long that balance is no longer a process here; it is the shape of the thing.

## The Sound

This is the part that surprises.

You would expect a dark, cold, sparse, unchanging place to be quiet. It is not. It is *loud*, all the time, at a volume that would be uncomfortable in air and is merely total in water.

The storm drums the ceiling. Sixty kilometres of taut living membrane, hammered by sleet at a rate of millions of impacts a second, coupling that energy directly into the water beneath. The result is a broadband roar, a white sound with a floor to it, felt in the chest as much as heard.

And the columns ring. Each one is a tube of specific length and diameter, driven at its top by the drumming of the roof, and each one answers at its own pitch, a note in the deep register, sustained without pause. There are perhaps eight hundred columns. There are therefore eight hundred notes, and because the columns grow at a rate that changes their pitch by a fraction of a semitone per millennium, the chord is constant within any lifetime and within any thousand lifetimes.

The sea is a chord. It has been the same chord since before the animals in it had their present shapes. Sound travels a kilometre and a half a second here, so the far rim is thirty seconds away by ear and the whole sixty-kilometre bowl is audibly present at every point within it. Nothing can be seen and everything can be heard. The place is sparse to the body and crowded to the ear.

## The Fauna

Abundant, and organised entirely around the two facts: the fall comes down, and the chord tells you where you are.

At the floor: acres of small pale scavengers working the fresh snow, sifting, tunnelling the top layer, never breaching the anoxic line. Flat blind grazers by the million on the column bark, rasping the bacterial film that grows on the sap-sweating surface, migrating up and down the stem on schedules governed by nothing but their own bellies. Long-bodied hunters that patrol the column-floor junction, where the felt heaps into a soft skirt full of burrows.

In midwater: netters. They hang from the ceiling on threads of secreted fibre, twenty or fifty metres of it, and spread mucus nets to intercept whatever swims up. They are sessile, patient, and everywhere, though widely spaced, one every hundred metres or so, so that the open water between columns is not empty but very thinly furnished.

And the drummers. Large, slow, three metres or so, with a thickened plate on the head. They strike the columns. A drummer swims up to a stem, sets itself, and hits it, once, hard, and the column's note swells and the whole sea hears it and the drummer listens to the reflections come back off the other seven hundred and ninety-nine. This is how they navigate, and how they find each other, and how they announce. It is the closest thing to a tool in this world and it is not a tool; it is a jaw and a habit. On any given day some hundreds of these strikes ring out, and they are the only events in an eventless place.

## Rules

One: the entire structure, ceiling and columns and floor, is a single organism. One clone, one genome, propagating by extension for a length of time that does not resolve into years. Everything here that is *made* was made by it, and it made nothing that was not itself. There is no stone, no shell, no built thing that is not tissue. To be in this sea is to be inside a plant, in a cavity it maintains at a stable temperature, holding open with pillars, roofing against the storm, snowing food into.

Two: nothing changes. A column falls perhaps once a century; the crash is heard everywhere and remembered by nobody; the ceiling above it sags into a shallow bowl, and over three or four hundred years a new stem climbs and takes up the load and the bowl lifts and the chord readmits its missing note. That is the fastest thing that ever happens here.

Three: at the rim, where the felt thins and the grey comes through, there is a band a hundred metres wide where the water is almost bright and the animals are denser and stranger and some of them still have eyes. Everyone who lives out in the black knows the rim exists, because it is where the chord goes muddy, damped by the shallows.

Four: this is the finished state. Not a stage. The mountains are done wearing down, the plant is done spreading, the storm is done arriving. Whatever happens next will not happen for a long time, and will not be an improvement, and will be heard by everything at once.
