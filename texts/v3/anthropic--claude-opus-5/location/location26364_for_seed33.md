## The Equilibrium City

Begin with the altitude, because everything else is a consequence of it.

The range stands above the layer where weather is possible. Below, at a depth you can measure only by the ache in your ears, there is a floor of white — not cloud, not water, but a suspension of powdered feldspar held aloft by the thermal column, a dry sea that rolls in slow lobes and never rains. Above that floor: nothing but the peaks. Two hundred kilometres of them, give or take, though the count changes and we will come to that. They rise out of the white like the fingers of something buried past the wrist, and their upper thirds are city.

The sky at this height is not blue. It is a deep bruise-violet at the zenith going almost black, with stars showing through all day — hard, unblinking, too many of them. The sun is present but reduced: a small white coin that gives light the way a lamp in the next room gives light. Everything here is lit at the level of a corridor at four in the morning. Shadows are soft-edged and long and pool in the streets like spilled ink. You can see clearly, but you never feel you have seen enough.

## Nobody built it

This is the first thing to understand and the hardest.

The stone is a feldspathic material with an orthogonal cleavage habit — it splits at right angles, always, at every scale, from the grain to the cliff. Add three hundred million years of thermal cycling in a dry, thin, ferociously clean atmosphere. Add wind that carries abrasive dust in one prevailing direction for the first hundred million years and the other for the second. Add no water, therefore no rounding, no dissolution, no softening of any kind.

What you get is architecture.

Not architecture-like. Architecture. Streets four metres wide, running straight for a kilometre and then turning ninety degrees. Stairs — thousands of flights of them, riser and tread within a centimetre of comfortable, because the bedding planes are that thick and the joint spacing is that regular. Doorways with lintels. Colonnades where a resistant dyke has stood while the softer rock between it retreated in equal columns. Window openings, square, at head height, opening onto four hundred metres of nothing. Plazas. Balustrades. Something on the eastern face of the third peak that is, to any honest eye, a flight of temple steps leading up to a portico leading into a room with a raised dais at the far end.

There were no builders. There were no tools. There has never been a hand here. The rock simply has this shape, the way a snowflake has six arms, and it has had it for so long that the shape has stopped changing.

That last part matters. The erosion is not finished; it is *settled*. Every day the wind removes some quantity of grains from the windward faces and deposits an equal quantity in the lee. Every night the cold cracks a flake loose and every morning the dust cements a flake back. The city is in exact equilibrium — endlessly renovated, permanently unaltered. Nothing here is being destroyed. Nothing is being made. The matter moves and the form holds, the way a flame holds while the gas passes through it.

The stone is cool to the touch. Not cold. Cool at almost exactly the temperature of a cellar, of the inside of your own mouth on a winter morning, everywhere, at all hours, on the sunward and shadowed faces alike, at the summit and in the deep interiors. This is not physically reasonable. It is simply the case.

## Empty

The city has the density of a lung.

You can stand in a plaza the size of a village and be the only vertical thing in it. The next district is on the next peak, and the next peak is nine kilometres away across open air, and between them there is nothing whatsoever — not a bird, not a wire, not a wisp. The gap is so clean that the far district looks painted on. You can read the joints in its stairs from here.

And the mountains are hollow. Exfoliation has been working from the inside as well, along cooling joints, so that the peaks are shelled: chamber inside chamber inside chamber, each one bigger than a cathedral and containing a floor of undisturbed dust, a ceiling lost in dimness, and nothing else at all. The chambers connect by square passages. Some of them open to the sky at the top and hold a single shaft of that thin sunlight, standing in the dark like a pillar you could lean on. Sound in these rooms does not echo. It should. It doesn't.

## Life, such as it is

On the permanently shaded faces there is a crust. Grey-green, mineral-thin, growing perhaps a millimetre a century, feeding on light and the trace of nitrogen the wind carries up from the powder sea. It is not a plant. It is not really a lichen. It is something older and simpler than the categories, a chemistry that has learned to copy itself and never bothered with anything further. In the deep interiors it goes pale and glows faintly — a cold buttermilk luminescence, just enough to see the shape of a room by, which is the only reason the inner chambers are not absolutely black.

And there are the grazers.

You will see one perhaps twice in a week of walking. They are flat, roughly a metre across, the colour of wet slate, with an underside of fine plates like the gills of a mushroom. They move by peristalsis along walls and stair-treads at the speed of a minute hand. They do not eat the crust.

They eat sound.

Whatever they are, whatever they are made of, they take vibration out of stone and air and metabolise it. The plates ripple when they feed. Around a feeding grazer there is a bubble of perhaps twenty metres in which noise simply fails: you can strike two rocks together and feel the impact in your wrist bones and hear nothing at all, not even a click, not even the absence sounding like an absence.

Between them, over three hundred million years, they have grazed this range down to near-silence. What remains is a hush with texture. There is a dry sift of dust moving over stone, a sound like someone turning a page in the next room, constant, from every direction. There is a very low tone, felt more than heard, when the thermal column below shifts. There is your own blood, which becomes loud, and then intolerable, and then, after a few days, ordinary.

## The rules thin out

Above a certain contour — and you can find it, there is a line where the crust changes colour — the world becomes imprecise about itself.

It does not tear or flash or scream. It relaxes. Distinctions that are load-bearing lower down are simply not maintained up here, the way a language stops conjugating tenses it no longer needs.

**The peaks cannot be counted.** Count them left to right: twenty-three. Count them right to left: nineteen. Count them twice the same way and you will get two answers and both counts will have felt scrupulous. There is no correct number. The question is malformed and the landscape declines it politely.

**Places recur.** There is a plaza with a broken corner on the fourth step, a particular ring-shaped stain on the flagstone, a lintel with a fracture through it like a lightning fork. You will find it on the second peak. You will find it again, forty kilometres away, on a peak you reached by walking in a straight line for two days. It is not a copy. The chip is the same chip. If you leave a mark in the dust in one, it is in the other when you get there, aged by exactly the time it took you to walk. The city has fewer places than it has locations.

**Shadows are early.** Yours precedes you around corners by a second or so. If you stop suddenly it goes on a little way and comes back. It is not alive and it is not trying to communicate. It is a rendering error in a world that has stopped bothering to synchronise.

**Falling is slow and negotiable.** Step off a four-hundred-metre balustrade and you will descend at something like the speed of settling dust, turning gently, with plenty of time to reconsider. Reconsideration works. Reach out for a ledge that is nine metres away and it will be within reach, because the distinction between *nine metres* and *within reach* is one of the ones that stopped being maintained. Nobody has ever hit the powder sea. It's not clear the powder sea is a place you can arrive at.

**Duration decouples from distance.** A street can take one step or an afternoon. Neither is a shortcut or a delay; there is simply no reliable exchange rate up here between how far and how long. Your body keeps its own honest accounts — hunger, fatigue, the growth of a fingernail — and these do not match the sun, which crosses the violet sky at a rate that varies and sometimes stops for a while at the meridian, dimming slightly, as though thinking.

**Names slide off.** Whatever you decide to call the peaks, you will find within a day or two that you have forgotten which one you meant, and that the names have quietly redistributed themselves onto other summits. The city does not accept labels. It has an enormous, patient resistance to being referred to.

## The processes

For all its emptiness the place is busy.

Twice a day the thermal column below inhales and exhales, and dust rides up the couloirs in long grey plumes that pour over the cols and run down the streets on the far side — dry rivers, ankle-deep, moving with a slow braided current exactly like water and nothing at all like water, silent, warm-cool, parting around your boots and closing again. They fill the plazas to a certain depth and then drain through the square passages into the hollow interiors, where they lie in flat grey lakes for a few hours before the evening reversal lifts them out again.

At the terminator — the moving line where the dim sunlight leaves a face — the rock ticks. Cooling contraction, tiny fractures, a sparse irregular pattering, the only genuinely loud thing here, and it is quieter than rain on a roof. The line moves across a peak over about an hour and the ticking moves with it, and if you stand still and let it pass over you, you will hear the whole mountain speak once, faintly, and then stop.

The crust grows. The grazers graze. The equilibrium holds, grain for grain, flake for flake.

And the city, which no one built and no one has ever lived in and which cannot be counted, named, mapped, or entirely left, continues to be exactly the shape it has always been, in the cool and the dimness, several kilometres above anything that could reasonably be called the world.
