## The Basin of the Weeping Wall

There is one way in, and it is the way the water goes out: a slot gorge, waist-wide at the bottom, that climbs three kilometres of switchback ledge from the tree line below. You hear the place long before you see it. At first it is nothing, a hush like blood in the ears. Then it becomes weather — a pressure against the sternum, a sense of an approaching train that never arrives. By the last two hundred metres the sound has stopped being sound and become a medium, something you are wading in, and your own shout is a small dry click somewhere behind your teeth, conducted through the bones of your skull.

Then the gorge opens and you are standing on the lip of a bowl four kilometres across, at an altitude where the sky is the colour of a struck match, and everything in front of you is white, wet, and unbearably bright.

## The Headwall

The far side of the basin is a cirque wall seven hundred metres high, and it is bleeding.

Above it, out of sight, lies a plateau — two hundred square kilometres of fractured rock that behaves as a single enormous sponge. Snow falls there, and mist condenses there, and none of it runs off in channels; it goes down into the joints and travels sideways and finds the cliff. So the wall does not carry a river. The wall *is* the river, delaminated across its whole face: sheets and veils and hair-fine jets, punch-holes where the head pressure is high enough to throw water out horizontally for ten metres before gravity remembers it, long dark seep-stains that never dry, and twelve great spouts, each the thickness of a man's body, that leave the rock without touching it again until the floor.

Those twelve are the bass. Everything else — the ten thousand smaller mouths, the hiss of atomised spray, the drumming on the terraces — is the middle and the top. Together they make a broadband white roar with no attack and no decay, a wall of noise so complete that it has no direction in it. You cannot tell where the loudest part is. You cannot tell whether a sound came from your left. There is no such thing as *behind you* here.

The output at the gorge is constant. Not seasonally constant — constant. Snowmelt above is buffered by two decades of transit through the rock, so the plateau breathes out at the same rate in April as in October, in a wet century as in a dry one. Whatever is happening on the surface of the world, the wall bleeds at the same pressure.

## The Column Field

The floor of the bowl is packed. This is the thing that takes longest to accept: there is no clearing anywhere, no plaza, no open ground. It is a forest of stone columns standing shoulder to shoulder, and moving through it is a matter of turning sideways, of choosing between one gap and another and being wrong.

They are travertine — calcite laid down out of solution — grown over cores of dark volcanic rock. The shortest are hip-height stumps with terraced tops; the tallest go up forty metres and lean into each other and fuse, so that in places the field becomes fins, screens, colonnades, arcades with roofs. The material is pale bone-white where it is young, grey where it is old, and streaked with ochre and rust wherever the water has come through iron. It is porous as coral and it rings hollow when struck, though you will not hear it ring.

Every surface is wet. Not running, not submerged — *wet*, permanently, a film half a millimetre thick over everything, refreshed faster than it can evaporate. The tops of the columns are stepped with rimstone: hundreds of little dams holding pools the size of a cupped hand, each pool spilling into the next, the whole thing a staircase of overflow. Underfoot the same terracing runs across the floor between the columns in shallow scallops you can walk in ankle-deep. There is nowhere in the basin to sit down dry.

## The Accounting

Here is what makes the place strange, though it takes a while to notice, because it looks like nothing at all.

The columns grow. Water arrives at their tops loaded with dissolved lime, degasses in the thin air, and drops calcite — perhaps a millimetre a year at the crown. The columns are also eaten. The water on the floor, fresh from the wall and hungry with carbon dioxide, dissolves their bases at almost exactly the rate the crowns accumulate. Almost is not the word. *Exactly.* The rates are locked to each other by the chemistry of the incoming water and the fixed pressure of the fixed air at this fixed altitude, and the whole field has been standing in the same shapes, to within the thickness of a fingernail, for longer than there is any way to say.

Which means the stone here is not an object. It is a pattern that mineral is currently passing through — a standing wave, the way a hump in a river is a standing wave. The atoms in the column beside you were dissolved rock in the plateau twenty years ago and will be silt in the lowlands in ninety thousand. The shape has not moved.

You see this most clearly in the small things. On the north side of a particular fin, in a thumbprint hollow, there hangs a bead of water that does not fall. It is fed from above at precisely the rate it loses to the air, and the surface tension holds the rest. It is the same bead your grandmother's grandmother would have seen, if anyone had come here, which no one did. The water in it is four minutes old. The bead is older than the species looking at it.

## Sound, and the Quiet Places

The roar has structure. It cannot help having structure: an invariant source in an invariant geometry produces standing waves, and the basin is full of them.

Scattered through the column field, perhaps forty or fifty of them, are nodes — pockets of destructive interference where the sound simply falls out. They are small. Most are the size of a coffin standing on end. You find them by accident: you edge through a gap between two fins and the world goes quiet, not silent but *domestic*, a murmur like a distant motorway, and you can hear your boot squeak on the wet rock, and your own breathing, and a fine high ringing that is your ears complaining. Lean six inches to the left and the roar slams back into you like a shoulder.

The nodes do not move. They are pinned to the geometry, and the geometry does not change. They are as permanent a feature of this landscape as the columns are — rooms made of nothing, in a place with no rooms.

## Light

At this elevation the air is a third gone and the sun is a hole. The upper basin holds a permanent spray-mist to a height of about fifteen metres above the floor, and below and through that mist the light does something intolerable: it comes from everywhere. Down from the sky, sideways from the mist, and up — hard, up — off four kilometres of wet white calcite. There are no shadows here. Not soft shadows: none. Nothing has a dark side. Which means nothing has volume, and you will misjudge every distance you attempt, reaching for a rim that is a metre further off than it looks, stepping down onto a floor that isn't there yet.

You cannot look at it with an open eye for long. Around midday the mist throws a full circular rainbow, no horizon to cut it, and it sits low enough that you walk through the arc of it and the colours run over your hands.

At night the glare goes out and the stars come into the bowl and the roar does not change by a decibel. The rim above the mist-line ices with rime in the small hours and sheds it by mid-morning: the one oscillation in the place, and it nets to zero.

## What Lives Here

Nothing green. Not a smear, not a stain, not a green film in the pools — and this is not for want of water or light, which are the two things in surplus. It is that everything which settles on this rock is entombed. Dust, spores, pollen blown up from a hundred kilometres of lowland, a moth's wing, a flake of your skin: within days the calcite plates over it and it becomes a small white lump in the banding. The rock is a lithified archive of arriving debris, and nothing gets a second week to take root. The ultraviolet, on the surfaces that face up, would handle whatever the mineral missed.

So the living things here are all commuters and scavengers, and there are three of them that you will see.

Small sickle-winged birds, sooty dark, nest in the splash zone directly behind the water curtains on the headwall — the only place in the basin that is sheltered, if you can call it that. They fly out above the rim to feed on the aerial drift that rises from the valleys, and come back through the falling water like thrown stones. They do not call. Whether they have given up calling or never had it is unclear.

In the rimstone pools, if you crouch and shade the water with your body and wait for your eyes, there are translucent shrimp two millimetres long, blind, grazing the incoming dust off the calcite before the calcite closes over it. They are in perhaps one pool in twenty. They have no way out of the pool they are in.

And once or twice a season a large animal comes up the gorge by mistake, stands in the light and the noise for a while, and goes back down.

## Rules

Nothing here was made. There are colonnades and stairways and arcades and long fluted walls with the look of masonry gone soft, and all of it is precipitate. The absence is loud, in its way: a landscape wearing the exact shape of a ruined city, containing not one worked edge, not one tool, not one cairn.

Nothing here is happening. The whole basin is at rest at full volume — maximum flow, maximum sound, maximum light, zero change. This is difficult to hold in the mind for long. The body reads roar and spray as *event*, as something in progress that will resolve, and the place refuses to resolve, and after two hours of it something in you starts looking for the exit.

And nothing here notices you, with one exception. On the way out, in the soft new rim of a gour pool near the gorge mouth, you will have left a bootprint. It will not erode. In a month it will be sealed under a skin of calcite, and after that it will be in the record — a small foreign flaw in an accounting that has otherwise balanced perfectly since before there was anyone to balance it.

Then the gorge, and the descent, and somewhere around four hundred metres down the sound finally releases you, and the silence comes in like an injury, and you will hear the ringing in your ears for three days.
