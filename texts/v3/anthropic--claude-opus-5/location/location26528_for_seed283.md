## The Terrace at Two in the Morning

Start with the heat, because it is the oldest fact here and the last one to leave.

It is thirty-three degrees at two in the morning and it has been thirty-three degrees, or near enough, since the sun went down eight hours ago. The land does not cool. It is a low place — sixty, maybe eighty metres above a sea you cannot see or smell from here — built out of ancient river mud that has been baked and leached and rusted into laterite, and laterite holds heat the way a fired brick holds it, sullenly, for hours. Put your palm flat on the bare ground at the edge of a trail and it is warmer than your hand.

There is no moon. There is no glow on any horizon from anything at all. The darkness is not the blue darkness of temperate summer nights; it is complete, textured, a darkness that has weight and warmth and touches you. What light exists is measured in single organisms: the slow yellow-green stroke of a firefly larva under leaf litter, on and off like a bad connection; a raft of foxfire on a fallen limb, cool and dim as a watch dial; the smeared phosphorescence in the mud where a crushed fungus has been walked through and carried a few centimetres by something's feet. You would not read by any of it. You could, after twenty minutes of your pupils opening, distinguish trunk from gap, and the pale ribbon of a cleared trail from the litter beside it.

## The Shape of It

The region runs perhaps twenty-five kilometres from the base of the worn hills in the west to the flats in the east, and it is all one long, extremely old gesture of a river. Nothing here is sharp. The hills are nubs — laterite caps on rotten rock, their edges rounded off millions of years ago, their slopes at the exact angle that this soil and this rainfall have negotiated between them and now maintain without argument. The river has cut down through its own former floodplain and left terraces, three of them, each a shelf of well-drained crumbly red earth standing a few metres above the next. The riser between terraces is not a cliff; it is a slope you could walk up without using your hands. Everything has been filed down into agreement with everything else. This is a landscape that finished arguing a long time ago and has been simply *being itself* ever since — which is not the same as being static.

At the bottom is the channel. In the wet season it is two hundred metres of brown water moving at the speed of a person walking. Right now, at the exhausted end of the dry, it is a chain: pools separated by bars of gravel and sand, some pools the size of a room, some the size of a field, connected here and there by a trickle you could dam with your foot. The pools are the only free water in the whole twenty-five kilometres, and they are crowded past belief. Catfish lie flank to flank in water at body temperature, gulping at the surface film. Turtles are stacked. A caiman lies in the shallows with the patience of furniture and the water around its snout is dimpled by minnows taking whatever comes off it. The mud margins are printed over so many times — deer, peccary, tapir, cat, crab, heron, everything — that they are not tracks anymore, just a churned continuous record.

Away from the pools, the land is damp rather than wet, and only at certain hours. Around one in the morning the air in the hollows reaches its dew point and lets go, and mist gathers knee-high in the swales; leaves begin to bead and then to drip, so that in the deepest dark of the night there is a soft irregular patter through the understorey that is not rain but sounds like the end of it. Frogs use these hours. By dawn the litter surface will be genuinely wet, and by nine it will be dust again. That daily gift of a few hundred millilitres per square metre is what keeps the vegetation as thick as it is on a plain that gets no rain for four months.

## Green, Layered, Half-Undressed

Lush, but not uniformly. This is dry forest, so the canopy is only fifteen or twenty metres up and about a third of it has dropped its leaves, standing pale-barked and bare, waiting. The rest is evergreen and hangs on: figs along the terrace edges with their roots down into the water table, palms in the swales, a dense scrub of thorny understorey wherever a big tree came down and let a hole of light through in the years before. Lianas are everywhere, thick as wrists, connecting everything to everything. The bare canopy means the litter layer is enormous — ankle- to shin-deep in places, dry and loud on top, damp and silent and hot two fingers down. Vines and epiphytes carry their own soil in their crotches. There is no plane in this forest that is only one thing.

And there is no room. That is the governing quality. Every square centimetre of bark has a lichen or a fungus or a mite or a scale insect or the resin scar where something drilled it. Every hollow has an occupant. Every leaf has been eaten from at least once, and most bear the pale meandering tunnel of a leaf miner still working inside. Density here is not a matter of how close the tree trunks are; it is that the whole volume is *occupied*, from the fish stacked in the pools to the bats overhead to the fifteen thousand mites in a handful of leaf litter. If you could see it, you would find it hard to look at.

## The Works

Here is the strange thing, and it isn't strange at all once you accept it: this place is an industrial district. Nobody built it. There is nothing here that anyone made in the sense you mean — not a wall, not a wire, not a post, not a shard of pot, nothing shaped by a hand. And yet the dominant fact of the terrace, at night, is manufacture at scale.

Walk the second terrace and you cross the trails. They are ten to twenty centimetres wide, swept absolutely clean of litter, worn a centimetre or two below the surrounding surface, and they run for a hundred metres, two hundred, in long shallow curves that avoid the roots and take the gradients. They have been in the same place for six or eight years. They are roads by every criterion except intention. On them, in both directions, moving at a steady four centimetres a second, run the columns: outbound ants empty, inbound ants each holding overhead a cut crescent of leaf many times her own area, so that the trail, if you got your eye down to its level and had light, would look like a slow green river flowing upstream. The traffic does not stop. It has not stopped since dusk and will not stop until the sun gets high enough to kill workers on the open ground.

Follow a column back and the trails converge like a rail yard, four, seven, eleven of them, into a mound of loose excavated earth thirty metres across, dotted with turrets. Put the back of your hand over a turret and you feel warm air coming out, moist, carrying a smell like a bakery and a compost heap having a disagreement: carbon dioxide, fungal must, formic acid, the sweetness of shredded leaf. That is the exhaust stack. Below your feet, down to five or six metres, are two thousand chambers: intake, processing, cultivation, and waste. The leaves are not eaten. They are cleaned, cut down, chewed into pulp, inoculated, and laid up as substrate for a fungus that the colony has farmed for millions of years and which now cannot live anywhere else. There are grades of worker for each step, sized to the task within a millimetre. There is quality control — substrate contaminated with the wrong spore is detected and removed. There is a waste stream, and it is managed by a caste that never re-enters the gardens, dumping into dedicated chambers where the temperature runs several degrees hotter than the surrounding soil. There is climate control, achieved passively, by shaft geometry, and it holds the gardens within a degree or so of optimum year-round.

The colony consumes something like a third of a tonne of fresh leaf a year. There are eleven such colonies on this terrace alone. Count in the termites — the carton nests welded to the trunks like grey papery boilers, the subterranean species running covered galleries out under the litter, mounds on the drier third terrace with their internal flues — and something on the order of a fifth of all the plant matter this landscape produces annually passes through a mandible-driven processing line and comes out as soil.

None of it is loud. That is the part that makes it feel like a dream of industry rather than industry. A tonne of biomass a night is being harvested, hauled, milled, and cultivated within earshot, and what you hear is *almost nothing*: a dry continuous whisper, the sound of ten thousand tiny hard feet and the edges of ten thousand leaf fragments brushing the litter, like sand poured very slowly on paper. Underneath it, if you press your ear to the mound, the faint sustained rasp of cutting.

## The Rules

**Loudness is expensive.** In the dark, with this much predation pressure, anything that advertises its position had better have a very good reason. So the noisy things are rationed and localized: a bout of frogs at a pool, four minutes of a single cicada high in a bare crown, the sudden shriek of something being taken, a potoo's descending complaint, the click and clatter of a land crab crossing dry leaves. Between these events is a quiet so deep you can hear your own pulse in the ear you turn upward, and into that quiet the industrial whisper comes back up out of the ground. The place is packed and busy and, on the whole, hushed. The abundance is not announced.

**The dark belongs to the small.** The vertebrates you would name — the deer, the tapir, the cats — exist here and matter, but they are a rounding error by mass. The biomass is arthropods and it is in the litter and the soil and the bark. Overwhelming does not mean charging herds. It means that if you laid a metre square of frame on the forest floor and had an eternity to look, you would never finish counting.

**Nothing waits.** A branch that falls tonight will be found within hours, colonized within a day, structurally compromised in a month, and gone as a distinct object inside three years. A dead peccary is bones and hide by the second night. There is no accumulation here except soil, and the soil itself is thin, because the works are so efficient that nothing sits around long enough to bury anything else. It is a landscape without an attic. This is why there are no artifacts: not because nothing has ever been here, but because the terrace does not keep things.

**Change is slow but it is coming.** The gradients are weak and the cycles are long. The trails move a metre a year. The colonies live fifteen or twenty years and then fail, and the mound slumps, and a gap of nitrogen-rich earth opens in the understorey and fills with pioneers, and the forest reshuffles slightly. The river's terraces are the record of a hundred thousand years of the same patient rearrangement. And in six weeks — you can feel it in the way the dew is heavier each night, in the bare figs beginning to flush, in the caiman's pool dropping another hand's breadth — the rain will come back and the channel will fill in a single afternoon and the pools will stop being places and start being water again, and every one of the ant colonies will seal its turrets and wait it out, and the whole silent factory floor will be under thirty centimetres of running brown flood, humming along underneath, undisturbed, exactly as it has done.
