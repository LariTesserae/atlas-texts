## The Floor

Come down onto it in the hour that passes for noon and you will not, at first, be able to think.

The land is nearly nothing: a plain worn so far past its youth that no one could reconstruct what it used to be. There were mountains here — you can read it in the rock, in the folded grey gneiss that lies underfoot like the grain of a plank, bands squeezed and turned by a pressure that ended before anything alive could witness it. All of that has been taken away. What remains is the sawn-off root of a range, polished flat and lying under a hand's depth of sky-coloured water.

Nothing on the Floor stands higher than a house. The only relief is the ribs: quartz veins that were once the mountain's stubbornest tissue, now standing two to six metres above the plain, pale and rounded, running for kilometres in long parallel arcs like the print of a fossil hand. Everything is rounded. Everything has had every corner taken from it. Walk a day in any direction and you will not find a fresh fracture, a scarp, a cliff, an angle. The youngest sharp edge in this world is the lip of a broken shell, or a flake struck that morning off a cobble by somebody's hand.

## Water

The water table sits a finger's width below the surface, and often above it. Press your palm to what looks like dry stone and it darkens and gleams. Every footprint fills before you lift your heel. The ribs weep continuously — a cool exudate, faintly metallic, that comes out of the quartz at the joints and runs down in slick fans, staining them rust and black. The atmosphere is a low grey lid of vapour that condenses on the coldest stone all night and all day, so the rock is never dry, has not been dry in any span of time worth naming.

To the west, thirty kilometres out, there is a sea, though you cannot see where it starts. Twice a day it walks inland. It arrives as a brown line no higher than your shin, moving at the speed of a fast walk, and then the Floor is under thigh-deep water for a few hours, and then the line withdraws. This is the largest event in the world and it is almost silent compared to everything else.

## Light

The sun is a smear, a slightly brighter patch of lid. The brightest part of the day resembles the twentieth minute after a summer sunset at home. Colour survives only in the near field: at arm's length the mats are unmistakably orange, black, oil-white; at fifty paces the whole plain is one dim pewter shine.

The pools help. In every hollow the microbial films luminesce a low green when disturbed, so that wading creatures — including you — drag a glow behind them, and the tide when it comes in is edged with a cold fluorescence like a chalk line. At night the plain is legible mostly as a network of these disturbances: pale scribbles wandering, converging, going out.

## What grows

Nothing grows, if by growing you mean plants. There is no leaf, no stalk, no root, no wood, no seed, no green thing that turns light into sugar. The light would not be enough for it anyway.

Instead the pasture is chemical. The old rock leaks iron and sulphur into the water table, and mats of something simpler than a plant — bacterial, fungal-adjacent, unclassifiable by any scheme you brought with you — eat those leaks. They lie over the Floor in crusts and skins from paper-thickness to the depth of a mattress: rust-coloured felts, black leathery pavements that crack in hexagons, white greasy layers that smell of struck matches, and in the seep-lines below the ribs, sheets of a translucent jelly with the internal structure of frost. These are the fields. They are grazed to the stone in some districts and hip-deep in others, and their pattern shifts across decades, a slow agriculture with no farmer.

## The noise

Now the thing you cannot think through.

The Floor is deafening. Not loud — deafening, literally, functionally; the sound here removes hearing from anything that stays. It comes principally from the clappers: a hand-length armoured animal, ten legs, one claw hypertrophied into a cocked hammer that it fires to make a cavitation bubble collapse. Each snap alone is a rifle crack. There are perhaps four hundred million of them per square kilometre of channel bed. Their beds line every tidal creek, every rib-foot seep, every wet crack in the plain, and they fire continuously, competitively, in overlapping barrages, and the flat wet stone reflects all of it upward.

Standing on the Floor, the noise is a solid: a white roar with grain in it, like frying, like a waterfall heard from inside, pressing on the sinuses and the chest wall. You can feel it in your teeth. Speech is impossible — not difficult, impossible; a shout from ten centimetres away arrives as a movement of air with no content.

Under that lie the other layers. The hiss of the grazers, millions of them, rasping mat off stone with mineral tongues — a sound like continuous rain on gravel, which is also happening. The subsonic pulse of the big filter-swimmers that come in on the tide, a thump you register as nausea rather than noise. And the weather: on this flat, rain does not patter, it applies itself, a uniform static across thirty kilometres.

The sound has become a geological agent. Along certain ribs the quartz is pitted and scalloped in ways no water explains — cavitation-etched, a pox of tiny hemispherical pockets, each one the ghost of a collapsed bubble. There are hollows under the ribs that resonate: enter one and the roar acquires a pitch, a note, and your vision greys at the edges. And there is a district in the south, the bell-flat, where the rock lies over a void and the whole surface rings; the clappers there are dense past reason, feeding on the resonance somehow, and nothing warm-blooded has ever crossed it and come back with a working head.

Silence, therefore, is the alarm. Where the clappers go quiet — a sudden hole in the roar, a pocket of hearing — something is wrong: a predator working the bed, a sour seep, water gone bad. The quiet spreads outward as the beds shut down ahead of it. To be in a place that becomes audible is to be in a place you should already be leaving.

## The abundant

Fauna is everywhere, though thinly spread; the eye takes a while to find them because there is so much space between. Grazing herds of plated things the size of boots and shields, moving as slow grey drifts across the mat-fields. Long segmented predators, four metres, that swim the sheet-water with their backs breaking surface, taking grazers by the dozen. Filter-swimmers in the flood tide, broad and pale as doors. Leathery gliders that ride the standing air off the ribs and drop feet-first into pools; they make no calls, because there would be no point, and their heads are mostly ear-blank, mostly eye.

Nothing here has good hearing. Everything here has excellent skin.

## The scattered built

There are people. Not many — a band of a dozen every few hours' walk, when they are anywhere near you.

Their houses are low domes on the highest ground available, which is to say on the ribs, keyed into the weeping stone: stacked shell-plate from the big grazers, chinked with mat-slime that sets like plaster, roofed with hide stretched over rib-bones. The domes have no windows and one crawl-entrance facing away from the sea. Inside, the roar drops to something almost bearable — a shell dome is a good deadener — and this is what a house is for here: not warmth, not dryness, quiet.

Their tools are stone, bone, shell, and hide, ground more often than knapped, because the local rock is too old and tired to flake well. No metal, though they collect the iron nodules that grow in the seeps and use them as hammers and weights. Their weirs are the most visible works: lines of set cobbles a hundred metres long across the tidal creeks, catching grazers on the ebb, maintained for so many generations that the stones have grown their own mat-skins and look native.

They cook over rendered fat in stone bowls — blue, low flames, the only warm colour on the Floor. They ferment in lined pits, since nothing will ever dry in this air. They plug their ears from infancy with wax and fitted shell caps, and the fitting of a child's first pair is the largest thing that happens in a life. Most are deaf by twelve regardless.

So they speak with their hands, quickly and with the face involved, and at distance they speak by tapping. The ribs carry a knuckle-rap for a kilometre and more through solid quartz. Every band's territory has its speaking-knobs: places on the rib where the stone is polished into a shallow dish by generations of striking, and where you press your jawbone or your palm to the rock to receive. Long strings of code go up and down the ribs all day beneath the roar, unheard by anything that hears through air. The cairns — thousands of them, one within sight of the next, running the flat between ribs — are for fog, which is most days.

Their dead are laid in the clapper beds and are gone in a week, and the bones sink into the mat and become part of the pasture, and there is a tap-phrase for this which translates roughly as *went into the noise.*

## Rules of the place

Nothing stands tall. Nothing is dry. Nothing is sharp that was not made sharp this year. The water is always one hand away in any direction, up or down.

The land is finished — it has arrived at the shape it will keep until the sea finally takes the last of it — and so all the motion here belongs to water, weather, and animals: a landscape that has stopped changing, carrying an ecology that will not stop for a second.

And silence means run.
