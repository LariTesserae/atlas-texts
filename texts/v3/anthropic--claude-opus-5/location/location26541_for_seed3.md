## The Kell

The mountain has been rising for eleven generations and it is still not finished coming up. It rises about a thumb-width a year, which is nothing, which is everything, which means the funicular rails must be re-shimmed every spring and the oldest streets in the city have a slow inward tilt like the walls of a mouth.

Nobody agrees on its height. The barometers here read a pressure that does not correspond to any altitude in the tables; the surveyors' triangulations come out different every time they are run, not by error but by disagreement, the mountain apparently having opinions. The charter of the city forbids assigning it a number. Officially the summit's elevation is *pending*. Unofficially: high enough that the air is thin and sweet and slightly wrong, that a flight of stairs is a decision, that the flocks pass below you.

What you notice first is that the rock is *new*. Not new like a fresh roadcut, which is old rock freshly exposed. New. Never touched. The great arêtes come up out of the cloud-deck like blades still bright from the grinder, grey-blue, with the greasy sheen of a fresh fracture, and they end in nothing — no talus fan at their feet, no apron of rubble, no scree, no debris. There has not been time for the mountain to shed anything. A cliff simply meets a shelf at an angle no cliff should be able to hold, and holds it. There are no valleys. There is no watershed. Nothing has ever run down this mountain long enough to carve a line. The slopes are unlined the way a face is unlined, and it is not a pleasant youthfulness. It is the youthfulness of something that has not yet been obliged to have a history.

And unwritten rock does not reliably stay put.

## The Line

Everything in the city organizes around the Lichen Line: the altitude above which the stone has not yet been weathered, witnessed, rained on, or otherwise made to happen.

Below the Line, the world behaves. Water flows downhill at the accustomed speed. A measured distance measures the same twice. Shadows fall away from lamps. Above the Line, the world is provisional. The air in places is *dumb* — you can shout into it and lose the shout, and then hear it four minutes later, from the wrong side. Distances measured with instruments come out fractional and unstable; only distances *walked* are legally binding, which is why the Guild of Paces exists, and why a pacer's certificate is worth more in court than a theodolite.

The upper mountain is also where the light fails. There is no sun here — not hidden, not obscured: not placed. The sky is a lamp beneath a sheet, an even smeared lumen that brightens and dims on a cycle of roughly twenty-seven hours which the city has had to legislate into eight watches of unequal length. Noon is a slightly paler grey. At its brightest you can read a broadsheet held close. Everything else is arc-lamp, carbide, coal-gas, the orange bloom of the smelters on the underside of the cloud, and moths.

## Water

There are no rivers, because rivers require valleys and valleys require time. There is no ice, either, which surprises visitors — at this height there should be glaciers, and there are none, because the mountain is still warm. It came up from somewhere deep and it has not cooled. Heat leaks out of every crack in long slow exhalations, so the Kell stands at a permanent cool damp, six or seven degrees, breathing.

That breath meets the cloud-deck and condenses, and the condensation is the water supply. The ridges above the city are combed — literally combed, ten thousand vertical frames of copper mesh and hung sisal rope, black with moss, rows and rows of them along every windward spur, humming in the airflow like a bad organ. Fog walks into them and comes out the other side dry. The water runs down the ropes into slate channels, into cisterns cut in the raw stone, and from there to the taps, the boilers, and the Acid Terraces. In a good month a comb-field yields enough to run a mill. Rain is a separate matter, and mostly manufactured; on the Kell, rain is a *product*.

## The City

Hantle occupies the third ridge and spills onto the fourth by way of two suspended trusses, and it holds perhaps forty thousand people in a smear of construction eleven kilometres long and nowhere more than four hundred metres wide, because there is nowhere else to put it. It is a city of one dimension. It is all street, no square.

The construction is layered and improvisational and yet organized in the way an aqueduct is organized: gantries stacked over roofs, stairs built through the interiors of other buildings, tenements corbelled out over drops of a kilometre and a half on brackets of riveted iron. Every structure is bolted to the mountain with pins driven eight metres deep, and the pins are inspected quarterly, and the inspection is not really about the pins.

The infrastructure is magnificent and unlovely. Four funiculars with counterweighted cars of oak and brass. A cable-haul network — eighteen kilometres of it — moving ore buckets and grit hoppers and, on the passenger lines, wicker gondolas that swing sickeningly in the updraughts. A pneumatic post. Electric light in the works and in the Registry, gas everywhere else, nothing in the alleys. Boilers fed on peat brought up from below and on the mountain's own vented heat where they can tap it, which they do more each decade: iron pipes going down into cracks and coming back hot, the whole city slowly learning to drink from the thing it stands on.

Between the built stretches, emptiness. Half an hour's walk out of the Grit Row and there is nothing: bare shelf, one comb-field, a shepherd's hut with a tin chimney, a flock of stept moving across a slab a kilometre wide, and the sound of the stamp-mills coming to you across all that open air, undiminished, because there is nothing to absorb it. This is the character of the place. Loud and empty at once. Sparse and roaring.

## The Work

The industry of Hantle is the manufacture of erosion.

This is not a metaphor and it is not a trade in luxuries. Unweathered rock is unstable in a way that has nothing to do with mechanics. It slips — not avalanche-slip, but *change-of-mind* slip. A path that was there on Tuesday is a face on Wednesday, and not because anything fell; nothing fell; there is no rubble; the path was simply not sufficiently the case. Men and women who work beyond the Line come back with details altered — a coat that buttons the other way, a middle name off by a letter, a scar migrated to the other wrist. Nothing dramatic. Attrition. The world up here is thin because nothing has ever happened to it, and the cure for thinness is history, and the cheapest available history is weather.

So: **the Grit Mills.** Six of them, the largest four storeys of iron stamps, forty strokes a minute, a heartbeat you feel in your teeth from three kilometres off. They take quarried stone and beat it to sand. The sand goes up the cable-hauls to the Sowing Towers on the high spurs, where centrifugal fans throw it out into the wind in long grey plumes to scour the faces above. Grit is also the nucleus that makes rain: seeded cloud gives up its water, and the water runs, and running water is the deepest kind of writing.

**The Acid Terraces.** Sulphur is captured out of the smelter flues — the ore here is rich and virgin and unleached, since nothing has ever percolated through it, and the smelting is the city's only export trade — and married to comb-water in lead-lined vats. Etch-crews go out with copper hoses and hand-pumps on rope stages, five hundred metres of nothing below their boots, and spray the cliffs. On a good shift they can age a hectare by two centuries. The runoff is collected below; nothing is wasted; the terraces are stepped so the same water etches nine times before it is dead.

**The Lichenhouses.** The most patient of the three and the most respected. Crustose lichen grown in trays under electric light, in a long low glass building where the air is kept at the exact damp of the outer fog, and then carried out by hanging crews and pressed onto prepared stone with wooden paddles. Lichen makes the first true soil. A hectare properly lichened will hold moss in forty years, cushion-plants in ninety, and after two hundred it will carry a candle-alder. There are eleven candle-alders in the city, each with a brass plate and a guild, and people leave things at their roots.

**The Stare-Wardens.** Because attention is a weathering agent — weak, but free. Six hundred wardens, mostly the old and the injured, sit in tin huts at assigned stations and look at assigned faces of rock, in four-hour shifts, and afterwards write down what they saw in ledgers. *Ninth hour: mist on the east buttress, cleared. A stept, alone, crossing left to right. Nothing else.* The ledgers go to the Registry, which is the largest building on the Kell, a windowless nine-storey block of dressed stone lit by its own generator, and the Registry is understood by everyone to be the actual foundation of the city. Stone that has been written about stays. Deeds, gossip, court records, the paper's back numbers, the name of every dog: all of it ballast. The dead are buried high, in raw rock, deliberately, to give it something to have had happen to it.

## The Living

Fauna are everywhere and everywhere thinly spread, which is what happens when a great deal of life is stretched over a great deal of space.

Fog-moths, first — palm-sized, ash-grey, faintly luminous along the abdomen, in their millions. They drink at the comb-fields and at dusk the comb-ridges go up in a soft grey shimmer that can be seen from the fourth ridge. Their guano is scraped from the frames and is the best manure the city has; the moth-scrapers' guild is old and touchy about precedence.

Stept: hoofed, long-jawed, wool like wet felt, and able to walk on unweathered stone without slipping, because — the herders say, and the herders are not wrong — they do not entirely touch it. They are milked (the milk tastes of flint and is good in tea), sheared, and hired out as guides. A stept wearing a bell can walk into the provisional country above the Line and come back with all its details intact, and a person following close behind it will usually do the same.

Kites, high up, where the wing-count is unsettled: seen against the pale sky they have four wings, or six, and as they drop below the Line they resolve to two and become ordinary hunting birds, which they then are, and always were.

In the cisterns, blind shrimp. In the steam-gardens over the vents, where the exhalation keeps a pocket of warm wet air, real lushness — tree-ferns, pale tall grasses, curtains of hanging moss two metres long, frogs, and a small green snake that is protected by ordinance. These gardens are a few hundred paces across and there might be sixty of them on the whole mountain, green coins on a grey table.

And below everything, felt rather than heard, on the long slow schedule that governs this entire place — a sub-bass that comes up through the pins and the rails and the soles of the boots, once every few minutes, patient, unhurried, and not going to stop: whatever it is that is pushing.
