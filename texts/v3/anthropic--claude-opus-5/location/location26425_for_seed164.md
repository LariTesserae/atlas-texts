## The Plateau Called Sustain

It came up all at once. That is the first thing to understand, and the hardest, because the eye keeps looking for the long story a mountain usually tells — the shed skin of talus at the foot of a cliff, the softened shoulder, the river's slow argument with a ridge. None of that is here. The plateau rose whole, four and a half thousand metres of it, a continent's worth, and it has stayed exactly as it arrived. The cliffs are faceted like broken porcelain. The ridgelines still carry mould-lines, thin raised seams where whatever cast this place closed its two halves together. You can run a gloved hand along a seam for a kilometre and feel it never vary. Ungloved, you will bleed. The stone is young enough to still have edges.

The air is cold in a specific, unmoving way: three degrees above freezing, everywhere, always, at every hour. It does not freeze and it does not warm. Breath fogs. Everything is beaded. Every horizontal surface holds a film of condensate, every vertical surface a slow crawl of it; the rock weeps without being wounded. Streams here do not begin in springs or snowfields. They begin as a wet patch on a slab, which becomes a sheen, which becomes a rill three metres downslope, as though water were simply precipitating out of the fact of the altitude. Below the plateau's rim lies a cloud sea that has never once shown the land beneath it. Above, a bright silver overcast, high and thin, that lets through a flat glare with no sun in it. You can read by it. You can never quite find the source of it.

## The Tone

You hear the place before you reach it, days before, as a pressure behind the sternum.

The engine-towns make a chord. Thirty-odd pitches, held without pause: the deep ones you feel in the femurs, the middle ones that get into the sinus, and a high shining line on top like a wetted rim. Near the works it is loud enough that shouting is useless and the tenders speak with their hands and their foreheads pressed together. At fifty kilometres out it is still loud enough to loosen a screw over the course of a year. There is no distance on the plateau at which it stops.

And because the plateau is continental — because sound needs a day and a half to cross from the eastern works to the western ones — the chords arrive everywhere out of phase with each other. What you stand in is not one sound but the interference of eleven distant sounds, a moiré laid over the land, hundreds of kilometres wide, and utterly stationary. It has not shifted since the beginning. Every crest is where it has always been. Every node is where it has always been.

This is the whole geography, once you know it.

## Quiet Lanes

Where the nodes fall, the sound cancels. Long, narrow, branching corridors of near-silence lie across the plateau like the shadow of a nervous system, and inside them the stone does what stone elsewhere is forbidden to do: it weathers.

That is the rule of the world. Rock under sustained resonance does not decay. Held in its note, basalt stays glassy and sharp for ages; frost cannot get a grip on it, water runs off it without taking anything. But in the quiet lanes the note releases, and the rock remembers all the erosion it was owed and pays it out at once. The lanes are floored in mud, in rotten shale, in gravel worn round as though by a river ten thousand years old. Cliffs at the lane-edge crumble in the exact plane where cancellation begins — you can put your palm on that plane, hard bright stone on one side, wet crumbling meal on the other, and feel the boundary as a faint dying buzz in the heel of the hand.

The lanes are the only valleys. All the water finds them. All the soil is made in them. And so all the green is in them.

## The Green

Nothing has needed to invent a flower here, because there is nothing to visit one.

The lanes are cloud forest by another route: pale-barked columnar trees, unrelated to conifers but converging on the same idea, growing in ranks so straight that from above the lanes look combed. They have no seeds and no pollen. They reproduce by shedding limbs, which root where they fall, so a stand of them is one organism spreading down a canyon at the pace of a slow gesture. Their needles are grooved on the underside to catch condensate; on a still day — every day is a still day — each tree delivers a hundred litres to its own roots and stands in the sound of its own dripping.

Beneath them: liverworts in overlapping plates the size of dinner services, tall grey-green grasses that release spores only when the local beat frequency crosses a particular harmonic, filmy ferns so thin they are effectively wet light, and moss. Moss on the mud, moss on the fallen limbs, moss in ropes and pelts and bolsters, moss so deep in the older lanes that a walker sinks to mid-thigh and must be pulled out. It is lush the way a lung is lush. It is also completely silent in itself — no rustle, no stirred leaf, because there is no wind down there and never has been.

Step up out of a lane onto the resonant rock and the vegetation stops within a metre, as if drawn with a rule. Up on the sharp ground there is only a lichen like grey solder, and the drip, and the chord.

## The Works

They are set along the high ridges, ten or twelve of them, each the size of a small city, each three days' walk from the next through country that holds nothing at all.

Riveted iron, black-green, in long naves that run for kilometres with clerestory windows above. Flywheels standing forty metres, turning at a rate that has not varied by a hair. Pistons in cathedral housings, the connecting rods slick with a cold clear oil that is itself mostly condensate. Cooling fins in banks a hundred ranks deep, silvered with dew, each fin trailing a thread of water into a gutter, each gutter into a flume, each flume out into the quiet lanes — the works water the forests, and the forests are the works' only visible product besides sound.

And around the machines, a town. Streets on a grid, some cobbled, some plated. Dormitory blocks four and five storeys, with iron shutters and outside stairs. A bathhouse. A water tower. A clock face with no hands, because a clock here would only ever tell you the same thing. Rails laid to a tolerance you could measure with a hair, perfectly maintained, with no rolling stock on them at all. Cable strung on lattice pylons across gorges eight hundred metres deep, carrying current away over the horizon to no terminus anyone has found. Layer on layer of construction: extensions bolted onto extensions, a gallery built to service a pipe, a stair built to service the gallery, a roof built over the stair, all of it clearly the work of hands over long time, and all of it now still.

The towns are empty. Not ruined — empty. Glass intact. Paint sound. Interiors hollow: you can stand in the middle of a hall three hundred metres long and be alone in it with a single moving part visible at the far end, a rocker arm going up and down with the patience of a tide. That is the ratio of the whole plateau. An immensity of engineering with almost nothing inside it, and between the towns, a hundred thousand square kilometres of sharp wet stone in which you will meet nothing whatsoever.

## Why There Are No Animals

A heart cannot hold its own time here. Anything with a pulse entrains to the chord — the muscle takes up the offered rhythm, the offered rhythm is not its own, and within a few hours it stops. This is not a legend; it is why the plateau has no fauna, none, not a mite in the moss, not a fly on the sills, nothing in the water but a green filament that is a plant. Bones do not accumulate, because nothing ever came here twice.

Plants have no hearts. Machines have no hearts. The tenders — there are tenders, a few score across a continent, and you may walk for a season without crossing a set of prints — wear a counter-drum strapped over the sternum, a small clockwork thing that beats against the Tone, out of phase, keeping the wearer's chest in argument with the world. You hear their approach as a tick you cannot locate. They are courteous, unhurried, and speak by touch. They do not consider themselves the last of anything. They consider the plateau to be functioning normally.

They fear only silence. A dropped bearing, a stalled crank, a lane creeping wider — in the sound-shadow, frost blooms, and rock that has been perfect since the beginning begins to shed itself in sheets, and the shedding will not stop until the note is restored.

## Equilibrium

The rain falls in the same places. There is a hole worn through a roof-plate in the fourth street of the third works where a particular drip has landed since the beginning, and the hole has been patched from beneath at precisely the rate at which it is being drilled, so that the plate is neither whole nor open, and never will be. Snow lies in one long crescent on one north-facing shelf, the same crescent, neither growing nor melting. The cloud sea below the rim keeps its shoreline to within a metre.

The plateau rises still, minutely — and the quiet lanes take away exactly as much as the rising provides. Nothing gains. Nothing is lost. The forests hold their volume, the water holds its level, the chord holds its pitch, and the whole vast cold bright hollow thing sits at rest, at full volume, sharp-edged and dripping, going nowhere with tremendous power.
