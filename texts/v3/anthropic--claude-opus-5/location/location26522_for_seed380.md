## The Ease

### Getting down

There is a hatch in the pavement of a lane that no longer has a name, and beneath it a stair of engineering brick, blue-black, salt-glazed, seventy-one steps with a lead handrail worn to the shape of a grip. The air changes at step nine. It stops smelling of the surface and starts smelling of cold iron, wet lime, and something faintly sweet, like the inside of a clean bottle. By step twenty the sound of the world above has gone entirely — not faded, gone, as if pinched off — and what replaces it is not silence but a soft, upholstered absence, the sound a room makes when it has been lined with wool.

At the bottom the stair opens into the chamber, and the chamber is the whole of the world here. It is not large. Cathedral, yes, in proportion — but a small cathedral, a parish thing: perhaps sixty paces across, and high enough that the roof is a guess rather than a fact. You could walk its boundaries in ten minutes if the walkways permitted, which they do not.

### The fabric

It was built with real conviction. Whoever raised it expected it to be looked at. Cast-iron columns rise from the water in two staggered rows, fluted, with capitals of stylised acanthus that no one was ever meant to see, and they carry riveted plate girders and a barrel of brick laid in stretcher courses so fine you could shave with the joints. There are corbels. There is a lead-lined string course with a chevron in it. Somebody, a hundred and sixty years ago, stood in a drawing office in the cold and decided this drain deserved a chevron.

All of that is now half-swallowed. Calcite has come out of the walls in the slow patient way water does its arithmetic, and it has hung the ironwork with draperies the colour of old candle wax. The chevron survives as a suggestion under three fingers of flowstone. Iron staples weep ochre down the brick in long rust-coloured beards. Where the seepage is heaviest the wall has grown *moonmilk* — a white paste, cool and yielding as curd, that takes a fingerprint and holds it for a decade. The moonmilk is why the room is quiet. It eats sound. A dropped spanner makes one flat clap and then nothing, no ring, no echo, the noise absorbed into the walls like ink into blotting paper. The chamber has been listening to itself for a century and a half and has learned how to stop.

### The water

The floor is water. Black, and so still that the columns descend into their own reflections without a seam, which doubles the height of everything and makes the room feel like a held breath.

It rises and falls once a day. Not tidally — nothing here has any business with the moon — but with the slow filling and easing of the ground above, so that between dawn and mid-afternoon the level creeps up perhaps forty centimetres, laying a wet gleam over the lowest catwalk, and through the night it goes down again. You can read the whole cycle off the brick: a band of bright green algal film at the top of the range, then a broad zone of iron-black slime, then, below, clean stone the colour of a slate roof. The bands are perfectly level and perfectly parallel. They are the most exact instrument in the room.

The water is cool without being cold — the temperature of a stone cellar, unvarying, the same in August as in February. It is also extremely clear. Look down and you see the original floor eight feet under, its brick still crisp, and a scatter of things dropped over generations: a zinc bucket, a boot, a padlock, a shilling, all of them furred with the same pale mineral coat, all becoming the same object slowly.

### The engine

At the far end, on a pier of its own, stands the machine.

It is a water-column engine — a thing driven by the weight of the water it exists to move. No fire, no boiler, no steam; the head of the incoming main presses on a piston the size of a cider barrel, and that piston pushes a beam of cast iron up through an arc, and the beam pulls a rod, and the rod lifts water out of the chamber and away into a culvert nobody living has walked. The whole business is powered by the problem it solves. It has been doing this, without interruption and without supervision, since long before anyone now breathing.

It moves once every eleven minutes.

That is the central fact of the place, more than the water or the dark. Eleven minutes of absolute stillness, and then a shudder somewhere in the pier, and the beam begins to swing — enormously slow, the way a horse's ribs move when it is asleep — and at the top of the stroke there is a single deep *knock*, felt in the soles of the feet rather than heard, and a great gout of water heaves out of the discharge with a noise like a bath emptying two streets away, and then the beam settles back and the room returns to its held breath. Grease the colour of honey stands in the bearing caps. The flywheel, which does nothing but smooth the stroke, wears a coat of pale moss on its upper rim, kept alive by the warmth of the shaft.

Off a gear train the engine also turns a small dynamo, a brass and mahogany thing under a glass bell, and the dynamo feeds nineteen carbon-filament lamps strung round the chamber on porcelain insulators. Nineteen lamps for sixty paces is not enough, and the filaments are old, so the light is a dim ochre — orange-brown, sufficient to see by if you are patient, insufficient to read by, and it *pulses*. It brightens very slightly on the stroke and sags in the eleven minutes between. Everything in the chamber therefore breathes in the same rhythm: the light, the water in the discharge, the shadows of the acanthus capitals moving a hand's width and back across the brick.

### What lives here

A great deal, and nearly all of it quiet.

The eels first. Pale, unpigmented, arm-length, they lie in the deep water in loose knots and drift out at the stroke to hunt the midges the discharge concussion shakes down from the roof. There must be two hundred of them. They are unhurried to the point of insult. They align, when at rest, along the same axis — head-on to the dynamo, every one of them — and nobody has explained it.

Then the springtails, which lie in the corners of the walkways in grey drifts like pollen and puff up in a soundless cloud where a boot falls. Cave crickets, long-legged as chandeliers, spaced along the moonmilk with the exact courtesy of strangers on a bench — never two closer than a hand's span. Snails grazing the white paste in slow arcs, leaving readable script. Freshwater shrimp in the shallows, in numbers that make the water look like it is faintly boiling if you stare too long. Crayfish under the pier, one to each brick niche, all of them the colour of a fingernail.

Rats, old and fat and entirely unfrightened, who walk rather than run, and who know the eleven minutes so well that they cross the low walkway only immediately after the stroke.

And, standing on the third column from the stair, a heron. It has been in the chamber for years — came up the outfall in a flood, presumably, and never found the way back or never wanted it. It has gone the colour of the moonmilk. Its eye has lost most of its yellow. It fishes on the stroke, once every eleven minutes, with one movement, and hits every time, and between strokes it does not move at all, not a feather, so that visitors regularly take it for a piece of the ironwork until it turns its head.

Nothing here makes much noise. Whatever squeaked or sang has been bred out of it, generation by generation, in a room that gives nothing back. The loudest animal sound in the chamber is the wet click of the heron swallowing.

### What grows

Little. Under each of the nineteen lamps there is a green stain, and in the better-lit stains, a fern — hart's tongue mostly, pallid, small, propagating on eight watts of Victorian light. Liverworts in flat rosettes on the north wall, where the seep is steadiest. Algal film in the tide band. On the warm bearing housing, the moss. And one root: an elder, from a yard above, has pushed a finger of itself down through a defective joint in the vaulting and hangs a metre into the room, white and blind and shaggy with rootlets, dripping. The eels gather under it. It grows perhaps a centimetre a year and it has been coming for a long time.

### The rules

- **Stillness costs.** Anything that does not move gets a mineral coat. There is a canvas coat on a hook by the stair, hung up by someone at the end of a shift, that is now a lumpy white sculpture of a coat, permanently. There is an enamel cup fused to a shelf. The rule applies to visitors on a long enough scale, and the standing advice is: shift your weight.
- **Don't speak; tap.** Voices die within two metres. The two people who still come down — you can tell there are two, from the chalk — communicate by knocking on the handrail, which carries beautifully, and the handrail has been polished to a satin gleam at intervals of exactly one arm's reach along its whole length.
- **The stroke is the clock, the calendar, and the permission.** Cross on the stroke. Fish on the stroke. Hatch on the stroke. If the stroke is late, everything in the chamber stops and waits, including the heron, including the rats, and you find that you are waiting too, with your hand on the wet rail, holding your breath in a room that has been holding its own for a hundred and sixty years.

### The ledger

On the pier beside the engine there is a slate, and on the slate a tally in chalk: strokes counted, water levels, initials. The most recent entry is not old. Below it, in a different hand and a different decade, someone has written a single line and it has been carefully re-chalked over the top of itself many times, by many people, so that the letters stand out from the slate in low relief:

**IT DOES NOT NEED US. COME ANYWAY.**
