## The Addition

### The ground

It begins as a cut. If you come from the sea you see it first as a low dark line, then as a wall six or seven meters high, banded in horizontal courses like a wet ledger — and no beach, no shingle, no slow negotiation of sand. Just the edge of the land, squared off, with the water working at its base and getting nowhere.

Behind that edge the plain runs inland for forty kilometers, rising so gradually you would call it flat if your inner ear didn't insist otherwise. It is made of plates. You can see the seams from anywhere: hairline joints, dead straight for a kilometer and then turning at a precise obtuse angle, where one sheet of new ground was set against the next. The plates are perhaps four hundred meters on a side. Grass has taken the seams first, so from a height the plain reads as a pale grey field ruled into a green graph.

Nothing here is worn. Every terrace still has its arris — the sharp upper lip where the riser meets the tread — and if you run a hand along it you will cut yourself, which is a stupid thing to do and which everyone does once. The stone is a printed basalt they call **grain**, laid down in courses two centimeters thick, and it comes out of the extruders warm and off-gassing and smelling faintly green, like a cucumber sliced in a cold room. Forty years on, some plates still give off a thread of that smell at dawn where the vents haven't sealed. Put your palm to certain slabs and they are body temperature from beneath.

The land is young because it is *kept* young. This is the first thing to understand. Age is not being withheld from the Addition by the calendar; it is being scraped off every night.

### The sky, and why it is always evening

Four hundred meters up there is a shell. Not a dome — it has no edge you can find, it simply is the sky here — a lamina of what the wardens call **slow glass**, though it is not glass and its slowness is not a metaphor. Light entering the shell at noon does not arrive at the ground at noon. It is held, spread, and released over some eleven or twelve hours, so that the day comes down diluted and even, a grey-gold hush with no direction to it. There is no full brightness. There is also no dark: midnight on the Addition is a blue-grey the color of a photograph of a room, bright enough to read a large-print sign, not bright enough to read a face.

Two consequences, both of which visitors find unpleasant for about a week and then stop noticing:

Nothing casts a hard shadow. Objects sit in soft pools of their own dimming, and if you move quickly you can see your shadow catch up — a lag of a third of a second, no more, a smear at the heels. Children born here do not see the lag. Nobody knows whether that means the shell is settling or their eyes are.

And: there is no way to tell time by looking up. Time is told by the rain.

### Water

Humidity sits at ninety and the ground is dry. This is not a contradiction, it is an arrangement.

Standing water is not permitted on the plates — the substrate wicks it — so the plain has no puddles, no ponds, no marsh. What it has instead is a daily tide of air. Just before what would be dawn, the sea exhales and the fog comes inland at walking pace, a wall of it three meters deep, and it flows *up* the terraces rather than down, drawn by the grain. For two hours the city stands in a white lake with its towers out and everything below the third floor gone. Then the light thickens, the fog lifts and dissipates, and every surface in the Addition is beaded.

Then, once per day, at an hour that shifts by four minutes daily so that it walks slowly around the clock across the year, it rains. Hard, vertical, without wind, for eleven minutes. Nobody has ever explained the eleven minutes to my satisfaction. Afterward the plates are dry within the hour and the only water left is in the channels.

The channels are the strangest thing on the plain and the most watched. They were cut straight — eleven of them, running seaward, four meters wide, revetted in grain. They were not meant to be interesting. But water put in a straight channel does not stay straight, and the channels have begun, in the last decade, to want to bend. There are places now where the flow has cut a millimeter into the left bank on one side of a straight and a millimeter into the right bank forty meters down. This is the beginning of a meander. People come out to look at it. There is a bench.

### The city

It is called Meniscus, because of the way the dew stands proud on its surfaces before it climbs.

It is a real city — full-scale, layered, provisioned. Trunk mains, three levels of them, walkable. A tram network on the seams. Sixty-some towers, none of them built in any sense you would recognize: they were **spun**, extruded upward from a single point of origin on the plate, whorled, jointless, tapering, so that the walls are also the floors are also the stairs, and there is not a single right angle above the second story. They are dark pewter, and matte, and warm.

And they are mostly empty.

Density is the thing you feel before you have language for it. The towers stand two, three, four hundred meters apart, with lush ground between them — not lawn, not park, actual seeded forest, dew-fig and cathedral bamboo and the tall siphon-grasses that pull moisture out of the fog and pass it down to their roots with an audible tick. So you walk a wide clean avenue through what is functionally a jungle, and you pass a tower, and then there is nothing but green and grey for six minutes, and then another tower. At night each one shows perhaps four lit windows out of six hundred. The trams run their timetable regardless, lit, empty, quiet, sliding along the seams like something rehearsing.

Perhaps ninety thousand people live in a city plumbed for four million. They cluster: three towers near the harbor cut, a dew-farm settlement out at the eleventh channel, the warden barracks. The rest of Meniscus keeps itself immaculate for nobody. This is not decay — nothing here is decaying, that's the point — it is a kind of readiness that has gone on so long it has become the local character. People are patient in a particular way that is hard to be around at first.

### Sound

The grain eats bass. Whatever the substrate is doing under those two-centimeter courses, it takes the low frequencies out of everything, so:

Footsteps have no thump, only a dry click. Voices carry oddly well and sound as if spoken beside your ear regardless of distance, which makes conversation across a plaza feel conspiratorial. Thunder is felt in the sternum and not heard at all. A dropped tool sounds like a dropped pencil.

Against that, the ambient sound of the Addition is: the tick of siphon-grass, the drip-fall from fig leaves for an hour after fog, the tram's hiss, and beneath everything a hum at the very bottom of hearing that you locate, wrongly, in your own teeth. That is the **underwriting**.

Twice a month the hum changes pitch for about ninety seconds, and everyone stops what they are doing, not out of ceremony but because it is genuinely difficult to continue. This is the underwriters adjusting. The Addition is not floating and it is not supported by anything in the ordinary sense; it is held, and holding requires correction. Old-timers claim they can hear which quarter of the plain is being trimmed. During an adjustment, water in a glass forms a standing pattern of six rings, and the fog, if there is fog, briefly stands still.

### Ecology

Rare, and not for lack of trying.

The plants took. They took extravagantly — the dew-figs have gone from ankle to twelve meters in a human generation, and the cathedral bamboo throws a stand up in a season, hollow, pale, and so acoustically dead that a stand of it registers as a hole in the world. There is moss in every seam, three species, all of them engineered and one of them now doing something it was not designed to do at the seam-corners, which the botanists are pleased about in a guarded way.

The animals did not take, mostly. There are ants — a farming species, tended, that builds condensation combs on the north faces of towers and harvests fog like the grasses do. There is one spider that strings mist-nets between bamboo, catching water rather than prey, and drinking it. There is a beetle.

And there are the herons. Pale, tall, grey-white, seeded eleven at a time out of a program that hoped for wetlands and got channels. They walk the trunk mains. They walk *inside* the mains, in the dry service galleries, single file, for kilometers, and come out at inspection hatches with their feathers filmed in dust, and they are the only large living thing you will meet down there. There are maybe three hundred of them. People count them. Everybody in Meniscus can tell you the current number and will, unprompted.

Above the shell there are supposedly birds. You see them sometimes as smudges — delayed, of course, so you are watching a bird that has already gone.

### Rules

A short list, learned by everyone in the first month:

Do not sleep on bare grain. It wicks.

Do not put anything in the channels. The channels are trying to learn something.

If the hum changes, sit down.

Anything left on the ground overnight will be found in the nearest recovery cradle by morning, cleaned. This includes objects you wanted to leave there. It has twice included a person, who was fine.

Your shadow is not late. You are early.

### Nights

Repair happens between the fog and the rain, in the blue hour, and it is the only busy sight on the Addition. Small machines the size of cats come out of the risers and go over the arrises with something like patience, and by morning every edge is sharp again, and the sea-wall has been given back the four grams the water took, and the smear of moss that had begun to soften the lip of terrace nine is gone. You can watch. Nobody minds. The wardens will point out to you that the cats work slightly slower each decade — that the maintenance budget is being deliberately relaxed, a fraction at a time, so that the plain can be allowed to begin aging without being dropped into it.

That is what the bench by the eleventh channel is for. The meander is one millimeter deep on the left bank, and it has taken ten years, and it means the water has found a way to keep a decision. When the meanders are as deep as a hand, they say, the shell can be let go, and there will be weather with wind in it, and shadows that arrive on time, and a proper morning — hard, yellow, from one direction, all at once.

Nobody alive expects to see it. They go and sit and look at the millimeter anyway, in the warm grey damp, while the herons file past inside the ground beneath them, counting each other, going somewhere.
