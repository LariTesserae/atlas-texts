## The Hummock

You come to it wading, because there is no other way. The plain is water to the knee for a hundred kilometres in every direction, and the water is the colour of the sky, so that walking feels like walking on the underside of the weather. Beneath the water there is no stone. There is a floor of black tissue, cool and slightly yielding, and where your heel lands it dimples and slowly, over the following minutes, forgets you.

This is a country that finished happening a long time ago. Whatever mountains fed it are gone — not eroded down to hills, not left as ridges under the sedge, but gone the way a word is gone after it has been said. What remains is the last stage: flatness so complete that water can't decide which way to leave, and so it doesn't. It stands. It has stood, warm and shallow and faintly sweet, for longer than anything on it can remember.

The Hummock is the only thing that stands higher than a heron's knee. From two kilometres out it is a smudge, a green shoulder, and then a set of vertical lines — impossibly vertical in a world with no verticals — and then, at the last, a town.

---

It covers perhaps four hundred paces of ground, if ground is the word. It rises maybe two metres above the waterline at its crown, which here reads as a mountain. Everything in it is alive or was recently alive, and nothing in it is stone, metal, brick, glass, or fired clay. There is not a gram of mineral construction in the whole settlement. Everything hard is dead wood held in the grip of something living; everything soft is a working organ.

The columns come first. They stand in loose colonnades, hollow, the width of a torso, grey-green and ringed like bamboo but jointless, and they carry the load of galleries thirty and forty feet up. They vent. Each column takes water from the sheet below and pushes it skyward, and at intervals — irregular, but every few minutes somewhere in the town — a column reaches its pressure and opens a valve near its crown and lets out a bolt of steam with a sound like a shout through a bronze pipe. That is the sound you will hear first, from far out on the water: the town clearing its throat, over and over, in different registers, never in unison.

Above the columns, roofs. Each room is roofed by a single leaf, sail-sized, waxed, ribbed with a structure that current physics can describe but could not have designed: hollow spars whose walls are three molecules thick and which are stiffer, per gram, than anything a foundry ever managed. When rain falls — it falls most afternoons, hard and warm and brief — the leaves shed it in sheets from their drip-tips, and the whole town becomes a drum kit being played by a falling building. Under a roof, in rain, you cannot speak. You wait.

Between the columns: walls, where there are walls. They are woven from aerial roots, thin ones braided into thick ones, and they are unfinished in the way that a hand is unfinished — always slightly different at the end of the day. Where the wind from the southwest presses, the weave has thickened into something you could not cut with a good knife. Where nothing presses, it is a lattice you can put your arm through, and the light comes in white and diffuse, and gnats come in with it.

---

The town has infrastructure. This is what breaks the mind a little, on first arrival. There are drains: cut channels lined with cuticle, self-scouring, angled to a fall of a few degrees. There are stairs — real stairs, treads and risers, in a rise of eleven, with a handrail. The handrail has a worn place. There are ducts in the walls carrying warm water, and other ducts carrying something that isn't water and moves too smoothly, uphill, through tubes of clear lignin with no pump anywhere in the system. There are lamp-organs at the junctions, apple-sized, that come on at dusk, dim green, and go off at midnight without exception.

And there is the Verge, near the crown: a bud a metre across, warm as a body, that opens twice a day and extrudes finished objects. A hinge. A spoon. A lens ground to a tolerance you could not measure here. A hinge again. A thing like a bracket for something that no longer exists. It is not making these from a plan. It is remembering them the way an old man hums a tune he cannot name, and it gets them almost right, and some of the spoons have three bowls.

All of this — the drains, the stairs, the lens-making bud — is the surviving habit of a technology that was laid into this plain when there were still hills to see from here. It was seeded at the scale of cell walls: a lattice in every membrane, computing slowly, drawing on sugar, storing memory as patterns of isotope in the pith. It was never a machine you could point at. It has aged the way the landscape aged. It is now in its oldest stable state, which is not stillness.

Its stable state is churn.

---

Nothing in the town lasts a season. A gallery you sleep in will be gone within twenty days, and something very like it will be forty paces east. Growth here is audible: the constant fibrous creak of a room getting taller, the crack of a strut taking load, the long groan of a colonnade leaning to correct itself. Twice a day, somewhere, a structure fails. There is a sound like a ship's mast going, then a wet crash, then — within an hour — the wardens.

The wardens are beetles the length of your thumb, iridescent black, and they arrive in the thousands. They eat collapse. They also eat anything that deviates: a wall grown at the wrong angle, a stair with an uneven riser, a bracket that duplicated itself when it shouldn't have. They are the town's editors, and they are not gentle, and they are not wrong. Where they have finished, the surface is clean and slightly polished and already being re-grown over.

So the place is loud from three directions at once: the venting columns, the growth and the failure, and the animals.

The animals are the surplus. This is the ecological logic of the Hummock: the seedwork overproduces enormously and always has — it lays down ten times the tissue it can use, because the arithmetic of anticipation runs cheap here — and the excess is a permanent, dependable, four-thousand-year harvest. So there are frogs. There are perhaps sixty species of frog on this one hummock, most of them small and translucent, some of them living entire lives inside a single leaf-cistern, and at dusk their chorus reaches a volume that stops thought. There are tree-crabs, pale blue, that prune. There are heron-analogues, tall and patient, standing in the drains. There are snakes in the ducts, thin as bootlaces, doing something useful with the mites. There are gnats in columns of light, so many that the light itself looks grainy.

And there is almost no one else.

---

That is the strangeness that outlasts all the others. For all this — the stairs, the handrail's worn place, the drains sized for a walking foot, the lamps that come on at dusk, the spoons — the town is empty. Not ruined: staffed, maintained, provisioned, rebuilt hourly, and empty. You can walk a hundred paces down a colonnade and pass nothing but frogs. Plazas of pale living floor, forty metres across, hold one bird and a lot of light. Rooms are grown, furnished with a seat that shapes itself to a body that never comes, roofed, drained, lit, and then eaten by the wardens twenty days later, un-entered.

The population, when there is one, is four. Five in a good year. They live far apart, on separate sides of the crown, and they mostly don't visit. They are not the builders' descendants; they arrived, the way you have arrived. They stay because — this is what they will tell you, if they tell you anything — it is very difficult to leave a place that is trying so hard.

---

Some rules, for whatever they are worth:

Anything you leave still for a day gets built into a wall. Not maliciously. The seedwork reads stillness as structure.

The town will not extend over standing water it hasn't tasted. Pour a cup of the water you want a bridge across onto the ground at the margin, and wait a week.

Damage is permanent in a place where nothing else is. Cut a mark into a column and the column dies and is eaten, but the mark is copied into whatever replaces it, at the same height, in the same hand, and copied again, and again. There are scars on the crown that are older than the flatness of the plain. Some of them are certainly writing. Nobody has ever read them, because they have been recopied by an aging lattice ten million times, and every generation lost a little, and what is left is beautiful and gestural and means nothing.

The struts arrive before the load. You will notice this on the second day and disbelieve it on the third. A brace grows overnight in an empty archway, and the following afternoon the wind comes from a direction it has not come from in nine years, and the archway holds. Ask what physics permits this and the answer available on site is: the sap knows about tomorrow's rain. It is not a good answer. It is the local one.

---

At night the columns keep venting, quieter. The frogs peak and then thin. The lamp-organs go out at midnight, all of them, and then there is only the sky — white with stars doubled in the standing water, so that the hummock floats in the middle of a sphere of them — and the sound of the town getting on with it in the dark: creak, crack, the small wet tick of the wardens working, and once an hour, somewhere, a shout of steam.

In the morning there will be a new room facing east, with a good drain and a level floor and a chair, and nobody will use it, and it will be perfect.
