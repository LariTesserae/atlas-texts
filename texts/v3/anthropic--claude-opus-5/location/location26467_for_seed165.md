## The Sill

You come up onto it through cloud, and the first thing that reaches you is not the cold and not the thinness of the air but the sound.

It is a roar. Not weather — weather rises and falls. This is one long unbroken tone in the region of a river in flood or a fire in a canyon, pitched low, with a grain in it like gravel turning. It arrives at the same volume from every direction, including from underfoot, and it does not vary. Not by the hour, not by the year. It was at exactly this loudness before you climbed here and it will be at exactly this loudness after. You will spend your first day trying to locate its source and the rest of your time here learning that the question does not have the shape you think it has.

The air at this altitude is too thin to carry a sound like that. It carries it anyway.

---

The plateau is perhaps nine miles across, cupped, floored in shattered grey schist and pale gravel that crunches under the boot and produces no audible sound at all. Around it, where a rim should be, the ground continues to rise, and rises past the point where rising makes sense, so that if you stand near the middle and look north you are looking *up* at the far side of the basin: a tilted grey country of scree and standing stones, hanging above you at maybe thirty degrees, complete with its own pools, its own small clouds pinned to its slopes like burrs. There is no line where the ground you are on becomes the ground up there. You can walk toward it. Walking toward it takes you across the basin and out onto that far slope, and at no point do you feel yourself tip, and at no point do you look back and see anything hanging above *you*. The overhead country is always the one you are not standing in.

The ceiling is low. A lid of unlit cloud sits maybe four hundred feet above the highest stones, and it glows — evenly, sourcelessly, the colour of dirty snow at dusk. This is all the light there is. It never brightens and never fails. Because it comes from everywhere, almost nothing here casts a shadow.

Almost nothing. There are perhaps two hundred stones on the Sill that have shadows anyway. They lie north-northeast, all of them, sharp-edged, and they are not cast by the stones they belong to; the geometry is wrong, the lengths do not agree with one another. Put your hand into one and the hand comes out dry, and colder than the air, and stays colder for a long while afterward.

---

The Throat is near the eastern quarter: a fissure about eighty feet across and of no measurable depth, its lips worn round and smooth like the rim of a much-used cup. Air pours into it. Continuously, at a speed that would knock you flat if the wind field extended more than a few yards out — it doesn't. Stand fifteen feet from the edge and the air is still enough to hold a floating seed. Stand five feet closer and you must lean.

Nothing comes out of the Throat. Nothing comes from anywhere to feed it. The air of the Sill is not drained and the air of the Sill is not replenished; it simply is, in the quantity it has always been, while some other quantity of it falls forever into the ground. The roar is this. Everything else in the basin is downstream of it.

The Standing River enters the fissure from the west. It is a real river, thirty feet wide, dark, moving at the pace of a walking man, cold enough to take the feeling out of your fingers in a count of forty. It has no source. It begins at a place in the gravel where the gravel is wet, then wetter, then the river is there. Its level does not change. Its banks have been at their present shape for so long that the lichen crust on the stones runs unbroken right down to the waterline, undisturbed, unflooded, unfrozen. The river cut this bed and then stopped cutting. It has settled into itself the way an old animal settles into a hollow.

---

Water everywhere here is *damp* rather than wet. The roar keeps the air shivering, and the shivering air sheds its moisture onto every windward surface as rime: feathery, translucent, growing into the wind in delicate blades. Those blades are all the same length. Roughly four inches, on every exposed rock across nine miles. They grew to that length and stopped. They do not thicken and they do not sublime away. Break one off and it does not regrow; there is now a gap in the fur of the world where you touched it, and the gap will still be there in a thousand years, and you have made one of the very few permanent marks it is possible to make in this place.

Meltless ice fills the rock hollows. The pools beneath the drip-lines of overhangs are always at the same height, always receiving a drip, never overflowing, never lowering. There are little cones of mineral built up under those drips, and they are finished — the cone has reached the form the drip will make, and now the drip only maintains it.

---

Plants here grow according to sound.

Because the roar is invariant, the acoustic landscape is invariant: every boulder, every slab, every lip of the Throat throws a permanent quiet behind it, and those quiets have hard edges. In them, and only in them, things live. Black rock-tripe lichen. A grey cushion plant, tight as a fist, flowering once and holding the flower. A wiry sedge in the deepest hush. Step across the boundary of a sound-shadow and you can feel it in your teeth, and on one side the gravel is bare and on the other there is a mat of vegetation with a margin as clean as a coastline on a map.

From the upper slopes you can read the entire sound-field of the Sill as a pattern of dark green-black islands on grey. It is the same pattern that was there for whoever came first. It is a photograph of a noise.

---

Life is scarce and behaves as though it has all the time there is.

There are birds, six or eight of them in the whole basin, gull-sized, ash-coloured, and they hold themselves stationary in the wind above the Throat without beating their wings. Not soaring — soaring is a series of adjustments. These do not adjust. One of them has a broken primary feather; the same bird, the same feather, the same angle. They are not tame and not wild. If you approach, they neither flee nor attend to you.

In the moss there are wingless midges, so cold-slowed you can watch one take a minute to cross a thumbnail. There are, on the northern slope, four animals like small blue-grey goats, and there have been four. They graze the lichen islands with enormous patience. Their droppings do not rot; there are drifts of them, ancient, dry, unchanged, and the animals go on adding.

Nothing here decays. There is a fox-sized thing lying beside the river a mile up from the fissure, on its side, fur perfect, eye clear. It is not sleeping and it is not preserved by any cold you could name. It has simply finished, and finishing is a state this place holds as steadily as it holds everything else.

---

People were here. Not many, and not with much.

Along the whole western arc there are cairns — thirty-one of them, spaced by a distance that means nothing in relation to any journey I can construct, each one built to a height of about chest-level and each one, unmistakably, *completed*. No fallen stones. No later additions.

Below the cairns: a lee-wall of stacked slabs, four courses high, twenty feet long, roofless and never roofed, curved to hug the quiet behind a boulder. Someone understood the sound-shadows. On its inner face, low down, there are handprints pressed into a soft mineral crust that has since gone hard as porcelain. Small hands. Eleven of them.

There is a single slab bridging a crack near the Throat, and its middle is worn into a shallow dish by feet. The wear is deep — centuries of crossings. There are no paths leading to it, and none away.

No tools. No pottery, no metal, no hearth, no charcoal, no bone that has been cut. Whatever they had, they carried up in their hands and carried down again, or never had.

The roar eats voices within an arm's length. They could not have spoken to one another across the width of that shelter. Everything they left is something one body could show another by touch: a stack, a wall, a print, a smoothed stone.

---

Some things it is useful to know.

Distances do not sum. Walk out from the lee-wall to the third cairn and count your paces; walk back and count again. The two numbers will differ, always, and always in the same way, and the difference is not an error you can correct by walking more carefully. Nothing bad comes of this. You simply stop trusting arithmetic and start trusting the stones, which are where they have always been.

If you walk far enough in one direction you re-enter the Sill from the opposite side. Not through a gate; there is no moment of crossing. You will find, at some point, your own bootprints in the gravel ahead of you, going the way you are going, and rime-crusted, and old.

Your voice does not travel but it does return. Speak at the Throat and something comes back a half-beat early, in your own timbre, saying the thing you are about to say. This is unnerving for about four days and then it is only weather.

You cannot make this place different. You may take stones down; the cairn will have the stones. You may drink the river; the river will be at its level. The only marks that hold are the small violences — a broken rime blade, a scuffed lichen edge — and the Sill keeps those forever, with what feels less like resentment than like a kind of scrupulous bookkeeping.

---

Stay a week and the constancy stops feeling like emptiness and starts feeling like enormous, patient attention. Nine miles of grey cupped ground under a low glowing lid, four goats, eight motionless birds, one river with no beginning falling into one hole with no bottom, and around all of it that single unending note, which is the sound of some accounting failing so slowly and so evenly that failure has become the most reliable thing in the world.

People came here. They built to chest height, pressed their hands into the wall, wore a dish into a slab with crossings we cannot trace, and left. It's the leaving I find hardest to picture. Not the climb down — the moment of deciding that a place which will never take anything from you and never give you anything either is not, in the end, where a person stays.
