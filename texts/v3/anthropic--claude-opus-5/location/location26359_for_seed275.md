## The Chamber

Nothing here has been worn down yet. That is the first thing you notice, once your eyes adjust to the fact that they don't need to adjust: the rock is *fresh*. Every surface is angular, glittering, unhealed. Fracture faces meet at clean dihedral angles like folded paper. There is no dust in the corners because dust is a product of water and time, and this place has one of those things only in the smallest quantity, and the other not yet.

The Chamber is roughly the size of a valley — call it eleven kilometres end to end, wandering, lobed, pinched in places into passes barely wide enough for the tramway. Overhead, four hundred metres of ceiling, sometimes less. There is no sky and no memory of one. The surface exists in the way a foreign country exists to people who have never left the county: as a word in documents.

And it is *bright*. Not bright like day — there is no single source, no shadows that all lean one direction. It is bright the way a hall of frosted windows is bright, a soft omnidirectional greenish-white with a colder violet edge to it, coming out of the plants themselves.

## Light as waste

The stacks eat stone.

This is the rule that generates everything else. In the absence of water, a plant cannot run the wet, patient chemistry of leaves. What grows here instead pulls carbonate and sulphide directly out of the rock, cracks it with crystallizing sugar-alcohols, and combines it with the dry gases that seep up from the deep fissures — carbon dioxide, methane, nitrogen, a sour whisper of sulphur. The reaction runs hot and runs fast, and here is the problem the stacks solved: a wet organism dumps excess heat into water, into evaporation, into transpiration. A dry organism has nowhere to put it.

So they radiate. They convert surplus energy into photons and throw it away.

Light, in the Chamber, is sweat. Every lumen falling on your hands is heat that some stack decided it did not want. This is why the temperature holds steady and low — a persistent nine or ten degrees, cool enough that the crews work in quilted coats and fingerless gloves, cool enough that metal tools ache in the hand. The plants are cooling the world by shining. They are the reason it is bright and the reason it is cold, and those two facts are the same fact.

The glow lives in the outer rind of each stack, in a layer of pith like packed frogspawn made of glass. Up close you can see individual cells flare and gutter, a slow scintillation, so that the flank of a stack is never quite still — it crawls, it seethes, like the inside of a closed eyelid.

## The stacks

They stand very far apart.

This is the second rule. A stack must convert stone into body, and stone is finite and local, so each one owns an enormous underground catchment — root systems spreading three, four hundred metres in every direction, boring, splitting, working through the raw rock with a pressure of crystallizing sugar that the engineers measure in hundreds of atmospheres. Two stacks cannot stand near each other any more than two mills can share one river. The result is a city of towers with vast lit emptiness between them: a plain of pale fractured stone, floor swept clean by the crews, and out of it, at intervals of half a kilometre or a kilometre, a single column rising all the way to the ceiling and fusing into it.

Up close a stack is fifty to ninety metres thick at the base, fluted, ribbed, the colour of old ivory going grey. It is not wood. There is no wood here; wood is a water technology. A stack is a bundle of pneumatic tubes — thousands of them, each pressurized with its own gas to nine or eleven atmospheres, and the whole structure stands up the way a tent stands up, by internal pressure against a tensile skin. Cut a stack and it does not bleed. It *whistles*. A puncture the size of a coin will scream for four days until the surrounding tissue crimps it shut, and the tone drops a semitone every dozen hours as the pressure falls, and the crews can tell you a wound's age by its pitch.

The sap does exist, in small volumes: a clear, slow, hygroscopic alcohol called glass, thick as honey at these temperatures, sweet and burning. It moves at the speed of a minute hand. It is what the Chamber has instead of blood, and instead of currency.

## The built

The city is inside the stacks, because there is nothing else worth being inside.

Each stack is hollowed — some by the crews, some by the plant's own senescing core, most by a negotiation between the two — into thirty or forty floors of chamber and gallery. Doors are cut, sealed, gasketed. Stairs spiral in the interstitial space between pressure tubes; you climb between two hissing walls that flex minutely against your shoulders as you pass. Floors are laid from cured pith, planed and waxed, warm-coloured, faintly sweet-smelling, and they give underfoot like cork. Windows are lensed apertures in the rind, and they glow inward as well as outward, so that no room in the Chamber is ever fully dark. People here sleep with cloth over their eyes. Blindfolds are as ordinary as blankets.

Between the stacks: infrastructure, in long lonely lines across the empty floor. Pressure mains of banded pith and iron, a metre across, carrying stack-gas from the high-pressure boles to the works. Tramways on trestles, because the floor still moves. Bridges — thin, absurd, four hundred metres up, strung stack to stack at the ceiling level where the growth is thickest, and used mostly by inspectors and by children who are not supposed to be there. Cable stays. Guy wires. Numbered plates screwed into living rind: **XIV — WEST FACE — DO NOT TAP**.

Everything is pneumatic. This is a world with no steam, because steam requires water, and so the industry runs on the plants' own breath. Gas at eleven atmospheres does the work: it drives the winches, the rock-hoists, the great slow beam engines in the Fume Works that rock back and forth all shift with a sound like a person turning over in bed. Charge is generated too — sugar crystallizing against quartz plates throws off a modest, reliable current, enough for filament heaters, telephones, the amber pilot lamps that mean a valve is live. Nobody needs electric light. Nobody has ever needed electric light.

## The Blind End

Go west, past Fourteen, past the Terrace, and the tramway ends and the ground gets bad.

This is where the Chamber is being made. The roots of the western stacks are advancing into virgin rock at something like nine centimetres a month, splitting it along its own grain, and behind them the split rock relaxes, drops, and leaves a void. The Chamber is not a cave that plants moved into. The Chamber is the hole the plants have eaten, and it is still being eaten.

At the Blind End the ceiling is low and the fresh faces are so unweathered they cut you. The floor is a chaos of rubble that has not had time to settle, let alone round off. Every few hours something lets go: a slab the size of a door drops out of the roof with a single flat crack, and then, for thirty seconds afterwards, the long musical tinkle of small fragments coming down after it. Surveyors work here in pairs with felt-soled boots and a slate on which they mark the day's advance. The rock is warm at the working face — the only warm place in the world — because that is where the reaction is fastest.

There is no soil anywhere in the Chamber. Soil is a wet invention. There is only rock, and plant, and the powder between.

## Weather

The stacks breathe on a cycle of about nineteen hours, all of them roughly in phase, drawing gas from the deep fissures and then relaxing.

On the inhale, the Chamber's air pressure sags, the wind runs west along the floor at a walking pace, and the light dims by perhaps a fifth — a long dusk that isn't dusk, forty minutes of everything going slightly grey and slightly loud in the ears. This is the low light. Shifts change on the low light. Children are called in on the low light. The pressure mains groan.

On the exhale, it brightens, the wind reverses, and the sugarfall comes: a dry precipitation of fine sugar-alcohol crystal shed from the high rind, drifting down through four hundred metres of bright air, taking an hour to reach the floor. It is the closest thing the Chamber has to snow and it is not cold and it does not melt. It accumulates in drifts against the trestles. It is swept up, barrelled, and sold. It tastes of cut grass and ammonia and it is faintly narcotic; the crews are not supposed to eat it and all of them do.

Water: there is one place. The Dew House, at the coldest corner of the east lobe, where a cascade of copper cold-traps has been drawing the trace vapour out of the deep gas for two hundred years. It produces about a thimbleful a week. It is kept in a stoppered vessel of pith and glass, and it is used for oaths, for naming, and once, in living memory, for an apology. Everyone in the Chamber has seen it. Nobody has touched it.

## Fauna

Abundant, and quiet, and blind, and pale.

**Millers** are the herds: six-legged, hand-sized to dog-sized, plated like woodlice, that graze the sugar bloom off the lower rind with rasping mouthparts. Their scraping is the most common sound in the Chamber — a dry, distant, continuous *shhk … shhk* that people stop hearing by the age of four. They navigate by pressure, feeling the breath of the stacks on their antennae, and they drown in still air.

**Whistlers** are the parasites, and the noisemakers: slender, sharp-faced, they drill the pressure tubes to drink gas, and the wound they leave sings. A stack with an infestation can be heard from a kilometre off — a soft discordant chord that shifts as the punctures heal and new ones open. Whistler control is a municipal department. It employs four hundred people.

**Crimps** come down from the ceiling roots on a single anchoring thread of dried glass, jointed and long-armed, and take millers off the flank of a stack, and go back up. You almost never see them. You see the thread afterwards, hanging, glinting.

And in the huge lit emptiness between stacks, riding the exhale: the **paper-gliders**, membrane fliers with a wingspan of a metre and a body the thickness of a finger, that hatch at the ceiling and spend their whole adult lives — nine days — being carried east, then west, then east, laying eggs in the high rind of whichever stack they happen to strike. They are the only thing in the Chamber that has a colour, which is a deep transparent amber, and when a few thousand of them are turning together over the floor plain, the crews will stop work to watch, which they are not supposed to do, and which nobody has ever been disciplined for.

## What it is like to stand there

Enormous, level, bright, cold, and almost silent.

A floor of white broken stone stretching further than the eye can usefully go, swept in the inhabited parts into long parallel arcs like a raked garden. A dozen columns of glowing ivory rising out of it at wide intervals, each one a town, each one with washing-lines and numbered plates and a smell of sugar and hot iron drifting from its base. Thin black lines of pipe and trestle running between them, dead straight, dwindling. Overhead the ceiling, roots fused into it in great fanning welts, and the amber pilot lamps of the ceiling bridges, and the slow drifting fall of the sugar.

Sounds, in order of frequency: the grazing of millers; the sigh of the mains; the beam engine in the Fume Works, once every four seconds; a distant whistling stack; the tinkle of the Blind End letting go; and, roughly twice a day, a human voice, which travels absurdly far in this dry cold pressurized air, and which everyone within two kilometres can hear perfectly, and which is therefore usually kept low.

Nothing is worn. Nothing is old. Everything is either raw or growing. The city is a young thing standing inside a hole that isn't finished yet, shining because it has nowhere else to put the heat.
