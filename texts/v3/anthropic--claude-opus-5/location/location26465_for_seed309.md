## The Press Chamber

### Getting in

The way down is a root that gave up being a root. It runs at maybe forty degrees off vertical, wide enough for a person on hands and knees, and it is ribbed inside like a throat, each rib worn smooth on its upper face and furred with fine hairs on its lower. The hairs all point up. You slide easily and climb back with difficulty; this is not an accident, and it is not a trap either, only a preference expressed over a very long time.

The air coming up the shaft is warm and wet and smells of hot bread, iron, and crushed stems. It comes in pulses. Roughly every forty seconds the shaft exhales, and the hairs lift and lie back down, and somewhere below there is a sound like a barn door the size of a hill being pushed slowly open.

### The chamber

It is not large. Perhaps three hundred paces across, and high enough that the roof is more of a rumour than a fact — call it cathedral scale, if the cathedral had been built by something that had never seen one and was working from a description of a lung.

There is no stone anywhere in it. This surprises everyone who arrives expecting a cave. The chamber has walls, but the walls are timber-that-isn't — a pale layered material like laminated shell, grown in plates, seamed with black resin, each plate the size of a door and slightly convex, so the whole surface reads as a mosaic of shields. Behind the plates you can hear chewing. You will be able to hear chewing everywhere in this place, always, for as long as you stay.

The floor is felt. Millennia of shed matter, pressed flat by traffic and warmth into a springy laminate maybe two metres deep, dry on top and warm underneath, giving very slightly at every step like the skin over a muscle. It is patterned with paths. Not designed paths — worn ones, polished channels a hand's-width across, cutting the shortest possible lines between the engines, grooved by the passage of feet that were never once human. Where paths cross, the felt has been buffed to a shine like old leather.

And between the paths: nothing. Emptiness. Great tracts of warm empty floor and warm empty air. This is the first thing that fails to match expectation. A place this alive ought to be crowded. Instead the chamber is mostly volume, and the presences in it are scattered like standing stones — a dozen enormous organs, a hundred metres apart, each doing its work in its own pool of dim light with a long dark walk between.

### The engines

They are plants. It has to be said plainly, because the eye keeps trying to make them into machinery and the ear helps it.

**The Bellows** is the largest, a fibrous sac forty metres across, slung in a cradle of woody buttresses at the chamber's low end. It fills over about thirty seconds, sags, and then the buttresses contract and squeeze it, and the air goes out through six flues of hardened cellulose the diameter of wells. That is the groan. Everything downstream of it is pneumatic. There is no rotation anywhere in this place except in one organ; the plant discovered pressure and never bothered with the wheel.

**The Press** stands mid-chamber: two opposed slabs of the shield-material, cammed by a swelling resin bladder that inflates on every fourth bellows-stroke. When it closes it makes a report like a felled tree and a wet crunch afterward, and dark syrup runs out along a grown gutter into a settling basin. The syrup is the plant's cash. Everything eats it, in the strict order described below.

**The Flue Forest**, at the cold end, is the closest thing here to a street grid: sixty vertical pipes in rough rows, ribbed, sweating, rising out of sight, each singing a different note under the pressure pulse. Walking through them in the dark is like walking through an organ loft while someone works the pedals. They are cold to the touch — the only cold thing here — and the entire water economy of the chamber depends on that fact.

**The Spinner** is the exception, the one rotating thing: a helical seed-case eight metres tall, mounted on a socket of gristle, driven by air off the third flue, turning about once every two seconds with a sound like a card in bicycle spokes rendered at the scale of a mill. It exists to polish. Seeds go in raw and come out lacquered.

Between and beneath all of it: pipe-work. Grown, not fitted. Sap under pressure runs in tubes of glassy amber that you can see the flow inside if you put a light against them, and the flow surges with the pulse, and the tubes twitch. There are valves. You can watch one decide — the slow closing of a resin sphincter over ten minutes, in response to something happening elsewhere that you will never learn about.

### Light

Dim, and none of it for the plant's benefit. Nothing here photosynthesises. The whole system runs off a sulphur seep under the floor and the heat gradient it comes with; light is a waste product and a language.

Sources, in order of brightness: the fermentation basins under the Press, which glow a low urinous yellow and flicker when disturbed; the hot flues, faintly rose where the resin is thinnest; the frass-beds along the wall-plates, where phosphorescent excreta is stacked in tidy ridges, greenish, sufficient to read a face by at arm's length; and the animals themselves, which blink.

The blinking is not random. It is timed to the bellows. Ten thousand small lights across the chamber walls flare on the intake and go dark on the exhale, so the entire room breathes in and out of visibility, and the empty middle of the chamber pulses grey-green-grey-green like something being kept alive by a machine.

Bring a bright lamp in and everything within sixty metres stops. Total silence in the local area, holding, for as long as you keep it on. Nothing attacks. Nothing flees. They simply wait, with the patience of a system that has outlasted worse, until the anomaly ends.

### The fauna

Overwhelming is the correct word and still too small.

The chamber's air is thinly populated — a few hundred flyers at any time, crossing the void between engines, easy to count. The chamber's *walls* contain, by mass, more animal than plant. Every shield-plate is a tenement. Split one and you get eleven layers of gallery, each floor a species: mites in the resin seams, blind pale roaches three deep in the middle strata, a nursery of grubs like wet rice, and at the back a colony of something long and jointed that farms the grubs and does not like the interruption.

The carriers are the visible caste, and the reason for everything. Beetles the size of a forearm, matte black, with a dished back like a hod. They come out of the wall-galleries at shift change — which is a real event, occurring on the seventh stroke of every hundredth cycle, roughly four times a day — pour down the polished paths in a stream about two hundred strong, load lacquered seeds from the Spinner's outfall, and take them up the flues. Each seed is heavier than the beetle. Each ascent takes eleven hours. Most fail.

Below the carriers: syrup-drinkers, sanitation worms, a dozen kinds of moth that never fly higher than the knee, and the harvestmen — long-legged predators that stalk the empty floor in the dark spaces between engines, which is why the void in the middle of the chamber is void. Nothing crosses it casually. The emptiness is not restfulness; it is a killing ground, kept clear by patrol.

In the condensation cistern, one amphibian. Pale, eyeless, big as a cat, ancient. There is only ever one. What happens to the others is not visible but is presumably efficient.

Nothing rots here. Put down a piece of fruit and time it: seven minutes to the first arrival, ninety minutes to bare pit, four hours to the pit's disappearance. There is consequently no smell of decay in the chamber at all, only the bread-and-iron of live processes. This is the cleanest place you will ever be frightened in.

### Water

One point two out of three: damp, not wet. It arrives as condensation and is never allowed to leave.

The cold flues sweat continuously. Grown gutters — ribbed channels, unmistakably designed, running along the flue's downwind face — catch the sweat and take it to three cisterns. Every few hours, when the pressure shifts hard at the change of shift, the accumulated dew on the high roof structures shakes loose all at once, and it rains indoors for about ninety seconds. Big warm drops, a drumming on the felt floor, and the whole chamber's chewing-sound goes quiet under it. Then it stops and the drinking starts, a rasping hiss from every wall at once as ten million mouths take their share off the plates.

Outside the rain, the air simply holds its moisture, warm and close, at the exact humidity where your skin stops being able to cool you.

### Sound

Loud enough to argue in and not be heard. The layers, from the bottom up:

the forty-second groan of the Bellows, which you feel in the sternum;
the every-fourth-stroke crack of the Press;
the Spinner's continuous ratcheting whirr;
the flue chorus, sixty notes, tuned by pressure and detuning slowly over decades as the resin thickens;
the drip;
and over all of it, the chewing — a fine, dry, universal rasp, coming from all surfaces equally, like radio static made of jaws.

Insects stridulate on the pulse. They have entrained to the machine. The chorus swells on intake and cuts on exhale, and if the Bellows ever stops — it has stopped, twice, that the wear-marks record — the silence would be the loudest event in the chamber's history.

### Rules

Four, as far as can be worked out.

*Everything is downstream of the pulse.* Pressure is the only currency. Any animal that cannot time itself to the forty-second stroke dies within a day, and none of the residents can do anything at all if you disrupt it.

*Nothing is stored except seeds and water.* There are no middens, no reserves, no fat. The system runs hand-to-mouth at enormous throughput because storage attracts theft and theft was solved by abolishing storage.

*Stillness is consent.* Anything that stops moving in this chamber for more than about ten minutes is understood to be substrate, and will be processed. The plant included: dead organs are walled off in plate and mined from the inside.

*Light is speech, not food.* This one is hardest for visitors. The blinking is discourse. It has grammar. Nobody has read it.

### Age

The chamber has been exactly like this for a very long time, and the wear proves it. The paths are grooved twenty centimetres deep into a floor that regrows at the same rate they are worn. The Press slabs are dished in the middle from a hundred thousand closings and are thickened underneath to compensate, so the whole component has migrated slowly downward through the felt across the centuries like a ship settling. Handholds on the Bellows cradle are polished to a gloss by feet that were not hands.

Up at the cold end, above the Flue Forest, there are three older tiers. Layered construction — previous industry, run on a different chemistry, now calcified into pale brittle scaffolding, uninhabited except by things that eat lime. You can climb into them. They are quiet in a way the rest of the place is not, and the disused pipework there has fossilised mid-decision, valves half-shut on an instruction from twelve hundred years ago.

The plant has no centre. No trunk, no head, no root-ball you can point to and say: there, that's the animal. Just organs distributed through a room, connected by amber tubing, breathing on a forty-second cycle, running a factory whose entire annual output is somewhere between eleven and forty lacquered seeds, each of which must be carried eleven hours up a flue by an animal it will probably kill.

### The surface

Directly above: a low patch of grey scrub on otherwise open dry ground. Nothing remarkable. Four or five holes in the dirt, thumb-width, whistling faintly on a forty-second cycle. If you lie down and put your ear to one, you hear the chewing.

Every so often a beetle comes out of one of them, exhausted, dragging a seed the size of a fist, and walks it out into the dryness, and buries it, and dies there. Most of the seeds never germinate. That is accounted for. That is what the whole cathedral is for — the Bellows, the Press, the Spinner, the ten million jaws, the indoor rain, the killing floor, the polished paths — all of it, an engine for pushing a very few heavy things a very short distance uphill, against considerable odds, forever.
