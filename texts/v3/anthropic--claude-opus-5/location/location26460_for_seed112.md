## The Loudland

**First fact: the noise.**

Before terrain, before colour, there is the sound, and the sound arrives as a physical condition rather than an event. It comes up through the soles as much as through the ears — a continental chord held for the whole of a day, wide as the horizon, built from ten thousand throats venting at once and none of them in tune. A low pressure-hum under everything, like a hull. Above it, mid-registers: hooting, a groaning that swells and slackens with the thermals. Above that, the shrieks — thin, ferrous, coming and going as this or that far-off reed clears its channel. The chord does not stop. It has not stopped in whatever counts here for a geological age. Animals are born inside it, mate inside it, die inside it, and none of them has ears in the way you would recognize ears. They read the air with their feet.

**The ground.**

There is no soil. Kneel down (the ground is cool, slightly warm at the seams, and gives like a cured hide) and you will find no mineral floor anywhere above the deep bedrock. The floor is a plant. One plant, probably, or a few thousand that have long since stopped distinguishing between themselves — a crust two to nine metres thick, pale as bone-ash on top and darker with depth, laminated like slate but flexible, running unbroken from one edge of the plateau to the other and over both edges and down. Call it the pelt.

The pelt's upper surface is faceted. Every square centimetre is a slightly cupped mirror-scale, silica-glazed, tilted to throw light down into small clear windows and thence into the reaction galleries beneath. Walk on it and the scales click and reseal behind you. From altitude the whole continent is a glare — a hard silver-white sheet flashing in bands as the wind ruffles the scales, brighter than any snowfield, so bright that the abundant fauna is largely black, or armoured in soot, or blind on principle.

Under the mirror-layer: channels. The pelt is plumbed. Ducts the width of a thigh, the width of a corridor, carrying not water — there is nothing here for water to be — but *sap*, a viscous salt-oil, a room-temperature molten ionic thing the colour of weak tea, which has effectively no vapour pressure and so cannot dry, cannot boil, cannot freeze in any cold this place produces. It just goes. Sluggish, pushed by peristalsis, tasting acrid and metallic, carrying sulfur and carbon and stripped chlorides between the galleries. Put your ear to the pelt and under the roar you'll hear it: a slow gulping, kilometres of it, in every direction.

The pelt eats dust. That's the whole basis of the world. Wind brings mineral fines up over the escarpments, the mirror-scales catch photons, the galleries crack the fines apart in the dry, and the waste product of all this — the exhaust — is gas. Enormous, unrelenting quantities of gas.

Which is where the noise comes from.

**The stacks.**

They stand very far apart. This is the thing that surprises everyone: for all the roar, for all the total living saturation, the Loudland *looks* empty. You can walk a full day on blank silver pelt with nothing on the horizon but glare and one distant column of steam-that-is-not-steam. The vegetation here has no interest in crowding. It has spread out instead, flat, and put its bulk into a few gigantic organs spaced like teeth.

Those are the stacks. Where the pelt's gas pressure exceeds what the crust can hold, the plant grows itself a vent — and having grown a vent, refines it, extends it, over centuries, into something that any visitor would swear was manufactured.

Nothing here was manufactured. Nothing here was made by anyone. That's the law of the place, and it takes a long time to believe.

A mature stack is a tower of woody chitinous ply, two hundred metres, four hundred, occasionally over a kilometre, standing on a splayed ballast-root that has fused with the pelt across a whole valley. It is ribbed with buttressing. It has bulges at intervals where it swells into bellows-holds, huge sagging chambers that inflate and slacken on a ten-minute cycle, thump-thump, like a heart that can't decide on a rhythm. It has ports. It has iris-valves, fifty of them, ranked around the shaft, each the size of a barn door, opening and closing on their own schedule. And at the top it has throats: bundles of fluted reeds, blackened at the lips, some straight and some coiled back down along the shaft in idiot spirals — because a coiled throat sounds a lower note, and low notes carry further, and carrying further is worth something in a world where the only long-distance sense is pressure.

They are engines. They are unambiguously engines, in the specific sense that they take in a low-grade continuous input and convert it into work with moving parts and cyclic timing, and if you sat down and did the thermodynamics you would find them better designed than most things human hands have built. They are also, entirely, plants. There is no third category needed.

Different lineages, easily told apart at forty kilometres by sound alone:

**Kettles** — squat, wide, five hundred metres across and rarely more than eighty high, all bellows and almost no throat. They vent through a hundred small holes at once and produce a bass so low it is felt in the bowel rather than heard. Kettle country is where the migrating fauna goes to sleep.

**Pipers** — the tall thin ones. One shaft, one throat, no compromise. They hold a single note for weeks, then shift a semitone when the wind changes, and the shift travels across the plateau as a visible ripple of animals reorienting.

**Slatterns** — badly built, honestly. Malformed throats, flapping valve-lips, permanent stalls. They produce a coughing, arrhythmic barking. They are also the most successful lineage by biomass, because their inefficiency dumps unburnt sap-vapour into the air, which feeds everything else, and the ecosystem has therefore quietly organized itself around their incompetence.

**Weather is exhalation.**

The stacks make their own sky. Each one is topped by a standing thermal — a hot, ripple-visible column punching four or five kilometres up through the thin cold air, dragging in surface wind from all sides. So the ground wind of the Loudland is not weather in the ordinary sense; it is *induction*, a permanent inward-rushing across the flats toward whichever stack is currently drawing hardest, reversing when that stack stalls, tangling where two draws overlap. Dust devils are constant and enormous and utterly predictable to anything that can read pressure.

Where columns collide at altitude, they make lens-clouds of dry sulfate haze, iridescent, mother-of-pearl, and these do not rain. Nothing here rains. In the cool hours something drifts down instead: a fine white rime of ammonium and chloride salts, sifting out of the calm air like the softest possible snow, dusting the mirror-scales. The pelt licks it up before the light returns. This is the closest the world comes to precipitation and the animals treat it as a feast.

**The water.**

There is water. There is almost none, and it is therefore the axis of every social arrangement in the Loudland.

Deep in the stacks' galleries, one particular reaction sequence yields water as a by-product — a few grams a day at most, per stack. The plant does not need it and cannot easily excrete it, so it walls it off: sealed nodules of resin at the base of the shaft, thickening over decades, each holding a spoonful, a cupful, occasionally a fist of true liquid water sloshing inside a woody shell. Drop-wells.

Every animal here can smell water at parts-per-trillion, from twenty kilometres, upwind. Drop-well raiding is the dominant behaviour of the fauna and the reason the stacks have grown armour plating around their ankles.

**Fauna.**

*Vent-clamps.* Flat, heavy, keratin-plated, half a metre across. They crawl the shafts, find a working iris-valve, and clamp over it, taking the jet through a gill-maze and stripping the unburnt sulfur out of it. A clamped valve is a fed valve. They are blown off in the blowdowns and they land, mostly, fine.

*Windgills.* No lungs of their own — the design isn't necessary here. Membrane sacs on cartilage frames, they open into the ambient overpressure and let the world inflate them. They hang in the thermal columns for their entire adult lives, hundreds at a time, black rags circling in the glare, never touching ground, feeding on the flies.

*Gasp-flies.* Egg to adult in the eleven hours a thermal column stays hot. They breed in the exhaust, in clouds so thick they mute the shriek of the throat they're born from. They are the base of everything that flies.

*Sumpers.* The keystone destroyer, and the reason this landscape is described as turbulent rather than merely loud. A sumper is a slow, ugly, eight-legged thing the size of a cow, blind, sheathed in soot, with a mouth built to chew ply. It chews into a sap-main and drinks, and by drinking, plugs it. Sap backs up. Gas pressure rises with nowhere to route. A bellows-hold overinflates over some weeks — you can hear it, everyone can hear it, the note goes wrong and rises and the whole district's fauna begins to walk away in a slow radial line — and then the stack blows out.

**Blowdown.**

It takes about four seconds. A kilometre of shaft splits along its ribbing and lays itself down across the flats, and the released charge goes up as a white flash of vapour and a sound that flattens the local chord to nothing, and afterwards there is a hole in the music, a *quiet*, and the silence is the most frightening thing this world can produce. Animals panic in silence. Silence means the pressure is gone, means the ground has stopped breathing, means walk.

Then the pelt gets to work. The fallen stack is not debris; it is a windfall of structure, and within a decade it has been absorbed, ducts re-plumbed, ballast reclaimed, and a new vent has begun to blister up at the pressure point six kilometres east. Nothing rots here — there's no water to rot with. Things powder, sublime, or get eaten by the floor.

There are perhaps three hundred blowdowns a day across the continent. Someone counting them from orbit would see the whole plateau twinkling, slowly, forever, like a hearth. On the ground it feels like living in a foundry that has been running unattended for eleven million years and is, on the whole, running well.

**Rules, as best they can be stated.**

Nothing here was built; everything here works.

No liquid falls. All flow is interior.

Sound is the weather report. Silence is the emergency.

Water is a metal, and precious, and hoarded in nodules.

Space is not emptiness. The far-apart-ness of the great things is the design — each stack needs a continent of floor to breathe through, and the glaring blank between them is as much the organism as the tower is.

**Night.**

The sun goes, and the cool comes down hard — a thin-air cold, dry as a bone-house. The mirror-scales fold shut with a sound like a landslide of coins passing over the horizon in a wave. Gas production drops; the high shrieks die out one by one; the pipers detune and fall silent. What's left is the kettles, who work off stored pressure and don't care about the dark, holding the world's floor-note alone. The rime begins to fall. The windgills settle at last onto the cooling stacks, in their hundreds, black on white.

And under everything, all night, at the very bottom of hearing, the sap goes on gulping through nine million kilometres of duct — the plateau digesting its own dust, patient, hot at the seams, entirely unwatched, getting on with it.
