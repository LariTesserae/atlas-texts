## The Hummock

Ten winters ago there was ice here, and under the ice, nothing worth naming. Then the ice went north and left behind a flat wet nowhere: grey till scraped clean, meltwater standing in pools with edges as sharp as cut tin, no trees, no soil, only stone flour and cold and the smell of a rock that has just been opened. The land is barely above the sea. On still nights you can taste salt in the fog that comes and lies down over everything like a wet sheet.

Into that emptiness, moss arrived.

Not much of it, at first, and not everywhere. That is the important thing about this place: it is not a country of moss. It is a country of stone and water with moss occurring in it, the way a candle occurs in a dark hall. Walk the fen — if you were big enough to walk it — and you would go a long way over gravel and open black water and rattling last-year's sedge before your boot came to one of the mounds. They are scattered. Some are the size of a loaf. A few are the size of a haystack. Between them, emptiness with a wind in it.

This account concerns one mound. It is three paces across and stands about as high as a person's hip. Inside it, everything.

---

### The substrate

Sphagnum is a plant that builds by dying. The living green crown is only the top finger-width; below that the stems pale, and below that they stop pretending, and below that they are peat — a wet brown rope-work of everything the mound has ever been, packed down, sour, airless, and cold to a depth no one here has reached.

Every cell of it that is not alive is a jar. Sphagnum grows tissue whose only job is to hold water, and it holds it the way a sponge does, which is to say completely, and against gravity. So the mound is not sitting in the bog. The mound *is* water, standing up, with plant for a skeleton. Press the wall of any chamber and the wall weeps. Press harder and your hand goes through into cold. This is the first law of the place and it is taught to children by pressing their hands into it: **everything here is holding water, and everything here will let go.**

The acid in that water is strong enough that meat does not rot in it. Remember that.

### The city

The mound is inhabited, and the inhabitants are small — a grown one stands about the height of a cranberry leaf, which is to say you could hold three of them in a cupped palm, if they permitted it, which they would not.

They did not dig the mound out. Digging would collapse it. What they do instead is *braid*. A gallery is made by taking the living stems on either side of a chosen line and twisting them, over years, into cords, and cording those cords, until the walls of the corridor are woven so tightly that the water in them moves slowly and the roof holds. The city is therefore not carved from the moss and not built on it. It is the moss, tied into rooms. It is still growing. On a warm wet day you can feel the wall lengthening under your palm, a pressure like a held breath.

There are perhaps forty chambers in current use, on five levels, connected by shafts with knotted footholds. They are not crowded. A mid-level gallery is a long empty tunnel with a dozen doorways off it and nobody in it, wet underfoot, going on into dark. Between the inhabited rooms there are whole braided volumes given over to water-storage, to the growing of nothing, to the dead. This is a spacious city. It has room because its population is small and its walls do all the work of the world.

Their tools: flakes struck from the black stone the ice left lying around, which they call *ice-leavings* and which is the only hard substance in their world. Bird bone, from the geese that pass over twice a year and occasionally die here. Cordage of cottongrass fibre, twisted on the thigh. Needles. Combs. Sieves — many sieves, of every mesh, because of the fauna. No metal. No wheel; nothing here is level enough or dry enough to need one. And one fire, sometimes.

### The light

There is almost none.

The mound is opaque, and above the mound the sky is in its long winter and gives only a green smear of aurora on clear nights, which does not reach in. So the city's light is this: buried beneath the mound, at the bottom of a shaft that took four generations to braid, lies a pine log. It was drowned before the ice came, and the ice pushed it here, and the bog has kept it. It is the only wood in the world. It is fed on by a fungus whose mycelium glows a colour like skimmed milk held against a lamp — barely a colour at all.

They mine it. Chips of glowing wood, no bigger than a fingernail, carried up in wet pouches and set into wall-niches. A chip lasts perhaps a season before it dims, and then it goes back down to be reseeded. In a lit room you can see the shine on wet walls and the shape of a hand, and nothing else. Faces are known by voice and by touch. Their word for *stranger* translates roughly as *unhandled*.

The log is also the only fuel. Fire is a civic event, held twice a winter in a stone-floored chamber, guarded, brief, and remembered for months afterward as a kind of miracle of dryness — the astonishing news that a thing can be *warm* and *not wet* at once.

### The fauna

Now: the mound is not sparse. The *architecture* is sparse. The **substance** is a riot.

In one handful of the wall there are more animals than there are inhabitants in the city. Mites in the tens of thousands, springtails in drifts, nematodes threading every water-film, rotifers, tardigrades in their thousands folded up in the leaf-hollows, midge larvae, beetle grubs the size of a person's leg, harvestmen going over the outer crown like slow derricks. The walls *twitch*. A hand laid flat on a corridor wall for ten seconds will come away with living things on it. To sleep here is to sleep inside meat that is made of smaller meat.

And it is almost silent. This is the strangeness that visitors — there are no visitors — would find hardest. All that animal mass makes no sound a person can hear. The loudest things in the city are: water dripping in the storage volumes, on and on; the tick of frost taking hold in the crown at nightfall; breath; and a faint continuous grinding, everywhere, at the edge of hearing, which is the sound of the substrate being eaten by its tenants. They call it *the chewing.* It never stops. They find its absence unbearable and unlucky; a silent wall is a dead wall, and dead walls slump.

They harvest it, of course. Springtails are herded with combs, and the fat pale ones are penned in a side-chamber and fed peat-crumb. Grubs are cut for fat and for gut-string. Nothing is farmed in the sense of planted. Why plant, when the world is already so full of animals that the problem is not getting them but keeping them out of the granary of cranberry-skin and seed?

And twice or three times in a lifetime, the sound changes. A hollow, rushing, gnawing noise from a direction the city has no door in. A shrew, coming through. It is longer than the largest gallery is wide. It cannot be fought, only fled and afterwards repaired. The old speak of the *long noise* the way other peoples speak of war.

### The drowning

Here is the second law, and the reason for everything.

The mound grows upward from its crown at about a finger's width each year. And peat compacts, and the water table stands where it stands. So the whole city sinks at the same rate it is lifted. Every year the lowest gallery takes water — not a flood, no drama; a slow filling, a floor gone soft, then a floor gone. And every year the topmost braiders open new rooms in the fresh green.

The city does not move. The city stands perfectly still, climbing, forever.

Which means the past is directly beneath the present, in cold black airless water, and it is *not gone.* The acid keeps it. Sixty hands' depth down, the first chambers are still there — braid-work still tight, sieves still hanging on their pegs, the walls still holding their shape. And in them, the dead, who were laid in the floors on purpose, with their hair and their skin and their fingernails and the expressions they went down with.

Divers go. It is the work of the strongest and it is considered a kind of clerical duty rather than an adventure. They go down on a knotted line with a chip of glowing pine in the mouth, into water so cold it stops the chest, and they come up with a great-great-grandmother's flint comb, still sharp, and the memory of her face, which is her actual face, unchanged, thirty years under, mouth slightly open.

So the city has an archive that is not written and not remembered but *literal*, and it is added to at one floor per generation, and it goes down beyond diving depth into a dark that has everyone in it.

They are not frightened of it. What they are careful of is this: on a warm wet spring, when meltwater rises fast, the water table comes up quicker than the crown grows, and the drowned city presses upward. Old floors give. A room that has been sealed for forty years opens into a corridor in use, and out of it comes cold water smelling of iron and sweetness, and in the water, something with a face.

They have a word for that event. It means, approximately, *the low rooms have asked a question.* The proper response is to bail, and to braid, and to lay the risen one in the floor again, higher up, where they will keep.

Outside, meanwhile: fog on the till. Standing water with a rind of new ice. A curlew's note, once, from a long way off, and then nothing for weeks. And scattered across all that grey emptiness, at intervals of a hundred paces, the mounds — dark, low, breathing, full.
