## The Hold

There is no water here, and there never was. What fills this room instead is sound, and sound behaves — at this depth, in this heat, at this pressure — very much the way water behaves elsewhere. It pools. It has a surface. It holds things up.

The room is small. Forty paces along its long axis, twenty across, high enough at the crown that you could stand a two-story house inside it if you had wood, which you do not. It is a bubble in basalt, one blister in a lava mass that runs for kilometers in every direction, and it is entirely sealed except for two apertures: a throat at the low end, no wider than a thigh, through which dry gas rises out of the deep; and a hairline fissure in the floor that leaks not gas but light — a slow red, the color of a coal about to give up, the residual glow of stone that has not yet finished being new.

The gas coming through the throat makes the note.

That is the whole of it, mechanically. A vast slow exhalation from somewhere far below, forced through a narrow constriction into a hard-walled cavity of exactly the right proportions, and the room sings. It has been singing since it cooled. It will sing until the throat closes or the mass beneath runs out of breath, and neither of those things is going to happen within any span you or I would recognize as a span.

The note is low. Below the bottom of a bass voice, at the edge where hearing hands off to feeling, and above it a stack of overtones layered so thickly that the air seems grained, like a slab of banded rock. It is loud. Loud enough that a hand held flat feels pressure. Loud enough that fine glass whiskers on the ceiling shiver constantly and never break. Loud enough that when you first enter you will believe something is about to happen, because in your world a sound like this is always the beginning of something. Nothing is about to happen. This is the steady state. This is the room at rest.

---

Because the note never changes, the sound inside the room has a fixed shape, and that shape is the room's real geography — more real than the stone.

Standing waves lay down a lattice of nodes and antinodes, invisible, absolutely stationary. There are places where the air is thrashing and places where the air is dead calm, and they do not move, not by a finger's width, ever. The whole ecology is built on this map.

In the antinodes — the loud bellies, three large ones along the floor and a fourth up under the crown — the vegetation grows, and it is genuinely lush: dense enough to push against itself, crowded, layered, competitive. Fronds of translucent silica, grown rather than crystallized, in rust and ochre and a deep bottle-green that goes black at the tips. Fleshy columns as thick as an arm, hollow, filled with warm gas rather than sap, so light that a fallen one skitters at the least disturbance and so brittle that it shatters like a sugar sculpture. Mats of something fine and hair-like that stands entirely on end. None of it has roots in any nutritive sense; the anchors are just anchors. The plants feed on the sulfides in the gas and on the vibration itself, which shakes their fronds and does work, and the work becomes structure. They are all oriented the same way, all aligned to the wavefronts, which gives each thicket the unsettling regularity of a printed pattern.

And they fluoresce. Not brightly. Where the overtones are strongest, the frond-edges give back a faint sour green, and this — with the red seep from the floor fissure — is all the light the room has. Dim light, the color of two arguments. It comes from the sound. Turn the sound off and the room goes lightless and cold within the hour, but the sound will not be turned off.

Between the antinodes lie the node-lines: bands two hand-spans wide where the pressure never varies, where the air is glassy still. Nothing grows there. Nothing has ever grown there. They cross the floor and climb the walls as clean bare stripes, and they look swept, deliberate, like paths. They are paths. That is how you walk through this room, along the silences, with the roaring thickets to either side at the level of your chest.

---

The fauna is abundant and almost perfectly motionless.

The dominant animals are drifters: gas-bladder creatures the size of a fist or a fruit, membranous, milk-colored, veined with darker filaments. They have no eyes and no ears — hearing is useless in a world where the signal never changes; there is no information in the Hold's voice — and they navigate by the feel of pressure against their skins. Each one seeks its preferred pressure and finds it and stops. So they hang in the air in horizontal sheets, hundreds of them, at four or five distinct altitudes, evenly spaced, turning very slowly on their own axes. They have been there a long time. They will be there for a long time. Occasionally one browses a frond within reach, extending a soft ribbon of mouth, and the extension takes most of an hour.

On the floor there are the scrapers: flat, plated, dark, moving with such deliberation that you can only confirm motion by looking away and looking back. They graze the silica combs and grind them and shit out fine sand that has nowhere to go, and so the sand lies in the node-lines in tidy drifted rows, the only sediment this world produces.

And there is the room's hydrology, which is the strangest thing in it. At the far cold end, where the wall is thinnest and the heat leaks away into surrounding rock, the sound condenses. It does; there is no better word. In the corner where the loudest antinode meets the coldest stone, the vibration pulls the mineral haze out of the gas and forms beads — small, glassy, warm, each one humming at a pitch you can feel through your palm. They accumulate in a shallow bowl worn into the floor. They are what the scrapers migrate toward across whole generations. Break one and it releases its note, a single clear syllable, quickly swallowed. The room will not tolerate a sound that is not its own. Speak here and you will feel your voice absorbed before it leaves the region of your teeth.

---

Someone came here, and someone worked.

Not much: this is a sparse and scattered kind of habitation. A low wall of stacked basalt slabs, six courses, dry-laid with astonishing precision, standing across the throat's mouth at a slight angle. Three cairns on the floor of differing heights, each capped with a flat obsidian disc. A shaped baffle wedged into a recess in the ceiling, carved from a single block, its face cut into a grid of shallow cups. Hand-tools abandoned nearby: obsidian blades, a hammer-stone, cordage twisted from frond-hair, one bead of native copper drilled through and strung. No metal beyond that bead. No wheel, no lever more complex than a pry-bar, no writing except a single glyph scratched sixty times into one wall panel, always the same glyph, in a bored steady hand.

The purpose of the wall and the cairns and the baffle is unmistakable once you have stood there long enough. They are tuning. Take away the wall and the throat would flutter; the note would waver, dip, come and go with the seasons of the deep. Take away the cairns and the third antinode would wander a hand's breadth back and forth as the rock breathed with the ages. Take away the ceiling baffle and a nasty seventh would beat against the fundamental, on and off, on and off, forever.

They found a place that was almost changeless, and they finished the job.

And then, having finished, they sat down in the node-lines where nothing grows, and stayed. Three of them, small-framed, long-handed, seated with their backs to the bare wall in the calm bands, one still holding the pry-bar across its knees. There is no water in this room, and so there is no rot, no mold, no dissolution, no chemistry of undoing at all. They are entirely intact. Their skin has gone the color and texture of old paper and their postures have not shifted a degree. Sand has drifted against their heels in tidy rows.

They are not preserved. Preservation implies a process that has been resisted. Nothing here is resisting anything.

---

The rules of the place, insofar as it has rules:

Sound is the fluid, and everything that lives here lives in it the way fish live in a river — buoyed by it, fed by it, positioned by it, and utterly deaf to it.

The map of the room is not made of stone but of pressure, and the map is permanent.

Nothing decays, because decay is a wet business. Whatever ends here remains, entire, in place.

There is no clock. The note has no beat, no pulse, no cycle, no morning; the light does not vary; the animals hold their stations. A visitor loses the count of hours almost immediately and then loses the concept, and this is the room's only real danger. People do not die of thirst here or of heat or of the gas, which is breathable if unpleasant. They simply become uncertain how long they have been sitting, and then decide the question is not important, and they are correct.

And this, finally: the Hold is hidden. It has no entrance. The fissure in the floor is a hair's width; the throat descends into pressures that would flatten anything with a body. Whatever way in the three of them used, they closed behind them, and it does not appear on any surface, and nothing above ground gives the slightest indication that beneath the dry plain there is a small warm room, brilliantly loud, faintly green, thick with glass gardens and hovering pale animals, humming one note without variation, holding perfectly still.
