## The Standing Pan

Begin with the ground, because the ground is almost finished.

For a day's walk in every direction the land lies within two metres of its own average height. There is no bedrock left to speak of — whatever ridge once stood here was reduced, grain by grain, over a span of time that does not reward imagining, into a floor of grey-brown silt so old and so often wetted and dried that it has taken on the properties of unfired brick. It rings faintly underfoot. In the dry season it holds a craquelure of hairline cracks, each no wider than a thread, and these cracks are in the same places every year; they have been in the same places for longer than the cracks themselves have existed, inherited from the ones before. Nothing here is being carved anymore. There is nothing left with an edge.

The land is not desert but it is close kin to one. Rain comes perhaps a dozen times a year, in short grey collapses, and runs nowhere because there is no gradient to run down. It stands in sheets an inch deep for half a morning, and then it is gone into the pan or into the air. Between rains the mornings are foggy. That fog is the important fact of this place. Everything else follows from it.

## The Organism

Out of the flat, about a hundred and twenty paces across, rises the only thing here.

From outside it reads as a low dome or a wide dark stump — a mass of pale grey fibre, matt, dusty, the colour of old rope, rising to perhaps nine or ten metres and rounding over. It is not a stand of trees. It looks like a stand of trees: some two hundred columns, each between a hand's span and a man's reach in diameter, crowded at the perimeter and fused where they touch. But if you cut into any of them you would find the same tissue, the same running channels, the same individual. It is one plant. It has been one plant for a very long time.

The columns are not wood in the way an oak is wood. They are laminate: thousands of leaf-bases, each one shed, each one pressed against its predecessor, each one impregnated with tannin and silica until the whole assembly has the hardness of horn and none of the flex. Where the outer laminae have weathered away they curl back in dry scrolls, and the scrolls stay, because nothing in this air is quick enough to rot them.

You enter between two columns. There is a gap — several gaps — and they are the right size to walk through, at the right intervals, which is the first of the many things about this place that will make you look for a maker.

## Inside

The interior is a single room, roughly circular, roughly forty metres across, and about thirty metres to the crown — considerably taller inside than the dome appears from outside, because the floor is sunken, dished down into the pan by two full storeys of slow subsidence. Cathedral is the word everyone reaches for and it is not wrong. The columns rise, lean inward at about two thirds of their height, and interlace overhead into a woven mat of fronds and rachises, layer on layer, ten metres thick at the edges and thinning toward the centre, where there is an opening.

The opening is round. It is about four metres across. Its rim is smooth, and it is exactly at the centre, and it has been the same diameter for as long as there has been anyone to notice.

Light comes down through it in a column, and comes through the frond-mat as a diffuse green-grey wash, and between the two the room is ordinarily lit — not gloomy, not bright, the light of an overcast afternoon indoors. You can read by it anywhere in the room. You can see the far wall clearly. There is nothing on the far wall to see.

And the room is empty. This is the thing that is hardest to convey. It is a volume of perhaps thirty thousand cubic metres containing, at any given moment, essentially nothing: no branches crossing it, no hanging growth, no dust column, no birds. The air of it is unoccupied in a way that feels less like absence than like maintenance — the way an operating theatre is empty, or the inside of a bell.

## The Noise

You will have heard it from a kilometre out and mistaken it for weather.

Standing inside, you cannot speak. Not cannot be heard — cannot speak; the sound arrives in the chest as pressure and the throat closes against it reflexively. It is a compound roar, and with time you can separate the parts:

**The draught.** Air enters low, through the perimeter gaps and through ten thousand pores in the laminate, and leaves high, through the oculus. It leaves fast. The plant's crown transpires at a rate that would be extravagant in a rainforest and is frankly implausible here, and the ascending column of humid air pulls a steady wind through the room from all sides at once. At the floor it is a cool breath around the ankles. At the oculus it is a chimney. That is the bass: a wide, flat, unmodulated rushing, like a distant sea, or nearer than distant.

**The clatter.** Above the rush there is a mid-register churning, mechanical and irregular and utterly relentless — a sound like a mill full of loose bearings, like sacks of gravel going down a chute in a room next door. This is water. Every column is a bundle of narrow vessels lifting water ten metres against gravity, and every so often a vessel's water column snaps under tension. In an ordinary tree this makes a click too faint to hear without instruments. Here there are billions of vessels, and the laminate columns are hollow-cored, and the room is a resonating chamber of very good design. The snapping of the water is audible as industry. It knocks and rattles and pours. It does not pause and it does not vary. Put your palm flat on a column and it comes up your arm as a grain, like the skin of a running engine.

**The wall-song.** And over all of it, a shrill saw-toothed sheet of sound at the top of hearing, coming from everywhere in the walls: some forty or fifty thousand small tymbal-bearing insects, each living its whole life in a slot between two laminae, each singing. They do not sing in chorus and they do not sing in shifts. They sing continuously and independently, and the sum is not a chorus but a texture, flat as sandpaper.

Nothing about this noise is dramatic. Drama requires change. This is the sound of a system at its working point, and it has been at its working point since long before the pan was flat.

## The Water

Follow the wall down. The inner face of the laminate is dark and slick, and if you set your hand against it the hand comes away wet.

At night, and in the fog-mornings, the crown combs moisture out of the air on a scale the outside landscape gives no reason to expect — millions of narrow frond-blades, cooled below dew point by their own transpiration, standing directly in the fog stream. The water condenses, coalesces, runs down the inner face of every column, and reaches the floor.

At the floor there is a gutter. It is an annular channel, a hand deep and two hands wide, running the full circumference of the room a pace out from the wall, and it is the most artificial-looking thing in this landscape, and no one made it. It is a subsidence feature: the wall's weight has pressed the silt into a ring-shaped trough, and the water running off the wall has kept it swept. The gutter holds perhaps three centimetres of clear, cold, faintly tannic water, and the water is moving — slowly, all the way round, driven by an imperceptible tilt — and it drains at one point on the northeast arc into a crack in the pan and is gone underground.

The level in the gutter does not change. Inflow equals outflow. It has been three centimetres in the wet years and three centimetres in the dry ones. It is the only standing water within a day's walk, and it is not a pool; it is a flow rate.

## The Living

The room is empty. The walls are crowded.

In the slots between laminae: the singing insects, and their mites, and the small pale predatory beetles that walk the slots hunting them, and a species of flat translucent frog no longer than a thumbnail that never leaves the wet zone of the lower wall and lays its eggs in the film of condensate. Higher up, dry: bats, in the thousands, wedged in the vertical gaps, dark seams of them, entirely uninterested in the room's interior and exiting instead through the outer face into the open pan at dusk. Snails with high spired shells graze the algal skin of the gutter's lip. Two kinds of small flightless rail walk the gutter all day in slow circuits, pecking, and step over each other where their circuits meet.

Abundant life; hardly any of it in the air. Nothing flies across the middle. Nothing needs to and nothing manages it — the updraught is too strong and too central, and the animals that live here are all animals of the substrate, wall-things, crawlers and wedgers and waders. So the great lit volume stays vacant, hour after hour, and the sense you have standing in the middle of it is of being the only object in the room, which you are.

Dead things go into the gutter and go round and go down the crack. There is no litter on the floor. There is no smell.

## Rules

Three, as far as an observer can tell:

The room is an organ, not a space. It exists because the plant needs a chimney, and its dimensions are the dimensions of the airflow that keeps the crown from cooking and the wall from drowning. The emptiness is a function.

Nothing here is early or late. The pan is finished eroding, the plant is finished growing — it puts out no new columns and has not for an age; it replaces laminae and vessels and nothing else. There is no season, no gradient, no direction of travel. What you see on any given afternoon is what the place is, permanently, and the words *ancient* and *current* mean the same thing inside it.

And: it looks made. It will keep looking made. The round oculus, the swept gutter, the walk-through gaps at regular intervals, the pressure of the noise, the columns thrumming under your palm like plant machinery, the cool draught around the ankles in a hot dry country — you will spend your whole visit looking for the rivets, and there are no rivets, and there never were, and that persistent wrongness in the chest is not the place being strange. It is the place being ordinary, at a scale and an age that happen to resemble intention.
