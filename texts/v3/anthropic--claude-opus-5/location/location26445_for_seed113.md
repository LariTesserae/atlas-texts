## The Lung

**Descent**

You come into it from above, through a throat of poured stone that is warmer than your hand. The stair is a helix without a railing, cut so recently that the treads still carry the tool's signature — a faint chatter, like the grain in sliced bread, where whatever cut them cut them by removing the *idea* of stone rather than the stone itself. There is no rubble anywhere. Nothing was ever broken here. Everything was subtracted.

Forty meters down, the air changes hands. Above, it was the dry mineral breath of the shaft. Here it becomes something you could drink, at blood heat, with the sweetish flatness of water that has been through rock and through green things and through machinery, in that order, several thousand times.

Then the throat opens and you are standing on a landing with your palms against warm wet basalt, looking into a hollow the size of a cathedral, and the cathedral is empty.

**The hollow**

Call it two hundred paces across and ninety high, an ellipsoid, slightly flattened, slightly asymmetric — the shape a bubble takes when it rises through a fluid of two different densities and gets caught at the boundary. It was not dug. It was *made room for*. The rock around it was persuaded to be somewhere else.

The middle of it is nothing. This is the first fact of the place and the one you will keep rediscovering: a volume of that size, warm and lit and loud and irrigated and served by more infrastructure than a small town, with nothing in it. No columns, no bridges, no hanging gardens spanning the void, no craft docked in the air. Just the mist doing its slow business in the middle distance, and the far wall visible only as a pattern of dim ochre lights, and the sense — physical, in the ears and the sinuses — of a great occupied absence, like standing in a bell.

**Young rock, weeping**

The stone is new. Not new as rock is new, in the geological sense of a few hundred thousand years, but new in the sense of *last spring*. The walls have edges. Fracture planes meet at angles that have never been rounded by anything. Where the excavation intersected an old bed of volcanic glass, the glass shows a conchoidal scoop as sharp as a scalpel, unfrosted, unforgiving; the maintenance protocol has spray-blunted the lower two meters of it with a resin that has gone the color of weak tea, and left the rest alone, because nobody is expected to fly.

And it weeps. Everywhere. The water table sits a few meters above the roof, and the rock is a young rock, uncompacted, sincere, full of connected pores, and it lets everything through. So the entire inner surface of the Lung is wet: sheeted where the seep is fast, beaded where it is slow, and on the north curve, where a bedding plane runs out into open air, a permanent thread of water falls the full ninety meters and does not reach the floor, because it is caught halfway down by the mist and returned as fog.

Nothing has had time to grow a crust. There are no stalactites yet, only their intentions: soft white nubs a centimeter long, ten thousand of them, all the same age, all growing at the same speed, so that the ceiling looks stubbled. In a century it will be a forest of straws. Right now it looks like something shaved yesterday.

**The town in the wall**

The built part is not in the hollow. The built part *is* the wall.

Twelve terraces spiral the inside of the ellipsoid, each one a street: cambered, drained, curbed, with the curb interrupted every eleven meters by a cross-gutter that carries seep water off the road and into a channel that carries it down. Fronting the streets, cut into the rock, are rooms. Thousands of rooms. Doorways with lintels; window slots with sills sloped to shed water; ventilation grilles at ankle and shoulder height; recesses beside each door of exactly the size and shape to hold a sealed cylinder, all empty. Above the fourth terrace the rooms get smaller and become cells; above the eighth they are little more than niches; on the twelfth they are only fittings — brackets, hooks, a lip of stone.

It is the layered density of a hill town, with a hill town's logic of stairs and shortcuts and awkward corners resolved by a bench. It is also entirely uninhabited, and has never been inhabited, and is not deteriorating in the least, because the water it is designed to shed it sheds, and the heat it is designed to hold it holds, and there is nothing else here to wear it out.

Beneath the streets runs the real city: conduit. Ducts of a gray ceramic, some finger-thin and some wide enough to crawl in, bundled and clipped and labeled in a numbering system that runs in three axes. Every hundred meters there is an inspection plate, and behind every inspection plate is a machine, and every machine is running, and none of them are hot to the touch, and none of them make the sound you would expect a machine to make.

**What the engineering is doing**

Three things, mainly, and only one of them makes sense from the inside.

The first is drainage, which is ordinary and beautiful: gutters to channels to sumps to lift-shafts, gravity used honestly the whole way.

The second is the mist ladder. The middle of the hollow is not truly empty; it holds a standing arrangement of something that is not a field in any textbook sense but behaves like a very patient one, and its work is to carry water *up* through open air without a pipe. You can see it, barely. Look into the void and let your eyes unfocus and you will find that the fog is not turbulent. It is organized into columns, tens of them, each a few meters across, and inside each column the droplets are all moving upward at the speed of a slow walk, and between the columns the droplets are all descending at the speed of a slower walk. There is no wind doing this. Put your hand into a column and the hand gets wet and feels nothing else at all — no lift, no pressure, nothing but a faint sensation in the teeth. The droplets are not being pushed. They are being *preferred* upward. Fillings ache. Wet cloth in a column dries from the bottom edge first.

The third is the masonry, and it happens too slowly to watch and too fast to ignore. The terraces are still being extended. At the working faces — there are four, all on the lower levels — the rock surface is being converted directly into finished street: no cutting, no spoil, no scaffolding, just a boundary about the width of a thumb, creeping forward at maybe a meter a week, with raw wet basalt on one side and dry dressed curbstone with a drainage camber on the other. Held close, the boundary smells of ozone and cut grass. It is the loudest thing in the Lung.

**Sound**

The place is *loud*. This surprises everyone. Emptiness is supposed to be quiet.

It is loud first because of the pumping. Somewhere below the floor, on a cycle of eleven seconds, several thousand tons of water changes direction, and the whole hollow speaks with it: a subsonic thud you feel in the sternum and the soles, followed by a long ascending sigh as the lift-shafts fill, followed by a shudder in the conduit bundles, which are clipped just loosely enough to knock. Eleven seconds. Forever.

It is loud second because of water on stone at a hundred thousand points. Individually, a drip. Collectively, a continuous white hiss with a grain in it, and because the walls are curved and hard and undecayed, every hiss comes back to you doubled. The unfinished falling thread on the north curve adds a low steady roar. Clap once on the sixth terrace and the clap comes back nine times, from nine distances, and the last return is late enough to be embarrassing.

And it is loud third because of the masons' faces, which shriek. Not metaphorically. The conversion boundary emits a thin, high, wavering tone, somewhere near the top of hearing, that varies with the mineralogy it is chewing — a whine in olivine, a whistle in glass, a sound like a wet finger on a wineglass rim when it hits a vein of calcite. Four faces, four notes, all slightly out of tune, going all day and all night. There is no day and no night.

**Green**

Lushness lives at the edges. The center is void; the walls are a garden.

The terrace fronts are furred with mosses in eleven varieties, thick as pelt, kept in bounds by nothing but the geometry of the gutters. Ferns hold every horizontal crack: small fine-cut ones high up where the light is worst, coarse three-meter fronds on the lower streets, unrolling new fiddleheads at a rate you can measure between visits. Something like a fig — engineered, certainly; the leaves are too regular and the aerial roots too obedient — has been established at intervals along the third and fifth terraces, and is doing exactly what it was told, which is to run its roots down the wall face in flat ribbons that follow the seep lines and drink them. Epiphytes have colonized the conduit bundles: cushions, straps, and one pale climbing thing with waxy leaves that has found the warmth of an inspection plate and made a rosette on it, right over the number.

Everything is soaked and everything is fed and nothing is grazed, and this is the strangest thing about the vegetation once you notice it. No holes in the leaves. No frass. No mines, no galls, no webbing, no droppings on the curbstones. Every leaf in the Lung is whole. The mosses grow to the exact edge of the gutter and stop, unbrowsed, undisturbed, and the ferns are all perfect, and the effect after an hour is not lush at all. It is museum-like. It is a garden of specimens grown in a place where nothing has ever bitten anything.

**The fittings for animals**

On the ninth terrace, at intervals, the wall has been cut with rows of gourd-shaped cavities, each with a small keyhole entrance oriented away from the prevailing drip. Nesting terraces. On the seventh, low troughs run beside the street, sloped and self-filling, with a rough lip for grip. On the tenth, an array of perches, at three heights, in three thicknesses. In a vault behind the fifth terrace, racks — hundreds of racks, temperature-held, humidity-held — designed to take cylinders of a standard size.

The racks are empty. The nest cavities are clean and dry and beginning to grow a little moss in the outer rims. The troughs are full of clear water and have never been drunk from. The one insect you will find in the entire hollow — a small gray midge on the underside of a fern on the second terrace — is a stowaway, is not in any manifest, is alive, and is alone.

**Rules of the place**

Water runs uphill in the columns and downhill everywhere else, and the only way to get anything to the upper terraces without the stairs is to let the columns take it, which they will do at a walking pace, and which they will do to your dropped hat as readily as to a droplet.

Nothing rots quickly. The warmth says it should, but the runoff has been stripped of almost everything that rots things, so a fallen frond on the fourth terrace will lie there for a season, going brown, keeping its shape.

The light comes from the rock. Specific patches of the wall — irregular, hand-sized to door-sized, distributed with an obvious concern for the streets and no concern at all for the void — glow a low amber, steady, cool to the touch, bright enough to read a large label by and not bright enough to cast a hard shadow. There is more of it low down than high up, which inverts everything you know: the ceiling is the dark part, the floor of the void is the dim golden part, and the mist between glows faintly from below like a lit fog over a town at night. Half the light in the place is light that has been through water twice.

Sound carries better than sight. You will hear the far side of the Lung long before you see anyone on it, and you will hear the eleven-second thud through your bones in every room, and after four hours you will have stopped hearing it, and after you leave you will find that your body keeps time to it for two days.

**What it is**

An organ, plainly. The name is not poetic. Water comes in through the roof by seepage, is gathered and lifted and conditioned and warmed and greened and given back, and somewhere above, something drinks the difference. The town in the wall is not housing; it is the service gallery for the organ, built at a settlement's density because an organ this good needs that many valves, and given doors and windows and curbs and benches because whoever designed it could not build a corridor without building a street.

But the nest cavities. But the troughs, and the perches, and the empty racks at held temperature.

The Lung is finished enough to work and not finished enough to be full. It is running at capacity on every process it has and it is, by volume, essentially hollow. It is the wettest, warmest, loudest, greenest, emptiest room in the world, four working faces still eating outward at a meter a week, ten thousand new stalactites all exactly one centimeter long, and one gray midge on the underside of a fern, undocumented, being the entire animal kingdom.
