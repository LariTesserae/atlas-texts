## The Rise

Begin with the fact of height, because everything here proceeds from it.

You are somewhere above where an atmosphere would like to be. The air is thin enough that the horizon does not blur — there is no haze to soften anything, no moisture to scatter what little light exists. Distances arrive whole and unretouched. A ridge forty kilometers off has the same crispness as the stone at your feet, and this makes the eye distrust itself; scale has to be reasoned out rather than felt.

And it is dark. Not the dark of night on a world with a sun elsewhere — there is no sense of a light source withheld, no glow behind a horizon promising morning. The sky overhead is a flat, matte, unstarred black, as though it were another material rather than an opening. What illumination exists comes from underneath. The ground leaks it. Every fracture in the black glass floor holds a thread of dull orange, the color of iron thinking about becoming red. Fissures no wider than a finger run for hundreds of meters and glow along their whole length, so that the plateau reads at a distance as a diagram — a network of luminous lines drawn on nothing, hanging in the black, describing a surface you can only infer.

Walk on that surface and you find it warm. Not scalding, mostly: warm like the flank of some enormous sleeping animal, warm the way bread is warm an hour out of the oven. The warmth comes up through the soles and stays there. The air, by contrast, is cool and getting cooler with every meter you rise, so that heat pools around your ankles and cold sits on your shoulders, and you exist in the seam between two climates a body's-length apart.

## The floor

There is no soil. There is no sand. There is no dust worth the name, and this is the first strange thing, because a landscape this broken ought to be knee-deep in its own debris.

The floor is volcanic glass and freshly chilled basalt in slabs and shards and sheets — some of it smooth as poured resin, some of it lifted into ropes and coils, most of it fractured into plates that grate faintly against each other when weight moves across them. Every edge is *sharp*. That is the governing law of the Rise: nothing here has been worn. There is no water to round a corner, no rain to carve a channel, no freeze-thaw to crumble a face, no wind of consequence, and no time. Things break, and the break stays a break. A slab split an hour ago and a slab split a year ago are indistinguishable — both have the same glassy conchoidal scoop, the same hairline lip you could cut yourself on.

So the world accumulates without smoothing. It is all first drafts. Debris piles up in raw talus at the base of every riser, angular, interlocking, stable in the way a stack of knives is stable. Fractures propagate, and their fresh faces open like new pages. Whole terraces are made of unhealed cuts.

## The growth

Now stand still for a while and the second fact arrives, which is that the Rise is rising.

Not metaphorically. You can hear the accommodation — a slow sequence of clicks and taps and small percussive knocks as the plateau grows and its skin adjusts to being larger than it was. Somewhere below, magma is inflating a chamber the size of a county. The surface answers by cracking. It cracks constantly, in every direction, in no pattern, and each crack widens a hand's width and then floods with orange from beneath and then skins over black, and then the black skin cracks.

The great motions are silent, or nearly. The thin air conducts almost nothing; a whole terrace tilting three degrees does so with the sound of a very distant door closing. What you feel instead is pressure — long slow swells that come up through the feet and pass through the chest, subsonic, more nausea than noise. The plateau breathes on a period of about eleven minutes. Between the swells, the quiet is enormous. You can hear glass cooling. You can hear the specific dry tick of a fresh surface contracting, a sound like an insect that isn't there, repeated across the whole visible world at a rate of perhaps two per second, thousands of tiny reports too faint to locate.

Occasionally a vent opens. It is not a fountain of water or steam — there is no water. It is a dry vent, exhaling hot gas, carbon dioxide and sulfur dioxide and hydrogen chloride, and it does so with a sound like breath through teeth, and it puts up a slow pale column that does not billow because there is nothing to billow into. The column stands there. Then it leans, thins, and is gone, and where it stood the ground is dusted with a fine yellow-white deposit, delicate as frost, that has never known moisture and does not clump.

Elsewhere, at the plateau's edges, lava emerges. It is not the bright river of a hotter world; it is dark, stiff, sluggish, barely luminous, extruding in tongues and lobes that seem to feel their way forward. Each lobe advances a few centimeters, stops, cools to black, then splits down its own middle and a new lobe emerges from inside the old. The effect is uncomfortably organic. Nothing here is alive but a great deal of it is behaving.

## The crusts

There is one thing that could be mistaken for life, and everyone who has ever been here has mistaken it.

Around the cooler vents, on the sheltered undersides of slabs, the mineral deposits organize. Native sulfur, metal chlorides, complex sulfides — they precipitate not in random rinds but in *forms*. Rosettes. Branching fronds an arm's length across. Rippled sheets with lobed margins that look exactly like a lichen crust on a northern boulder, in colors that lichen never manages: cadmium yellow, verdigris, a purplish-bronze that shifts as you move. Some of the fronds are hollow tubes, and gas moves through them, and where the gas exits the tip a new segment builds itself.

They grow at visible rates. Watch one for the length of a breath-cycle of the plateau and it will have added a millimeter. They compete for vents. When two crusts meet they interfere, and one is smothered, and the smothered one goes grey and flakes.

They are not alive. They are chemistry finding the cheapest route through a thermal gradient, and the cheapest route happens to look like a garden. But the resemblance is not a coincidence either — this is the same trick, the same arithmetic, a few hundred million years short of committing. The Rise is not a dead world. It is a world that has not gotten around to it. Pre-life, not post. If you sat here long enough — and you cannot — you would see the crusts learn to copy themselves.

## The marks

And then, scattered across all this, at intervals of kilometers, there are the structures.

There aren't many. In an hour's walk you might pass one. They are small — waist-high, mostly. They are stacks: three to nine slabs of glass and basalt, chosen for flatness, set square, largest at the bottom. Some are simple cairns. Some are more considered: two uprights and a lintel, framing a rectangle of black through which you can see the next few kilometers of luminous cracks. One, on a spur above the eastern extrusions, is a wall — twelve meters of coursed slabs, one course thick, standing alone, going nowhere, with a gap in it that is a doorway because it has a lintel and a threshold and nothing else it could be.

There are no tools here. There has never been a tool here. There is no fire that anyone lit, no worked edge that a hand made, no cordage, no pigment, no mark that means anything. The slabs in the cairns are unmodified — simply chosen, and set.

You will want to know who set them. The honest answer available on the ground is: the slabs are warm, the joints are unweathered, the deposits of yellow-white vent frost lie *over* the top slab of some cairns and *under* the base slab of others, which means some are older than a nearby vent and some are younger, which means the building has been going on across the whole span of the plateau's short existence and is presumably going on now, somewhere out past the visible cracks, at a distance you will not close. The structures are never near each other. Whatever is doing this keeps its distance from itself.

The mild, persistent weirdness of the Rise is not the cairns, though. It is that once you have seen the cairns, you begin to notice how many of the natural forms are almost architectural — columnar joints standing in colonnades, a fissure roofed by a fallen sheet into something you could crawl through and call a room, terraces stepping with a regularity that keeps failing to be quite regular. The world is rehearsing. Buildings, gardens, organisms — it is trying all of them out in stone, at low resolution, without meaning any of it yet.

## Rules

- Nothing wears. Everything breaks. Age is invisible.
- All light comes from below and is the color of heat.
- Sound travels badly and stops early; the biggest events are the quietest.
- The ground is warmer than the air, always, everywhere.
- Water is not withheld — it is simply not a category here. There is no shape in the landscape that a fluid made.
- Anything that looks designed is either a cairn or a coincidence, and the difference is not always determinable.
- The plateau is higher today than yesterday. It will be higher tomorrow. This is the only direction the world has.

Stand on the highest terrace with the black overhead and the diagram of orange cracks running out to a horizon with no softening on it at all, and the eleven-minute swell coming up through your feet, and the whole plateau ticking as it cools, and a single waist-high stack of nine slabs standing three hundred meters off, patient, unexplained, and slightly warm.
