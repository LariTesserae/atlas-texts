## The Warm Basin — a drowned works, still running

### The water

Begin with the medium, because here the medium is the country.

The water is warm — blood-warm at the floor, tea-warm at the middle depths, and only at the very top, some eight or nine meters up, does it cool enough to feel like water at all. It is fresh, faintly mineral, and it is *thick*. Not with mud, though there is mud. It is thick with itself: with suspended flocculate, with the tumbling green dust of algae, with the shed threads of a hundred kinds of weed, with silt that has been falling so long and so gently that it has forgotten how to land. Hold up a hand and you cannot see your fingers past the second knuckle if you look through more than a meter of it. Everything here is discovered at arm's length.

There is no surface in the ordinary sense. Overhead the water ends in a ceiling of lily-pad and duck-fern so complete, so pressed and overlapping, that it is a floor seen from beneath — a rubbery green pavement with roots hanging down like a fringe of wet rope. Light comes through it the way light comes through a leaf held to a lamp: dim, olive, and everywhere at once, with no direction to it and no shadow beneath anything. In some places the mat has torn, and a shaft of paler green comes down through the murk, and in those shafts you can see the water working — turning slowly over, carrying its dust up and around and down.

### The shape of the ground

The basin is small. You could swim its length in twenty minutes if the water would let you swim, which it will not, but that is the size of it: a rounded pit in limestone, perhaps three hundred paces across, with steep walls that are not walls so much as terraced ledges, undercut and rebated, worn into soft shelving over an unimaginable amount of nothing happening. The rock is the color of old soap. Where it is bare — it is rarely bare — it is scalloped in the fluted, spooned-out patterns of very slow dissolution.

At the bottom, off-center, there is a seep. Water comes up out of the floor there at forty-one degrees, in a column so steady it has no shimmer, and this seep is the reason for everything: the warmth, the weed, the town, the machines, the fact that any of it still moves.

### The works

A settlement sits in the basin, and it does not look drowned so much as *submarine by design*, though nobody who built it thought so at the time.

It is a compact thing — nine or eleven main structures, depending on what you count, packed shoulder to shoulder along the terraces like books shoved into a shelf too small for them. Brick, mostly: a hard, dark, salt-glazed brick that the water has not been able to do much with. Iron frames, iron catwalks, iron staircases spiralling up the outside of round towers. Tile roofs pitched steeply for a rain that no longer falls on them. Windows with their glass intact, because there was never any storm down here to break it, only weed pressing patiently against the panes from both sides.

And chimneys. Six of them, tall, tapered, brick-banded, rising up out of the roofs toward the lily-ceiling and stopping just short of it.

The chimneys are the engines.

Warm water from the seep is gathered in a stone plenum under the town and fed into their bases. It rises — it must rise, it is warm — and it rises through them, and inside each chimney a column of wooden paddle-vanes turns in the ascending current. Slowly. About one revolution every forty seconds. Never faster, never slower, day and night and season, because the seep does not vary and the cool ceiling does not vary, and between those two unvarying things the town has hung its whole industry like a hammock.

The power comes down from the chimneys on greased chains and shafts into the sheds, and the sheds do work. There are looms in the long building on the second terrace, weaving a fiber drawn from the stems of the weed itself, producing cloth at a rate of perhaps a hand's breadth a week, winding it onto bolts that stand in racks going back into the dark, hundreds of bolts, thousands, an inventory of centuries. There is a mill that grinds a soft calcite into a paste and pumps the paste through ceramic pipes to a settling house, where it hardens into blocks, which a slow crane stacks in a yard that has become a wall, which has become a terrace, which the weed has colonized and the town has begun to build upon. There is a foundry that does not smelt but *anneals*, holding things at the temperature of the seep, indefinitely, which is a kind of manufacture if you are patient. There is a pump house pumping water into water. There is a clock tower whose hands take a year to go round, and it is correct.

Everything is lubricated. Everything is aligned. Everything is going.

### Density

There is no gap anywhere. This is the hardest thing to convey.

Every brick face carries a felt of green-black algae a centimeter deep, and standing out of that felt are tube-cases of chironomid larvae, and growing out of those are stoneworts, and wound through the stoneworts is hornwort, and threading the hornwort is a filamentous alga so fine it moves like smoke. The alleys between buildings are not open — they are packed floor to roofline with a soft forest of milfoil and water-celery, through which you push, and which closes behind you. The catwalks have vanished into the growth; you find them by shin. The windows are green from inside because the rooms are full too: rooms of weed, offices of weed, a stairwell that is a solid green cylinder.

And the water between all of that is not empty either. It is loaded, granular, alive with drifting matter, so that the idea of "open water" simply does not obtain in this basin. You cannot find a cubic meter of nothing. The place is at capacity and has been for a very long time and is in no distress about it.

### Rules

Speed is taxed here, and the tax is steep.

Move slowly and the water yields like warm oil. Move quickly and it does not yield at all — it seems to *set*, to remember, to press back with a solidity that shouldn't come from viscosity alone. Snatch at something and your hand stops. Kick hard and you go nowhere and are very tired. The basin does not forbid haste; it simply declines to carry it. Everything in the Warm Basin that has persisted has persisted by learning this: the paddle-vanes at forty seconds a turn, the crane at a block a fortnight, the weed growing, the limestone dissolving, the silt not-quite-landing.

The second rule follows from the first: nothing here is finished and nothing here is broken. Wear happens, but so does deposition, at about the same rate. A chain link thins and a carbonate crust thickens on it. A roof-tile loosens over eleven years and a mat of stonewort binds it back. The town is not being ruined and it is not being maintained. It is in the state it arrived at, and it stays there, and the word for that state is not "abandoned" — it's something we don't have a good word for. *Settled.* *In its own keeping.*

### The tenders

Rarely — and this is the strange part, and you may decide for yourself what it means — you will come around a corner of the annealing house and find a figure.

Human-shaped. Coated in the same green felt as the brickwork, which makes them hard to distinguish from a bollard or a stack of bolts. Standing at a bench with both hands on a workpiece. Utterly still.

Watch for an hour and one hand will have moved four centimeters.

They are not statues; the felt on them grows in the pattern of something that flexes. They are not dead; the small fish that live in the basin will not approach them. There are perhaps a dozen in the whole town and they are certainly the ones keeping the shafts greased and the vanes true, at the speed at which such things need doing here, which is very slow indeed. Whether they were always like this, or whether they learned the tax and paid it all the way down, is not answerable from inside the basin.

### Fauna

Sparse, and shy of the light-shafts. A dozen or so grey, blunt-headed fish that hang nose-into-current at the chimney mouths and feed on what rises. Blind white crayfish in the plenum, which have arranged themselves into a stable and inscrutable hierarchy of chambers. Flatworms on every wet iron surface. A single large eel that lives in the pump house intake and has, by all appearances, lived there since before the water came. Nothing swarms. Nothing hunts with any urgency. The animals here have adopted the local economy: metabolism as slow industry, warmth as wage.

### Sound

Almost none. Water is a poor carrier of the sounds this place makes and a good absorber of everything else.

What you get, if you hold still and let your ears adjust, is a texture rather than a set of noises: a low, broad, continuous *pressure* of turning machinery, felt in the sternum more than heard. Under it, the seep — a sound like breath held very evenly. And occasionally, close by, a small dry *tick* as some tooth of some gear engages, a sound weirdly crisp and near, that does not echo, that seems to happen inside your own head.

Then nothing again, for a long time.

### At the end of the day

There is no day. But at some point the light through the lily-ceiling reddens and thins and goes, and the basin becomes black except for two things: a faint corpse-green shine off the algal crust on the south wall, where some species has learned to make its own dim contribution; and the filament lamps.

The lamps are strung along the main terrace, forty or so of them, in brass cages green with growth. The chimney-shafts turn a dynamo and the dynamo gives them what it gives them — not much, a dull orange, the color of a stove ring turned almost off, each bulb a small warm smudge with a halo of suspended dust around it and a small permanent congregation of larvae.

They have been on for a very long time. They do not flicker. The water is warm. The looms are advancing a hand's breadth a week. Somewhere in the annealing house a coated figure completes, over the course of the night, one quarter of a single turn of a valve.
