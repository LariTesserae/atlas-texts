## The Terrace Country

Begin with the flatness, because the flatness is the first fact and it takes a long time to stop being the only one.

From any standing place the land runs out level to a horizon that seems too far away, and it is too far away — the curve here is gentler than it should be, the world larger under your feet than habit expects, so that the edge of sight sits back another few kilometres and the haze has room to stack up in layers. Pale ground, pale sky, and between them a band of shivering white where heat rises off the surface in sheets. Midday light comes down hard and comes back up almost as hard, reflected off a floor the colour of bone and old milk, faintly apricot where the crust is thickest. There is no shade taller than your knee for a day's walk in any direction.

The floor is not stone in the way stone is usually meant. Bend down and it is layered like pastry, like the inside of a shell: fine bands, some no thicker than a fingernail's edge, each one a slightly different white. It rings dully when struck. In places it is still soft, a wet chalky paste that takes the whole print of a hand and holds it, and the print will be sealed under new layers within the season, preserved, meaningless. Nothing here was quarried or carried or set. All of it was *laid down*, precipitated out of water by things too small to see, mat after mat, film after film, over a span of time that has no monuments in it and no witnesses.

This is a wholly made country. There is no bedrock underneath in any sense a walker could reach — dig, and you get more of the same, band on band on band, going down further than a hole can be dug in a day, further than it makes sense to dig. The continent is its own construction. It is also, by any reckoning of geology, brand new: unfinished, unworn, still going up.

You can tell it is new because of the edges. Everything has edges. The plain is not a plain but an immensity of shallow basins, terraces, scallops — rimstone, grown at the lips of water films where evaporation runs fastest, so that the ground has organized itself into a fractal of pools within pools. The smallest rims are a millimetre high and enclose a puddle the size of a palm. Above them, rims to the ankle, holding basins the size of a room. Above those, ridges knee-high and a kilometre across, curving in long clean lobes, and beyond them faint terracing at a scale you cannot see from the ground at all and would only read as a slight tilt in the light. Every rim is crisp. Every crest is a knife of fresh carbonate, unrounded, unbroken. Nothing has yet come along to knock the corners off the world.

And there is no sand. Not a grain, anywhere. Sand is a product of history, of things being broken and broken again and rolled until they forget their shape, and nothing here has had time to be broken. The absence is unsettling once you notice it — a landscape with no debris, no scree, no dust except the little that the wind can lift off drying mats, no gravel bar, no talus, no soil. Just surface, arriving from underneath.

### Water

The water is intermittent and it is everything.

Nights are hot but not as hot, and by the last third of the night the air gives up its load: condensation beads on every rim, every crust, a faint universal sweat that runs downslope in films thinner than paper. At first light the whole terrace country is very slightly wet and very slightly darker, and the mats respond — greening, swelling, letting go of small strings of gas that catch in the shallows and sit there as clusters of silver bubbles until the sun burns them off. By mid-morning the films are gone, the surfaces are dry and chalk-bright, and the day's business shifts to whatever water is held deeper in the basins, where a hand's depth of it might last until noon in the shadow of a rim.

Then there are the seeps. Scattered widely — you might walk four days without meeting one — springs rise where the deep layers are pressurized, and they come up warm, slick, alkaline, tasting of chalk and faintly of soda. Each seep is the centre of its own small country: a mound built by its own overflow, tiered like a stack of plates, a metre high or fifty metres high, spilling water down its terraces in sheets a hair thick. The water at the lip is clear. Two rims down it is threaded with soft filaments. Ten rims down it is thick amber and green, and the ground there is spongy, and the smell is warm and mineral and slightly sweet, like wet limestone and bread.

And there are the rains, which are the great engine.

### Weather

The country makes its own weather because it is big enough to have to. All day the pale floor pumps heat upward. By early afternoon the convection stops being invisible: towers stand up over the plain, isolated and enormous, twenty or thirty kilometres apart, each one anvil-headed, brilliant white on top and slate underneath, marching very slowly across a sky that is otherwise empty. Most of what they drop never lands. You can see it — long grey combs of rain hanging below the cloud base, thinning, vanishing in the dry hot air a thousand metres up. The word for this is virga. It is the ordinary case here: rain that fails.

But some storms hold together, and then the sheet-flood happens, and it is the most important event in the world.

There are no rivers. This is the thing that most marks the place as unfinished: the land has no plumbing. Water arrives and does not know where to go. It has cut no channels, because the surface builds faster than the surface can be incised, and so the flood spreads — a skin of water a few centimetres deep, advancing at walking pace across tens of kilometres, brimming one basin, tipping over its rim into the next, brimming that. Standing in it, you feel it come around your ankles and keep going. Looking out, you see the plain turn to a mirror, and the sky doubled in it, and the terraces suddenly drawn in silver outline for as far as sight reaches. It lasts hours. It ends by evaporation. What it leaves behind is a fresh millimetre of white over everything, and the rims a millimetre higher than they were.

The plain rises a few centimetres a year. Nothing on it is old.

### The living part

Sparse, and all of it builders.

Underfoot, everywhere, the crusts: mats of microscopic life, felted together, mostly the colour of the ground, distinguishable only by a faint sheen and by the way they darken to olive and blue-green within minutes of getting wet. They are the masons. Everything you are standing on is their sediment, their spent architecture, their discard. They live at the top and die downward into stone.

Above them, at wide intervals — a scatter, a hundred to the square kilometre, sometimes far fewer — the columns. Waist-high to twice head-height, knobbed and cauliflowered, growing from the basin floors like slow fat candles. Same makers, different habit: the mat colonizing a high point keeps building upward toward light and away from the smothering flood-silt, and over centuries produces a pillar of itself. Break one (nothing breaks them, but if you did) and it is banded through like an onion. They are warm on their sunward side. They tick faintly as they dry, a sound like a distant clock in another room.

And in the wet margins, real plants of a sort: low fleshy things, salt-tolerant, blue-grey, no taller than a hand, growing only in the lee of rims and on the flanks of seep-mounds where moisture is reliable. They are the country's only vertical green, and there is very little of it, and it does not spread far, and around each cluster the bare white starts again immediately.

That is all. Nothing walks. Nothing burrows. Nothing has yet been invented that eats another thing, and so nothing needs to run, and so nothing needs to be quick, and so nothing needs to make a sound.

### Sound

This is the quietest large place imaginable.

There is wind, but wind needs something to catch on, and there is nothing — no leaves, no grass, no loose grains to hiss along the ground. The wind over the terraces makes a low, almost sub-audible moving pressure, more felt in the ears than heard.

Beyond that: the ticking of drying crust. The occasional soft crumple of a gas pocket releasing under a film of water. At the seeps, a thin continuous trickle-and-drip, which at fifty metres is inaudible and at one metre is the loudest thing for a day's walk. During a sheet-flood, a whisper the size of a continent — the sound of two centimetres of water moving over a very slightly uneven floor, everywhere at once, and it is still quiet.

Thunder, when it comes, is shocking out of all proportion. It arrives into silence, rolls, and leaves the silence back exactly as it was.

### Rules of the place

Nothing here is broken. Everything that exists is first-generation. What is made is made by growth, not by work — there is no tool, no cut, no join, no fired thing, no fibre twisted into another fibre, and the total absence of these registers less as poverty than as earliness. This is not a ruin. It is not even a settlement. It is a floor being poured, and the pouring is being done by the thinnest possible layer of life, patiently, at a scale where patience becomes geology.

The active processes are all constructive: precipitation, accretion, upward growth, the daily sweat and the daily drying, the afternoon towers, the seasonal floods that raise the rims. The one process almost entirely missing is the one that eventually makes a world legible — wearing away. There are no valleys, so there are no directions. There are no landmarks with names, because names require someone to have come back a second time to a place they recognized, and recognition requires the place to have been *shaped* rather than merely extended.

Walk in any direction and it goes on. Not identically — the terracing changes rhythm, the seeps cluster and thin, whole regions go a warmer ochre where iron finds its way up, and somewhere out past several weeks of walking the crusts stop and there is a great pale flat where nothing lives at all and the ground is a dead white pavement, unbanded, brilliant, hot enough to blister a palm. But it goes on. It has the room to go on for months.

At sunset the light comes in almost horizontal and the whole fractal of rims throws shadows east, and for about twenty minutes the flatness resolves into what it actually is: a countryside of tens of millions of shallow bowls, edge on edge on edge, every one of them holding a little dark. Then the shadows merge, and the plain goes lilac, then grey, and the heat begins its long reluctant leaving. The columns tick. The seeps run on, unhurried, laying down another hair's breadth of the world.

By morning everything is wet again, and slightly higher.
