## The Level

Begin with the water, because everything here begins with the water.

It is warm — blood-warm at the surface, a shade cooler against the ankle — and it is everywhere, and it is shallow. Knee-deep in the avenues. Thigh-deep in the old quarry basins. A film half a finger thick across the broad pavements where it goes silver and shivers and shows the sky. It does not vary. This is the first rule you learn and the last one you believe: the depth is *held*. Walk for a month in any direction and you will not find a beach, a dry step, a puddle, a dry-season crack. The water is maintained at its level the way a note is maintained by a bow.

And the plain it lies on is flat. Not flat like a lakebed — flat like a machined surface, flat past the point where a world is permitted to be flat. Standing in it you can see the wrongness before you can name it: the horizon is too far away and very slightly *convex*, bowed upward at the edges like the lip of an enormous shallow dish, because the ground has been levelled against the curve of the planet and the water levels itself against gravity, and the two disagree, and the disagreement is visible. Long sightlines shimmer and lift. Towers you should not be able to see stand up out of the haze in the wrong order.

## The city that is also the geology

There is no soil. There is no wood, no leaf, no root, nothing green, nothing that grew softly. What there is instead is construction, and it goes on to the limits of the light.

The oldest fabric is black — a glassy mineral, volcanic in look but not in origin, laid down in courses so vast that a single course can run for forty kilometres without a joint. On top of that, and through it, and inside it: pale accreted stone, something like aragonite, laid in fine daily laminations by the maintenance systems, so that every wall has the banded look of a stalagmite sawn open. Colonnades. Aqueduct arcades that carry nothing, or carry water in one direction while the flood goes the other. Slab-roofed halls at the scale of railway termini, roofed at three metres, so you walk them stooped. Sluice throats. Fluted piers in ranks of hundreds. Low blunt ziggurats of louvred stone. Cisterns with no lids. Stairs — thousands of stairways — that climb four steps and stop.

Nothing rises high. Thirty metres is a landmark; forty is a wonder; the whole continental sprawl keeps itself pressed low against the water, as if height were expensive, or forbidden, or simply beside the point. What it has instead of height is *layering*: floor under floor under floor, four and five deep, all of them wet, the lower ones lightless and pumping, so that the city is less a skyline than a sponge with the top sliced off.

And it is weathered. This is the thing that makes it feel ancient rather than merely enormous: every pier base is scalloped identically at the waterline, every corner is rounded to the same soft radius, every threshold is dished by traffic that stopped being human-shaped a long time ago. The erosion is not damage. It is a *form*. Somewhere the maintenance decided that the worn state was the correct state, and now it repairs the city back to worn — replaces a dissolving column with a column already dissolved to precisely the right degree, lays down new stone already gently sagging. The Level is in balance with its own destruction, and has been for so long that the balance itself is the architecture.

## Sound

You cannot talk here. Understand this properly: you cannot talk here, ever, anywhere, at any hour. There is no shelter from the sound and no season of quiet.

Its floor is the water itself — the sheet-thin flood moving over stone across a continent, which by itself would be a hiss like frying but which passes through several billion colonnades and comes out as a roar with structure in it, a roar that has *chords*. Over that: the pumps in the sub-floors, an infrasound you feel in the sternum and the teeth. Over that: the plates. Great hinged louvres, hectares each, opening and closing on the sluice halls with a boom that travels visibly as a ring of shocked water. Over that: animals, uncountable, most of them clicking.

And twice a day, the Comb.

The tide here is not raised by a moon. It is a resonance — a standing wave that fits the basin the way a note fits a pipe, driven and trimmed by the same systems that hold the depth, and its front arrives as a bore: a step of water half a metre high travelling faster than you can run. It comes in through the colonnades, and the colonnades divide it, and it emerges on the far side combed into forty parallel rollers running dead straight and perfectly in step, hissing, throwing up a white mist that hangs at chest height for an hour afterward. The sound of the Comb crossing a district is not describable as noise. It is a pressure event. Every animal in the city goes silent for the eleven seconds before it, and that silence — that vast withheld breath across ten thousand square kilometres — is the only quiet the Level has, and it is worse than the roar.

## The living

No plants. But not sterile — far from it. Under everything, on everything, is the mat: a chemolithotrophic film, mustard and rust and bruise-purple, feeding on the mineral leachate of the city and on the warm reduced water that seeps up from the pumped floors below. The city eats its own foundations, slowly, deliberately, and the mat is how. That is the base of the food web: stone, dissolved.

Grazing it, in numbers that make the pavements look like moving carpet: the hobblers. Flat, palm-sized, six-legged, shells the exact banded pale of the walls, scraping mat with mouthparts like little chisels and producing the endless *tik-tik-tik-tik* that is the city's high register. They will cover your feet within seconds of your standing still, and they are perfectly harmless, and they are the reason nothing here is ever slippery.

On the pier faces, in the current-lines: filter-fans, anchored in ranks, spreading translucent membranes into the flood and folding them shut when the Comb comes. They fold in sequence, upstream to downstream, so the arrival of the tide can be *watched* running away across a hall as a wave of closing.

In the drowned lower floors, in the dark, blind swimmers with lateral lines down their whole length, feeding on hobbler larvae, navigating by the sound-shadows of the pillars. Their world is a negative image of the architecture, made of where the noise isn't.

Wading the shallows on legs like calipers: the tallwaders, three metres of grey stilt and neck, eyeless, heads down, feeling for prey with a bill full of nerves. They are the closest thing to a large land animal, and they are everywhere, thousands in a sightline, absolutely still and then absolutely fast.

And in the roof voids, in every hall, packed shoulder to shoulder: the flyers. Small, leathery, warm, roosting in such density that a ceiling looks upholstered. They do not use sound to find their way — they cannot; there is no acoustic room left. They use polarised light off the wet floor, and they communicate by touch and by brief pulses of green bioluminescence along the flank. At dusk, the underside of every roof in the city begins to flicker with green conversation, and the flicker propagates, district to district, a slow tide of talk running perpendicular to the water.

Nothing here shouts, because shouting is useless. Everything here glows a little, or taps a neighbour, or waits.

## Turbulence

The Level looks eternal from a distance and is chaos up close.

Because the maintenance never stops. Repair swarms move through constantly — knuckle-sized, million-strong, mineral-white, laying laminations by night and dissolving them by day, and the animals treat them as weather and as food and as building material. Something collapses somewhere in earshot roughly every twenty minutes: a louvre bank shedding a slab, a pier reaching the end of its scalloping, a floor giving way into the pumped dark beneath with a boom and a column of brown upwelling water. Something is rebuilt at the same rate. Channels migrate: a current that ran east through a hall for a decade abandons it in a night and the whole ecology of that hall reorganises within a week, filter-fans dying on the dry side, hobblers streaming, tallwaders arriving in ranks. The mat blooms and crashes in slicks kilometres across, and the slicks change the colour of the water, and the changed water changes what the flyers see, and their green talk shifts key.

Nothing is empty. That is the last thing to say, and the hardest to convey. There is no unclaimed volume in the Level. Every wall is upholstered in life or in machinery or in accreting stone. Every cubic metre of water carries swimmers, larvae, suspended mat, working swarms. Every roof void is at capacity. The stairways that climb four steps and stop are stacked with roosting bodies. The sound occupies the air the way the animals occupy the stone: totally, with no gaps, no room between presences. You do not walk through this landscape so much as press into it, and it closes behind you.

Above it all: normal light. Ordinary, bright, high overcast — a warm white sky, the sun a smeared brightness that you can look near, humidity so complete that anything cooler than air beads instantly and runs. Your own skin never dries. Metal weeps. The stone weeps. The whole vast level plain weeps, gently, upward and downward at once, holding its depth, holding its worn shape, roaring, and full.
