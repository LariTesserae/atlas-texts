## The Turning Ground

You come to it over a low bank of clinker, and the first thing you notice is that the noise of the world stops.

The bank is about four metres high, made of a century of furnace waste — slag with its glassy blue-green fracture, brick rubble, ash gone grey and greasy in the rain, a scattering of clay pipe stems and bottle bases so worn they've lost their sharpness. A cart track runs along the crown, rutted, holding water in the ruts. From up here the whole thing lies below you and it is not large: perhaps three hundred paces across, kidney-shaped, bounded on all sides by the same low ash rim, so that the place is a shallow dish set into a flat coastal plain. You could shout across it. You won't want to.

Inside the dish is a plain of silt, and standing out of the silt are the piles.

---

There must be four thousand of them. Oak, driven when someone still had a use for this ground — foundations for a saltworks, a tannery, a drying floor, nobody local agrees — and sheared off at every height between ankle and shoulder. The wood has gone black and dense as coal, bog-hardened, and where the tops have worn they've worn into soft rounded knuckles, each one glossed with a film of water. They stand at a spacing of an arm's length or less, in ranks that the eye keeps trying to resolve into rows and mostly fails to. Between them, at intervals, iron: threaded stanchions with their nuts fused on, the cropped ends of six-inch pipe, a gate-frame lying half-buried like a dropped ladder, one rail standing vertically with its foot in the air. All of it the same colour as the mud, a dull ferrous purple-brown, so that iron and oak and ground read as one substance with three textures.

The density is the thing you feel first in the body. It is a room absolutely full of uprights. You cannot walk ten steps in a straight line. The place has the crowding of a warehouse of columns, a flooded orchard, a crypt — cathedral-scale in the vertical sense that the sky is very wide above it, but grove-scale in the way it holds you in and gives you no view further than fifty metres in any direction unless you climb the bank again.

---

The floor is wet everywhere. Not flooded: wet the way a sponge held above a bowl is wet. Water does not come into this basin from anywhere you can see; it comes up. The whole silt plain sweats. Put your hand flat on it and within a minute there's a shining print, and in three minutes the print is a pool. Around the base of every pile is a small collar of standing water, and because there are four thousand piles there are four thousand small bright rings, and in low sun they all catch at once and the plain goes white and lattice-like, an illegible circuit diagram.

In the cool of the morning — and it is nearly always cool here, a temperature that makes your knuckles ache before you notice it — the exhalation condenses. Fog forms at knee height and stays there, exactly there, a flat white layer half a metre deep with the pile-tops standing out of it. From the bank you look across a mist floor with a black stubble growing through it. It does not drift, because inside the rim there is almost no wind.

---

And under that stillness the ground is in absolute uproar.

Watch one place for twenty minutes and you will see: a silt bank the size of a table collapse into itself with a sound like a swallowed word; a channel open where there was no channel, run bright and fast for ten minutes, then go slack and silt and vanish; a pool of maybe two hundred litres drain away entirely through a hole that seals behind it; a dome of pale grey mud rise fifteen centimetres over a minute and a half, hold, and subside, leaving a ring of cracks that fill with water and are gone by afternoon. Silt boils: little upwellings the size of a coin where sand grains circulate endlessly like something simmering, except cold, and silent. The whole floor rearranges itself hourly. It is the most restless ground you will ever stand on.

Here is the strangeness, and it is the sort of strangeness you have to stay a while to feel. Nothing is going anywhere.

There is a set of surveyors' photographs of this basin taken in 1911, 1948, 1972 and every March since. Different plates, same picture. The same number of pools, give or take three. The same braid pattern in the central runnel, displaced a metre or so left, then a metre right. The same three humps of higher ground with their crust of lichen. The same nine piles that lean at more than fifteen degrees — not the same nine piles, that's the point, the leaners change, but there are always nine. The turbulence is total and the form is fixed. Whatever wanted to happen to this landscape finished happening a very long time ago, and what's left is the motion, running on, conserved, going nowhere with tremendous energy. Erosion here has arrived at its own settled state and the settled state is a riot.

---

The riot has a cause, and the cause is still under warranty.

At the north edge, half sunk in the bank, is a brick pump house the size of a small chapel. Roof gone at one end; the surviving end holds three courses of blue engineering brick and a cast lintel with a maker's name eaten to *—ON & SONS, LEEDS*. Inside, sitting on a concrete block that is itself now tilted, is an electric pump. Not old iron for show: it runs. A cable comes to it on four poles across the plain, sagging, with porcelain insulators the colour of tooth, and the cable is live. Someone, somewhere, in some ledger of some successor body of some drainage board, still pays for it, and the meter still turns, and the pump lifts water out of a sump at the basin's lowest point and puts it out through a cracked headwall — into the basin, forty metres from where it took it.

It has been doing this for an unknown number of decades. Its outflow undermines the floor; the undermined floor feeds the sump; the sump feeds the pump. The engine of the chaos is a closed loop of futile municipal diligence, and everything you see moving out there — every collapsing bank, every ten-minute river — is downstream of that one motor.

You would expect it to be loud. It isn't. The mud takes sound the way a mattress does. Standing beside the pump house you hear a hum somewhere around the low end of hearing, more felt in the teeth and the soles than heard, fifty cycles, absolutely steady. Ten paces out among the piles it's gone, and what remains is the register this place actually works in: a tick of water finding a level, a faint tearing as a crust of dried silt lifts and curls, the click of a small shell settling, and every so often that soft *ock* of a bank going down. Quiet sounds, close by, all of them wet. A place that is violent and has no volume.

---

Vegetation has more or less declined the offer. What grows here grows where the floor doesn't move: on the three humps, on the crowns of the piles, along the ash rim. Glasswort in fat translucent beads, going red at the tips in autumn like something scalded. Sea aster in flat rosettes. A crust of black and orange lichen on the ash. Films of algae, and in patches a bacterial mat of an improbable purple that you will assume is oil until you touch it. On the western rim there is one willow, leaning at forty degrees over the basin, and it has been leaning at forty degrees since at least 1948, because its roots have gone down past the layer that moves and are holding onto the still world underneath.

Animals are rare and pointed. A single heron works the basin most days — always one, never two, standing on a pile with its shoulders up, waiting out the ground's fidgeting. Eels in the outfall, felt more than seen. Ragworms below, in numbers, doing nothing visible. Once or twice a winter a fox comes along the crown track at dusk and does not descend. Birds pass overhead in flocks and do not stop; the plain is not sound-dead to them, it must read as unstable, which it is.

---

The place has rules, arrived at by people who worked here and then by people who came to look.

*Stand on the piles.* The oak tops are your stepping stones; you cross the basin in a series of small deliberate hops, like someone learning to be a bird, and this is why nobody crosses it in a hurry.

*Never step where the silt is pale.* Pale is fresh. Fresh has no crust and no bottom you'd care for.

*The ground gives things back.* This is the part that unsettles people. The basin circulates its contents. A shoemaker's last surfaced in 1974, went under in the spring, and surfaced again — the same last, with the same iron pin, in the same quadrant — in 1991, and again around 2009. Porcelain insulators. A horse's molar. Half a set of brass scales. A boot with hobnails, coming up and going down and coming up. Nothing here sinks permanently and nothing works its way out to anywhere. Objects rotate through the mud on a decadal orbit, like items in a slow lottery, and if you lose your keys you should probably not wait, but you should know that they will be back.

---

Light is ordinary and generous: white overcast most days, a sky far too big for the basin, and everything below it lit evenly and legibly. No mystery in the illumination. That's what makes the place hard to hold in the mind afterwards — you saw it perfectly clearly. Four thousand black stumps in a grey dish, water coming up out of the earth, a hum in the teeth, a heron, a plain rearranging itself continuously and to no purpose, and none of it hidden, none of it dim, none of it loud.

Late in the day the sun gets under the cloud at the western rim and the whole lattice of collar-pools lights up at once, and for about four minutes the basin looks like a circuit board on a bench in a workshop where someone left the power on and went home.

Then it goes flat, and the fog re-forms at knee height, and the ground goes on turning over in the dark, quietly, in perfect balance, and the meter turns with it.
