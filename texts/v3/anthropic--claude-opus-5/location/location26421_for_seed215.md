## The Grade

At four thousand metres the air has a taste like clean metal and the horizon does not curve the way you expect it to. That is because the horizon here is still rising. Not fast — nobody can see it happen — but the surveyors' marks say eleven millimetres a year at Bench Forty, more toward the interior, and over the eight hundred years of the works this has added up into something that has to be called a continent, since there is no smaller word for a slab of new basalt two thousand kilometres across.

It has no proper name. The engineers call it the Grade, from the drawings. Everyone else says *up here*.

---

The rock is young in a way that is hard to describe to people from the old lands. It has no soil. It has no valleys. It has almost no curves. The plateau surface is a mosaic of pressure ridges, cooling polygons, and blocky rubble fields where the crust broke as it flexed, and the edges of everything are still sharp enough to cut a boot sole in a season. Where the sun catches a fresh face the stone is blue-black and glassy; a metre away, under a decade of frost-work, it has gone dull grey and begun to shed grit. That grit is the whole future of the place. In ten thousand years it will be plains.

Because there has been no time for erosion, the water has not decided anything yet. This is the first thing visitors notice and the last thing they get used to. Snowmelt does not run in rivers. It *sheets*. It spreads across acres of flat rock in a film two centimetres deep, finds a low polygon, fills it, spills over the far lip, fills the next. From a rise you can watch a whole quarter of the landscape lying in still water under a white sky, ten thousand ponds in a shallow lattice, all of them communicating, none of them going anywhere in particular. Where the plateau is tilted the water gathers into braided threads that shift their courses between one week and the next. The Long Line's engineers gave up bridging in the second century and now simply lay the rail on adjustable pontoon spans and move them when the water moves.

And the rock weeps. New basalt at this depth still carries water from where it was made, and it gives it up slowly through hairline cracks, so that any exposed face is beaded, dark-streaked, faintly steaming on cold mornings. The cliff at the Weeping Face produces four hundred litres a minute out of dry stone under a dry sky and has done so for as long as records exist. The towns drink from faces like it. The water is very old, very cold, and slightly sweet with dissolved silica, and it furs the inside of a kettle in a month.

---

Weather up here is local and self-made. Warm air rises off the black rock in the long day, hits the cold, and builds a lens of cloud that sits over the interior for weeks: not storm, just a low white ceiling, condensing on everything, so that the whole plateau sweats. Then the lens tips and slides off some edge and for three days you get glare so hard the crews wear slit goggles and the ponds throw the sky back up at you in ten thousand pieces, and the light is ordinary daylight but there is far too much of it and nothing at all to absorb it — no leaves, no dust, no grass. Under that light distance stops working. A figure at two kilometres looks like a figure at four hundred metres who is somehow very small.

Snow falls in every month, lightly, and lies where the wind lets it.

---

Vegetation is present, and it is a serious thing here, but you have to go looking. The primary cover is crust: a black-green varnish of cyanobacteria and lichen laid over the flatter rock, brittle when dry, slick and swelling when wet, and it holds the beginnings of everything. Step on it and you have undone eighty years, which is why the paths are marked and why nobody walks off them, not out of law but out of a settled cultural distaste, the way one doesn't spit indoors. In the cracks: cushion moss, a hard little sedge, saxifrage with roots down in the frost-water. On the pond margins, mats of filamentous algae so thick you could lift them like wet felt. Nothing stands higher than your ankle in a thousand kilometres.

Which makes the animals astonishing.

They arrive with the melt. The plateau in high summer is the largest predator-poor freshwater wetland on the planet, and every waterbird in the hemisphere knows it. The ponds hatch fairy shrimp and midges in biomass figures that the ecology office reports in tonnes per square kilometre; the midges come off the water in columns you can see from the rail twenty kilometres out, hanging like smoke over the flats. Behind the midges come the geese, the phalaropes, the four species of sandpiper that nest nowhere else, the brindled gulls that lay directly on scree with no nest at all. Behind them the skuas and the falcons, and along the pond edges the little grey foxes that came up on the works trains eight hundred years ago and now den in the polygon cracks and get fat in July and hungry the rest of the year. Ravens live at the towns permanently and know the shift-change hour.

The wind feeds all of it as much as the water does. The plateau is a sink for everything the lower continents throw upward: pollen, seeds, ballooning spiders, chaff, dead insects. It falls out here in a constant fine organic snow, and the mites and springtails in the crust eat it, and the sandpipers eat them. The land, having no soil of its own, eats the sky.

---

There are eleven towns. Between them, nothing but the Line and the survey markers, so the density of human presence is roughly one settlement per two days' travel, and each one appears out of the flats like a decision.

Sill, on Bench Forty, holds eleven thousand people. It is built in terraces of cut basalt and cast fibre, dark and squat and very well made, with deep window reveals and roofs pitched to shed snow and gutter every drop of condensate into cisterns cut into the rock beneath. The streets are stepped rather than sloped. Everything — every building, every mast, every rail bed — stands on jacks. This is the defining civic fact of the Grade: the ground rises unevenly, three millimetres here, fourteen there, and so each spring the levelling crews go around with their instruments and turn the great threaded feet a quarter-turn, half a turn, and the town keeps itself plumb against a floor that is slowly bulging. Children learn the sound of the levelling wrench the way other children learn the sound of church bells.

The most valuable property in Sill is not the meeting hall or the terminus. It is the soil bank: four hectares of shallow pans under glass, containing eight centuries of accumulated compost, night soil, fish waste from the tanks, milled crust, and the dead. Everyone goes in eventually; it is not thought of as a grave so much as a contribution. It is the only place up here that smells warm. People take visitors there and watch their faces.

---

The works themselves are quiet, and this is the thing nobody expects.

Somewhere under the plateau — the depth is public information and means nothing to most people, a number with too many zeroes — the crust is being thickened. Mass is being brought in from below and laid down, layer on layer, and the isostatic response is this slow patient continental rise. The machinery that does it is not visible. What is visible is the Instrumentation: slim grey masts spaced every eleven kilometres in a grid across two thousand kilometres of rock, each one three metres tall, each one perfectly cold to the touch even in sun, doing something to gravity that the field manuals describe operationally and not otherwise. Near a mast, water beads differently. A dropped tool falls a fraction slow. The foxes will not den within forty metres, no one knows why, and the ecology office has been asking politely for two centuries.

It makes no noise. The whole apparatus of the largest engineering project in the history of the species makes no noise at all.

So the sound of the Grade is: wind, thin and constant, without trees to speak through, so it says almost nothing. Water moving over flat stone, which is a hiss rather than a rush. Birds, but spaced far out, single calls arriving across kilometres with the strange flat clarity of high cold air. Boots on grit. The levelling wrench. And, a few times a day, the reports — deep single cracks from the rock as it cools and flexes, arriving up through your feet before your ears, like a door slamming in a house much bigger than the one you're in. Newcomers flinch for the first month. After that you stop hearing them, and then years later you leave the plateau and cannot sleep because nothing is settling.

---

Rules of the place, as they'd be written on the first page of a survey handbook:

Nothing here is old but the rock, and the rock is new. There are no ruins, no fossils, no middens but the ones we made, no legends attached to any hill because the hills are younger than the archive. Everything historical up here is administrative.

Water is everywhere and belongs to no one; organic matter is scarce and belongs to everyone; both are accounted for.

The ground moves. Build accordingly. Level in spring.

Walk on marked stone. The crust is the only thing here that is trying to become something.

And on the last page, added later by some clerk and never removed: *In two hundred thousand years this will be an ordinary green country and no one will believe it had to be made. Keep the drawings.*
