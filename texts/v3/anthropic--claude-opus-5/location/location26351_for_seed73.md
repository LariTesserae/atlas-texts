## The Bowl at the Top of the Weeping Face

**First, the wrongness of the air.**

You climb for four days through country that behaves itself — scree, cold wind, lichen in coins on grey rock, breath getting shorter and thinner as the numbers on any altimeter rise past four thousand metres, then five. Then you come over a col no wider than a farm gate, and the air changes the way water changes when you step off a shelf into deep swimming.

It thickens. It warms. It is suddenly *heavy* with breathable substance, sea-level rich, blood-hot, and so saturated that your sweat stops working and your shirt gives up. Behind you, ten paces back, is a cold thin wind that would kill you slowly. Ahead is a bowl fifteen kilometres across, ringed by summits that pierce the top of the pocket and stand bare and frozen and streaming with cloud, and the bowl below them is *green*. Not alpine green. Not tussock. Layered, dripping, insect-loud, tree-on-tree-on-tree green, the green of a lowland river delta somehow hauled six kilometres into the sky and left there.

The locals — there are a few, and I'll come to them — call the boundary **the Lip**. You can see it on a still morning as a faint distortion, like the seam in cheap glass, running along the ridgelines and closing overhead in a dome you can't quite find the top of. Birds hit it and turn without panic, the way a fish turns at the wall of a tank. Smoke from a cooking fire rises to it, flattens, and slides sideways for kilometres.

**The rock, and the thing in the rock.**

The massif is dark and old — a basalt-like stone with the grain of something that cooled slowly and has been standing in its own shape for a very long time. Every exposed face is shot through with pale filaments, thinner than a fingernail's edge, the colour of skimmed milk, arranged not randomly but in an easy branching that looks less like a mineral vein and more like the vasculature of a leaf. They are warm. Not warm like sun-struck stone; warm like a body. Press your palm to any cliff, in shade, at midnight, in the rain, and you get the same thirty-nine degrees. Always. Wherever you dig, however deep you go, the filaments are there, finer and finer, and the temperature does not vary.

Whatever built this network did not intend it to be admired. It is infrastructure. It has been running, apparently without maintenance, long enough for the mountain to have grown a forest on top of it, and long enough for the forest to have grown a set of rules around it and become respectable.

Best guess, and it is only a guess: the filaments hold the pocket. They hold heat drawn from somewhere far below or far away, and they hold *pressure* — a standing region of dense atmosphere pinned to the peaks, tended at its boundary by something that behaves like surface tension but works on air and has no business existing at this scale. Physics as we have it can describe the effect but not the mechanism, the way you can describe a sail without knowing wind.

**Water that goes the wrong way.**

There is no catchment above the springs. This is the thing that undoes visitors.

Water climbs. It moves as a film along the filaments, upward, against every reasonable instinct, from deep wet rock to the high shoulders of the bowl, and there it emerges — hundreds of springs at the *tops* of things, on ridge-crowns, on the crests of buttresses, at the summits of freestanding towers where nothing drains from above. The whole massif is a fountain running from its own head.

So it is always raining locally, in the specific way a wrung cloth rains. The great western wall, the **Weeping Face**, is nine hundred metres of vertical stone with water sheeting off every centimetre of it, silvered, veiled, so that the rock behind seems to be dissolving and never does. Wherever water crosses a broken lip of stone it falls free, and the bowl holds — I stopped counting — something like two hundred permanent falls. Beneath them: pools, terraces, travertine steps built up in slow ochre and cream, moss-lipped, spilling into each other down the **Spillstairs** for two kilometres before the whole gathered flood reaches the bowl's low eastern gate, drops through the Lip, and is instantly, violently converted to cloud. That is what the mountain looks like from the outside, from the valleys far below: a peak with a permanent white plume off one flank, and no explanation for it.

**Sound.**

It is loud here in a way that stops being noise and starts being weather. Constant broadband roar from the falls, near and far, layered into a single pressure against the ears. Over that, the insects: a shrilling from the canopy that rises and falls in slow tidal waves, forty seconds up, forty seconds down, thousands of individuals synchronising across whole hillsides. Under everything, felt more than heard, a low tone somewhere around the bottom of hearing, which you eventually locate as coming from the Lip itself — the boundary humming as air is held. On still nights it beats slowly against the frog chorus and the two interfere, and you lie awake in it, sweating, listening to a mountain run.

You cannot have a quiet conversation in the bowl. People here speak loudly, or close, or with their hands.

**The green, layer by layer.**

The soil is thin, because erosion here reached its truce a long time ago — the water works at the stone, and the filament network slowly re-knits what the water opens, and the two have settled into a standoff that has left the landforms basically finished. Nothing here is sharp. Nothing here is collapsing. The ridges are rounded, the pools are mature, the travertine is old and stained. It is a landscape in its own state, and it will look almost exactly like this in a thousand years.

So the forest grows mostly on itself. Root-mats a metre thick lie over bare rock like carpet over a floor. Trees are short and enormously thick, wind-shorn flat at the top of the pocket's calm layer, and every one of them is a substrate: moss on the bark, filmy ferns on the moss, orchids and bromeliads in the crotches of branches, and a second forest of fine white rootlets hanging down from the epiphytes, drinking straight out of the air. In the wetter gullies the density becomes near-solid; you do not walk, you push, and everything you touch sheds water onto you. Leaves are large, drip-tipped, and often pale on the underside — they trade in condensation rather than sun, because for two-thirds of any day the bowl is inside its own cloud.

Certain trees on the cliff faces grow *downward*, seeded in the summit springs, hanging in long green beards fifty metres off the rock, flowering at their lowest tips into the spray.

**Fauna.**

Abundant, and slow.

Frogs first, because you'll hear them before you see anything: dozens of species, from thumbnail-sized things in the bromeliad wells to a heavy black creature the length of a forearm that sits in the travertine pools with only its eyes out and calls like a struck plank. Tree crabs — genuinely crabs, freshwater, olive and pink, climbing the wet trunks in their thousands at dusk to graze algae, so numerous that the bark clicks. Long-bodied lizards with a warm belly and a habit of lying pressed to filament-rich rock like invalids on a heated floor. Moths the size of hands, and a family of night-flying pollinators that find flowers by heat rather than scent — you can lure them onto a warmed stone and they will crowd it, patient and stupid, for hours.

And the browsers. There are perhaps two thousand of them in the bowl, and they are the reason the forest looks tended. Waist-high, grey, six-limbed in a way nothing else on the mountain is, hide crusted with its own private moss garden. They move about four metres an hour. They eat epiphytes off the lower branches with a slow scissoring motion and they have no fear of anything, because in this bowl nothing hunts adults; there is no pressure to be quick. Cut one — please don't — and you find a filament in it. A single pale thread, running the length of the spine, warm. The same substance as the rock. Nobody knows which came first.

**What was built.**

Very little, and all of it strange for its scarcity.

A stair, cut into the Weeping Face, eleven hundred steps, worn concave, going up under the falls and ending at nothing — a blank wall with a rectangle traced on it in filament, which the people here call the **Slow Door** and which has opened, they say, twice in living memory, for about an hour each time, onto a corridor of the same warm milk-coloured material, empty, dry, dust-free, going in.

A field of low cairns on the eastern shoulder, five hundred of them, each capped with a lump of vitrified gravel. Beads of glass in the ash of old fires. And every eight hundred metres or so, at intervals too regular to argue with, a pin of grey metal set flush into bedrock, thumb-thick, showing no wear at all after however many millennia — not scratched, not oxidised, not warm, the only cold thing on the mountain. Sun-hot rock all around them and they stay cold. Water beads on them and slides off.

Then the human additions, apologetic by comparison: a few dozen huts of split trunk and travertine slab, roofs steep and mossed; drying racks that never quite dry anything; walkways of lashed pole over the root-mats; a hanging bridge across the second gorge, rebuilt continuously for generations, its ropes green and living. Perhaps four hundred people in the whole bowl. They farm the terrace pools, they harvest tree crab, they are unhurried and slightly deaf.

**The rules, as they are understood locally.**

1. Water climbs. Do not look for the source above a spring; there isn't one.
2. The stone is always blood-warm. There is no cold season, no cold night, no shaded relief. You will sweat here until you leave.
3. Do not cross the Lip in a hurry, and never at a run. Going out, the pressure drop will put you on your knees. Going in, you will faint at the warmth. People make the crossing sitting down.
4. Nothing burns well. Everything is too wet; fires are small, smoky, tended, and prized.
5. Broken filament re-knits. Slowly — a hand's breadth in a human lifetime — so a quarry scar heals in ten generations, and everyone plans accordingly. This is why the paths are ancient and no one cuts new ones.
6. Don't hurry the browsers, don't move the cold pins, and don't sleep in front of the Slow Door.

**And the long view.**

The bowl changes, but not on any scale you can watch. The travertine grows a millimetre a year. The falls migrate a metre a century. There are three old dry lines on the western terraces where the Lip apparently stood, once, higher up — the pocket has been contracting, or breathing, at a rate of about a vertical metre every eighty years, and nobody knows whether that is decay or a cycle with a period longer than recorded memory. The lowest of the old lines is at the height of the huts.

What you notice at the end of a week is that it's peaceful, which is an absurd thing to say about a place this loud and this hot. But the noise never changes and the heat never changes and the shapes of the hills are finished, and the only motion is water falling, insects breathing in and out in their long waves, and grey browsers moving four metres an hour through a forest that is eating cloud. Something enormous was built here and then left running, and life crawled onto the warm machine, and the machine has not once, in all that time, been asked to explain itself.
