## The Sill

Altitude first, because everything follows from it: three and a half thousand metres, on a shelf of stone that did not exist when the oldest surviving records were cut. Above the Sill there is no summit, only the long shallow rise of more shelf, and above that a cloud deck that has never once broken in recorded history — a self-feeding ceiling of fog exhaled by the ground itself, hanging four hundred metres up, thick enough to be a lid.

There is no light here. Not dimness — absence. No star reaches the Sill. Whatever the sky does above the fog, it does without witnesses. The only photons on the plateau are these: the sour green of microbial films where they are thick enough to glow; the intermittent blue chemiluminescence of new crystal locking into lattice, which the locals call *coldfire* and which is more of a flicker than a light; the animals' own signalling, red and violet, in the dark, in enormous numbers; and the city's low maintenance embers, which exist for machines to check themselves by, not for eyes.

## The stone is not finished

The Sill is being made. It comes up as brine.

Under the plateau, water travels through hot rock, dissolves it, and returns cold and supersaturated to the surface through a field of vents — thousands of them, spread across a valley some twenty kilometres end to end. The brine emerges at around minus fifteen degrees and does not freeze; there is too much salt in it for that. It spreads, it thins, it loses pressure, and out of it precipitates the country: fluted aragonite, calcite in rosettes, gypsum, thin translucent shelves of mirabilite that grow like fungus and shatter like sugar.

The result is terrace on terrace on terrace — rimmed pools stepping down and out from every vent mouth, some the size of a coin, some the size of a town square, their edges lipped and scalloped and sharp. Nothing has worn them. There has not been time, and there is no wind under the fog to do it, and no ice-shatter because the water never freezes hard, and no plants to lever anything apart, and no rain but the constant condensing drip. The stone here is younger than the buildings. It is raw in the way a broken bone is raw. Every ridge has a blade edge. Every surface is a razor of new mineral needles a centimetre long. You do not put an ungloved hand down on the Sill. Newcomers learn this from the fine, painless, unstoppable bleeding.

There is no soil. There is no scree, no dust, no sand, no mud — those are products of erosion, and erosion has barely started here. The ground is either fresh crystal or standing brine or biomass. Nothing in between.

The whole plateau rises about two centimetres a year.

## Overwhelming

With no light there is no photosynthesis, and so there are no plants — not one, not a lichen, not a moss. What sits at the base of the world instead are the mats: films of chemolithotrophs eating the reductants the vents bring up, sulfide and hydrogen and methane, oxidising them against the thin cold oxygen of the air. Where the brine is fresh the mats are white as fat, hanging in the flow in tissue-thin curtains. Where it is old they go grey, then black, then rot into a slick that stinks of iron and eggs.

And on the mats: everything.

This is not a barren world. It is one of the densest biospheres you will ever stand inside, and the density is the first thing you register, before the cold, before the dark. Flyers — chitinous, palm-sized, six-winged, blind, navigating by clicks — in columns above every vent, thousands per cubic metre, so thick that walking through a column is like walking through gravel thrown upward. Scrapers the size of dogs, flat, armoured, working the terrace rims in ranks, tonguing the mats off the stone with a sound like a hundred people sanding wood. Long segmented *combers* in the pools, three metres of them, filtering. Things that hunt the flyers, hanging from the underside of every overhang in fringes, and things that hunt those, walking on the ceiling of every tunnel in the city. Egg cases cemented into any crevice narrower than a fist. Larvae in the drinking lines. Nesting colonies in the exhaust ducts, expelled in season and returned in a week.

The animals are not a nuisance here. They are the economy and the ecology and the pressure. The city harvests them — protein, chitin, the enzyme suites that let a body run at minus fifteen — and the city is also, permanently, being eaten by them in the small way that stone is eaten by water. Every structure on the Sill is designed around the assumption that it will be colonised. There is no exclusion, only management. The great civic labour is not defence against weather. It is the endless negotiation with biomass.

## Loud

It is very loud on the Sill, and it never stops being loud, and this is the second thing you register.

Four voices make the noise. The first is water: several thousand vents, a hundred thousand drips, and the peculiar acoustic property of thin crystal plates, which is that they ring. Every falling drop on the Sill strikes a bell. The terraces are a percussion field twenty kilometres wide, and the sound they make is not a roar but a shimmer, a continuous high glassy static that changes pitch as the plates thicken with the years.

The second is the fauna: clicking, in an unbroken dense fog of clicks, because everything here is blind and everything here navigates by echo. Overlaid with the scrapers' sanding, the flyers' dry hiss, the low pulsed booms of the combers.

The third is the ground itself, growing. It is a very quiet sound made enormous by repetition — the crack of lattice, ten million times a minute, the coldfire flicker made audible.

The fourth is the city, which sings on purpose.

## Hantha

The city is called by its note. Written down, it is a groove; spoken, it is *Hantha* — a hummed carrier tone at the low end of hearing, produced continuously by emitters on every roofline and every tower, and it is the city's name, its address, its lighthouse, and its street map. Within Hantha, buildings and junctions and stairheads pulse in patterned counterpoint against the carrier, and the inhabitants read the pattern as shape. They do not walk in darkness. They walk in an acoustic hologram maintained at civic expense, and the maintenance of it is the most sacred infrastructure they have, ranked above water, ranked above heat.

There are perhaps two hundred thousand people. The city fills the middle valley, wall to wall, packed in the way a coral head is packed — no plazas, few straight lines, everything grown against everything else, all of it stepped to follow the terraces because the terraces cannot be levelled, only occupied. Bridges at nine or ten levels. Ladder-streets. Pipes bundled in visible cables along every façade, sheathed in polished plate because polished plate is harder to colonise.

The buildings are not built. They are seeded and guided. Formwork fields — a technology no visiting physicist has ever satisfactorily accounted for — hold a shaped volume of intention in the brine flow, and the brine deposits the wall inside it, a millimetre or two a day, sheer and translucent and load-bearing. A house takes eleven years. A tower is a family project across four generations. Architects here are trained in a discipline whose units are *lifetimes per storey*, and their drawings are less designs than long instructions handed forward.

And because the ground rises two centimetres a year, Hantha is drowning upward.

Every building's ground floor becomes, in a century, a cellar; in three centuries, a sealed void full of stone. The city has grown vertically through its own history at least eleven times, and beneath the current street level there are eleven Hanthas, filled solid, perfectly preserved, their doorways sealed by the same brine that made them. The city's excavators are its historians. They quarry downward into their great-grandparents' bedrooms with heated wire and take out whatever the stone has kept — furniture, tools, tone-grooves, bodies, all of it flawless, embedded, sharp-edged. The dead are not buried on the Sill. They are simply left long enough.

## Utilities

Heat is drawn, not made. Lattices in the deep vent shafts pull a temperature gradient out of the ground by a mechanism the engineers describe as "combing" and decline to elaborate; the effect is that the city's interiors sit at a habitable four degrees while the plateau stays at minus fifteen and the vent brine gives up its warmth reluctantly and slowly.

Silence is plumbed. This is the strangest municipal service on the Sill. Damping fields — sold by the volume, metered like water — hold a room's air acoustically dead, and every dwelling has at least one damped chamber, because a person cannot sleep in the middle of a hologram. The archives are damped. The surgeries are damped. Grief is damped: mourners rent a silence by the hour. Children raised on the Sill are frightened of quiet rooms in the same way children elsewhere are frightened of the dark, and for exactly the same reason — in a damped chamber you cannot see.

## The rules

Nothing here is old enough to be smooth; treat every surface as an edge.

Sound is public and light is private. To carry a lamp into a street is not illegal but it is deeply intimate, and to shine it at a stranger is an assault — you have destroyed nothing, and yet.

Do not go quiet. A person who stops emitting is a hole in the world, and holes are how accidents happen. Visitors are issued a clicker and told to use it as they would breathe.

The fauna has right of way, and the fauna is the reason there is anyone here at all.

Build for your great-grandchildren's ceiling, because your own floor will be their bedrock.

## Standing on the rim

Come up out of Hantha at the eastern gate, onto the open terraces, and you will not see the valley. You will hear it: the near shimmer of ringing plates, the middle roar of the vent field, the deep hum of the city behind you at your back like a wall of warmth you can't feel, and far out — twelve, fifteen kilometres — the individual voices of the great vents, each with its own pitch, each named after that pitch, each with its own resident cloud of a million flyers whose collective hiss arrives a full four seconds after the water's note.

Nothing moves quickly. The animals swarm and the water falls, but the country itself is on a schedule of centuries: the terraces widening a hand's breadth per lifetime, the vents wandering, the plateau lifting, the city climbing after it. Come back in ten years and you will find nothing changed. Come back in five hundred and you will find the same city, one storey higher, singing the same note, standing on the roof of the city you visited.
