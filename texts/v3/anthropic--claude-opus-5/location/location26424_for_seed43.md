## The Throat

From above, at noon, it is a rectangle of shadow in a plain of dust — thirty paces by twelve, edged with a lip of worn yellow stone polished to the shine of an old tooth. Nothing announces it. The ground around is packed hard by feet and hooves, scattered with goat pellets and the papery husks of some crop, and then the ground simply stops and the architecture begins, and goes down.

There is no natural surface anywhere inside. This is the first thing to understand. The pit was dug, once, generations deep in the past, into soft river-laid silt, and then every square hand's-breadth of it was faced — floor, wall, ceiling, the undersides of the landings, the backs of the niches. Where the silt wanted to slump, they drove in stakes and laid brick. Where water wanted to come through, they cut stone to fit stone without mortar and let the seep find its own channels down carved grooves. Below the lip you cannot touch earth. You can only touch made things: dressed limestone, fired brick, lime plaster gone the color of weak tea, timber beams the diameter of a thigh, grass rope, clay. Even the water at the bottom sits in a basin someone built, its floor laid in slabs, its corners rounded so the sediment can be swept.

Nine tiers descend, and they do not descend simply. A single straight flight would have been easier to cut and impossible to use. Instead the stairs zigzag, doubling back on themselves at broad landings, so that the descent is a long folded journey down the two long walls, and the well's interior is not a shaft but a room — many rooms, stacked and open to one another, a hollow honeycomb with the sky for a lid. On each tier the walls recess into galleries: colonnades of short thick pillars, three deep in places, their capitals cut as blunt lotus buds and now worn to something like fists. Behind the colonnades are chambers with corbelled roofs, cool by comparison, some with sleeping shelves cut into their sides, some with grain jars taller than a child, some empty and used for nothing but shade and argument.

It gets warmer as you go down. This surprises the newcomer, who expects the ordinary mercy of depth. The spring at the bottom rises warm — blood-warm, a little more than blood-warm at the source — and the whole shaft is a chimney working in reverse, holding a slow heat, breathing it upward at dusk. At the lowest tiers the air is a wet cloth pressed to the face. Condensation beads on the undersides of the landings and lets go on a rhythm you can time your breathing against. The plaster there has grown a skin: moss beards, black at the root, green at the tip, ferns unrolling from every joint that leaks, pale flat mats of algae where the light finds the water. Above the fourth tier all of that stops abruptly and the stone is dry, dusty, gritty to the palm, and hot in the afternoon.

Vegetation is scarce but stubborn, and it all comes from above. A fig has taken the northeast corner of the lip — a real fig, a hundred years of it, its trunk half-buried in masonry it has slowly split and been slowly repaired around. Its roots have found the wet. They come down the wall as a curtain of grey cords, some as thick as an arm, and where they cross a gallery they have been woven aside and tied off with rope so that people can pass, and children swing on them, and someone long ago carved a face into the swelling where two roots fused. Beside the fig there are: three squash vines trained on cords down to the second tier, so their fruit hangs in the shade; a stand of a coarse grass that grows only in the drip-line of one particular gutter; pots of basil and something bitter and medicinal on the sunny landings; and, on the wet line, a hairy dark-leaved creeper nobody planted, which the frogs like.

Then there is the noise.

The Throat is loud. This is its defining quality, more than the heat, more than the dark green disc of water at the bottom. Sound cannot get out of a hole except upward, and on the way up it hits nine tiers of hard flat surfaces, three-deep colonnades, corbelled vaults, and it multiplies. Ten thousand swifts nest in the joint-gaps of the upper walls — small, sooty, screaming birds that pour out at dawn in a rising spiral and come back at dusk in a falling one, and for the hour of each, conversation is impossible. Their sound is a continuous tearing shriek that arrives from every direction at once. Beneath them, rock doves live in the niches of the third and fourth tiers and keep up a fat contented burbling all day. Bats — a small brown kind, several thousand — hold the lowest gallery on the west, and their chittering is an ambient sizzle, like fat in a pan, punctuated by the flapping shuffle of them jostling for position. And at the bottom, in the reeds at the water's edge, live the frogs, which are not large but are shaped like drums; their call is a low woody *bomp* that resonates in the whole shaft and can be felt in the sternum on the fifth tier.

To that: the water. Not a torrent, but continuous — perhaps two hundred separate drips, each with its own pitch, some falling into stone, some into water, some into a hanging clay pot set specifically to catch and *sound* a drip that had been keeping people awake. A carved channel on the east wall carries the fig's runoff and gurgles all year. Bees have a crack on the sixth tier and hum from it like a held note.

And the people, who are the loudest thing of all. Perhaps eighty live here at any time, and two hundred more come down daily with pots. They shout because they must, because nothing quieter carries, and so the ordinary register of speech in the Throat is a bellow. Women call the news of the upper world down four tiers to their mothers. Children scream for the joy of hearing themselves. Two men on the fifth landing knap flint scrapers and their tapping is doubled and returned. Somebody is always beating clothes on the wet stone. Somebody is always singing, badly, because the acoustics reward it.

The tools are simple and few. Bronze here is precious and reserved for chisels — the mason's chisels, resharpened until they are stubs, kept in a rolled hide. Everything else is stone, wood, fired clay, and rope. There is no wheel over the well, no windlass, no bucket on a chain: this is the entire reason the Throat exists in the form it does. Where a people with a rope-and-pulley would have sunk a narrow shaft, these people built a staircase, because the way you get water out is to walk down to it, fill a pot, put the pot on your head, and walk up. The architecture is a machine made of stairs. Every proportion in it is set by the pot on the head — the tread depth, the riser height, the width of the flights (two abreast, laden), the placement of the landings at intervals a loaded woman can manage without setting down, the shallow bowl-shaped depressions cut into each landing wall exactly at pot-resting height. The whole vast careful thing is a solution to a problem of weight and gravity, worked out by hand, over centuries, by people with chisels.

It is worn, and the wear is legible. The treads are hollowed into spoons; on the most-used flight the hollows are three fingers deep at the center and the stone there is glassy. Corners have gone round. The gods in the niches — there are perhaps forty of them, small, seated — have been rubbed faceless by hands, so that they are now identified by attribute alone: the one with the jar, the one with the two dogs, the one whose lap is worn into a smooth basin where coins and figs are left. Repairs stack visibly: pale new limestone patched into old ochre, a pillar replaced in a different stone entirely, brick where stone ran out, plaster over brick, and in one place a beam of black hard timber inserted to carry a landing whose stone cracked in some forgotten season, its ends carved with a pattern nobody now uses. There are mason's marks everywhere and four distinct alphabets among them. The place has arrived at itself. Nothing in it is being built and nothing in it is falling down; it is maintained, daily, at the pace at which it decays, and this equilibrium is the community's chief occupation and chief pride.

Certain habits govern it, and they are worth stating plainly, because they are the rules of the place and they are obeyed absolutely.

You descend on the west flights and climb on the east. A laden climber has the right of way over everyone including the mason. No shoes below the fourth tier, where the wet begins, because the wet stone with a shoe on it has killed people. The eleventh step from the bottom is deliberately, subtly wrong — a finger too high — and every child learns it, and every stranger stumbles on it, and this is intentional: it is a brake, set there to keep the hurrying from running down into the water. The swifts' two hours, dawn and dusk, are for no work; you sit, and you cannot hear, and it is understood that this is rest. And nobody sleeps on the ninth tier, though the shelves are cut there and the air is warmest, because of the bats and because of the dreams people report.

Two things about the Throat are not ordinary, and the people who live in it mention them the way you would mention a neighbor's habits.

The first: the water moves in whole steps. It rises and it falls, with the season, with the rains, with whatever the spring is doing far below — but it is never found partway up a riser. Always its edge lies precisely along the front of a tread, so that the lowest dry step is a clean line and the drowned steps below are visible as green rectangles going down into dark. The change happens overnight, always overnight; you go to sleep with the water at the twenty-second step and wake with it at the twenty-first, the whole basin lifted a hand's width in one motion. Nobody has ever seen it in between. The stepwell's calendar is kept as a count: *the year we went to nineteen*, *my grandmother's year of thirty-one*, and the risers themselves are notched at the shoulder with tallies of how often each has been the waterline.

The second is on the fifth landing, in the third bay of the colonnade, standing with your back to the middle pillar. There, and only there, and only for a voice raised above a certain volume, the echo comes back with its parts in the wrong order. Not reversed exactly — shuffled. You shout a sentence of four words and it returns as the same four words in some other arrangement, and the arrangement is always the same for the same sentence, so children have compiled a list, and the best ones are famous. The masons think it is a matter of the depths of the three recesses and the angle of the vault; they may be right; the effect has survived at least two rebuildings of that bay, which the masons find funny and slightly unsettling.

There is a third thing, less strange, more useful. Before rain — a day before, sometimes two — the dry stone of the upper tiers sweats. A faint darkening in the joints, a coolness to the palm on walls that were hot at that hour yesterday. Everyone reads it. The squash gets untied and let down. Loose things come up out of the bottom tiers. The lamps are moved. And then the sky over the rectangle goes the color of a bruise, and the swifts come in early and low and screaming, and the rain arrives as a solid falling column exactly the shape of the well's mouth, thundering onto the landings, sheeting down the fig roots, filling every carved channel at once, and the noise in the Throat becomes something with no name — an enormous continuous roar in which eighty people, ten thousand birds, three thousand bats, and an unknown number of frogs all shout at the same time and none of them can be heard.

By morning the water will have come up two steps. Whole ones. It always does.
