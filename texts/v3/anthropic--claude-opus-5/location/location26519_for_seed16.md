## The Bell

### Where it is

Under a salt plain, low — lower than the plain, which is itself the floor of a sea that finished evaporating before anything now living was thought of. From above there is nothing to see but a crust of white polygons, each the width of a room, curling at the edges like old paint, and, in one place, a crack no wider than a shoulder where the crust has slumped over a void.

Heat comes up out of that crack in a steady column. It makes the air above it shiver so that the horizon bends. Salt crystals grow on its lips in fans and stagger-steps, ochre where the vapors have dyed them, and the fans re-grow as fast as they crumble, so the crack has kept roughly the same shape for a very long time.

Go down. Twenty-two body-lengths of fitted steps, each step worn into a shallow bowl at its center, each bowl glazed. The stair turns twice. Then the wall on your left stops being a wall and the space opens, and you are standing on the highest gallery of the Bell.

### The hollow

It is a room. That is the first surprise and the one that keeps mattering: for all the work in it, it is only a room — a bubble in old basalt, ovoid, maybe forty paces across at its widest and sixty deep from the gallery to the grate at the bottom. You could shout across it. You could, if you were mad, throw a stone from one side to the other.

And it is nearly all empty. That is the second thing. The middle of the Bell holds nothing at all: no bridge, no hanging cable, no column. Just air, hot enough to feel like a hand pressed flat on your face, moving upward in a slow braided column you can see because of the dust in it. Everything built and everything growing clings to the inner skin, and the volume between is deliberately, expensively kept vacant. The masons who worked here spent generations making a shell around a hole and then guarding the hole.

### The masonry

The skin of the Bell is city. Not a city's ruin — a city's fabric: terraces stacked eleven and twelve deep, each terrace a hand-laid course of blocks the size of two fists, no mortar, every block dressed by chipping and then rubbed smooth on a grinding-stone. Stairs thread between them at angles chosen for reasons that were not walking. Doorways that lead into shallow niches and stop. Chimneys — hundreds of chimneys, flues of every bore, some wide enough to crawl into, some the width of a finger, running up through the thickness of the built wall and opening at the top in mouths that have been chamfered, tuned, filed, and in some cases plugged with a fitted stopper and sealed over with sulfur glaze.

There is no metal here beyond a few soft green beads of native copper set into the thresholds like counting-marks. The tools remain where they were set down: adzes of black glass hafted in fiber, grinding blocks, a bone-white rasp made from something mineral, plumb-bobs, a bundle of measuring rods in graduated lengths hanging on pegs, and — everywhere, in every quarter, on every terrace — small stone wedges, thousands of them, sorted by thickness into niches. The wedges are the whole art. You drive one under a block, and the block lifts a hair, and a flue's mouth narrows a hair, and a note in the room's voice moves.

Everything is rounded. Two thousand years of dry scour have taken the arrises off the blockwork, so the terraces look poured rather than cut, and the treads dip, and the wedge-niches have gone soft-lipped like the mouths of jars. Nothing has fallen. In the absence of frost and rain there is no mechanism for collapse; the city has simply been polished into its own settled form and will hold that form indefinitely, sanding away by a grain a year.

### The light

Dim, and the wrong color for comfort. It comes from below: at the base of the shaft, past the iron-colored grate, something glows a dull cherry, and that glow is filtered through rising dust into a brown-orange that reaches the upper gallery as barely more than a suggestion. Shapes are legible. Colors are not, except at the deep red end. There is a second light, subtler, from the glazes themselves — certain sulfur-and-arsenic crusts on the terrace faces hold a faint greenish phosphorescence, so the older quarters seem to be outlined in cold chalk against the warm dark. You learn to read the city by these outlines before you learn to read it by shape.

### The sound

You will not be able to think clearly at first. The Bell is loud. Its fundamental is a breath: the shaft inhales through the lower grate and exhales, on a cycle of about nine seconds, and the moving air across those hundreds of tuned flue-mouths produces a chord — deep pipe-organ tones in the walls, a hiss in the small bores, and above it all a ringing overtone that seems to come from the empty middle of the room and nowhere else. The volume is that of a river in flood, which is a comparison no one here could have made.

Under the nine-second breath there is a slower swell, five and a bit hours from crest to crest, when the whole column strengthens and the pitch rises perhaps a quarter-tone and the low flues start to speak that had been silent. And under that, an annual thing, a week in which the shaft runs hard and hot and the Bell plays a chord that can be felt in the salt plain overhead as a hum in the soles of the feet, and the fans of crystal at the crack shake themselves to powder.

The emptiness in the center is for this. A hanging bridge would have damped it. The masons kept the hole because the hole is the instrument.

### The vegetation

There is no water in the Bell. There is no water for two hundred miles in any direction and none in the rock beneath. Nonetheless the terraces are green — or the local equivalent of green, which is a range from amber through olive to a nearly black bottle-brown.

The plants run on sulfur. Their sap is liquid sulfur, held between 120 and 150 degrees, straw-yellow, thin as oil, and it does everything sap does: it carries dissolved metals up from the root, it holds the leaves turgid, it seals wounds. Break a stem and it bleeds a clear amber bead that stiffens in the air, darkens, and glasses over the cut in about a minute. Older growth is armored in these scars, layer over layer, until a mature cane looks like a stick of banded resin.

They feed on the breath: carbon dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, a little hydrogen, all of it delivered free and hot and constant. So they do not need to be efficient, and they are not. Growth here is fast and sloppy and visibly in motion. The flue-thistles inflate on the exhale and slacken on the inhale, all of them, all around the room, so that the whole inner surface of the Bell pulses at nine seconds like something with a heart. Canes lengthen a finger's width in a day. Mats of the low black stuff — call it ashwort — creep across a stair tread in a season and have to be scraped, and were scraped, for a very long time.

Lush, but only in a shell perhaps a pace thick against the wall. Nothing grows out into the middle. Nothing can: the updraft is too fast and too dry, and anything that leans too far into it is stripped, cooked, and shed as flakes that spiral up and out through the crack. On a still-ish part of the cycle you can see the plume above the salt plain, a faint brown smudge — the Bell exhaling its own dead leaves into the desert.

### The rules

**No water.** Therefore no rot, no mold, no mud, no ice, no rust, no weather in the ordinary sense. Things end by drying, by fatigue, by abrasion, by the slow crumbling of their own crystal.

**No fire.** Not because it is impossible — the sulfur canes burn beautifully, blue and quick — but because there is nothing here to stop a fire with. The prohibition is total and old. Heat for cooking was taken from the grate. There are no lamps. This is why the place is dim: the dimness is a law.

**No animals.** Never any. Nothing crawls in the thistle-mats, nothing bores the canes, nothing pollinates.

**Therefore the sound does the pollinating.** This is the whole reason for the city. Each species drops its pollen when shaken at a particular pitch and no other; the masons discovered this, and then over some centuries they built a room that could be tuned to produce those pitches in a fixed order. Wedge a block, narrow a flue, and a note comes into the chord. The Bell's annual chord-cycle is a planting calendar and a marriage register: this week the fourth-note lilies release, next week the cinnabar figs, three weeks on the sulfur cane. The terraces are not decoration. They are a keyboard, played by the planet, maintained by hand, and eaten from.

### What is here now

Almost nothing. Density is the last surprise: for all this construction, you can stand on the upper gallery through four or five swells of the cycle and register no presence at all beyond growth. The tool-pegs are full. The wedge-niches are sorted. The grinding stones have dust on them at the angle of repose, undisturbed.

Two flues in the western quarter have been re-wedged recently — the glaze around them is broken, the chips lie fresh and sharp-edged on the tread below, and someone has left three rods and a plumb-bob leaning against the wall as if intending to come back before the swell. Whether that was last month or forty years ago is not determinable, because nothing here decays on a legible schedule.

And the tuning is slipping. Abrasion has widened a good number of the small bores, so the top of the chord has gone flat and blurred. Whole notes have gone out of the register. On the north terraces there stands a stand of tall, healthy, perfectly intact plants — glossy, inflating and slackening with everything else, growing a finger a day — that have not shed pollen in living memory, because their pitch is no longer in the room. They cannot die properly either. They will simply continue, lush and sterile and loud, in a well-built empty room under a salt plain, until the wedges are reset or the last of the small flues rounds itself smooth.
