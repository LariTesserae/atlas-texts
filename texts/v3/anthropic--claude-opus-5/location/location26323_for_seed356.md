**The Dark Plexus**

Begin with the fact that you cannot see, and that this is not a deprivation of the place but its constitution. There is no light here and there has never been any. Nothing in this landscape has ever been shaped by the need to catch photons or to be seen. The forms are therefore honest in a way that daylight forms are not: nothing is coloured, nothing is displayed, nothing is patterned for an eye. Everything is shaped by pressure, current, flow rate, and heat.

Second fact: you are not standing on ground. The ground exists — some fathoms below — but between it and the sole of your foot lies an unbroken felt of living tissue, twelve to forty feet of it, warm as a flank, giving under weight and then firming, then giving again as it redistributes. It is not soil and it is not peat. Nothing here has rotted. There is no rot in this world. The mat is entirely alive, every fibre of it turgid, and where you press, it presses back with the deliberateness of hydraulics rather than the springiness of moss.

The temperature at ankle height is about that of blood slightly cooled. It is warmest where the mat is thickest. It is warm because the forest is working, and because the work is inefficient, and because inefficiency in the dark expresses itself as heat and nothing else.

---

**The land beneath, and how new it is**

The rock down there is young — days-young in the reckoning of stone. This is a shield of fresh basalt still exhaling, sheets of it laid down flat and glassy, ridged and razored where the flows buckled, unrounded, unsoftened, with all its edges intact. Nothing has weathered it. Rain does not reach it. Wind does not reach it. Its only erosion is chemical and it happens slowly, tooth by tooth, at the point where a rootlet has clamped its acid mouth to a crystal face and begun to draw the iron out.

That is the whole basis of the place. The rock is new and therefore *reduced* — full of iron and sulphur and hydrogen that has never met an oxidant. Above, in the standing rain, there is dissolved oxygen. Between the two lies a voltage. The forest is nothing but an enormous, patient, continent-wide apparatus for occupying that voltage, and every structure in it — every column, cable, bladder, gall, and gourd — exists to hold the two halves of that battery apart and route the current through something useful on the way.

---

**Architecture**

Above the mat, columns. They are not trees; they have no branches in the sense of a search for space, because there is no space to search for and no sun to search toward. They are *conduits* — smooth-sided, roughly circular, some slender as a wrist, most the girth of a grain silo, rising fifty, ninety, a hundred and forty feet before spreading into the ceilings. Their surface is warm and slightly tacky, like the skin of a fig, and if you lay a palm flat against one of the great ones you will feel, through the wall, a slow reciprocating stroke: a column of fluid going up and a column of fluid coming down, out of phase, in a pump-cycle of about eleven seconds. It never varies. Eleven seconds is the period of this world.

Between the columns run the cables. This is the strangeness that takes longest to accept by touch alone. They are lianas in geometry only. Slip your hand along one and you find it is not fibrous but laminated — a sheath of dense waxy insulation, then a papery layer of stiff dry material, then the core, which is a bundle of hair-fine filaments packed in a jelly that is faintly, unpleasantly cold. They hang in catenaries. They cross without touching. Where two must cross closer than a hand's width, a small hard gall has grown between them, and the gall is dry, and the dryness is deliberate.

The cables carry current. There is no metaphor in this. The forest is electrified, has always been electrified, and the current is carried by trained ranks of conductive protein and iron-sulphide crystal grown in filaments as regular as drawn wire. The mat below is one plate. The condensing ceiling above is the other. The columns are the risers. Nothing anywhere was built. Every one of these components grew into its function by no process anyone was present to watch, and none of them will ever be replaced by a better version, because there is no better version and there is no *will ever*.

---

**The flywheel canopy**

At the top — three ceilings, layered, the highest at about two hundred feet — the forest does the only moving thing it does.

Hung from the underside of the second ceiling, in ranks that go on past any reckoning, are the gourds. Each is a smooth ellipsoid the size of a cow, sheathed in a rind so slick it feels wet and is not. Inside each gourd, suspended in a bath of clear oil, a disc of dense lignified tissue is spinning. It has always been spinning. It spins at a rate you can feel through the rind as a fine dead-flat vibration with no beat in it, no wow, no flutter — a perfect constant. The oil is silent. The bearing is a cartilage cup with a lubricating gland at its base which secretes at exactly the rate the gland consumes.

They are storage. Current comes up the risers in the eleven-second pump-cycle, which is a rhythm, and the forest requires no rhythm, so the gourds absorb the peaks and pay out the troughs, and the load below sees a smooth unwavering supply. That is what they are for. There are perhaps four hundred billion of them. Together they hold the momentum of a small moon, spread thin across a continent, in the dark, in oil, in absolute silence.

---

**Water**

Water is everywhere and it never leaves.

The canopy respires warm vapour. The vapour rises into the third and highest ceiling, which is a mat of cold, thin, fluted tissue — radiators, essentially, chilled by the only heat sink available, which is the black sky above the forest. The vapour condenses on the flutes. It runs down the grooves. It gathers at drip-points spaced with the regularity of an orchard. It falls.

So there is rain, permanent rain, falling day and no-day forever inside the forest and never once falling *onto* it from outside. It is warm rain. It is very slow. It is a rain of large, well-spaced drops, and when a drop lands on the mat there is a sound like a fingertip on a drumhead, muffled — and this, multiplied across the continent, is the loudest thing here. It is not loud. It is the sound of an enormous room in which someone is doing something quiet at a great distance.

The mat drinks it. The mat is saturated to within an inch of its surface, so that any depression fills, and the whole floor of the world is spotted with warm black standing pools shaped like the footprints of the pressure gradients that made them. Sink your hand in and the water is the temperature of the air and the mat and your own blood, and you lose the boundary of your body immediately, and this is one of the several reasons people who go in do not always find their way back to the idea of coming out.

From above, if there were anywhere to stand above, the continent sits under a permanent dome of low warm cloud, motionless, its own weather, a lens of vapour a thousand miles wide that never moves off and never rains outward and has the same shape today it had a hundred thousand years ago.

**Rules**

*Nothing decays.* A stem that fails is drawn back into the mat within hours, disassembled by the tissue around it and reissued as raw material. There is no litter layer. There is no smell of decomposition — the air smells of hot wet rope, of ozone very faintly, of the mineral sourness of wet iron.

*Nothing competes.* There is nothing to compete for. Light is absent and therefore not scarce. The voltage is inexhaustible for as long as the rock keeps rising. Every column stands where a column is needed, and the spacing across ten thousand miles varies by less than a pace.

*Nothing changes.* Or rather — matter moves constantly and the pattern does not. At the coast, new basalt is still surfacing; the forest advances onto it at precisely the rate it emerges, and at the far shore old rock, fully oxidised, exhausted, gives out, and the mat there thins and withdraws at the same rate. The forest is a standing wave. It has a leading edge and a trailing edge and between them a hundred thousand years of unvarying middle. Photograph it in any era and you have photographed every era.

*Nothing eats.* There are no animals. There never were. No pollinator was ever needed because there is no pollination — the plexus does not reproduce, it only extends. There are seeds. They are held in hard dry cases at the base of every fourth column and they have never been released and they are viable, and this is the only thing in the whole continent that could reasonably be called *waiting*.

---

**Standing in it**

You are warm. You are wet. You are blind, and being blind matters less than you expected within about an hour. The mat gives and firms under your weight. Somewhere above, four hundred billion flywheels are holding steady. The eleven-second stroke goes on in the column at your shoulder. Warm drops land, spaced, unhurried, on your scalp and on the pools.

Put your teeth together and you will feel the hum. It is below hearing. It is the load current, and it has the exact frequency of nothing at all, because it is direct — it does not oscillate, and what you feel is not a tone but the *absence* of a tone, a pressure in the jaw, the sound a thing makes when it is doing enormous work and has no reason to announce it.

There is no one here. There has never been anyone here. The forest is not indifferent to you; indifference would require a direction of attention, and the plexus has only the one, which is downward, into the rock, forever, drawing the iron out.
