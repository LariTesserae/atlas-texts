## The Standing Water

At three thousand metres the sky is thin and hard and very close, and beneath it, held in a bowl of soft weathered rock, is the water — one basin, perhaps a mile and a quarter across, filled to its lip and holding still. Rain comes to it most afternoons. The rain does not disturb it much. There is a plant in the way.

**The bowl**

The rim is old. Whatever violence made this hollow — a crater, a slumped shoulder of mountain, the argument between two ridges — happened long enough ago that the evidence has been rounded off. The walls are grey-brown, furred with lichen in scabs the size of dinner plates, grooved by a thousand small runnels that have found their courses and kept them. Nothing here is being cut any more; things are only being smoothed. Where the rim dips lowest, on the northeast, a shallow lip lets the overflow out in a sheet no deeper than a hand, and that sheet has been leaving by the same route for so long that the stone beneath it is polished to the colour and gloss of an old tooth.

The air is cool and wet and tastes of green. Twelve degrees at noon, six at dawn, and the difference between summer and winter is smaller than the difference between morning and afternoon. Cloud comes over the rim in slow tongues, sits in the bowl for an hour, and is gone. Light is ordinary daylight — grey-bright, undramatic, sufficient — except that everything it lands on has already been strained through leaves.

**The plant**

The basin is full of water and the water is full of a single plant.

Call it cane. It is one organism, or so the people who live in it maintain, and the evidence is on their side: cut a stem here and a stem forty metres away weeps. It rises from the silt floor sixty metres down and comes up in jointed columns as thick as a man's chest, pale green going to bone-white in the depths, ringed at every two metres by a node like a swollen knuckle. Between nodes the stems are hollow, and the hollows are full of gas.

At the surface the columns spread. Each one opens into a whorl of flat leaves, each leaf broad as a door and slightly cupped, floating, overlapping its neighbours, so that the top of the water is a scattered pavement of green — not a closed one. This is the important thing about the basin: it is not crowded. The columns stand ten, fifteen, twenty metres apart. Between them is open water, or a leaf-raft, or nothing at all, just the reflection of thin sky. The plant fills the space the way a colonnade fills a nave. There is more room than architecture, and the room is what makes the architecture legible.

Below, the stems are furred with a fine red weed and hung with a slime that is not unpleasant to the hand. The columns do not sway. They are too rigid and the water too still. If you swim among them you will believe, briefly, that you are walking in a flooded hall where the pillars go down farther than the eye.

**The town**

There is a town. It is built into the cane at three depths.

The high town is the leaf-rafts: platforms of living leaf reinforced from beneath with lashed sticks, tenanted by drying racks, fish-traps, sleeping shelters of felted fibre stretched on bent withies. Rain drums on these all afternoon. Smoke is rare — fire is a difficulty here and lives in fired-clay bowls, fed on dried stem-pith, guarded like a small animal.

The mid town is at three to eight metres. Here the builders have chosen columns and trained them: bent young stems together over years until they grafted, so that whole rooms are walled by living wood that grew into the shape it was asked for. Doorways are gaps kept open by hoops of bone. Floors are woven mats weighted with stones, hanging in the water like hammocks. The rooms flood, of course. All of them. That is not the point of a room here.

The point of a room is the chamber above it. Each internode of the cane holds a pocket of air — high in oxygen, faintly sour, fizzing on the tongue like unripe fruit — and a diver with a bone spile and a mallet can tap it in three strokes. The mid town is a settlement organised around punctured air. Every dwelling has its breathing-node, a swollen knuckle above the doorway with a plug of waxed pith in it, and the courtesy of the place is that you never open a stranger's node, and you never open one that has been opened twice already, because a stem breathed out too often goes soft and eventually the whole column comes down in a slow toppling that takes a day and a half.

The low town is abandoned. It is down at twenty-five, thirty metres, where the light is the colour of steeped tea and the silt has come up over the doorways. The people say their grandmothers' grandmothers lived there and that the water rose. The water has been rising for a very long time, a finger's width in a generation, and the town has been climbing after it at exactly that speed. Nobody has ever seen the town move. Everybody knows it is moving.

**Tools**

No metal to speak of. Bone spiles, obsidian flakes traded up from somewhere over the rim, mallets of dense root, cordage — endless cordage — twisted from the cane's own fibre, which is stronger wet than dry. Fish-hooks of thorn. Clay for lamps and for the little bowls that carry fire. Greased felt shirts against the cold, and pig-fat on the shoulders, because the water is twelve degrees and a diver's working day is measured in short bouts with long shivering between.

There are perhaps two hundred people. In a bowl a mile across, that is nobody at all. You can swim for ten minutes between columns and meet no one, then round a stem and find four women hauling a trap, all of them talking at once, very loudly.

**Sound**

The basin is loud. This surprises visitors, who expect a still water to be quiet.

First the frogs. A brown, warty, thumb-sized frog lives in the punctured nodes, stealing the plant's air, and inflates itself with it to make a honk like a struck plank. There are thousands. They begin at dusk and do not stop, and the sound is not a chorus but a scatter of hard reports across the whole bowl, near and far, with the far ones arriving flattened.

Second the rain, on ten thousand square metres of taut floating leaf, which is the sound of applause heard through a wall.

Third — and this is what the place is actually made of — the knocking. Sound travels through the cane the way it travels through a rail. A mallet struck on a node at the north rim is heard, faint and clear and instantaneous, at the south. So the town talks by knocking. Every stem near a dwelling is scarred with a shallow paddle-worn spot where generations have hit it. There is a whole grammar: two-and-one for come up, a long roll for a body in trouble, a slow triplet repeated for hours that means a birth is happening and nobody should tap idly for a while. Because everyone is far from everyone, and because water swallows shouting, the plant is the town's voice, and the plant is never silent. Under all the frogs and rain there is a constant irregular wooden pulse, a sound like a house settling that never finishes settling.

Beneath the surface, submerged, all of this becomes a single low hum with the knocks picked out sharp inside it, and your own skull is part of the instrument.

**The living mass**

Fauna is everywhere and mostly small. Silver fish the length of a finger move in sheets between the columns, turning all at once, and where they turn the light goes white for a moment. Something heavier lives on the bottom and is caught in bone traps twice a year — flat, whiskered, pale from the dark. Water-shrews, black and frantic, run on the undersides of the floating leaves and are eaten by a long-legged grey heron that stands on the rafts and does not move for hours and is considered lucky and is never harmed. Caddis larvae build their cases out of the town's litter — thread-ends, shell chips, dropped beads — and cement the woven walls of the mid-town rooms with their spit, so that the oldest houses are partly insect masonry, hard and gritty and grey. Snails graze the columns clean. A pale blind eel-thing loops through the deep town, in and out of the drowned doorways, and is left alone.

Nothing here is in a hurry. The cane grows a hand's span a year. The silt deepens a finger a decade. The water climbs, the town climbs, the rim wears down, the frogs honk, the mallets knock, and the whole arrangement has been holding its shape — not frozen, but held, the way a hand holds a bird — for longer than anyone's memory of it goes back.

If you leave over the northeast lip, walking out in the ankle-deep sheet of overflow on that polished stone, the noise falls away behind you all at once, in about ten paces, and the silence outside the bowl is shocking, and cold, and very large.
