## Cavity Nine

You reach it by ladder — eleven flights of welded rung inside a shaft barely wider than your shoulders, the metal so cold it feels wet, though nothing here is wet and nothing here ever has been. The last rung gives onto a hatch with a five-spoke wheel. Turn it and the sound arrives, not as noise but as a change of state, the way stepping into deep water changes you from a walking thing into a floating one.

The room is nine meters by six, four high. That is all of it. That is the entire world under discussion.

---

**The made ground.** There is no ground. There is a floor: cast iron plate in a diamond tread, laid in eight panels, bolted at the seams with countersunk hex heads that have never been turned since the day they were driven. In the corners the casting sand still sits in little grey drifts, unswept, because no one comes here to sweep. The arrises on every edge are sharp enough to score your thumbnail. Nothing in this room has been worn by anything. It is a new room. It will always be a new room.

The walls are plate steel, riveted on a 90mm pitch, painted once with a grey oxide primer and never finished — the second coat was scheduled and then the machine was started and after that no one could work in here long enough to hold a brush. The primer has a chalky bloom. The rivet heads are bright where the primer skipped them. There is no rust. There will never be rust. Rust requires water and there is no water in this world at all: not vapor, not frost, not the ghost of it in your breath, which comes out of you clear and disappears without a plume.

The ceiling is a coffered grid, and set into four of the coffers are day-panels — flat, caged, giving out a light of about five thousand kelvin, the exact grey-white of overcast noon in a temperate month. The light is very good. That is the cruelty of it. It is the light of an ordinary afternoon in an ordinary building, and it falls on nothing that has ever been ordinary.

**The cold.** Minus thirty-one degrees, held to a tenth. You feel it first in the eye sockets. The reason is arithmetic: the speed of sound in a gas depends on its temperature, and the note this room holds depends on the speed of sound, and so the temperature is not a condition here — it is a *specification*. It is as fixed as the dimensions of the room. The cold is part of the tuning. So is the dryness: the atmosphere is a scrubbed nitrogen-argon mix with the water taken out to parts per billion, because water in the gas would drag the note flat by a few thousandths, and a few thousandths, across the whole structure, would be catastrophic in a way no one has ever written down clearly.

So: cold because of the note. Dry because of the note. And because it is dry, no ice, no frost-flowers, no condensation on the cold walls, no dark stain anywhere. The room is *desert* in the strictest sense — an absolutely arid mineral interior — and the effect is not of a desert at all but of a photograph of a room, printed on metal, kept in a drawer.

---

**The note.** One hundred thirty-one decibels. Forty-one hertz, with the third and fifth harmonics riding it and a faint sixteenth that sits up in the range where it becomes a kind of white pressure behind your teeth. It does not vary. It has not varied. There is no rhythm in it, no beat, no cycling of load — the engines that make it are so many and so distributed that their individual imperfections have averaged out into a single unmoving column of tone, the way a million pebbles average out into a beach.

You do not hear it. Hearing implies a beginning and an end. You are *inside* it, and your body is the last few centimeters of the room, and the note is in your sternum and in the long bones of your forearms and in the fluid of your eyes, so that the rivets seem to shiver at the edges of vision even though nothing is moving. If you shout, nothing happens. Your throat works, your jaw aches, and the sound is simply not admitted; it is a pebble thrown into a beach.

The note is why the room exists. Cavity Nine is not a room that has a machine in it. Cavity Nine is a *volume* — a deliberate, precisely dimensioned emptiness bored into the upper body of something enormous, whose only function is to be nine by six by four meters of gas at minus thirty-one degrees and to resonate. The room is a note that the structure is holding. You have climbed eleven flights up inside a throat.

---

**The standing wave, and what lives in it.** At this frequency and these dimensions the wave in here is not an abstraction. It has a shape and the shape has furniture.

Dust hangs in the air in horizontal sheets. Four of them, spaced about two meters apart on the vertical, each perhaps a centimeter thick and the size of the room, grey and even as gauze curtains laid flat. This is acoustic levitation, plain physics, and it has been going on since the first startup, when the room's construction dust was shaken off every surface at once and never permitted to land. It will go on. Walk through the sheets and they part around you in slow curls and then reassemble within the minute, exactly as they were, at the height they must be. You cannot disarrange this room. It repairs itself into its only possible configuration.

Between the sheets, in the nodal planes and along the nodal lines where the walls meet, there is the vegetation.

Tin whiskers. Hair-fine single crystals of metal, extruded slowly out of the plated fittings and the solder fillets and the zinc on the conduit brackets — a real and stupid phenomenon, well known, unsolved, and here allowed to run without interruption. They grow at three or four millimeters a year and the room is not yet old, so the longest of them is the length of your hand. They stand in sparse thickets: a dozen here on the lip of a junction box, a fine grey fur along one meter of a cable tray, then two meters of nothing at all, then a single whisker rising alone from a bolt head, curved, catching the day-panel light in one bright line.

They only grow in the nodes. In the antinodes, where the pressure swings hardest, they are shaken off as fast as they extrude, and the metal there is instead very slightly frosted, matte, micro-polished by nothing but sound — the only erosion this world has. So the vegetation is a map. It draws the invisible geometry of the tone in silver hairs, and where the room is loudest, it is bare, and where the room is quietest, it grows.

There are no animals. There is no possibility of animals. There has never been an insect in Cavity Nine, and if one were introduced it would be dead of the cold before it was dead of the noise, and its body would not decay, because decay is also a wet process, and it would simply lie on the diamond plate looking surprised for the rest of the structure's operational life.

---

**The quiet spot.** In the northeast corner, at a height of about one meter forty, there is a node the size of a grapefruit — the intersection of three nulls, a small hard bubble of cancellation. Someone found it early and marked it with a chalk ring on the wall, and the chalk has not smudged.

Put your head in it. Not your whole head; there is only room for the region around one ear. The note drops away by nearly forty decibels, and into that hole rushes everything you had forgotten was underneath: the hiss of your own nervous system, the working of your jaw, and a fast, high, insistent knocking that you take a moment to recognize as your heart, which has been running at a hundred and ten since you came through the hatch. It is the only place in this world where anything happens on a schedule.

Move your head two centimeters and the note takes you back.

---

**Evidence of persons.** There is a coat on a hook by the hatch. Canvas, lined, industrial issue, hung there by someone who intended to come back for it. The hook is in a node and the shoulders of the coat are perfect, the weave crisp, the collar still creased. The hem hangs down into the antinode below, and it has been felted and then fibrillated and then reduced, over years, to a grey fringe, and beneath it on the floor there is a small neat pile of lint held in a ring by the pressure pattern, and it will not move from that ring.

There is a tally chalked beside the hatch: four strokes and a cross-stroke, and then nothing.

There is a logbook in a bracket, one entry, in pencil: *Started. Holds.*

---

**Rules of the world.**

Nothing may be added. Nothing may be removed. A single hex bolt taken out of the floor changes the volume and the surface impedance of Cavity Nine by an amount that is measurable four kilometers away, in a room where people you will never meet watch a needle. The needle must not move. This is the entire moral content of the place, and it is why the sand is still in the corners and the coat is still on the hook and the primer was never finished: because it turns out that the way to maintain this room is to never touch it.

Which means the room is finished. Not old — *finished*. It arrived at its final state on the day of startup and it has nowhere further to go. Young and complete at once: fresh-cut edges, unweathered paint, virgin sand, all of it fixed in place by the impossibility of change. The whiskers will grow another few millimeters a year, and the coat hem will fray another centimeter, and these are the only clocks, and they run so slowly that from inside a human hour the room is simply a still image with a roar laid over it.

---

**The port.** One thing more. In the west wall, at eye height, there is a thick quartz inspection port, twenty centimeters across, with a wire guard over it.

Through it: outside. Cold grey light, no sun visible, no cloud, no sky as such — just an even luminous haze. And falling away below the port, going down and down until the haze takes it, the outer flank of the structure: fresh girderwork, still bright from the mill, sharp-edged, unpainted, unrusted, its members crossing and recrossing in a lattice that gets finer with distance until it becomes texture and then becomes nothing.

No horizon. No ground. No sign of where the making stopped, because it hasn't.

You are very high up, and the height is not a natural height; it is a *built* height, accumulated, and it goes further down than it goes up.

Then you close the port cover, because the light through it makes the whiskers glitter and it is easy to stand there too long, and you take the wheel of the hatch, and you climb back down out of the note into the merely loud world below, and behind you Cavity Nine returns instantly and exactly to itself: four grey sheets of dust hanging in the air, a chalk ring in the corner, a coat with a fringe of felt, and the sound going on and on with nothing in it at all.
