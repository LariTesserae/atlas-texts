## The Cistern at the Top of the Rise

**Where it stands**

There is no ground here, and there never was. What holds this place up is a plinth of its own manufacture: a tapering column of pale precipitated stone, forty metres across at the shoulder, banded in fine horizontal courses like the inside of a kettle, and still growing. Underneath, in the dark of the column's throat, sinter-heads work the mineral out of the water and lay it down in rings. Six centimetres a year. The column has been rising for a long while now, patiently, and has carried its cargo up through the cloud deck into the thin bright air above.

The cargo is a basin. A single one, hamlet-sized — you could walk its rim in nine minutes if the rim were dry — with terraces stepping down inside it in seven concentric shelves, each shelf lipped with a glass weir. It is a cistern, and it is full: warm water to within a hand's breadth of the top course, water so still and so laden with green that from above it reads as a shallow dish of jade set on a white pillar, above weather.

**The water**

The water is warm the way a mouth is warm, thirty-two degrees at the surface, thirty-four in the lower galleries where the bus bars run. It came up the column as vapour and condensed in the lungs of the machine, and it has never been rained on, never touched a riverbed. It has no grit in it. It has no taste of stone. What it does have is the damping polymer — long slack molecules threaded through it in parts per million — and this is what makes the place strange before anything else does.

The polymer thickens the water's manners without thickening the water. Surfaces do not break into spray; they bulge, dimple, and reseal. Throw a wrench in and the water swallows it with a sound like a door closing in another building. Swim, and no wake follows you more than two body-lengths; the water forgets you at once, smoothing itself, returning to a plate of standing green light.

It is also stratified, and the strata are firm enough to lie on. Three layers, differing by a few grams per litre of dissolved carbonate: the warm bright top, the middle, and the dense lower body that fills the last two terraces. At each boundary there is a membrane that is not a membrane, a shimmering plane where the refractive index steps. Leaves that fall from the upper terraces do not sink to the bottom. They settle on the halocline and lie there in drifts, a false floor of yellow leaf litter suspended in mid-water, three metres above the actual floor, casting a dappled shade on the galleries below. In places the drifts have thickened until they are meadows. Things have begun to root in them.

**The made ground**

Everything is cast, poured, rolled, extruded, or welded. There is no natural substrate anywhere in the basin — not a pebble, not a handful of soil. The terraces are white hydraulic concrete with a smooth troweled skin; the growing beds are trays of sintered ceramic pellets, each pellet identical, kiln-marked; the retaining walls are bolted plate with the mill numbers still legible. Where the builders needed something like rock, they made something like rock: dark aggregate blocks with a deliberate fracture pattern, laid up into what a person underwater would call a cliff and a mason would call a wall.

And none of it has been worn. This is the second strangeness. The place has the geology of a thing brand new. Every concrete edge is sharp enough to cut a thumb. The weir lips are unfrosted glass, without a single chip. The plate steel wears its original blue-grey heat scale in peacock rings around each weld. Down on Terrace Four there is a run of epoxy that a worker poured and never smoothed, and it is still slightly tacky under the water; press it, and your fingerprint stays, and will stay, because nothing here abrades anything. There is no sand. There is no current worth the name. There is no frost, and no animal to scuff a corner. The basin will look this new for a thousand years, holding its formwork grain and its chalk marks, while beneath it the column patiently manufactures another hundred metres of stone.

**What grows**

And yet it is not sterile. It is choked.

Vegetation has taken the basin the way steam takes a bathroom. There is no free surface left. Every square metre of trowelled concrete is furred with liverworts in overlapping rosettes; every bolt head is a hummock of moss with a pale crown; the plate walls are shingled in hornwort and a flat black alga that grows in annual laminations like a fingernail. From the trays, water-milfoil and hair-fine ribbon weed rise in columns so dense you must part them with both arms, and they close behind you without noise. Tree ferns stand on the upper terraces with their crowns in air and their trunks in water, fronds unrolling at a rate you could watch if you were patient. Cycads squat on the rim. Something like a pondweed has learned to climb the conduit runs, and has sheathed a hundred metres of cable in green tubing.

None of it needs an animal. This is the selection principle, and it is absolute. The spore-bearers, the cloners, the ones that release pollen into the water as long sticky threads that find their way by drifting, the ones that split and split and split. The basin's flora is a catalogue of every strategy life ever invented for reproducing without a courier. What could not be got that way was engineered: on Terrace Two there are the combs, rows of ceramic tines on a shaft that turns once every eleven minutes and touches each grass panicle in sequence, shaking the pollen loose. They do this in silence, in green light, forever, standing in for insects that are not here and never were.

**The engines**

The basin is a factory, though it is hard at first to see what it makes. The engines are all geared down past the point of noise. There are eleven pumps in the lower gallery, each the size of a cottage, and each turns at four revolutions per minute; you can watch a crank travel through its arc the way you watch a minute hand. Beryllium-bronze impellers move the whole body of water through one complete turnover every nineteen days, so slowly that the leaf-meadows on the halocline are never disturbed. Precipitators hum below hearing, pulling carbonate out of solution and passing it down the column to the sinter-heads. Filament heaters glow dull orange in glass sleeves furred with algae. Everywhere there is copper, and everywhere the copper has gone the colour of the water, so that the machines seem to be made of the same stuff as the plants and to be a slower part of the same growth.

Smell, at the rim where air begins: warm wet stone, cedar from the formwork stacked and never removed, the green smell of cut stems, and under it a thin sharp trace of ozone from the bus bars.

**The rule of sound**

Sound does not carry. Say it plainly, because it governs everything about being here: in the hushwater, a voice dies within an arm's length. The polymer eats it. A hammer blow on plate steel is a soft knock felt in the sternum and gone. The pumps at four rpm produce, at three metres' distance, nothing at all.

So the basin is quiet the way a photograph is quiet. What sounds exist are small and intimate: the tick of your own jaw, the creak of a frond bending against your shoulder, a bubble leaving your sleeve and going up with a sound like a page turning very far away. To speak, you must be close enough to touch, and touching is how you begin speaking; there is an etiquette here, wrist to wrist, that the builders developed within a month and never wrote down.

On the rim there are bells. Eleven of them, brass, bright as the day they came off the truck, hung under little cast hoods with the founder's mark facing out. They were specified early, before the fluid, by someone who imagined a place that could be called across. They cannot ring. Nobody has taken them down.

**The census**

There is one animal population in the basin, and it is kept in a jar.

On the shelf in the gauge house, above the tide-marks and the chalked pump logs, a glass vessel the size of a fist holds a few millilitres of water from Terrace Five, and in that water live seven nematodes and a rotifer, arrived on a leaf, discovered in a filter basket, and — after some discussion — not destroyed. A card is wired to the neck of the jar. It gives the date, the finder's initials, and the word *CENSUS*, and beneath that, in a different hand, a running tally that has been amended twice.

They are the whole of the animal kingdom here. Everything else that moves, moves because a shaft turns it or the water lifts it.

**The long slow**

Nothing in the basin happens fast. The seasons are internal: the nineteen-day turnover, the eleven-minute comb, the annual black lamination on the walls, the six centimetres of column. Over decades the leaf-meadows thicken and sag until a section of halocline gives way and a hundred years of yellow litter slumps into the lower gallery in a soundless brown avalanche that takes four hours to finish. The tree ferns on the rim grow a metre in a human lifetime. The epoxy stays tacky. The bells stay bright.

And the whole apparatus, basin and flora and jar and bells, rises. Very slowly, and without any noise, out of the cloud, into the clean cold light — a warm green dish of engineered water carried up by a pillar it is secreting from itself, going nowhere, growing, holding its breath.
