## The Hollow at the Mill

You come into it downhill, which is the only way, because everything here is downhill from the ice.

The hollow is small — perhaps twenty paces across at the water's edge, forty if you count the shelf of gravel where the moss has begun. It is a kettle: a bowl left where a buried block of ice sat and rotted out of the ground, some decades ago, no longer than a human life. On three sides it is walled by a moraine ridge of loose grey rubble, sharp-crested, unslumped, still standing at the angle at which it was tipped out. On the fourth side, two hundred meters off and above, is the snout of the glacier, and everything in the hollow is a consequence of that.

The first thing is the noise.

It arrives before you see anything: not a roar exactly, but a compound sound, a stacked sound, the way a factory floor is not loud in one voice but in nine. Underneath, continuous, a bass hydraulic thrum from inside the ice, where a river is running in a closed pipe under pressure — you feel it in the shins before you hear it. Above that, the white shredding hiss of the outflow at the portal, milk-grey water leaving the ice under a low arch and immediately splitting into braids. Above that again, irregular percussion: cobbles knocking along the streambed, one every second or so, a tumbling of hard objects down a chute. And then, at unpredictable intervals, a report like a plate press slamming — ice cracking somewhere in the body of the glacier, the sound coming through rock rather than air, so that it seems to happen inside your own chest.

You cannot speak here at conversational volume. You would have to lean in and shout, and you would be shouted back at.

The impression of machinery does not fade with familiarity. It sharpens. The bass note has a period to it — it swells and slackens on a cycle of some hours, tied to the melt, so that in the late afternoon the hollow is deafening and by four in the morning it has fallen to a working hum, as though a shift has ended and only the maintenance crew is left running the pumps. The water that comes out is not water in any ordinary sense but a product: opaque, pale as skimmed milk, cold enough to numb a hand in fifteen seconds, and loaded with rock flour ground so fine it will not settle for days. Somewhere in there, under a hundred meters of ice, stones are being dragged across bedrock at the pressure of the whole weight above them, and the bedrock is being planed, and the stones are being reduced. It is a mill. It has a throughput. Its output is measurable in tonnes per day and it runs without an operator, without a wall, without a single made thing anywhere in sight.

That is the central fact of the place, and the strangest one: it is heavily industrial and entirely unbuilt. There is no bolt, no weld, no straight line except the striations.

---

Those you find on the exposed bedrock at the head of the hollow, where the ground has been uncovered so recently that it is still polished. Run a palm across it: glass-smooth, and then the grooves, fine as the marks of a coarse file, all parallel, all pointing the way the ice went. They are fresh. Nothing has yet had time to blunt them — no frost has pried the surface, no lichen has acid-etched it, no leaf litter has ever lain on it, because there are no leaves within a kilometer large enough to fall. The rock is younger, as a surface, than most of the birds that visit it.

Everything here is dated by release. The bedrock at the top: seven years out. The gravel shelf: maybe thirty. The moss on the seepage line: as old as the seepage line, which is as old as the drainage, which changes. Beyond the moraine crest, out of sight, the land grows steadily older and softer at the rate of a few meters a year, and turns eventually into something with soil in it. In the hollow there is no soil. There is grit, and there is water, and there is a black felt of cyanobacteria on the wet stones that is the beginning of soil, doing the first work.

---

The second fact is that everything weeps.

The air sits at its dew point and stays there. Fog forms in the hollow and does not leave it; it lies in the bowl like something poured, thinning at midday to a luminous grey, thickening at evening until the far bank of the pond is a rumor. The moraine sweats. The rubble is fine-grained enough to hold water and cold enough to condense it, so every stone face has a skin of moisture on it and the shaded ones grow small icicles overnight and shed them by ten in the morning with a tinkling nobody hears over the mill.

The water table is at the level of your ankles. Step off the gravel and the ground gives, then fills the print. Where seepage lines emerge along the base of the ridge, the moss goes extravagant: cushions of it, thumb-deep, saturated, in a green so saturated it seems to be lit from inside — an emerald that has no business in a landscape otherwise composed of nine greys. Squeeze one and it gives up half a cup. Liverworts plate the wet rock in flat overlapping tongues. In the trickles, filaments of algae stream out downcurrent, bright as new wire, combed all one way.

Everything green is horizontal. The tallest plant in the hollow is a dwarf willow, three fingers high, with a trunk as thick as a pencil and possibly forty years of rings inside it, sprawled flat against the gravel with its leaves turned up like small pale palms. There is one mountain sorrel in a rock crack, and a scatter of cotton grass on the pond margin, and at the edge of the shelf a single tuft of grass, alone, twenty meters from any other tuft of grass.

Which brings up the third fact: for all its noise and all its wetness and all its running processes, the hollow is nearly empty.

The pond has no fish, no delta, no reeds, no rim of drift — it is a clean grey lens of water in a clean grey bowl of stone, and its bottom drops away into a milk that goes from pale to blue to nothing at about two meters. Between the moss cushions there are long open floors of graded gravel with nothing living on them at all, not one plant, not one shell, swept and sorted. The volume of the hollow is mostly air and mostly fog. You can stand in the middle of it and be the only vertical thing for a hundred meters in any direction, and this is not a lonely feeling so much as a conspicuous one. In a place that loud, you expect a crowd.

---

The crowd exists. It simply doesn't hold still, and most of it is smaller than a grain of rice.

Out on the ice, on the dirty apron where the snout is melting down, there is a stippling of dark pits — cryoconite holes, each the width of a coin, each drilled by its own trapped freight of dust absorbing what light there is. Look into one: a cylinder of clear meltwater, walls of white ice, and at the bottom a few millimeters of black granular sludge that is alive. Rotifers. Tardigrades. Copepods the size of commas. Bacteria in the billions. Each hole a sealed pocket-world within a pocket-world, ten thousand of them across the apron, and each with its own weather and its own population and its own date of founding.

Walking on the ice between them, wingless and lightless, are the glacier midges — slow, deliberate, black, cold-loving, mating on a surface at the freezing point in an atmosphere no other insect will tolerate. They come down to the hollow in loose drifting hundreds when the fog lifts, and the birds know it.

The birds are the reason the hollow feels abundant despite the emptiness: they treat it as a station, not a home. A wagtail works the gravel bar at the outflow, flicking, running four steps, flicking. A wheatear owns the largest boulder and returns to it, chik-chik, inaudible against the mill, so that you see the beak open and hear nothing, which is unnerving. Snow buntings arrive in a party of nine, feed for eleven minutes on windblown seed, and go. A skua crosses at a hundred meters, twice, unhurried, on the diagonal. On the moraine crest there are fox tracks in the fine silt, going somewhere else, and along the shore a line of goose droppings from a flock that stopped for one night in spring and will stop again in autumn. The biomass here is enormous and it is nearly all in transit. The hollow is a service stop on a much larger circuit, deafening, cold, streaming with food, offering nothing to build a nest in.

---

Some rules of the place, as far as one can determine them by standing in it for a day:

Nothing here is older than the ice's retreat, and the ice's retreat is recent and continuing. There are no ruins, no fossils, no bones except this year's. History has a ceiling, and the ceiling is low.

Nothing decays. It is too cold and too new. What dies goes grey and stays whole. A midge that drowned last August is still a midge.

Nothing clears. The water is permanently turbid because the mill never stops feeding it flour. You will never see the bottom of the pond, and no one ever has.

Nothing is still. Not the water, obviously, but not the ground either — the whole hollow is trembling faintly, all the time, at a frequency you stop noticing and then notice again. If you set a cup down, the surface of it shivers.

And nothing is finished. The braids of the outflow will have moved by tomorrow. The moss cushion at the base of the ridge is being fed by a seep that will migrate when the next rockfall re-plumbs the slope, and then the moss will die and a new moss will start somewhere else, forty meters left. The moraine crest will slump eventually and the pond will silt and become a bog and then a meadow, and the hollow will stop being loud, and stop being cold, and be an ordinary damp green dip in a landscape — but not for a long time, and not while the mill is running.

---

Toward evening the light does the one genuinely peculiar thing. The overcast thickens, the fog closes, everything above goes to slate — and the ice begins to give light back upward. Not much: a low, cold, blue exhalation out of the crevasse fields and out from under the portal arch, so that the underside of the fog is faintly lit from below and the hollow is illuminated wrongly, from the ground, the shadows on your face pointing up. The moss goes black. The water goes luminous. The mill changes gear, slackens, and settles into its night rate — that steady hydraulic working hum, patient, unattended, throughput reduced but never zero — and you understand, standing there in a bowl of wet grey stone with no structure in it and nothing made by anything with hands, that you are inside a plant, that it has been running for ten thousand years, and that it will be running when you leave.
