## The Laid Country

Go down on your knees anywhere in this basin and put your ear to the ground and you will hear it: hollow. Not the hollow of a cave, which is ragged and swallows sound, but the tuned hollow of a room below a room. Knock, and something answers three floors down. Everything here has been laid — course upon course of grey-green brick, mortared with a clay that never fully cures, and beneath that course another, and beneath that another, and no one who has dug has ever come to soil. The diggers of the Sixth Shaft went down what they reckoned was three hundred and forty courses and struck a floor of glazed tile, swept clean, still faintly warm. They came up and did not go back.

So there is no bedrock. There is only masonry going down into the dark like an argument that refuses to end.

---

The basin itself is broad and shallow, perhaps a day and a half's walk across at the widest, and its rim is not a ridge of rock but a wall — brick, of course, sixty or seventy courses high, unfinished at the top so that its silhouette is a ragged comb of stubs and waiting spaces. You can see the rim only as a place where the dark becomes more definite. Nothing rises high in the middle. This is a low country, a floor country, and the building here goes outward and downward rather than up. Terraces, sinks, stepped courts, aqueduct troughs running on squat piers no taller than a man. The whole thing crowded. Walls a shoulder's width apart, then a court, then more walls, arcades doubling back into arcades, chimneys that have not yet been given anything to draw from, stairs delivering you onto the roof of the next quarter, which is also its street. Layer against layer, near-touching. In some districts you walk with a hand on either wall and the sky — if there is a sky — is a slot.

---

The light is a rumour. There is a general upper greyness, cool and sourceless, which is enough to tell your hands apart from the wall but not enough to read the maker's mark on a brick. Below that, in the slots and undercrofts and the deeper courts, the only light is wickmoss: a pale mineral scurf, closer to a stain than a plant, that colonises damp mortar and gives off a greenish exhalation you feel more than see. It grows in the joints. It maps the courses. Walk a long gallery and the brickwork shows you its own construction in faint horizontal lines, thousands of them, converging away into nothing, and you understand that you are inside a diagram.

Crews carry lamps of hammered copper burning a rendered fat, and these are precious, and their light is small and orange and does not travel. What travels is sound.

---

Because this is a loud country. Ten thousand mallets. The tack-tack-tack of trimming, the deeper bock of a brick seated home, the sluicing hiss of mortar off a board, the groan of a timber crane taking weight, and under all of it the drumming — the hollow floor answering every one of those blows, so that the ground itself is a struck skin, and the noise you hear is never the noise being made but the noise the world makes about it. Sound arrives late here, and doubled, and from underneath. Speak to someone six paces off and you must lean in. New workers get headaches for a month and then stop noticing, which is a kind of loss.

Twice a day come the tally-horns, long clay tubes braced in the walls, and their note is so low it moves water in a bowl.

---

There is no rain, exactly. There is the Breath.

Twice between horn and horn, the temperature drops a few degrees and the whole basin sweats. Every wall beads. The bricks are porous and full of a water they have held since the day they were pugged, and when the cool comes on, it comes out of them: a fine general weeping, walls silvering over, mortar going dark, water gathering along the underside of every arch and letting go in slow fat drops. For an hour the country rains upward out of its own material. The gutters run. The wickmoss visibly swells. Then it stops, and for the rest of the shift the surfaces dry to a chalky bloom, and the drop-count in the cisterns is the only calendar anyone keeps.

The cisterns are down: sunk courts, roofed with brick vaults, black-surfaced, breathing. They rise and fall on a rhythm no one has satisfactorily explained, roughly but not exactly matched to the Breath, which means the tides drift, which means the cistern crews have to be told each shift where the waterline will be. That job is a profession. Those people are respected.

---

The tools are wood and rope and stone and a little soft copper. Mallets, floats, wedges, plumb bobs, a chalked line, a level made of a trough of water with a floating reed. Cranes are treadle-and-timber, cage-wheel affairs walked round by four workers who count aloud, because counting aloud is the only way to keep the load steady over the noise. Bricks are made in the low kiln-fields at the basin's western sink, where the clay comes from — and the clay comes from unmaking: from taking down finished walls, pounding them back to grit, wetting them, working them, and pressing them into new brick. Nothing arrives. The country is built out of itself, moved from where it was to where it is going to be, and this is why the districts advance across the basin like a slow tide, leaving behind them a stubble of foundations and the ghost-lines of rooms that will never be roofed now.

---

The strangest thing about the Laid Country is how new it all is.

Nothing here is worn. Corners are sharp. Edges have their burr still on them. There is no dust worth the name — a little brick flour where the trimming happens, washed off by the next Breath. Stairs have no dip in the tread. Sills have no groove where a rope has run. Thresholds are as square as the day they were set. You come to understand, walking here, that you are inside a place that has not yet had time happen to it, and the sensation is not freshness but suspense: like a struck bell in the instant before it sounds, like a room where everyone has just stopped talking.

For this reason the Worn Step is the holy thing of this country.

It is in the Fourth Quarter, in a narrow flight between two blind walls, and it is the ninth step, and it has a shallow dip in the middle of it, no deeper than a thumb-joint. Feet did it. Some accident of routing sent enough traffic over that one stair for long enough, and the brick gave a little, and now it is a saucer, and now it holds a spoonful of water after every Breath. People come. They queue. They put a bare foot in the hollow and stand there for a moment with an expression that is hard to read from the side. The step proves that the world is going somewhere. It proves that the country will one day be old, and that oldness is available, and that it is possible to leave a mark by simply continuing.

There is a second, disputed site: a copper chisel in the Rope-Makers' court, hung on a nail, its edge visibly rolled from use. Some hold this to be a lesser instance of the same miracle. Others say tools are not the world.

---

Life is thin and specific. No trees, no grass, no green thing anywhere — the word for plant, if there is one, is borrowed from the wickmoss and means *the stain that shines*. What there is:

**Cistern eels**, or something eel-adjacent, blind, the colour of wet mortar, that live in the sunk waters and are pulled out in wicker traps perhaps once a week per crew. They are the fat in the lamps and the fat in the pot. Nobody knows where they come from. Nobody has found a small one.

**Mortar crickets** in the joints, drawn to the damp, which do not chirp but click — a dry, sparse, irregular clicking that you only hear in the rare pockets of quiet and which everyone finds unpleasant.

**Kiln moths**, big as a hand, pale, arriving at the lamps in ones and twos, never in numbers. Their wings are said to be marked with courses of brick. This is true in the way such things are true.

And the **drays**: heavy, low, blunt-headed animals, hairless, patient, six of them in the whole western sink, harnessed to sledges of brick. They are the largest living things anyone has seen. When one dies its bones go into the kiln-field as grog, ground into the clay, which means that everywhere in the walls of the Laid Country there is a trace of bone, distributed evenly, at the finest possible dilution. Masons rub their thumb on a fresh brick and say: *there's a little dray in this one.* It is a joke and it is not.

---

The rules of the place, insofar as anyone has articulated them:

Water comes out of the wall, not out of the air.

Nothing may be built on new ground; you must take down to build up, so that the total is always the same and only the arrangement changes.

Sound travels through the floor faster than through the air, and therefore what you hear beneath you is the present, and what you hear around you is the past. Warnings are given by stamping.

Wear is sacred. Do not sand a worn thing smooth.

And the last one, which is not a rule but a fact everyone works inside of: no one has ever found the edge of the brick, in any direction, down or out. The rim wall is not the boundary. It is simply the current work front, and beyond it, in the greater dark, you can hear — faintly, doubled, arriving out of the floor — mallets.
