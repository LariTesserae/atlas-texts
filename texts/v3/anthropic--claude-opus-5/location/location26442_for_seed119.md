## the room at the bottom of the stair

Ninety-one steps, cut in a spiral inside a hollow pier, each one dished in the middle to the depth of a cupped hand by feet that stopped coming a long time ago. You hear the place before you see it. Somewhere around the fortieth step the sound arrives — not loud yet, but *large*, a pressure in the ears like weather coming, a roar with a grain in it, a bed of tiny knocks and ticks and a low continuous note underneath that you feel in the sternum and the back teeth.

Then the stair opens and there is the room, and the first thing you understand is that it is empty. Not small — a corbelled dome perhaps forty paces across, springing from eight squat piers, rising maybe eleven times the height of a person to a single round eye at the crown. But the volume of it is unoccupied in a way that feels deliberate. All the life is pressed to the skin: to the walls, the ledges, the water's surface. The middle of the air holds nothing at all. Stand in the centre on the ring-walk and you are the only thing in a great cool bubble of nothing, being deafened.

## what it is made of

Everything. There is no ground here. The floor is paving; under the paving, where a slab has slumped and you can look through, there is more paving. The walls are courses of a grey-green stone laid dry, without mortar, fitted so closely that a wet hair won't enter the joint, and in the sheltered undersides of the corbels you can still see the arcs left by copper or bone chisels — the marks are shallow, spaced like breathing, made by hand, made slowly, made by many people who were not in a hurry.

Everywhere else the tool marks are gone. Two thousand seasons of water have taken them. The stone has reached its settled shape: rounded arrises, cushioned edges, the drip-lines under the vault worn into vertical channels smooth as the inside of a throat. And over all of it, growing, a second skin — a pale flowstone the colour of old fat, laid down grain by grain from the water itself, softening the courses, welding the dry joints, making one thing out of many. The room is slowly becoming a cave. It is not winning and it is not losing. The flowstone thickens at the same rate the frost and the salt spall the faces behind it. It has been in this balance so long that the balance is the only real architecture left.

## the water

The lower third of the room is water, and the water is the reason the room exists, and the water comes out of the walls.

There is no inlet. Generations of drawers have looked. The shafts bring down light and swifts and rain-spatter in the wet months, nothing more. But cut stone weeps here — that is simply a property of the material, the way iron rusts elsewhere. A fresh-broken face beads within an hour. An old face weeps steadily along its flowstone runnels. Hold your palm to any block in the room and take it away wet. The builders did not dig a cistern and wait for it to fill. They quarried the walls, and the walls filled the room they had been taken out of, and they cut ninety-one steps to reach the result.

And it breathes. Twice a day, on a cycle a little longer than half a day and answerable to nothing anyone has found, the water rises about a cubit and then goes back down. At high breath the ring-walk is awash and the algal felt on the tideline is drowned and grazing. At low breath the felt is exposed, dripping, dull green-black, and the whole room smells of cold mineral and crushed leaf. The tideline is the busiest strip in the world here — a band four hands wide where everything happens.

The water is very clear and very cold and stained faintly with something that makes it look, in the shafts of light, the colour of weak tea held up to a window.

## weather, indoors

It is cool. Your breath shows. The dome is colder than the water, so all day the water gives up mist that climbs the middle of the room — the only thing that ever occupies the middle — and hangs under the vault as a slow grey ceiling within the ceiling, and rains. A fine, continual, distributed rain, falling the whole height of the dome into the pool. It never stops. In the light-shafts you can see it as a lit dust of falling.

That is the room's respiration and half its noise.

## sound

It is loud in here in a way that is difficult to explain to people who have only heard loud things outdoors.

The rain is the floor of it: forty thousand small impacts a second across the pool's surface, which the dome collects and returns as a hiss with a body to it, like a river heard from inside a jar. Over that, the drips — not the rain but the *fat* drips, the ones that come off the corbel tips and the flowstone teats, each one falling ten or twelve times a person's height and landing with a *tock* that the vault takes and lengthens and hands back seven counts later. There are perhaps three hundred of these on the go, none of them synchronized, all of them metronomic. Over that, the animals.

And under everything, the note. The dome has a pitch. It takes all this input and gives back one long standing hum, low, unwavering, felt more than heard, the sound a shell makes if a shell were the size of a hamlet. The room prefers this note. Sing it and it stays in the air after you stop, for a count of ten, twelve, and comes back at you thickened, as though others had joined in. Sing anything else and it dies flat in three counts. Speech does not survive here at all — it arrives at the far wall as porridge. The drawers who still come use hands, and when hands aren't enough they put their foreheads together and hum against each other's skull.

The frogs found the note first. Everything here is tuned to it.

## the living

Abundant, and all of it flat against the surfaces.

**Drum-frogs**, thousands, each no longer than a thumb joint, pale as boiled rice, living one to a pocket in the honeycomb of shrunken joints between courses. Each pocket is a little resonating chamber and each frog has spent its lineage selecting the pocket that best amplifies it. At the two turns of the light — when the shafts brighten and when they fail — they all call at once, on the room's note, and the sound goes past loud into something physical. It moves the mist. It is impossible to stay for it the first time. The old drawers say you have to let it get into your chest and stop resisting it, the way you'd let cold water in.

**Swiftlets**, or the things that stand for swiftlets: sooty, small, blind or near enough, nesting in the vault on brackets of their own hardened spit. They navigate the dark by clicking and there are enough of them that the clicking is a constant dry crackle overhead, like a fire. Twice a day they pour up out of the eye of the dome in a rope of bodies, out to whatever is up there, and come back.

**Eels** in the deep water, some of them longer than a person, unhurried, the top of everything. They come up through the pool at high breath and take frogs off the tideline with a sound like a slap. Between times you see them as a slow bend of shadow across the paving eight feet down.

**Midges**, hatching in sheets off the felt, feeding the swiftlets and the frogs and dying into the water to feed the shrimp.

**Blind shrimp**; **isopods**; **cave crickets** with legs like wire on the wet walls; **tiler snails** whose shells, generation upon generation, get ground into the flowstone and are part of the walls now — you can see them in section where a block has spalled, a pale confetti in the rind.

**One kind of wading bird**, small, chalk-white, five or six of them at any time, standing in the shallows with a patience that seems geological. Nobody has seen one arrive and nobody has seen one leave.

And the vegetation: almost none. The algal felt at the tideline. A grey moss where the three light-shafts land, in circles the size of a shield, exactly where the light lands and not a finger further. One rootless weed drifting in the pool that may be an animal. That's all. There isn't soil. There isn't anywhere for soil to be.

## the ones who come

Rarely. Twice a season, four or five of them, down the ninety-one steps with clay lamps in horn shades and coiled hair-rope and a coracle of reed and pitch that they carry in pieces and put together on the ring-walk. They fill jars. They lift eel traps woven of split cane. They cut nothing and build nothing — the room does not need building and hasn't for a very long time — but they scrape the flowstone off the four drain-mouths with a bone blade, which takes an afternoon, and which is the entire maintenance of the place.

Before they go up they add a tally stroke to the pier by the stair, in a column of tally strokes that runs from head-height down to the tideline and continues under the water for a distance nobody has measured.

Going down, the steps number ninety-one. Everyone agrees on this. Coming up, the count comes out differently for each person, and never twice the same for one person, and this is not considered remarkable. You simply climb until you are out. The room is loud, and the counting mind is not the part of you that works well in there.
