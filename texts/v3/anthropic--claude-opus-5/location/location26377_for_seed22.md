## The Sounding Flats

### First, the sound

You will not hear the landscape. You will *be inside* it, the way a fly is inside a struck bell.

The noise here is not an event. It has no beginning, and nothing in the valley remembers a time before it. It comes up out of the ground more than it comes down from the air: a pressure in the sternum around forty hertz, a floor-tone you feel in the teeth and the soft tissue behind the eyes, over which sits a braided upper register — grinding, chiming, a sound like a cathedral organ being sawn in half very slowly, forever. At the loudest belts it is somewhere past one hundred and fifty decibels, which is not a volume so much as a weather. Rain cannot fall straight through it. Loose sand will not lie down. Standing in the wrong place, you can watch the hair on your forearm comb itself into stripes.

Everything else in this description follows from that.

### Terrain

The valley is wide — sixty, seventy kilometers from rim to rim, and the rims are low, more a suggestion of shoulders than mountains. The floor lies barely above sea level and slopes so gently westward that the eye reads it as flat. Beyond the western haze there is a warm shallow ocean, and the flats are advancing into it at roughly nine meters a year.

The ground is new. You can tell by touching it: the rock has no patience in it yet, no rounded shoulders, no soil-skin of any depth. It comes up in terraces, each one a handspan to a meter high, arranged in long smooth arcs and hyperbolas tens of kilometers wide, and the arcs are *concentric with the towers*. This is because the land is not sedimentary and it is not volcanic. It is precipitated. Mineral dissolved in the groundwater and mineral suspended in the fog are shaken out of solution at the pressure nodes of a standing wave field and laid down in laminae a few micrometers thick, night after night, node upon node. Cut a terrace face and it reads like a phonograph: every hour of the last four centuries, banded, legible, playable.

The edges are razor-fresh. Frost never touched them; nothing has had time to break them. But the sound does its own erosion, and it is a strange, selective kind. Where a resonance sits hard against a terrace lip, the stone is polished to a wet-looking gloss and eventually opens into a flute or a blowhole. The flats are perforated with these — chimneys forty centimeters across that exhale warm mist on the downbeat and inhale it back, breathing in time with something you cannot see. Walk near one and it will try to take your hat.

Between the terraces lie shallow damp pans, never quite ponds. Water here is scarce in the ordinary sense — it rains perhaps thirty days a year — but the air is at ninety-odd percent humidity all day, every day, and every hard surface wears a film of condensation. The valley is not wet. It is *sweating*.

### The criers

Forty of them stand within sight on a clear hour; ranks of them go back into the haze east and north, and the survey lattice suggests some eleven thousand across the whole apron.

Each is nine hundred meters tall and slender out of all proportion, like a reed. The material is not metal and not ceramic; it is a graded lattice that changes composition continuously from base to tip, so that its acoustic impedance never meets a discontinuity and it can pour energy into the ground without cracking itself. Where sunlight finds one, it is the color of a fingernail held over a candle. They do not sway. In gusts, they do something worse — they *hold*, absolutely, while the air around them visibly bends.

At the base of each crier the ground has been drawn up into a hill that was not there before, because the tower has been calling stone toward itself for four hundred years.

Nobody in the valley knows what physics they run on, in the sense that the people who built them wrote it down and the writing is preserved and it is nevertheless not the physics anyone else uses. They convert something diffuse and everywhere-available into coherent mechanical energy at very high efficiency, and they do it without a fuel line, an intake, or measurable waste heat beyond the sound itself. The sound *is* the waste heat. The sound is also the product. That is the whole design: they were built to manufacture ground, and the noise is what manufacturing ground sounds like when you do it well.

At dawn and at dusk the whole field shifts pitch — a slow slide of a minor third, taking about eleven minutes, as the node pattern is walked forward to lay the next lamina slightly seaward of the last. During those eleven minutes the valley is genuinely dangerous. Antinodes move. Standing water leaps out of the pans in vertical sheets and hangs there. Small stones migrate. Anything with a resonant cavity in its body — a lung, a swim bladder, a skull — will feel that cavity being *found*.

### Light and air

Dim, always. Not dark: dim, milky, shadowless, the light of an hour before a storm that never arrives. The sound keeps a permanent load of fine dust and aerosolized water in the lower two kilometers of atmosphere; the sun is a bright smear you can look at directly, and it casts no edge. Noon and four in the afternoon are indistinguishable except by the color of the smear.

Because the field dissipates as heat, and because the heat is delivered evenly across the whole floor, there is almost no thermal gradient — which means almost no wind. The air simply sits. Warm, close, thirty degrees at midday and twenty-six at midnight, tasting of wet limestone and crushed green. Fog forms and does not blow away; it stands in the pans in ribbed bars, combed into stripes by the wave field, and burns off from the top down. From the valley rim at dawn you can see the standing wave pattern *drawn in fog*, a hundred kilometers of interference figure, hyperbolas laid over hyperbolas, sharp as an etching.

### The plants

Lush, and lush in a very particular direction.

Nothing here grows tall and broad; broad surfaces are torn. The dominant strategy is the reed and the tube. The flats are covered — in bands, always bands, always following the nodal lines, so that from any height the vegetation looks like a contour map of something invisible — with a dense growth of hollow-stemmed things two to four meters high, green-black, with walls of a strange tough foam. They are tuned. Each species occupies a frequency band, and each individual grows its stem length to match the local field until it hums in sympathy. The valley floor is a fitted instrument.

They use it. These plants do not transpire much — there is no dry air to pull water into, and no wind to help — and they do not need to, because the vibration in their stems does two jobs. It drives sap: peristaltic acoustic pumping, cheap and tireless, so that even a seedling can move fluid nine meters. And it harvests: the outer skin of each stem is textured at exactly the scale that nucleates condensation, and it shivers, and fog becomes water on it at a rate of two or three liters per stem per night, which runs down and into the ground and comes out again as dissolved mineral, and becomes, eventually, more valley.

In the antinodes, where the pressure swing is savage, nothing grows but a low black crust with a mirror finish. In the nodes — the true quiet lines, where the field cancels itself — you get riot. Thickets. Broad leaves, at last, and vines, and a soft-bodied undergrowth that would be shredded a meter to either side. These node-thickets run in perfect arcs across the whole valley, twenty meters wide and forty kilometers long, green ribbons on gray stone, and they are the highways of everything that moves.

### The animals

Abundant, and none of them have ears.

Ears are a liability here; every vertebrate lineage in the valley either lost them or sealed them behind bone. What they have instead is skin. Pressure-reading papillae, lateral-line organs generalized across the whole flank, so that an animal here does not *hear* the wave field — it feels its own position within it, continuously, the way you feel which way is down. They navigate by interference pattern. They know where they are to within a few centimeters because they can feel the shape of the standing wave against their sides. There are no lost animals in the Sounding Flats.

The big herbivores are the gulpers: three meters at the shoulder, slow, wide-footed, skulls fused into solid bone helmets with the sensory organs pushed out onto the cheeks and throat. They graze the tuned reed-stems, and while grazing they hum, and the hum is not communication but *echolocation by disruption* — they can feel the hole they punch in the field, and the shape of the hole tells them where their calves are. Herds of forty move along the node-ribbons at the pace of a walking man, all day, all year, following the seasonal drift of the quiet lines.

The small things ride the quiet. Flocks of leathery gliders, hand-sized, breed exclusively in node-thickets and cross the loud ground between them in low fast dashes, folded tight, taking the hit. Something like a crab, in enormous numbers, farms the antinode crust. There are burrowers who have discovered that a meter of stone is a good baffle and who come up only at the eleven-minute pitch-slide, when the field is briefly incoherent and the whole valley floor boils with them.

And there is the hush.

It is a predator, roughly the mass of a large dog, long and low, with a dorsal array of soft plates. What it does is generate a destructive-interference shadow around itself: a moving pocket of near-silence perhaps four meters across. To an animal that navigates by feeling the wave field, the hush does not appear as a presence. It appears as a *hole in the world*, an absence with no shape and no location, and by the time the prey has understood that the absence is moving, it is inside it, deaf and blind and standing still in confusion. Hushes hunt the node-thickets, because in a node they are invisible in the ordinary way as well.

Larger quiet pockets drift through the valley too, born from the beat frequencies of distant criers — quietholes, tens of meters across, wandering at walking pace. Animals crowd into them and will not leave, and the crowding brings the hushes, and so a passing quiethole leaves behind it a print of bones in a rough circle, which the reeds grow up through in a ring within a season.

### The town

There is a town. Nobody is in it.

It sits in a permanent node — a true one, a fixed point in the interference geometry where the field has cancelled itself since the first day the criers spoke and will keep cancelling until they stop. Fourteen thousand people could have lived there. The infrastructure implies it: five levels of terraced arcade cut back into a rise, foam-baffled berms, a plaza with a fountain that still works, sunken streets roofed in acoustic mesh, a rail spur, storage for two hundred years of dry goods, a hospital, three schools, orchards under netting that have gone feral and enormous, and a beautiful little library with pressure-sealed doors and bone-conduction reading collars racked by the entrance in sizes down to child.

The population is zero. Not fled — *finished*. The record is unashamed about it: the criers were commissioned, the crew stayed forty years for commissioning, the commissioning succeeded, and the crew left, because the whole point of the project was that it would not need them. What remains is maintenance, and maintenance is done by things the size of your thumb that walk the arcades in loose columns and do not acknowledge visitors.

So the town is kept. Swept. The fountain runs. The orchard netting is repaired. Lights come on in the arcades at dusk, dim amber, one in six, enough to walk by, and go out at dawn. The plaza is full of gulpers most evenings, standing in the quiet with their eyes half shut, and gliders roost in the school colonnade in the thousands, and the maintenance columns thread between their feet, and the whole tableau is silent — genuinely silent, the only silence for forty kilometers — while beyond the berm the valley grinds on at a hundred and fifty decibels, day and night, laying down its nine meters a year, extending itself patiently into a sea it has not reached yet.

### Rules, if you go

You cannot shout. Your voice will not exist. Everything is done by hand, by touch, by written note, or by a collar against the jawbone.

You cannot use your ears, and after a week you will stop trying, and something in you will start reading pressure through your ribs instead — badly, but enough that you will know when you have wandered off a node before you know how you know.

Do not sleep in a quiethole. Nothing that sleeps in a quiethole wakes up.

Do not be standing on stone at the pitch-slide, dawn or dusk. Eleven minutes. Sit in the town, or sit in a node, or lie flat in a pan with your mouth open.

The ground you are standing on did not exist when your grandmother was born, and by the time your grandchildren come it will be four millimeters higher and thirty meters further out to sea, and the reeds will have moved with it, and the gulpers with them, and the hushes after the gulpers, and the criers will still be singing the note that makes all of it, and there will still be nobody here to hear it.
