## The Shoulder

At five thousand nine hundred metres, the air is too thin to carry a shout more than a few paces, and this is the first thing you learn about the place: it is enormous and it is quiet, and the two facts are the same fact. A rockfall on the far wall of the crater looks like a rockfall and sounds like sand poured into a cupped hand. Men working two hundred metres away move in perfect silence, their picks rising and falling out of sync with the faint tapping that eventually reaches you, so that the sound seems to belong to some other, slower crew working just behind them.

The mountain is called the Shoulder because from the salt basin, sixty kilometres west and four thousand metres down, that is what it is — one immense sloped deltoid of a range whose head has been removed. Up close there is no shoulder, no summit, no single shape at all. There is only the pinnacle field.

---

### The pinnacle field

Hydrothermal fluids have been chewing the summit dacite from underneath for something on the order of a hundred thousand years, converting it, vein by vein, into alunite and kaolin and pale sugary silica, and the wind and the sun and the nightly freeze have been taking away what the fluids softened. What remains is a forest. There is no better word. Pillars two to nine metres tall, packed so tightly that from any vantage the field reads as solid ground — a dirty-cream pavement in the middle distance, ochre and rust and sulfur-yellow where the vents have painted it — and only when you are in it do you understand that the pavement is a canopy of tops and that you are walking in corridors.

The corridors are barely a body wide. They branch, rejoin, dead-end, drop into slots you must lower yourself through backwards. There is no place on the summit plateau where you can see more than eleven metres in any horizontal direction, and no place where you can see less than sixty kilometres upward and outward if you climb a pillar. The field is packed. Nothing on the Shoulder is spaced out. Everything is jammed against everything else: pillar against pillar, vent against vent, colony against colony, the living crowded onto the same square metres of warm damp rind as the long dead.

Between the pillars the floor is lag gravel over ash, and it is hot. This is the second thing you learn.

---

### Heat from below

The sun at this altitude is a physical weight; it burns the backs of hands in twenty minutes and it can be dodged. The ground cannot. The heat here comes up. Sixty metres beneath the pinnacle field, the old magmatic plumbing is still warm enough to boil groundwater, and the crater floor and the corridor floors run at fifty to seventy degrees, hotter where the vents cluster, hot enough in three places to set a wool sole smoking.

So shade is not a relief. Shade is a trap: the pillars' northern lees are where the rising heat pools, undisturbed by wind, sixty-five degrees at ankle height and shimmering. The rule on the Shoulder is that you rest standing in the sun and walk in the shadow, and you keep walking. Everyone here has the same gait — long, unhurried, never stopping, the gait of people who have been taught by their feet.

The wind, meanwhile, is at minus four. You are cooked from below and chilled from above and the two meet somewhere around your sternum. Nobody who works here has ever been comfortable and nobody talks about it.

---

### The water

It does not rain. Or: it rains constantly and never arrives. Most afternoons a bruise-grey cell assembles over the eastern cordillera around two o'clock, throws down visible curtains of graupel, and the graupel evaporates eight hundred metres above the pillars. You watch rain fall towards you for an hour. The air smells of it. Nothing gets wet.

The water on the Shoulder comes out of the mountain instead of out of the sky. Every vent in the field exhales steam — some as a lazy trembling, some as a hard white jet with a note in it you can feel in your teeth but barely hear. All night, that steam condenses. It runs down the pillars in threads, collects in the flutes and pockets of the silica, beads on every overhang, and by an hour before dawn the whole pinnacle field is dripping and glistening and faintly warm, a rock forest sweating in the dark. By nine in the morning it is bone dry again and the corridors are back to smelling of matchheads.

Everything alive here is organized around that four-hour window.

There are also the pools. In the crater's low ground, six shallow lenses of hot brine, yellow-green and rimmed with concentric terraces of salt and sulfur precipitate, each terrace a different decade of a different water level. The pools are opaque. Things live in them anyway.

---

### The crowding of life

Vegetation is almost an afterthought: black crustose lichens on the wind faces, mustard-coloured lichens on the vent faces, and a horizontal band of moss and liverwort at exactly the height where the night condensation runs longest, so that every pillar in the field wears the same green stripe at the same elevation, kilometre after kilometre. Four cushion plants, hard as bread crust, in a sheltered gully where the ground is merely warm. That is the flora. You could count the individual vascular plants on the summit plateau in a long day.

The fauna is preposterous.

The brine pools make brine flies — not thousands, tens of millions, a black felt of them on the terraces that lifts as a single sheet when your shadow crosses it and resettles behind you. The flies make everything else. There are spiders in the corridors that eat nothing but flies, and lizards that eat spiders, and a small grey mouse-eared thing with a naked tail that lives in the pillars' hollows and appears to eat anything at all. There are three species of toad in water that would poach an egg at its inflow and merely scald you at its outflow; they keep to the millimetres between. There are flamingos — six hundred of them on the largest pool, high-altitude, pale, standing in water that steams around their legs, filtering the flies' larvae, and they are silent almost all day.

Twice a year the moths come up. Nobody knows why they climb; they arrive from below in numbers that fur the pillars, and for a week the mouse-eared things get fat and the mine crew sweeps drifts of wings out of the doorways.

And above it all, always, the birds: caracaras and a resident pair of black-chested eagles riding the thermals that boil off the pinnacle field, going up in silence, tilting, silent, cutting across the sun. The thermals here are ferocious. The birds do not have to work. It is the laziest predation on earth.

Nothing decays. That is the third thing. At this altitude and this dryness, a dead vicuña on the upper slopes does not rot; it desiccates, tightens, and stays. There are perhaps four hundred years of them scattered through the field, hide over bone, standing in some cases, wedged upright between two pillars where they fell. There are dogs from the 1920s. There is a mule with its pack still lashed on, in a slot, at the exact stage of leathery permanence it reached ninety years ago and will hold for another thousand. The corridors are crowded with the dead not as a horror but as a filing system. The mountain is a place where nothing goes away, and this is the deepest reason it feels packed.

---

### The works

The built things are few, small, and industrial out of all proportion to their size.

At the crater's northern lip: the sulfur camp. Nine buildings of stacked altered rock, roofed in flattened tin, walls stuffed with straw and llama hide, doorways hung with sacking. A latrine. A brick oven that has not been lit since the last resident cook died. One chapel the size of a garden shed, whitewashed, with a rack of candles that will not stay lit in the thin air and so are simply left standing unburnt, which everyone treats as sufficient.

And the plant. Two boiling vats for the autoclave process, cracked and abandoned. One diesel compressor under a lean-to, which is not abandoned — three or four days a week it turns over with a hollow, gutless cough (a diesel at 5,900 m makes perhaps a third of its rated power and sounds like it is apologizing) and drives two jackleg drills in the sulfur benches. A hundred metres of narrow-gauge track with four steel tubs on it. A workshop with a hand-cranked forge and a wall of tools worn thin by hands, hung in order.

And, going west, dropping over the edge of the world: the ropeway.

Twenty-two lattice towers descending nine kilometres and two thousand three hundred metres to the loading station at the rail spur. It is gravity-driven. Loaded buckets going down pull empty buckets coming up, and the whole system's speed is governed by a brake drum at the top that has been fitted with a small generator, so that the mountain's own sulfur, falling, makes the electricity: two strings of bulbs in the drift tunnels, one bulb over the workshop bench, and a radio nobody uses because nobody up here can be bothered to talk. The buckets go past on their cable at walking pace. The wheels make a soft periodic clack, and it is the loudest sustained sound on the Shoulder — a heartbeat with a nine-second interval, audible only within about thirty metres of the line.

Anywhere else this would be an industrial site. Here it is a few marks. The pinnacle field swallows it. From the fourth tower down you can look back and the camp is invisible; there is only a mustard-coloured pavement of pillar tops with a wire coming out of it.

---

### Anomalies

Four things about this place do not resolve.

The first is the sound. Everyone who works here reports that noises arrive late and from the wrong direction — an artifact of the corridors and the thin air, probably. But the crew also report a specific recurring phenomenon: the ropeway's clack, heard at night from inside the bunkhouse, at the correct nine-second interval, on nights when the brake is locked and the cable is still. They do not consider this remarkable. They consider it the mountain keeping time.

The second is the green stripe. It is level. Not approximately level — level, across nine kilometres of pillar field, to within a few centimetres, following no rock unit and no fracture, sitting exactly where the night condensation stops. This should be true and it is nevertheless the single most unsettling thing to see, because it makes the whole summit look like the tidemark of a lake that drained and could come back.

The third is the upcoming buckets. Roughly once a month, an ascending bucket arrives at the top station not empty but full — of sulfur, correctly graded, indistinguishable from what went down. The loading station below denies loading it. The crew tip it into the bins and note it in the ledger under a column that was ruled and headed by someone in 1954 for exactly this purpose.

The fourth is the breathing. The drift tunnels inhale and exhale on a cycle of about eleven minutes, strong enough to flutter a rag at the portal, and this is ordinary barometric cave breathing and entirely explicable. What is not explicable is that the interval is stable to the second and has been for as long as anyone has measured it, and that the vents in the pinnacle field — hundreds of them, scattered across the plateau — exhale in phase with the tunnels. Every eleven minutes the whole crater breathes out at once, and for about forty seconds the corridors fill with warm white steam, and visibility drops to an arm's length, and then it clears, and the field is dripping slightly, and the flies come back down, and the eagles are still going up.

---

Standing on a pillar top at four in the afternoon, then: the salt basin white and enormous to the west with its own weather sitting on it, the graupel falling and failing in the east, the pinnacle field crowded to the horizon and utterly still except for the periodic breath, sixty thousand flies rising off a terrace in silence, a bucket of yellow rock going over the edge and out of sight, one's boots slowly cooking, the wind cold in one's collar.

The Shoulder has been like this a long time. It is not becoming anything. It is at its own temperature, drinking its own water, eating its own flies, powered by its own falling weight, and it is very nearly noiseless about all of it.
