## The Held

There is no ground here. Not hidden ground, not ground under deep litter — none. If you dig, you find more of the same: laminations of pressed and woven trunk, layer on layer, each layer bearing the marks of the tools that laid it, going down further than anyone has ever gone and come back from. The oldest laminations, three or four kilometres down, are hard as slate and still show the adze-scallops. Somebody made those. Somebody knelt in the warm dark and worked that surface by hand, and then somebody grew a floor over them, and then someone grew a floor over that.

The whole continent is like this. It is a made thing all the way through.

---

**The material.** Call it the Stock. One lineage of grafted vegetable body, propagated for so long that species has stopped being a useful word for it. In the high country it takes the form of spars — trunks the diameter of a river, rising out of the underhaze and branching, and rebranching, the branches trained by generations of hands into arches, ribs, hoops, catwalks, the great vaulted galleries that people live in. Nothing in it grew by accident. Every fork was cut to fork. Every buttress was bent young, lashed, and held for eighty years until it set.

The Stock is blood-warm to the touch. Put your palm flat on a wall and you feel it: the slow respiration of the living laminations, a faint tidal give, warmer than your hand. The air in the galleries stays at the temperature of a body all year. You sweat here constantly and never dry, and neither does anything else.

Underfoot, the floors are woven — split withy laid in herringbone, tarred with the Stock's own resin, worn concave by traffic over such lengths of time that the wear itself is a kind of geology. Steps have hollows like cupped hands. Handrails have been polished into slender waists where the same three fingers have closed on them for two thousand years.

**Water.** The Held weeps. Every joint, every graft-scar, every place where two members were coaxed to fuse, leaks a slow clear sap-dew that beads and hangs and lets go. So the ambient sound of the world — and there is almost no other — is dripping. Not rain-drumming: individual drops, seconds apart, from a thousand different heights, into a thousand different depths. It goes on forever and never becomes rhythm.

Because the galleries are warm and immense and full of transpiration, they have their own weather. Air rises through the hollow spars all morning, cools against the high leaf-vaults, and by afternoon there is cloud in the room. Real cloud, with a base and a top, drifting between the ribs, and rain out of it, falling inside, so that people go about their work in a soft grey interior drizzle and duck under bracket-fungus awnings when it thickens. In the biggest chambers the cloud gets weather of its own within the weather — a small dark squall that circles a knot-column for days like an animal that has found something.

And where the floors were woven perfectly flat, on purpose, over hundreds of square kilometres, the water sits. These are the marsh-galleries: a hand's depth of still warm water going out under a low green-black vault, kilometres and kilometres of it, dotted with tended reed-stands and lamp-galls, so dim and so silent that the water and its reflection make one continuous space and you walk through the middle of a doubled world with your legs vanishing at the ankle.

**Light.** Nine parts of ten is dim. The leaf-vaults far overhead are ten or fifteen layers thick and what comes through them arrives green-gold and used-up, enough to see shapes by, not enough to read tool-marks by. For that you need lamp-galls: bred swellings on the Stock's minor branches, luminous, kept in wicker cages, which have to be scraped every few days to stay bright. A tended gallery is recognisable at a great distance as a faint necklace of pale spots. Below the fourth or fifth stratum there is no light at all except what you carry, and the true deep — the Dark Grain — has not been lit in living memory.

**Sound.** Almost nothing. The Stock is fibrous and wet and eats sound; the volumes are too large to echo. You hear your own breath as an event. A stone dropped — but there are no stones; a bead dropped — falls out of hearing without hitting anything.

Two exceptions.

The first is the bells. Bell-copper is the only metal, sweated out of ash-lick in clay hearths, and it is used for nothing but small bells, and the bells mean one thing: *this is tended*. A tender walking a boundary hangs one every few hundred paces. In the great quiet you can hear a single boundary bell from very far off, and the sound of it is the most information the world ever gives you at once. It says: someone comes here. It says: from here inward, things hold.

The second is the work. Every surface in the Held was shaped by hand and every surface remembers it. Stand still long enough in an old gallery — twenty minutes, an hour, longer than most people can bear — and you begin to hear, at the very floor of audibility, the tools. Adze-strokes. The creak of a lashing being hauled tight. Two people breathing hard in unison, lifting. No voices, ever; nobody has heard a voice come through. Just labour, faint, patient, ongoing, ten thousand years back and not finished. The tenders don't find this eerie. They find it companionable, and they find silence — real silence, the silence of a place where no one ever worked — unbearable, and they will not sleep in it.

---

**The rules.**

*Down follows the grain.* Gravity in the Held runs along the fibre of whatever you are standing on. Since the whole world is woven, the down is woven too: it braids, it splits at forks, it turns where a great member was trained to turn. You can walk up the inner curve of an arch without noticing, and look back, and find your own path is now a wall. Water does this too — streams that run *along* beams for a kilometre and then peel off into a curve nobody expects. In the knot-chambers, where a dozen major spars fuse, several downs cross in one volume, and the old builders exploited it: platforms set at what look like impossible angles, each with its own local floor, so that a knot-chamber is a hollow sphere with people living all over the inside of it and a dropped bead in the middle of the room that has been slowly orbiting the seam since before anyone's grandmother.

*Distance is made of attention.* A corridor that was built to be walked is short — you get to the other end sooner than the length of it suggests. A place no one meant you to go stretches. And the open volumes between structures, the un-built air, do not merely lack a path; they refuse one. Set out across a void toward a spar you can plainly see, and the gap thickens ahead of you, and after two days of walking the spar is exactly as far as it was. This is why the Held, which is continental, is nearly empty. Presences are held apart by unmade space the way islands are held apart by water. All travel is along tended lines. All tended lines were made by hand, one grafted arch at a time, and there are not many, and they take four generations to extend by a day's walk.

*Only made things hold.* This is the hard rule, the one everything else sits on. Nothing in the Held exists that was not intended by someone. A seed that lands where no one sowed it will sprout, and the sprout will thin — go glassy, go faint, cast less and less shadow — and in a season it has unhappened, leaving not a corpse but a slight absence of memory, a spot on the floor you find yourself unable to look directly at. The same is true of stones (there are no stones), of animals born outside the breeding-books, of children — and the tenders' naming rites are not sentiment, they are structural engineering.

The same is true of *place*. Where tending stops, the world does not fall into ruin. Ruins would be a kind of persistence, and the Held does not permit it. Instead the abandoned parts thin. First the light goes strange through them, then they lose detail, then they lose opacity, then they are void — and the void widens, and the distance across it thickens, and whatever was on the far side is now unreachable forever. Every few decades, somewhere in the continent, a stratum lets go. It makes the only loud sound this world has: a long soft tearing exhalation, the Sigh, audible for four hundred kilometres, after which there is a little more emptiness and a little less world, and every tender who heard it goes out the next morning and re-hangs their bells further out than they meant to.

---

**The living.** Fauna is everywhere and thin. Every animal in the Held is descended from something bred on purpose, and they all know it in their bodies; none of them will step off a tended line. Along the arches: silk-limbed grazers the size of deer, that crop the leaf-vaults and are silent because their ancestors were bred silent, communicating by pressing their long soft heads together. Splice-moths, palm-sized, pollinating on command — a tender rings a certain pitch and they come and do the work and are given sugar. In the marsh-galleries, frogs that flash instead of calling, whole hectares of them signalling in pale green, an entire conversation of light going on across still water in total silence. Lamp-gall keepers, small clever things with scraper-thumbs, who were bred to do the scraping and now mostly do it unsupervised and consider it their own affair.

There are predators, of course. The long walkers, six or eight metres, that come down the grain fast and quiet and hunt exactly along the paths people use, because they cannot leave them either. And, near the Thinning, the edge-eaters: pale flat things that graze the boundary where the world goes glassy, and eat, apparently, the unfinishing itself. Nobody has decided whether they help.

**The people.** Very few. A tender's territory is the size of a small country and she may see three other adults in a year. They carry: a thorn-adze, a gouge of seed-shell harder than glass, a grafting knife, a coil of cordage, a bag of bells, a scraper, a book of who is allowed to exist. No metal but the bells. No wheel — there is no flat run long enough to matter, and anyway the grain would take it. They work with their hands on a continent they did not make and cannot finish, dressing old grafts, coaxing this year's arch nine centimetres further into the void, and hanging bells behind themselves.

If you follow any tended line outward far enough you come to the last bell. Beyond it, the Stock goes untrained and gets faint, and the floor loses its herringbone, and the dripping stops, and there is that other silence, the one with no work in it. Everyone who reaches a last bell stands there a while. Then they turn back along their own path, and it is shorter going home than it was coming out — measurably, always — because somebody meant it to be walked.
