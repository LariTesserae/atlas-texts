## The Made Mountain

Begin with the rock, because everything here begins with the rock, and the rock is not rock in the way a granite ridge is rock. It is a graveyard of animals, and before that it was a city of animals, and it has never once in its existence been anything that was not made.

Two and a half thousand metres up, in the belt of permanent cloud, the massif stands as a series of grey towers and saddles, pitted and fluted, its surfaces the colour of old bone where they are bare and black-green where they are not. Break a piece off and hold it to the light that filters down: you will see the cross-sections. Coral heads the size of dinner plates, their septa still radiating. The coiled shells of gastropods. Bryozoan lacework, fine as burnt paper. Crinoid stems stacked like vertebrae. A shallow sea's worth of masonry, laid down by polyps a few millimetres at a time over some tens of millions of years, then shoved skyward by the slow shoulder of a continent, a millimetre a year, patiently, for longer than there have been mountains here to speak of.

So: the ground is architecture. There is no natural substrate underfoot on this plateau. There is only the abandoned work of an older fauna, weathered into shapes that a geologist would call karst and a mason would recognise as ruin.

---

The weather does not accumulate here; it passes through. Cloud comes up the eastern flank every afternoon and lies against the towers until midnight, and it is warm cloud — the equator is close, and this altitude only shaves the heat down to something even and blood-close, twenty-two degrees at noon and eighteen at four in the morning, month after month, year after year. Rain falls in bursts, hard and brief, and then it is gone. Gone *in*. The plateau has no rivers on its back. Every drop that lands is swallowed within minutes through a million joints and fissures and grikes, and what water you find above ground is water that has been caught rather than water that has been collected: a cupful in the axils of an epiphyte, a black pool in a solution basin the size of a hat, condensation beading on the underside of a leaf and running down its drip-tip into moss.

The moss is the wettest thing here, and it is everywhere the light reaches. It grows in mattresses a hand's depth thick, and squeezing it yields a surprising amount of tea-coloured water. Above the moss: ferns, filmy ones with fronds one cell thick, and tree ferns where a crack has held enough soil; then the small twisted trees that manage this pavement, oaks and laurels and podocarps stunted and thickened by wind and by the sheer difficulty of rooting in a lattice of dead coral. Their trunks are so upholstered in orchid, liverwort, lichen and clubmoss that you cannot tell where bark begins. The forest is not tall. It is *close* — a dense, sopping, over-furnished thicket at chest height, layered vertically until the layers press against each other, so that walking is less like walking through a wood than pushing through a hedge that goes on for eleven kilometres.

And it goes on until it drops.

---

The plateau is perforated. Great collapse pits — dolines — open in it at intervals of a kilometre or two, some of them a hundred metres across, some of them five hundred. From the rim you look down into a green well: the walls hung with roots and ferns and, lower, with a smooth curtain of moss where the mist eddies; the floor a scatter of house-sized limestone blocks with proper trees growing between them, trees that never feel wind, drawn up thin and pale, reaching for the ring of sky. Below the tree crowns the doline floor is dark at midday. And at one side of it, always, there is a mouth.

This is where the mountain's real interior begins, and where its inhabitants are.

---

Go down into the mouth in the middle of the afternoon and the first thing that changes is not the light but the pressure of sound. Outside, the forest is loud in the ordinary way — cicadas, the whistled monotone of a barbet, the two-note drilling frogs. Ten metres inside the entrance, that falls behind you and something else comes forward. A hiss. A vast, dry, continuous hiss, like rain on a tin roof heard through a wall, with a fine grain of clicking in it. Twenty metres further and it has become the whole air. Fifty metres and it is not a sound you hear but a medium you are inside of, and it does not stop, and it will not stop, and there is no point in speaking because your own voice reaches you as a private vibration in the skull and no further.

The hiss is wings. The clicking is navigation.

There are, in the largest chamber — a domed void some two hundred metres across, its ceiling lost in dark — three and a half million bats, and some hundreds of thousands of swiftlets, and the two of them have divided the day between them with an economy so old and so exact that it functions as a law. The swiftlets fly by day and hang by night. The bats hang by day and fly by night. They pass each other at dusk and again at dawn, in opposite directions, through the same doorway, and the doorway roars.

The bats cover the ceiling. Not sit on it: *cover* it, at a density of two or three thousand to the square metre, a living pelt that ripples continuously as individuals shuffle, quarrel, groom, and are displaced. Where the ceiling is too smooth for their claws, they have made it rough — generations of grip have picked the surface into a fine pocked texture that the next generation grips more easily. Where the ceiling is high and domed, the pelt hangs in tapering columns and gathers into folds. When something startles a district of them, the disturbance travels across the roof as a visible wave and the sound doubles, and dust and mites and small dry things rain down out of the dark.

The swiftlets take the walls. Each nest is a shallow bracket, a half-cup of the bird's own hardened saliva glued to the stone, translucent and faintly striated, the size of a large thumbprint. They are laid side by side, and above, and below, until whole vertical acres are shingled in them — millions of nests, generation on generation, the old ones darkened and thickened by re-use, the mass of them raising the wall's surface by an appreciable amount. There are places where the original limestone has not been touched by air in eight hundred years. The birds cannot echolocate as finely as the bats. They navigate the black by sonar clicks, sharp and audible, one per bird per moment, hundreds of thousands of birds, which is what gives the hiss its glitter.

---

Now look down. This is the part that decides the character of the whole world.

Everything the ceiling and the walls produce falls. Guano, in a continuous soft fall like grey snow, for millennia. The chamber's floor is therefore not a floor of stone but a landscape of excrement, and it has been shaped exactly as a landscape is shaped — cones beneath the densest roosts, rising twenty and thirty metres in slopes so steep they stand at the limit of what will stand; saddles between the cones; fans where drip-water has cut a channel and rebuilt it lower down; terraces; sumps of black liquid at the base. It is warm to the touch, several degrees above the air, working slowly on itself. It smells of ammonia and of something sweetish and fungal underneath, and the smell has weight; it sits behind the eyes.

And it moves. The whole surface of every cone is in motion, all the time, everywhere, in low light, and what moves is cockroaches. Millions. Layered two and three deep in the richest places, so that when you set a foot down you feel the give of bodies before you feel the substrate. With them: guano crickets with antennae four times their body length, tapping; earwigs; carrion beetles; hordes of blind pale mites; the larvae of moths that eat nothing but feather and hair. Predators walk on the walls above them — huntsman spiders the span of a spread hand, whip-scorpions feeling ahead of themselves with a single elongated leg, and centipedes twenty centimetres long, red-orange in the torch of a stray sunbeam, moving with an obscene fluidity across the shingle of nests, taking eggs, taking chicks, taking each other.

Where a shaft of light does fall — through a collapse in the ceiling, an aven open to the sky — the effect is theatrical and abrupt: a solid column of white haze full of drifting motes and crossing birds, striking a circle of the guano floor, and inside that circle, ferns. Ferns and seedlings and a violent, doomed green mat, fed absurdly well, growing at a rate the plateau forest above could never manage, and dying whenever the light shifts with the season.

Snakes wait at the entrance passages. Slender, long-bodied racers, four or five of them at a time, anchored by the tail in the nest-crust or in a crack, hanging out into the airstream with their forebodies free, holding that pose for hours, sampling the dark with their tongues. At dusk they take bats out of the flow. It is not a hunt so much as a fishery.

Deeper, past where the roosts end, the passages carry the water. Streams appear from breakdown, run for a hundred metres over clean scoured rock, and vanish. In their pools there are blind catfish, white and whiskered; freshwater crabs equally colourless; shrimp that graze the bacterial film. They live on what washes down from the roosts — the whole subterranean river system is, functionally, downstream of three and a half million bats. Beyond that, at the far end of the system, the water rejoins the light: a resurgence low on the massif's flank, where a cold black stream comes out from under a wall of rock, and where the forest downstream is noticeably richer than the forest above, for reasons the trees do not need to understand.

---

The rules of this place are few and easy to learn.

Everything travels downward. Height is the position of production, depth the position of consumption, and no organism here occupies both. The bats and the birds go out to the world for food and bring the world back in; everything below them lives off the difference.

The dark is not empty and not still. Anything that stops moving here is colonised within hours.

Sound is the local form of light. Two entire flying populations perceive this landscape as a shape made of returning echoes, and the shape is stable enough that individual birds return, in the black, to a particular nest among four million nests.

And nothing happens quickly. The reef rose a millimetre a year. The chamber was dissolved out of it over half a million. The guano cones gain a hand's breadth in a human lifetime. The nests thicken by a wafer per season. The colony's population has fluctuated by perhaps a fifth in three thousand years, and its two shifts have changed over at dusk and dawn, without interruption, without a single missed evening, for longer than that.

At sunset, then, the only fast event: the column. It begins as a thickening near the mouth, resolves into a spiral, and then pours out into the doline and up past the rim in a rope of bodies thirty metres wide that keeps going for ninety minutes. The sound of it, from the rim, is not a hiss any more but a low continuous surf, and beneath it the shrieks of the raptors that have come for the occasion — bat hawks, falcons, a serpent eagle — working the edge of the rope, taking one and one and one, which over ninety minutes is nothing at all.

Then the cloud closes over the plateau, and the forest resumes its wet dripping, and inside the mountain the swiftlets settle onto their brackets of spit in the dark, in a hall of dead coral, above thirty metres of warm moving ground.
