## The Warm Summit

You climb out of the cold into heat. That is the first thing to understand about this place, and nothing else in it makes sense until you accept it: the air thins as you go up, the sun burns thinner and sharper, the horizon drops away into blue haze — and the temperature rises. At three thousand meters you are in cloud forest, sweating. At four thousand you are in something closer to a steam room. By the time the ridge flattens out into the wide bowl of the summit country, the ground under your palms is as warm as a sleeping animal, and the air holds so much water that breathing feels like drinking.

The heat comes from below. This is a young mountain grown on an old wound, and the wound has never closed. Somewhere under the caldera floor a body of magma sits shallow enough to boil groundwater, and the water has been finding its way up for tens of thousands of years, long enough to have organized itself, to have carved its channels and armored them, to have settled into habit. The result is a tropical enclave at an altitude where there should be ice: a hot wet island floating four kilometers above a dry plain, cut off on all sides by cold, by cliffs, by the impossibility of anything walking up here and surviving the crossing.

---

### The Floor

The caldera is roughly twelve kilometers across, not circular — kidney-shaped, pinched in the middle by a low ridge of older lava that the forest has swallowed. The rim is broken in three places, and through those breaks the vapor pours out and down in slow white tongues, so that from far below the whole summit appears to be smoking. It is. It has been smoking, without interruption, for longer than anything alive here has a name for.

The floor is not flat. It is *terraced*, and the terraces are the strangest thing about the place, the one feature that makes a first-time observer stop and start looking for whoever made them. They descend the northern arc of the bowl in something like two hundred steps, each one a shallow basin rimmed by a lip of pale sinter — silica precipitated out of the hot water, laid down molecule by molecule as the water cools and loses its ability to carry dissolved rock. The lips are two hands high and curved with the unhurried, fluid, unmistakably *correct* geometry of things made by physics rather than intention. Some are the size of a bathtub. Some are the size of a small lake. Water moves through all of them, from the source vents at the top to the swamp at the bottom, at a rate you can only see if you watch a floating leaf for a while.

The color runs a gradient down the staircase. At the top, where the water leaves the ground at eighty-six degrees — it boils at eighty-six here; the thin air lets it go early, which is why the steam is so abundant and the pools so violent for their temperature — the basins are dead white and blinding, scoured, lined with nothing but glass. A hundred meters down, at seventy degrees, the first films appear: sheets of thermophilic bacteria in a hard chemical orange. Below that, olive. Below that, a rust-brown so thick and rubbery it can be peeled off in sheets, and beneath the sheets, small blind shrimp. By the time the water reaches forty degrees it is choked with life, and by the time it reaches the caldera floor it is a green-black marsh, so warm that the mud steams at night, so rich that the reeds growing out of it stand five meters tall and hollow.

Everywhere else on the floor: forest.

### The Engine

If you sit still for an hour you begin to hear the mountain working, and the word that arrives, unbidden and impossible to dislodge, is *machinery*.

It is a distillation apparatus of enormous size and complete simplicity. Water enters at the rim — cloud, drawn off the trade winds, snagged on the cold outer slopes and combed out of the air by the forest's leaves at a rate of several centimeters a day. It sinks. It travels down through fractured basalt, inward and under, for a distance nobody can measure. It reaches the hot rock and it flashes and rises. It comes back into the world at the vent field, screaming softly, saturated with silica and sulfur and boron, and it lays those minerals down in the terraces as it cools, and gives its heat to the air, and the air lifts, and the lifting air runs up against the cold at the rim and condenses, and rains, and sinks again. A closed loop with a fire underneath it. It has been running at this scale, on these settings, since before the last glaciation on the plain below.

And it *produces*. That is what makes the industrial impression so hard to shake. There is tonnage here. The terraces grow by a couple of centimeters a year and stretch for three kilometers. The sulfur beds on the eastern vents are meters thick, yellow going to orange going to a red so dark it looks charred, and they are actively accumulating; you can watch the crystals grow needles overnight in a warm hollow. There are borate flats bleaching in the one dry corner of the bowl. There is a natural chimney on the ridge — a column of sinter nine meters high, hollow, roaring faintly, built entirely by its own exhaust, with a lip at the top rounded outward exactly the way a chimney lip is rounded. It is the only thing in the caldera that looks built. It was not built. Nothing here was built. The resemblance is a coincidence of function, which is somehow more unsettling than a ruin would be.

And it *cycles*. The big vent at the head of the staircase runs on a period of about four and a half hours: a long recharge, a rising mutter, then eleven minutes of white column, then silence, then the whole terrace system flushing hot for a quarter of an hour afterward. Four other geysers keep their own schedules, and twice a day the whole vent field breathes — inhaling faintly as the barometric pressure rises, exhaling as it falls, so that the sound of the mountain has a slow tidal component you feel in your ears before you hear it.

### Quiet

For all of that, the place is quiet. This surprises everyone.

Part of it is the air: at this pressure sound doesn't carry, it goes flat and close and dies within a few hundred meters, so the caldera has no long acoustic reach. Part of it is the nature of the work being done. Pressure is quiet. Precipitation is quiet. Steam at low pressure through a wide throat is a hiss, and the forest absorbs hiss. The geyser is genuinely loud for eleven minutes out of every two hundred and seventy, and the rest of the time you can hear individual water drops leaving individual leaves.

There is no dawn chorus. The birds here sing at the pressure minimum in the early afternoon, all together, for perhaps twenty minutes, and then stop.

### The Green

The forest is not tall — nothing here is tall, the air won't support the plumbing and the wind at the rim shears anything ambitious — but it is *deep*. Six or seven distinguishable layers in twelve vertical meters, and they touch. Everything touches. Trunks are furred to the crown with moss half a hand thick and permanently saturated; squeeze any handful and get water. Out of the moss grow ferns, and out of the ferns grow bromeliad analogues holding their own private ponds, and each pond holds larvae and a frog, and out of those grow orchids the size of a fingernail, thousands of them, and the whole assembly is netted together with vines that never bothered to develop wood.

The leaves are the tell. Most of them hang face-down, dark, ridged, with drip-tips that point at the ground — not to shed rain but to *harvest steam*, which arrives from beneath and condenses on the cool undersides. Rain here is almost incidental. The forest drinks upward. Walk through it in the morning and the drip is continuous and warm, and it is not rain, it is the mountain's breath returning.

Roots run shallow and wide, because at forty centimeters the soil is too hot for them. In the vent margins the trees give up entirely and hand the ground over to specialists: a low sprawling shrub with waxy leaves and a taproot that stops dead at the thermal boundary, and a grass that grows in the sixty-degree mud and nowhere else on the planet.

### The Living

Fauna is generous without being crowded. The insects, of course — the mosquito equivalent is a small silent thing that hunts by heat and is therefore nearly useless in a landscape where everything is warm, which is the single best thing about the place.

Frogs in absurd numbers, each bromeliad pool a nursery, the marsh at night a low continuous creaking that reads as silence after ten minutes. A cat-sized grazer, dense-boned, with a huge chest for the thin air, that browses terrace-edge algae mats and can be watched from six meters because nothing up here has ever taught it to fear a biped. Bats by the tens of thousands in the steam caves under the eastern lava ridge, where they roost in air so humid their wings never dry. Snakes, patient, in the warm rock.

And above it all, always, the soarers. The mountain generates a permanent thermal — a column of hot air standing over the caldera like a pillar — and a population of long-winged scavengers has taken up residence in it. They do not migrate. They barely flap. Eleven or twelve of them hang in the pillar at any hour of daylight, turning slowly, four hundred meters up, and they are the reason the sky over this place always looks occupied.

---

### Rules

Three things hold true here, and the whole landscape follows from them.

**Down is the sun.** Energy comes from below. This inverts everything: the coldest part of the environment is the sky, the seasons are weak, night is barely cooler than day, and life orients toward the ground the way life elsewhere orients toward light.

**Nothing leaves.** The rim is a wall of cold. Species that arrived here — as spores, as seeds in a bird's gut, as a single gravid insect in a storm — arrived once, long ago, and then were sealed in. Everything is slightly the wrong shape as a result. Everything is a little bit endemic.

**The books balance.** Erosion strips the caldera at some rate; deposition builds it back at very nearly the same rate. The terraces grow and slump and regrow. The chimney falls every few centuries and starts again. This is not stillness. It is a landscape that arrived at its own answer a long time ago and has been repeating it since, with the patience of a process rather than the fixity of a monument.

Stand on the rim at midafternoon, when the birds are all singing at once and the geyser is building and the vapor is going out through the western notch in a slow white river, and you will have the strong and entirely false impression that somewhere below you a factory is running its shift.

There is no factory. There is only water, going around.
