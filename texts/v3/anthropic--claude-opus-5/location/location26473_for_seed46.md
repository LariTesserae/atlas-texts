# The Bud

Nine-tenths of the way up a mountain that has not yet been given a shape, there is a pocket about the size of a large room, and it is hot in there, and green, and almost silent, and nothing in it has ever been rained on.

You do not enter it. There is a fissure in the mountainside that will admit you, but it is not there when you are looking for it, only when you have stopped, and afterward you cannot say through which of the two nearly-identical clefts you passed. This is the first of the local rules and the mildest.

## The rock

The floor is fresh igneous — not lava, exactly, but something with the same manners, cooled last week or last epoch, the distinction being one the place does not maintain. Feldspar laths as long as doors lie half-emerged from a darker matrix, like planks that were laid into wet cement and then forgotten. Their faces are perfect. Not polished: *unstruck*. No dust has ever settled on them, because there is no dust; dust is a product, and here the machinery of ruin has not been switched on. Nothing has crumbled. There is no scree at the base of the walls, no fan of grit, no sand in the joints, not one loose grain anywhere in the whole pocket. Run a finger along an edge of quartz and it will part your skin as cleanly as a scalpel, because that edge is exactly as sharp as it was on the day it was decided, and no day since has argued with it.

The air, in consequence, is unnervingly clear. Distances are hard to judge without particulates to soften them. The far wall looks close enough to touch and takes eleven steps.

It is hot the way a kiln shed is hot in August — a dry, mineral, ringing heat that dries the inside of the nose in a minute and the back of the throat in five. The heat comes up through the floor. Under the floor, something is being made.

## What lives here

There are perhaps forty living things in the room, and they are spaced widely, three or four metres apart, with bare stone between. Nothing crowds. Nothing competes. There is more than enough of everything except time.

The largest are the **smelter trees**, of which there are three. A trunk of slag and bright unweathered metal, waist-thick, rising four metres and branching into a crown of hammered foil-leaves, each leaf a lobed sheet of something between tin and mica, angled to catch the sky and route the light down inward. They are not drinking the light. They are burning stone with it. The root is a small furnace bedded in carbonate, and the whole slow business of the tree is the reduction of rock into structure: carbon out of limestone, metal out of oxide, and a thin exhalation of oxygen and vaporized zinc that you can taste at the back of the tongue like a coin.

Their sap is alloy. By day it runs, and the trees go soft and slightly droop, the foil-leaves clinking as they settle. At dusk the sap freezes in the veins and the whole tree stiffens into rigidity, and the sound of that — a long tightening tick, tick, ticka-tick propagating up the trunk — is the loudest thing that happens here.

Lower down there are the **kilns**: bulbous, hollow, unglazed, the colour of biscuit-fired clay, waist-high, sealed. Through the hairline seams you can see an orange interior glow. Each is firing its own seed. Each firing takes something like eleven years and must not be hurried; when it completes, the kiln cracks along the seam with a report like a dropped plate, and a single vitrified seed rolls out and lies on the stone, and lies there, and lies there, waiting for weather that has not been invented.

There are **turbine bushes**, sprawling low, whose leaves are vanes on grown bearings, turning at maybe two revolutions a minute in the convection of their own hot base. They generate nothing. They simply turn, and the turning cools them enough to continue, and the hum they make is so faint that you notice it only when you have left the room and it stops.

And there are the **cable-vines**, single strands of drawn copper, bright as a new penny, running across the floor for metres, extruded from a nodule at one end at the rate of about a millimetre a day. Near the nodule the wire is still hot from the die. Touch it there and you are burned. Touch it four metres along and you are touching last decade.

Everything here looks manufactured and nothing here is made. There is no shed, no wall, no path, no cairn, no mark. A hexagonal nut of pyrite lies near the eastern overhang, threaded, machined-looking, six millimetres across, and it is a crystal, and it grew.

There are no animals. Animals would need a past — a somewhere-they-came-from, a history of appetite — and this place has not accumulated one. Nothing here has ever eaten anything that was alive. Nothing has ever been in a hurry.

## The sound under the floor

Every so often — the interval is long, an hour or a day, and it does not repeat evenly — there is a stroke from below. A press. A deep, felted *thud* that you feel in the soles before you hear it, followed by a settling, a subterranean sigh of hot gas venting through a seam somewhere behind the north wall.

That is the mountain being extruded. The rock you stand on is the current output of an industry with no operators, no chimneys, no rails, no visible plant: the whole massif is a factory floor that has grown its own product and has not yet finished the run. Between strokes, the room is quiet enough that your own pulse becomes furniture.

## Reality, locally

The three smelter trees are identical. Not similar — identical, down to a healed split in the bark at 1.4 metres and a torn leaf-edge in the crown. They are not copies of each other. They are one tree, held at three points, and when you circle any of them the other two rotate in sympathy, presenting always the same face at the same angle. If you stand where you can see all three at once and try to hold them all in attention simultaneously, you will get a headache like a struck bell.

The corners of the room are not finished. Look at the southwest corner for more than a few seconds and it flattens: the detail thins out into a generic grey, a placeholder rock-texture, an averaged stone that the eye slides off because there is nothing there for it to catch. Walk toward it and the detail arrives a moment before you do — crystal faces blooming into specificity two seconds ahead of your foot, warm with having just been decided. You cannot get to the unfinished part. It finishes on approach. But you can see it from across the room, out of the side of vision, waiting.

Cause runs a little loose. A leaf falls and you hear the small click of its landing first, and then watch it come down. Your footprints do not stay, not because they are erased but because the floor has no mechanism for having-been-stepped-on.

Names do not adhere. You may name the kilns something, and mean it, and by the time you have crossed to the next one the name has gone the way the water goes.

The place has no past tense. Everything in it is present or about-to-be. This is not a metaphor; it is a grammatical fact you will notice when you try to describe it to yourself while standing in it, and find that the sentences keep sliding forward.

## The drop

Under the eastern overhang, on the coolest square metre in the room, there is a bead of water the size of a pea.

It is the only water. It hangs on the underside of the rock, held by surface tension, slightly flattened, absolutely still, with a small bright point of sky in it. It has been about to fall for a very long time. Everything else here — the slow presses, the eleven-year firings, the millimetre-a-day copper, the unhurried alloy tides of the trees — is arranged, or has arranged itself, around the delay.

When it falls it will strike a particular ledge of feldspar and take with it, eventually, one grain. That grain will be the first thing this place has ever lost. After that there will be grit, and then dust, and then soil, and then softened edges, and weather, and a past tense, and after a while something with a mouth will come up the mountain and find the fissure exactly where it is, every time, because by then the place will have finished happening and will be an ordinary green hollow high on an ordinary peak, findable, describable, worn.

For now the bead holds. The kilns glow along their seams. The vanes turn twice a minute in the hot upwelling. And below, patiently, the press comes down again and makes another few centimetres of world.
