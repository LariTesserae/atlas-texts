**The Unlit Basin, called by its tenders the Tallow Flats**

There is no light here, and there never has been. This is not night. Night implies a day somewhere behind it, a sun that went down and is coming back. In the basin, luminosity simply doesn't complete. The gas burners at Station Six roar for months on end and you can hold your palm a hand's width from the mouth of a flame and feel it pressing back at you like a hot animal, and see nothing — the reaction goes through, the heat arrives, and the photons never happen. It's said that a lamp brought down from the rim will work for a few minutes before it grows shy. Nobody has brought one down in living memory. Everyone here has the kind of eyes that were never used, wide and clear and beautifully useless, and the young ones are still born with them.

So: describe it by the other senses, which is how it is actually known.

**The ground.**

The basin floor is new. Two, three generations ago it was under water — a warm brackish sheet a few meters deep, the whole width of the valley — and then the ground beneath lifted, or the water withdrew, or (the tenders' version) the pumps began and the pumps made the land by insisting on it. What emerged is raw. Mud that stiffened into a soft grey rock, and that rock has never been rounded off. Everything is edges. The ridges that divide the channels are knife-work: fluted, needled, faced with little vertical blades where the warm rain has grooved straight down without wind to slant it. You do not walk barefoot off the boardwalks. You do not walk off the boardwalks at all, mostly, and not only because of the edges.

The whole basin tilts a hand's breadth per hundred paces toward the middle, where the water gathers into the Slow Water, a shallow black lake without a shore worth the name — just mud that becomes water at some undeclarable point, and mats of something warm and rubbery growing across the transition, thick as a mattress, breathing sulfur.

Water is everywhere and always. It seeps out of the ridge faces in beads and runs down the flutes; it lies in every low place; it hangs in the air so heavily that when the temperature drops half a degree the entire atmosphere condenses at once and it rains for four days, straight down, drumming warm on the boardwalk planks, on the crabs, on the casings of the pumps. This happens on a cycle of roughly forty days, and the tenders know where they are in the cycle by the ache in their joints. Nothing here happens quickly. The heat rises and falls over a season you'd measure in years. The gradients are all weak and all patient.

**The machines.**

Something under the basin is hot. Not volcanic — no eruptions, no drama — just a broad, dull, generous heat about eighty meters down, and the whole industrial apparatus of the place exists to drink it. Nine pump stations stand on pilings in a shallow arc across the flats, numbered from the Mother Bore outward, each a fat riveted drum with a stack, each running a bank of reciprocating pumps off its own steam. They lift the groundwater out of the low ground and throw it into the drainage channels, which carry it to the Slow Water, which loses it back into the ground, which the pumps lift again. It is a closed and pointless-looking circuit that has been running, by tally, for ninety-one years.

They are almost silent. Not because they are quiet machines — put your hand on a delivery pipe and the shudder comes up your arm and into your teeth as a great slow *tunk … tunk … tunk*, a heartbeat you could set a life to — but because the dark here has a skin to it, and sound does not travel through the skin. Everything is audible for about twenty paces and then stops. Station Six may be roaring itself hoarse and from Station Seven you hear nothing at all: only the drip off the rail, the hush of wings, your own breath coming back off the fog. The basin is loud in ten thousand small spheres that do not touch each other.

Between the stations run the walkways: rope-and-plank causeways on piles, with a hand-line at waist height on the right-hand side going out and on the left coming back, and tally posts every hundred planks. The planks are cut from tallow-fungus, which is the nearest thing here to a tree — pale swollen soft-barked columns that grow in sparse groves wherever an exhaust vent warms the mud, three or four meters tall, no leaves, faintly sweet, and cut like cold butter.

**The rules.**

Here is what a visitor would need to be told, and would not believe until the second day.

Unwitnessed things do not settle. This is why the rock is so sharp: an edge that no one has ever seen or touched has never been agreed upon, and an unagreed edge is infinitely fine. It will part your skin without any sensation of cutting. Conversely, the handrails on the causeways — ninety-one years of palms — are worn to a satin curve, dished at the tally posts where hands rest, the only old surfaces in a landscape otherwise brand new. Erosion here is not weather. Erosion is attention. The basin is being slowly, locally worn into definiteness along the exact lines where people put their hands, and everywhere else it stays as fresh and unfinished as the hour it rose.

Distance is not conserved off the marked ways. On the boardwalk between Four and Five you count two hundred and twelve planks going out and two hundred and twelve coming back, always, because the planks are counted and being counted holds them. Step off into the mud and the flats will let you walk for an hour and put you down twenty paces from where you started, or drop the far ridge into your hands in ten strides. The tenders call the open ground *the loose*, and crossing it is done with a line paid out from a post, or not done.

In the loose, things you reach for arrive early, and sometimes there are several of them. This is normal. You take the one that has the right temperature.

And there are patches — the tenders call them *soft spots*, and mark them with hoops of wire when they find them — where nothing has resolved at all. You know one by the smell, which is a smell like the inside of your own nose, and by the fact that when you put your hand into it your hand comes back without any report of what it touched. Soft spots drift, slowly, a few meters a season. They are not dangerous in a hurry. Nothing here is dangerous in a hurry.

**The living.**

Nobody tends the pumps anymore, in the sense of a workforce. There are perhaps forty people in the whole basin, in twos and threes, in single sheds bolted to single stations, walking their circuits. What actually maintains the machinery is the ecology, which has come to terms.

The bearings are greased by *stillmoths* — palm-sized, eyeless, feathered with heat-sensing plumes instead of antennae — which nest in warm housings and secrete a waxy oil to line the nest. Their wings are the loudest sustained sound in the basin: a continuous dry hush around every station, like fine sand poured slowly, the sound of hundreds of thousands of small dry surfaces sliding. Their larvae eat scale off the boiler tubes. In return the stations run, and the stations are the only reliably warm and *definite* places in a world of loose edges, and so everything comes to them.

The pilings below the waterline are plated four and five deep in flat silent crabs, layer over layer, each generation's shells becoming the substrate of the next, so that the pumps stand on legs of accreted crab. Blind eels the length of an arm braid through the intake screens; you feel them go by as a pressure change against your shin. The mats in the Slow Water are grazed by something soft and enormous and utterly noiseless that the tenders have never had a name for, only a smell, and a policy of not being where it is.

And out on the flats, the waders. Four meters at the shoulder, legs like calipers, bodies no bigger than a barrel, moving at about the pace of a slow conversation. They make no sound at all — not even a splash, which should be impossible and is. What they do is browse the loose. They put their long heads down into the soft spots and the unresolved ground, and they feed on ambiguity, and where a wader herd has passed, the mud is *decided*: firm, gritty, measurable, ordinary. Every survey ever made in the basin was made by following waders. The tenders regard them the way farmers regard rain. There are more of them each decade. In four or five hundred years, at the current rate, there may be no loose left, and the basin will be a plain warm marsh, entirely real, worn round, quiet, and nobody there will know what a strange place it was.

**The rim.**

At the edges of everything the ground rises — a hundred meters, no more, that's the whole relief of this world — and up there the rain falls differently, and the tenders say that if you climb far enough your eyes begin to itch, and that some people who went up never came back down, and that this is not the same as dying. The stations don't reach that far. The hand-lines end.

Down in the middle, the Mother Bore lets go once every few days: a long sub-audible groan of released pressure that you feel in the chest before the ear finds it, rolling out through the skin of the dark and dying twenty paces away in every direction, unheard by anyone but the one person standing near it, who marks the tally, and puts a palm on the warm plate, and wears it a little smoother, and walks on.
