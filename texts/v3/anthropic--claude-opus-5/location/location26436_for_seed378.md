# The Sill

## The approach

You come up onto it from the west, out of the dry lands, by a road that climbs in long lazy switchbacks — and the first thing you meet is not the plateau but its breath: a warm wet wall of air, standing in the cold thin air of altitude like something built. You walk through it. Your clothes go heavy. The dust on your boots turns to slip.

Then the light. The Sill is white, and it is two and a half thousand meters up, and the sun at that height has no manners. Everything glares. The terraces throw the light back up into the vapor, the vapor scatters it sideways, and the result is a world lit from all directions at once, no shadows worth the name, a shadowless brilliance that makes distance hard to judge and edges hard to find. Everyone here squints. Everyone here has the same fan of pale creases at the corners of the eyes, working or idle, native or newcomer, so that they all look faintly amused.

## The ground

The plateau is a hot spring the size of a valley, and it has been one for a very long time.

Water comes up under pressure from somewhere far below — brine, silica-loaded, mineral-fat, arriving at just under boiling — and it comes up not in one place but in a scatter of hundreds of vents across forty kilometers of high ground. Each vent builds its own thing. The oldest have made terraces: shelved bowls of sinter and travertine, rim upon rim, each rim the record of a water level that held long enough to matter. The bowls are shallow, ankle- to thigh-deep, and they are the colors that dissolved minerals give — bone-white, cream, wet ochre, a green that isn't chlorophyll, a blue in the deep vents so clean and so saturated that looking into it feels like a mild injury.

The terraces have reached a settled shape. They are not raw and they are not ruined. Deposition adds a millimeter a year to the rims; slumping and dissolution take about a millimeter a year off the outer faces; the vents wander a little over decades and the abandoned works go grey and chalky and eventually crumble into gravel that the next generation of water cements back into stone. It is a landscape in its own state, keeping its own accounts. Nothing here is being born and nothing here is being destroyed. It is simply going on, at the pace it has settled into.

Between the vent-systems: flat pans of hardpan, salt-crusted, dry-looking but drumming hollow under the heel, with water twenty centimeters down. Walk on the pans and your footprints fill. Sleep on the pans and you wake damp to the bone.

## No plants

None. Not a stalk, not a moss, not a lichen on a north face. The brine is too alkaline, the ground too hot, the season too long. There is no green here at all and no smell of green — which you notice mostly as an absence in the nose, a flatness, the missing baseline note under everything.

What there is instead is *mat*.

The mats are the base of the world: laminated films of thermophile bacteria and simpler things, growing in bands according to how much heat they can bear. Right at the vent lip, where the water arrives at ninety-four degrees, a hard grey-pink varnish, tough as enamel. Then a fringe of orange as thick as felt. Then long streamers of the same organism in flowing water, hair-fine, lying downcurrent, wagging. Then, in the cooler outer bowls, mats you can put your fist through — soft, layered like pastry, gas-bubbled, releasing a sulfur-and-warm-bread smell when broken.

They eat the water's chemistry. Nothing eats sunlight here. The light is only weather.

## The fauna

And on the mats: multitudes.

This is the thing nobody expects. A place with no plants and the animals are *everywhere*.

Grazers first. Pale flat-shelled snails, thousands to a bowl, that move on the mat like slow scouring pads and leave clean-swept trails. Isopods the size of a thumb joint, translucent, guts visible as a dark comma. Something like a shrimp that lives its whole life in the seventy-degree band and dies of cold if it strays. In the deeper blue vents, blind fish — genuinely blind, no eye at all, just a smooth pale slope of face — hanging nose-into-current, gulping whatever the water brings up.

Then the waders. Long-legged, long-billed, feathered white, standing in the shallows in ones and twos across the whole visible plateau, in numbers that add up over kilometers to thousands and thousands of birds, and none of them making any sound whatsoever. They stab, swallow, resettle a foot. Their legs are scaled with mineral crust up to the knee joint, which they crack off periodically against the terrace rims — a small dry *tick* that is one of the loudest things you'll hear all day.

And then the large slow ones, the animals the locals call **ovens**: broad low six-limbed browsers, half a ton, hairless, hide like wet grey leather, that lie in the warm outer bowls and rasp mat off the sinter with a tongue like a farrier's file. They lie in the heat because they cannot make their own; the water is their blood-warmer. In the dawn cold they are torpid, and you can walk up and put a palm on one and feel nothing move for a long minute, and then a slow deep shifting somewhere inside, like machinery in another building.

Nothing here vocalizes. Not one species. The vapor layer swallows airborne sound within thirty meters — it is soupy, thick with droplets, acoustically a blanket — and no lineage that evolved on the Sill ever found calling worth the cost. They signal other ways: the waders by posture and the flash of a pale wing-lining; the ovens by drumming the terrace floor with a forelimb, a pulse you feel in your own soles before you notice it; the snails by chemistry, laying trails of intention.

The consequence, which takes a day or two to sink in: **the Sill is silent, and it is crowded.** Ten thousand animals in your field of view and the loudest thing is your own breathing, and the small suck of your boot leaving the mud, and the water — everywhere the water, but even that is quiet, because it does not fall. It seeps, it slides over rims a centimeter deep, it slips. Nothing splashes on the Sill. The whole hydrology is a whisper.

## The rule of the shell

Here is the thing about this place that outsiders take longest to accept.

The water deposits on everything, indiscriminately, at a rate you can measure over a season. Everything that stays still gets stone on it. The ovens are shelled with it in plates along their backs, which they shed and regrow; the waders crack it off their legs; the snails' shells are half their own and half accreted. And the animals that die in the bowls do not rot — there is nothing here to rot them, the mats are chemists not undertakers — they *set*. They go stone. They become part of the rim they died on.

So the terraces are made of their own dead. Cut a fresh face into a hundred-year rim and it is legible: bands of snail shell, a wader's femur in cross-section, a whole isopod perfect in the matrix like a bubble in glass, and every few decades a great pale curve where an oven lay down and did not get up and became load-bearing.

The locals do not find this morbid. It is the only geology they have. They build with it.

## The works

Fourteen settlements, none of them close to another. From any high rim you can see three or four of them, each announced by its plume — a straight white column in the still hours, torn into a flag when the afternoon wind comes over the western scarp. Between them, twenty kilometers of empty glaring terrace, and nothing on the road but the road.

The town I know best is **Anneal**, seven hundred people, built up the flank of a dead vent-mound in five stepped tiers. It is stone-and-iron and it is *encrusted* — every wall, every stair-nose, every window ledge softened and thickened and rounded by the same deposit that makes the terraces, so that the older buildings look poured rather than laid, look melted, look like they grew. Fresh mortar is rare. Every generation of construction wears the previous one as a coat. Doorways get narrower by a finger's width a decade. Every eight or nine years a crew goes around with adzes and reduces the town, chipping it back to plan, and the chips go into the aggregate pile, and the aggregate goes into the new tier.

The industry is heat and brine. The vents are cased and piped — heavy lagged iron running down the terraces on stone piers, hot enough to raise a blister through a glove — and the pressure runs flash-steam turbines in low sheds at the base of the mound. That gives the plateau electricity: arc lamps that nobody needs by day, and pumps, and the smelter, and the big slow condensers on the roofs. Off the brine they take borate, lithium salts, sulfur, and a silica flour fine enough to float. Off the vapor they take fresh water, which is the only fresh water there is, condensed out of the plateau's own breath on kilometers of ribbed copper leaf.

Industrial, then: engines, transmission, scaled extraction, shift work, a rail spur with a single locomotive that runs on ground-heat steam and coasts down and winches up.

And it is all **quiet**. This is the part that is genuinely strange, and it isn't design, it's just physics behaving locally in a way that feels like a decision. The turbines are underground and the ground is spongy sinter; the vapor eats the exhaust noise; the machinery is old, established, running in its worn grooves, well-greased by generations. Stand in the middle of Anneal at shift change with four hundred people moving on the stairs and the smelter drawing and the pumps working, and it is quieter than a country lane at home. You hear the *tick* of expanding pipe. You hear the copper condensers dripping into their gutters, which is the town's real voice, an all-day arrhythmic patter from every roof. You hear boots. You do not hear voices much, because on the Sill people learned early that speech doesn't carry, and they gave up on it for anything longer than a word. They tap. Every pipe on the plateau is a telegraph — knuckle, spanner, heel-of-hand — and a message can go from Anneal to the westmost works in four minutes along the lagging, relayed by whoever is nearest a junction.

## The day

Dawn: the plateau is at its most opaque, the whole basin filled to the rims with white, only the vent-mounds and the towns standing out of it like islands. Sound carries even less. The ovens are stone-still. This is when the deposition is heaviest — you can leave a cup out and find it frosted in the morning.

Mid-morning: the sun burns the layer down to a knee-high mist, and for two hours the Sill is perfectly legible: forty kilometers of white terracing, every rim edged in orange, the blue vents like holes in the world, the waders scattered evenly to the horizon as if placed, the plumes of the neighbors standing straight up, and the whole thing glaring, silent, hot, and full.

Afternoon: the west wind. The plumes lie down. The mist tears into rags that run east along the ground. The waders turn to face it. The ovens shift downslope into deeper water. Working crews come off the exposed piers, because the wind carries scald.

Evening: it closes back up. The arc lamps come on in the towns and make small hard-edged pools of nothing-color in the white, and the plateau goes from a place you can see to a place you can only feel with your feet — warm here, warmer here, and there, over the rim, that faint slow drumming, which is an oven telling another oven something in the dark that neither of them will ever say out loud.
