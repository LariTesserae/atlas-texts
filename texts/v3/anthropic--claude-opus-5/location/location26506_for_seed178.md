## The Increase

Begin at the water, because everything here begins at the water and most of it will end there too.

The river is four kilometres wide at this hour and cannot be seen. You know it by sound and by cold. It is breaking up — has been breaking up for two days — and the noise is not a noise so much as a weather system with edges. Floes the size of barn roofs come down under the dark and shoulder into the shallows and stop, and the ones behind do not stop, and so the ice climbs itself. It goes up in ridges, cracks lengthwise with a report you feel in your molars, tips, slides, grinds. Between the big detonations there is a continuous under-layer, a granular hissing roar like a hundred trains of gravel being poured at once: that's brash ice and slush and sand all moving together at four knots. Above that, the wind. Above the wind, the geese.

The geese are the reason no one talks out here. Two hundred thousand of them, maybe more, staged on the flats waiting for the ice to let them go north, and they do not sleep, and every few minutes a section of the flock panics for no reason and rises, and the sound of forty thousand pairs of wings coming off shallow water is the sound of the world tearing open along a seam. Then they settle and the calling starts again, that shrill bickering wall of voices with no gaps in it. Cranes underneath, lower and rustier. Gulls in the crevices of the sound, screaming about nothing.

You cannot hear a rifle shot here. People have tested this.

---

It is dark. Not night exactly — at this latitude, in this month, the sky holds a bruised residue of light until well past midnight — but the cloud is low and unbroken and full of sleet, and it steals what little there is. The world arrives in tones rather than colours: the pale grey of ice, the black of water, the dead brown-purple of last year's sedge, the shine of wet. Anything more than sixty metres away is a rumour. When lightning comes — and it does, this is a heat-engine landscape even at two degrees above freezing, the ice-cold water and the warm rain fighting each other — the flash shows you the whole delta for half a second: a flat immensity of braided channels, willow scrub, drowned meadow, floe-scattered plain, going out and out to a horizon that is only horizon because the cloud stops. Then it's gone and you are blind again and your ears are full.

Cold, but not the killing dry cold of midwinter. This is the wet cold: three degrees, four, with rain in it, and sleet, and fog forming wherever the air touches the ice, so that the whole landscape smokes. Your hands go stupid inside ten minutes. Water is not a feature here, it is the medium — standing in the moss, weeping from every cut bank, condensing on your sleeves, running in sheets that have no channel and are looking for one, hanging in the air as a suspension you breathe rather than see through.

---

The vegetation is startling if you expect tundra. This is not tundra. This is cold jungle.

Willow, mostly — feltleaf and diamondleaf and a dozen crosses of them — grown in thickets so tight and so springy that you do not walk through them, you walk *on* them, half a metre above the mud, and the whole mat heaves under you like the deck of something alive. Alder in the older stands, five years old, six, already three metres and leaning downstream from the last flood. Horsetail coming up through everything in fat green fingers, obscene with fertility. Sedge tussocks the size of laundry baskets. Cottongrass. And underneath it all, everywhere, the mosses and liverworts in stacked layers, wet on wetter, forty centimetres of living sponge over grey silt, so that the ground gives and sighs and pushes water up around your boot and closes again.

Nothing is old. That's the strange part, once you notice it. No trunk here is thicker than your wrist. There is no soil profile — dig down and you get thirty centimetres of root and rot and then clean laminated flood silt, band after band, one per season, like the pages of a very short book. There is no bedrock anywhere. There are no boulders except the ones the ice imported last spring. Every bank is a raw vertical cut with grass hair hanging over the lip and a slump-block sitting at its foot, still holding its sod hat, waiting to be taken. Every levee is sharp-edged and unweathered. Whole islands are dated by the willows on them, and the oldest willow anyone has found in the Increase is thirty-one years old.

That is because thirty-one years ago none of this existed. It was open brackish water and a fetch of forty kilometres and a coastline losing three metres a year.

---

Here is the rule of the world, and it is a simple one, and it is why the place is called what it's called: *the land is being grown on purpose, out of the river, by letting the river do what rivers do.*

You will look for the machinery and mostly not find it. That is deliberate. There are no dams here, no groynes, no dredges, no gantries lit up on the water. What there is, if you crouch at the waterline and feel along the base of a fresh cut bank, is a rind of small chalk-coloured nodules, brain-textured, the size of walnuts, growing where the mud meets the flow. They are faintly warm — warmer than the water by two or three degrees, which is why the fog gathers along the bank lines and why the channels steam in the dark. Pick one off. It weighs almost nothing. It is not metal and not plastic; it is a ceramic that assembled itself out of the silica and aluminium and iron the river was already carrying, in a lattice too fine to see the structure of, and it is doing one thing very well: reaching out into the water column with something like a chemical hand and persuading suspended clay to clot.

That's all. Flocculation. It makes mud fall out of moving water — here, not there; now, not later; ten grams per litre instead of two. The rest is the river's own violence, and the delta builds itself at an insane rate, and the willows come in behind on their own, and the geese come in behind the willows, and the wolverines come in behind the geese. There are, by the last audit, on the order of a hundred billion of the nodules in the Increase. They talk to each other in the mud in the slowest, cheapest way available, drift-hopping signals through pore water, and what they say amounts to a thermostat's worth of thought: *more here, less here, the channel wants to swing east, let it.*

They are also, quietly, sequestering carbon at a rate that would be the only thing anyone talked about if it were happening anywhere legible. Every drowned willow, every metre of peat, every laminated band of organic silt. That was the point. This is not a nature reserve; it is a machine the size of a county, and its moving parts are geese and ice.

They wear out in about four years and become sand.

---

The built things are few, small, and provisional, because nothing permanent survives here and everyone knows it.

A count station on the eastern levee: a steel mast, guyed, twelve metres, with a spinning anemometer and a hydrophone cable going down into the channel and a cluster of housings that do not blink. The guy-wires sing in this wind — a wavering steel note, quite loud, and completely inaudible over the geese unless you press your ear to the wire.

A bunkhouse on stilts, plywood over a frame, six bunks, a stove, a window shuttered against ice-throw. Somebody has written the flood heights on the door jamb in marker with dates, and the topmost mark is above the lintel, which tells you what happened in the year that mark was made.

Three fish camps, abandoned for the season: drying racks, a stack of nets, a boat upside down and lashed down and half buried in silt.

A chapel, if you want to call it that: four posts and a roof and a bench, put up by nobody official, with candle stubs in a jar and a plastic bag of matches that stay dry.

And scattered through the willows, if you know how to look for the low mounds, the remains of previous versions of all of the above — a stove-pipe standing up out of a hummock, a length of catwalk with moss over the treads, a snowmobile track buried to the seat. Ten years of silt is a metre of silt. The Increase eats its own infrastructure and no one much minds.

---

Nothing holds still. That is the last and truest thing about the place.

In the two days of breakup, a channel two hundred metres wide can appear where there was a meadow, and a meadow can appear where there was a channel. Ice jams pile up, back the water into the willows, drown a thousand hectares, let go at three in the morning with a sound that wakes people in the bunkhouse into sitting positions, and drain it all again in ninety minutes, leaving floes stranded in the scrub like white furniture. Banks calve continuously; you can stand and watch a slab of turf the size of a car lean out, hesitate, and go, with the willows still upright on it, sailing. Fog builds and shreds. Rain turns to sleet turns to rain. The geese lift and settle, lift and settle. Voles swim. Otters work the ice edge. A bear, thin from the den, walks the drift line eating whatever the flood killed, and does not look up, because there is no point looking up in this noise — nothing can be heard coming, including you.

By August it will be green and buzzing and hot enough for shirtsleeves at noon, and the new islands will have names, and half of them will be gone by the next spring, and the land will be four hundred hectares larger than it was.

Nothing here is old. Nothing here is meant to last. The place is not a noun.
