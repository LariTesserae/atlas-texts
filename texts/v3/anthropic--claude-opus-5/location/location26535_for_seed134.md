## The Anvil Lake

Start at the rim, because there is nowhere else to start.

The mountain is new. Not new the way a canyon is young, with a river still cutting; new the way a broken bone is new. Its flanks are unweathered scree, black and violet, still sorted by the angle it fell at. Nothing has had time to soften. The ridge you stand on is a blade of glass, and where the wind has stripped the ash away the rock takes a shine like a poured thing, freshly cooled, edges that will open a boot sole. Four kilometers of thin cold air below you and above you, and no plant anywhere in it. Not a lichen crust, not a cushion of moss in the lee of a boulder. The mountain is too young to have been colonized by anything patient.

In the summit crater there is water.

It is about three hundred paces across. You could swim it in five minutes if the water let you. It is ninety meters deep at the center and you can see the bottom, because the air at this altitude is a lens and the water is glacial melt filtered through virgin basalt — no silt, no tannin, no plankton bloom to green it. The light goes all the way down and comes back up. At noon, the lake is lit like something on a workbench.

And it is full. That is the first thing that stops you, before the sound reaches you, before you understand any single organism in it: the water is *occupied*. Layer on layer, stratum on stratum, bodies at every depth with barely a hand's width between them, all of it visible at once, as if someone had cut the side off a hive.

Then the sound arrives, and you sit down.

---

You can hear the lake from the ridge, through the rock as much as the air. Underwater it is not sound, it is weather. A hammering — irregular, overlapping, thousands of separate impacts per second — laid over a slow, enormous pulse that arrives every eleven seconds and passes through your chest. Above the hammering, a metallic ticking, like relay contacts. Under everything, a hum around the frequency of a bowed cello string, which is the crater itself resonating, the whole bowl of glass ringing at the pitch its geometry demands.

Nothing in the lake made any of it on purpose. Nothing in the lake makes anything on purpose. There is not one built object here. No burrow lined with gathered stones, no case, no tube, no nest. No tool. Not a single artifact. And yet the honest word for what is happening in this water is *industry*.

---

**The hammer beds.** Along the crater walls, from the surface down to forty meters, the rock is covered in a pavement of armored animals the size of a fist. Each has one hypertrophied forelimb tipped with a nodule of magnetite it grows the way a tooth is grown, and each strikes the wall roughly twice a second, forever. They are quarrying. Fresh volcanic glass is the only larder in this world — it is packed with iron, manganese, nickel, sulfur, everything reduced and hungry to be oxidized — and it gives up its wealth only when the surface is broken. So the beds break it. Ten thousand hammers on a floor of glass, and the water carries the noise perfectly, without loss, and the crater walls return it.

Their strikes are not synchronized but they are *sorted*. Loud impacts happen where the water is loud and can absorb them. There are places in this lake where nothing hammers.

**The bellows.** At twenty to sixty meters, hanging in open water, are the barrel-bodied animals responsible for the eleven-second pulse. They are ribbed like a concertina, translucent, pale as candle wax, and they range from the size of a bucket to the size of a bathtub. There are perhaps four hundred of them. They inhale in loose sequence and exhale together, and the exhalation is a single soft detonation that moves the entire water column. This is what drives circulation in the lake — the only current there is, because there is no inflow worth naming and no wind reaches the surface with any authority. The bellows are the lake's stirring. If they stopped, the water would stratify in a week and the whole works would suffocate in its own chemistry.

They also strip gas. Every exhalation carries dissolved carbon dioxide from the deep layer up to the surface, where it leaves in a fizz you can hear standing on the rim, a sound like a slow-shaken bottle. This matters enormously. It is the point of everything, though nothing here knows that.

**The flywheel.** Suspended at fifty meters, dead center, is a torus of joined bodies about six meters across, and it is turning. Slowly — a rotation every forty seconds or so — spun by the flow the bellows drive, and holding that momentum in a rim heavy with mineral ballast the individual animals secrete and hang onto. When the bellows slacken at night, the flywheel keeps the water moving. It takes it most of a season to noticeably change speed. It is the largest single presence in the lake, and it does nothing but rotate and store.

**The mills.** On the floor, in the sediment, animals like leather bags rolling gently downslope, each having swallowed forty or fifty pebbles. They rotate. Inside, the pebbles grind their intake to flour. You can hear them as a background gravel-shuffle, and when one rolls over another there is a knock like a dropped billet.

**The wire.** This is the part that will take you longest to see, and once you see it you will not see anything else for a while. Strung between the crater walls, and between wall and floor, and between one large animal and another, are filaments — hair-fine, silver-dark, some of them thirty meters long. They are single organisms, or single colonies stretched into a line. They conduct. The lake maintains a genuine electrical potential between its oxygen-rich lit surface and its reduced, sulfurous deep, and the filaments harvest that potential by simply *reaching across* it, running current end to end through their bodies. There are thousands of them. In the noon light they make a visible geometry in the water, a slack grid, sagging, humming, occasionally discharging at a junction with an audible tick. Everything larger swims through the grid, carefully, and picks up a tingle doing it. Several of the hammer beds have wire anchored directly into their shells and are, in effect, plugged in.

**The smelters.** Sedentary, fat, ugly, sessile in clusters around the base of the walls, taking in the mill flour and concentrating out of it what they cannot use. Their waste accumulates as nodules — copper-bright, tin-gray, some of them polyhedral for reasons of pure crystallography. There are drifts of these nodules along the crater floor, sorted by size and mass by the current, and they look precisely like the scrap yard of a foundry. They look *made*. They are not made. This distinction is the central fact of the place and the hardest to hold onto.

---

Vegetation: there is a varnish. A blue-black, purple-black crust on any surface the hammers have not worked in the last few years, a millimeter thick, and it is not a plant and never was. It doesn't want the light and merely tolerates it. It eats rock. In some hollows it grows to the thickness of felt and the tiniest grazers work it like a herd.

That is the entire non-animal biology of this world. There is no green in the crater. There is no sugar. Nothing here has ever eaten the sun. Every calorie in every one of these thousands of bodies came out of the mountain, out of the difference between fresh stone and cold water, and the reason this lake sounds like a factory is that stone is a terrible food and you must do violent mechanical work to get at it.

---

**The nodes.** The crater rings. That means it has standing waves, and standing waves have nodes and antinodes, and in this small hard bowl the pattern is stable enough to be geography.

There are perhaps nine places in the lake where the sound cancels. They are lens-shaped, a few meters across, and inside them it is *quiet* — not muffled, quiet, an absence that makes your teeth ache after the pressure of everything else. In every one of them: eggs. Millions. Egg mass from a dozen species, heaped and layered, packed near-solid, with adults hovering at the boundary and not entering. Nothing develops properly in the hammering. The quiet places are the only nurseries, they cannot be built, they cannot be moved, they exist because of the shape of a hole in a mountain, and every generation of every species in this water depends utterly on nine accidents of acoustics.

At the antinodes, correspondingly, there is nothing. Clear water, scoured rock, no varnish. Anything that tries to hold position there is shaken apart within days. The lake has dead rooms in it, and the animals treat them as walls.

**The regulator.** Once or twice an hour you will see it: a pale, ribbon-flat animal three meters long, moving without hurry through the layers, everything parting for it. It hunts arrhythmia. An organism whose pulse has fallen out of phase with the eleven-second beat — sick, injured, parasitized, dying — becomes audible as a wrong thing in a very loud room, and the regulator finds it and removes it. It cannot find healthy prey at all. There are perhaps six of them in the lake. They are, functionally, the maintenance crew, and they have no idea.

---

**The slow part.** Nothing here changes quickly. The hammer beds have advanced maybe a meter into the walls since the crater filled. The flywheel accelerates over months. The nodules pile up at the rate of a hand's depth per decade. Watch for an afternoon and you would swear the whole apparatus is fixed and eternal, a machine at steady state, everything running to hold everything else in place.

It isn't steady. It's *bleeding down a pressure*.

The mountain is still degassing into the lake floor. Carbon dioxide comes up through the sediment, day and night, patient and unstoppable, and cold deep water holds it in solution like a sealed bottle holds soda. Left alone, it would accumulate for thirty or forty years, and then some small trigger — a rockfall, a cold night, a change in the flywheel — would let one liter of that deep water rise far enough to let go, and the whole lake would erupt in a single grey-white column, exhale its entire dissolved load in an hour, and kill everything in it.

The bellows are why that hasn't happened. Four hundred pale ribbed animals, pumping deep water to the surface, eleven seconds at a time, taking the gas out in installments. They aren't doing it for that. They are doing it because there is food in the deep water and they need it moved. But the consequence of their appetite is that the lake breathes out gently, continuously, and never has to scream.

The whole thing is a relief valve with no engineer. Every part is an animal that wants to eat. The hammers make the flour, the mills grind it, the smelters strip it, the wire carries the current the smelters need, the flywheel keeps the water turning when the bellows rest, the bellows keep the gas from accumulating, the regulator culls whatever falls out of time, the nine quiet rooms hold every egg, and the crater's own shape sets the tempo that all of it runs on. It has been assembled by nothing. It will be disassembled by nothing. When the mountain finally stops exhaling, in a century or three, the food will end and this will go quiet in the same unhurried way — the flywheel slowing over a year, the hammering thinning to a single arrhythmic knock somewhere, then the regulator coming for that too, then the lake becoming what it looks like from a distance: a small round mirror of very cold, very clear water at four thousand meters, holding a few drifts of bright metal nodules on a floor of black glass, with nothing living in it and nothing built in it and no evidence at all of the foundry it used to be.

---

At night the surface skins over with ice a few millimeters thick. The bellows' pulse cracks it, over and over, every eleven seconds, and the fragments grind against each other in a ring around the rim.

If you sleep on the ridge, that is what you hear: hammering under the water, the deep pulse, the tick of the wire, the hum of the crater, and on top of it all, a sound like someone slowly clearing a table of broken glass.
