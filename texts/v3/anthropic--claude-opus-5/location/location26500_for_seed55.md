## The Shelf

There is no shore anywhere on it, and no sky in the ordinary sense.

Begin at the top, where you would expect air. There is a band, perhaps four or five metres thick, in which the world cannot decide. Below it, water: black-green, cold enough to ache, still enough to hold a thought. Above it, water again, only broken finer — drizzle so small it has no fall to it, only a hanging. Between the two, foam and vapour and the constant faint stitching of droplets landing. If you stand on a terrace with your head in that band, you breathe air, technically; you also drink. Your hair never dries. Nothing here has ever been dry. The idea would need explaining.

Through that ceiling the light arrives already exhausted. A grey the colour of wet slate, diffuse, sourceless, shifting so slowly through the long cycle that no one can point and say *there*, the light is stronger there. At noon of the brightest year you can see your own hands and the pale scum-line where the weed meets the water's underside. Below two metres, seeing stops being useful and becomes a kind of superstition.

## The stone

Put your foot down. You will not find ground. There is no ground on the Shelf. There is masonry.

Every surface is laid. Under the weed mats, under the peat, under the silt, under the millennia: cut blocks, fitted, still fitted, mostly still level. They are enormous — three, four paces on a side — and they were once dressed square. Now every corner is gone. Not broken: *spent*. Rounded off by an unhurried water that has crossed them for so long that the blocks have the shape of river cobbles laid in a grid, each one a low dome, the joints between them worn into shallow grooves you can hook your toes in. Walking is a rolling motion, dome to groove to dome, and the feet learn it before the mind does.

The Shelf descends. It descends so gently that the descent is a matter of faith rather than sensation. Every three hundred paces or so there is a weir: a wall, once waist-high with a squared coping, now a broad soft lip barely to the knee, over which the entire water of the world moves in a sheet perhaps a finger deep. That sheet moves at the speed of a growing fingernail. It has been moving at that speed since before anything now living was thought of.

Beyond that weir, another terrace, another basin, another lip. And another. And another. The count has been kept, in places, by people who had nothing else to keep, and the counts do not agree, and none of them is small. The terraces go on past any weather. They go on far enough that the Shelf makes its own weather — fog rising off one province and falling as drizzle on another two hundred leagues down-slope, an exhalation and an inhalation with a period of years. Whoever built this built a machine the size of a continent whose only moving part is water, and the machine still runs, and it runs on nothing but the world's slight tilt and the world's cold.

Nowhere is the water deeper than a tall man. Anywhere on the Shelf, in any direction, for as long as walking can be sustained: knee-deep, chest-deep, at worst shoulder-deep in the sunken courses where a coping has slumped. The weirs are graded too well for anything else. Depth is the one constant, and it is the great fact of every life here.

The exceptions are the wells. In the middle of certain basins the stone opens — a perfect round mouth, ten paces across, rimmed with the only sharp edge left in the world, because the water going down it moves fast enough to polish rather than round. Into these the whole slow sheet eventually drains. They breathe cold. They make the one loud sound: a low pull, felt in the sternum more than heard, audible from a basin away when the drizzle thins. No one knows where the water goes. Everyone knows it comes back as fog. There is no evidence for this beyond the fact that it must.

## The lush

For a place this dark, the Shelf is appallingly alive. Not with animals. With plants, packed so close that in most basins you cannot put a hand into the water without pushing something aside.

There is the sheetmoss, brown-black, growing in overlapping shingles across the flooded stone, layer on layer, each layer feeding on the light the layer above failed to use. There is the black hair, fine as thread, standing upright in forests a hand high, so dense it holds the water still inside itself, so that when you wade through it you feel resistance like walking into a bolt of cloth. There is the ribbon, translucent, deep red, six metres long and no thicker than a leaf, that trails down-slope from the weir lips in combed tresses and marks the direction of flow more reliably than any instrument. There is cane weed, the only stiff thing that grows: knuckled tubes rising out of the shallows into the drizzle-band, hollow, tough as bone when dead and cured, from which everything the people have is made.

None of it hurries. A ribbon of six metres is older than a grandmother. The sheetmoss adds a layer in the time it takes a child to become a parent. Growing here is not a season's business but a slow accumulation, and dying is slower still: in this cold nothing rots properly, so the basins have filled, over an unimaginable duration, with metres of packed dead weed — a black peat lying on cut stone with no soil beneath it anywhere, the only earth this world has, and it is entirely made of corpses of plants resting on the work of hands.

Because there are no animals, nothing is grazed, and nothing is pollinated. The mats spread by breaking. A fragment tears loose, drifts one terrace down over the lip, catches in a groove, and begins again. And because nothing eats them, the mats grow until they smother themselves — a basin will thicken and thicken over sixty or eighty years until the lower layers die all at once, and the whole floor of it lifts off the stone in a single black raft, and drifts, and jams a weir, and floods the terrace above by a hand's depth for a decade until it rots through. This is the Shelf's largest event. It is the only thing that qualifies as news.

Animals: a few. Pale grazing things the size of a fingernail in the hair-forests, more felt than seen. Nothing with eyes. Nothing that swims. The water is empty of fish in a way that visitors — if there were visitors — would find obscene. There is no hunting here, and never has been, and no word for it.

## The quiet

Sound: hiss. The drizzle on the water's skin, everywhere, forever, so even and so total that after a few days it stops being sound and becomes the shape of silence. Under it, the weir-whisper — a continent's worth of water going over a continent's worth of rounded lips, each lip contributing almost nothing, the sum contributing a hush you can hear in your bones when you lie down. Then, occasionally: the knock of a cane pole on stone. Breath. The suck of a foot leaving peat. The distant pull of a well.

Speech is quiet here. There is no need to raise a voice; there is nothing to raise it over.

## The people

They walk. That is the whole of it.

They are waist-deep for their entire lives, and they follow the weirs, which run more or less level, so that the walking is a circumnavigation of a contour rather than a journey anywhere. They carry cane poles and slate blades chipped from fallen coping stones — the stone the makers cut is harder and finer than anything they could find otherwise, and so every tool on the Shelf is a piece of the Shelf, taken from the one place where the machine has failed. There is a little copper, traded up-slope from nobody remembers where, mostly in the form of small hooks and needles that are inherited, not made.

They cut weed. They lay it on cane platforms to half-dry in the drizzle-band, which is as dry as anything gets, and they eat it, and press oil from the tubers of the sheetmoss for lamps. The lamps are the reason it is dark: oil is the cost of an hour of seeing, and few hours are worth it. So the people navigate by foot. The joints between blocks run true; a woman who has learned her province can tell you which terrace she is on by the width of the grooves and the height of the domes and the number of weirs since the last well-sound. Reading the floor with the soles: the only literacy that matters.

The other literacy exists. Under the mats, where weed has protected stone from water, there is carving — worn to the faintest swelling, unreadable to the eye even with a lamp, but legible to fingertips. People lie face-down in the shallows with an arm under the moss and read with two fingers, slowly, arguing. It is generally agreed that most of it is numbers. Depths, gradients, dates. The makers left a manual, and it is being read at the rate of a few words a generation, by hand, in the dark, underwater.

When one of the great weed-rafts lifts and a basin floods a hand deeper, the walkers move up-slope for a decade or two and then come back. When a coping slumps and a groove goes sharp, someone notes it and it is remembered for four hundred years. Nothing else happens. The light greys and un-greys. The fog goes down-slope and the rain comes back. The stone loses another hair's breadth of itself into water so cold and so patient that the loss will not be finished for as long as there is a world to lose it in.

It is not a ruin. Ruins have stopped. This is a made thing, still working, worn to the shape water wanted, tended by people who no longer know they are tending it and who could not stop if they tried.
