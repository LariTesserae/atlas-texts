## The Plateau of Standing Engines

**The floor.**

The ground is unweathered rock, and you can feel that with your feet before you understand it with your eyes. There is no soil, no dust, no grit — nothing has ever been broken down here. What you walk on is a pavement of pale feldspar and greenish glass, fresh from the cooling of it, with fracture faces still sharp enough to score a boot sole. The joints between plates are hairline. Where the plates have parted slightly, the gap shows the same clean crystalline material a centimeter down, and a centimeter after that. It is rock all the way, uninterrupted, unsorted, never having been mud or sand or anything else.

The plateau runs perhaps ninety kilometers before it steps up into the ranges. It is very high — the air is thin and dry enough to sting the back of the throat — and warm, a steady blood-warm afternoon that neither builds nor fades. The sky at this altitude is a deep gentian blue, nearly purple at zenith, and cloudless in the absolute sense: not "clear today," but cloudless as a property of the world.

**The light that does not move.**

The sun sits about forty degrees above the western ridges and stays there. This world turns once per orbit; the plateau lives in a permanent mid-afternoon. Every shadow on it is a fixed feature of the geography, as much a landform as a ridge or a bench. The long triangular shade cast eastward by a spire is not an event, it is a *place*, and it has been that same place for as long as the spire has stood. You can navigate by shadows the way you would navigate by dry riverbeds elsewhere.

Because nothing moves, nothing heats and cools. The rock never expands at noon and contracts at midnight. This is the first reason the world does not erode.

**Why nothing wears away.**

The second reason is that there is no water. Not "little" — the working chemistry of this place is anhydrous, and always has been. The atmosphere is nitrogen with a large fraction of carbon dioxide and a bitter trace of sulfur dioxide that you can taste at the back of the nose, a struck-match smell that never gets stronger or weaker. Nothing dissolves. Nothing freezes and splits. Nothing runs downhill.

So the mountains are raw. The ranges on all four horizons are not shaped like mountains you know — no valleys, no fans of debris at their feet, no rounded shoulders. They are *extrusions*. Slabs and blades and blunt pistons of crust, still being pushed up from beneath at a few millimeters a century, with their original crystallization surfaces intact: mirror-flat cleavage planes hundreds of meters across that catch the fixed sun and throw a hard white sheet of light down onto the plateau. Some faces are corrugated with the chill-marks of the melt they froze from, ripples the size of a room. At the base of a two-thousand-meter cliff there is no talus. There is only the line where the cliff meets the floor, as tight as a joint in cabinetry.

The whole landscape has the quality of a casting just knocked out of its mold, an hour old, twelve million years running.

**The growth.**

And it is lush. That is the thing that takes the longest to accept.

Across the plateau, spaced with enormous regularity — one every two or three kilometers, so that you can stand between four of them and be alone in a shallow bowl of light — stand the growths. Call them trees for now.

They are twenty to forty meters tall and built almost entirely of metal and mineral. The trunk of a mature one is a bundle of drawn aluminum cables, each the thickness of a wrist, twisted into a column with a slow left-hand lay. Where the bundle splits, the individual strands fan out and re-gather, and you can see that the metal was not cast but *grown*, extruded fiber by fiber from a growth ring at the base that is still, in some technical sense, working. The surface is bright. Aluminum does not tarnish much here; there is no moisture to help it along past its first molecule-thick skin of oxide.

The canopy is where the word *lush* earns itself. Each tree carries thousands of leaves — thin wafers of doped silicon and layered sulfide, hexagonal, palm-sized to bedsheet-sized, held on stiff silver petioles at exactly the angle that faces the unmoving sun. They are colored the way beetles and oil are colored: interference blues, bronzes, an oily magenta at the edges where the coating thins. From a distance, a full canopy reads as dark iridescent foliage, dense, generous, faintly quivering in the way a hot road quivers. Come closer and you can see the tree's underlayer of shade leaves cranked to catch light bounced up off the pale floor. Nothing is wasted. The efficiency is obscene.

Below ground — you can see this at the lip of a crack — the root system is not roots but a mat of black conductive filament, spread flat and wide, mining the rock. It reduces the local ores. That is what the tree does with its light: it electrolyzes. Alumina to aluminum, sulfides to sulfur and metal, silica to silicon, driven by a standing voltage of several hundred volts between canopy and mat. If you climb the trunk with an instrument, you find the potential gradient neatly stepped, like a transmission line.

**The industry.**

Here is the part that makes visitors quiet.

Each tree is a smelter, a refinery, and a fabrication line, and it produces far more than it needs. Halfway up the trunk, at the whorls, are the extrusion organs — hard black nozzles, dozens of them, each patiently issuing product. Wire, mostly: hair-fine aluminum wire, coiling out at a rate of maybe a meter an hour, falling in slow loops. Also ribbon. Also, from the larger organs, cakes of pale yellow sulfur, and small bright ingots of silicon shot through with dendritic flaws, and a fine graphite-black powder that drifts a few centimeters and settles.

Beneath every tree there is a mound. Cones of coiled wire two and three meters high, terraced with sulfur, dusted black, mixed with ingots like fruit fallen and never taken. Some mounds are old enough to have compacted under their own weight into a solid conductive slag, and the tree stands on its own output like a plant on a pot of its own making.

Nothing collects the wire. There is nothing here to collect it. There are no animals — not one, not a mite in a crack, not a spore-fed thing in the shade. There never were. The industry produces at scale, continuously, for no market, and the product simply accumulates until it becomes terrain.

Between the mounds, the plateau is empty. Truly empty: kilometers of clean glassy pavement with your own shadow and nothing else on it, and then, far off, another tree, small and dark and iridescent, standing in its own pool of light with its own hill of wire.

**Sound.**

You will notice you have been holding your breath to listen.

There is almost nothing. No wind worth the name — the temperature gradient that drives wind doesn't exist here, and the air just sits. The trees make three sounds, all of them small. A dry tick, irregular, as a leaf's stiff petiole adjusts by a fraction of a degree, or as a new wafer unfolds. A faint continuous hiss near the extrusion organs, which is metal being born. And, if you put your ear near the trunk, a hum at the very bottom of hearing, more felt in the teeth than heard, the sound of a great deal of current going somewhere very slowly.

Your own footsteps on the glass floor are shockingly loud. They do not echo — there is nothing to echo off — they simply happen and are gone, and the silence closes over the spot.

**The trace of water.**

There is water. About enough to fill a thimble, in the whole of the visible plateau.

On the permanently shadowed side of the eastern blades — surfaces that have never once seen the sun and sit sixty degrees below the temperature of the floor — there are pockets in the fresh rock where a whitish rime has formed. It is hydrate: water chemically caught into the mineral, plus a hair of actual ice in the deepest recesses. It came from the trees, which produce a molecule or two of water per mole of anything else, as a byproduct they have no use for and vent as vapor. Over geological time the vapor migrated to the cold traps and stopped.

It does nothing. It is not a resource, not a cycle, not a beginning. It is a rounding error that has been quietly filed in the shade for ten million years.

**The rules.**

1. Nothing decays, so nothing needs replacing.
2. Nothing is eaten, so nothing needs defending.
3. The light does not move, so nothing needs to anticipate.
4. Growth ended when the spacing solved itself. Each tree stands exactly far enough from its neighbors that their root mats do not overlap and their canopies do not shade. The pattern across the whole plateau is a hexagonal lattice, slightly distorted by the terrain, and it is complete. No seedlings. No gaps. There has not been a new tree in a very long time, and there has not been a dead one either.
5. The only thing still increasing is the product, and the product is inert.

This is a landscape at equilibrium that is nevertheless not finished — the mountains still rising, the wire still coiling, the world still, technically, under construction, at a rate so slow and so smooth that a human lifetime spent here would record no change whatsoever except the changes you made yourself.

**And if you did.**

If you knocked over a mound of wire, it would stay knocked over. The tree would go on adding to the new shape. If you snapped a leaf off, the stub would remain a stub. There is no healing here because there is no injury — injury requires a process that expects to be interrupted, and nothing here expects anything.

Your footprints will not appear on the glass floor. But the scuff marks from your boot heels will still be there, unblurred, bright against the matte, when the sun has moved forty degrees, which is to say never.
