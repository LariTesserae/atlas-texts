## The Sump

There is a low place under a low ridge, and it is full of water that never leaves by any road.

You come to it across a floor of grey shale that flakes underfoot like old paint, and the descent is so gradual you are inside the basin before you notice a rim behind you. The rim is the only real edge: a lip of brick and puddled slag about chest-high, running in an oval maybe eighty paces on the long axis, sixty on the short. Someone made it, a long time ago, out of what they had. The brickwork has been rounded off by weather until each brick is a soft-cornered loaf, and the mortar has withdrawn between them like gums from teeth, but nothing falls. It has finished falling. That is the condition of everything here: not ruined, but *settled* — arrived at a shape it intends to keep.

Inside the oval, the ground steps down in six or seven shallow terraces to a floor you cannot see, because it is under water, and the water is the colour of unlit pewter.

## Water as the governing fact

The air is at the exact temperature where it can no longer hold what it carries. Everything is wet without rain. The brick weeps. The shale weeps. Your sleeves go heavy within minutes and stay that way; a droplet forms on the underside of every projecting thing — every pipe flange, every eyebrow, every mineral fringe — swells, hangs, falls, and is replaced. Multiply that by a million surfaces and you get the basin's principal sound, which is not a drip but a *texture* of drips, a fine granular hiss with individual clicks resolving out of it and sinking back in, like static that occasionally remembers being rain.

The water table is not below you. It is a few fingers above your boot-soles at all times, seeping laterally through the terraces, so the terrace faces are not stone but flowstone — poured, lobed, drapery-thin curtains of pale carbonate and rust laid over whatever was originally there. The terraces work as a slow ladder of filtration. On the upper steps the seep is clear and cold and tastes of iron filings. By the third step down it has gone milk-blue with suspended lime. By the fifth it is orange, a thick ochre broth, and the flowstone there is not flowstone at all but *iron*: soft ferruginous cake, bright as fox pelt where a foot has broken it, and beneath the broken skin, black.

The pool at the bottom is not deep — a metre and a half at most — but it is very old water, and it is stratified. The top hand's-breadth is soft and drinkable-looking. Below that it goes glassy and refuses to mix, and if you disturb it, a sulphurous smell comes up like something being remembered against its will, and takes ten minutes to disperse.

Nothing drains out of here. Water arrives from the ridge and departs only as vapour, which is why the mineral load has been building without interruption since before the brickwork. The basin is, functionally, a slow evaporating dish the size of a chapel.

## No green thing

There is no plant. Not a moss cushion, not a lichen crust, not an alga's green haze on the wet stone. The light is too poor — perpetual dusk under a ceiling of its own fog and an overcast that never quite tears — and the water is too mineral, and something about the chemistry simply refuses.

What there is instead is *film*.

Every wet surface below the second terrace wears a bacterial pelt. On the iron steps it is rust-red and rubbery and comes off in sheets like wet wallpaper. On the sulphurous margins it is white, and hangs in beards eight or ten centimetres long, swaying in the seep-current with the exact motion of hair underwater. In the deepest shade, where the collapsed slab overhangs the pool, the film is faintly, genuinely luminous — a dim brass-coloured glow with no flicker in it at all, enough to see the shape of your own hand by, not enough to read. And there are fungal bodies: no mushrooms as such, but slick grey shelves on the iron, and a kind of pale finger-growth that colonises grease.

This is the base of everything. The world here does not eat sunlight. It eats stone, and rust, and old lubricant, and the sulphur coming up through the floor, and it does so silently and continuously and with no season.

## The machine

At the north end, a squat house of engineering brick, roofless, with one gable standing. Beside it, half-sunk, a flywheel four metres across, its rim broken into three arcs, the arcs now cemented into the flowstone as securely as fossils. A narrow-gauge rail runs eleven metres from nowhere to nowhere and stops. Bolts, a lot of bolts, standing up out of concrete plinths in patterns that describe absent objects.

And pipes. Cast iron, four-inch and eight-inch, running down the terraces in parallel, jointed with lead, sleeved in ochre cake, most of them dead.

Two are not dead.

They come out of the ridge somewhere above the rim and go into the pool, and there is a thermal difference between the deep rock and the basin air, and that difference is enough. A closed loop — no fuel, no fire, no attendant, nothing but cold water sinking and warmer water rising — has been circulating in those pipes without interruption for however long it has been. You can feel it if you put a palm on the metal: a slight, patient stir. Once in a while the loop burps, and a hollow *tonk* comes up out of the pipe, and the sound goes around the brick oval twice before it gives up.

The people who built this were doing something with condensate. Collecting it, cooling it, purifying it, feeding it to something further down the valley that no longer exists. They understood that a cold wet hollow is an engine if you fit it correctly. Then they left, and their fitting continued to be correct.

## The breath

Here is the strangeness, and it is the reason the fauna organise themselves the way they do.

Roughly every eleven minutes — it varies, it is not clockwork, it seems to lengthen in the small hours — the basin exhales. The fog ceiling shivers. A cool draught moves out across the terraces from the pool's centre with a smell of wet coins. The water level drops by perhaps a finger's width, visibly, and a rind of wet stone is exposed all around the margin. There is no sound to it except the general hiss going momentarily denser.

Then, over the following minutes, it comes back. The water climbs its rind. The fog thickens. The draught reverses so gently you feel it on the back of the neck before the face.

Some deep siphon in the ridge, some cavity filling and voiding; there is nothing supernatural about it, and it makes no difference to how it feels, which is unmistakably respiratory. The whole hollow is a chest, and you are inside the chest, and it is not in a hurry.

## What lives here

A great deal. The density is the first thing that becomes apparent once you stop looking for plants and start looking for animals.

**On the exposed rind, in the eleven-minute window:** *tin snails*, thousands of them, flat spiralled shells with a metallic sheen from the iron they precipitate. They graze the bacterial film. The moment the water withdraws they advance downslope in a front, and the moment it returns they retreat, and they have done this so many million times that the rind is scored with their radular grooves in a band exactly one finger wide.

**In the beard-films:** shrimps, blind, translucent, so that you can watch a dark thread of gut work inside them. They hang upside down in the white beards and comb them.

**In the deep pool:** something long. You see it as a change in the way the surface holds the light. It is probably an amphibian — the local word would be a *gutter eel* — a depigmented, gill-fringed thing of maybe forty centimetres that eats shrimp and snail and possibly its own smaller relatives, and comes up to gulp air at the top of each exhalation, when the oxygen at the surface is briefly better.

**On the brick, everywhere:** cave crickets. Long-legged, patient, in overlapping ranks so that a metre of wall may hold forty of them, near-touching, each one's antennae resting on the next one's back. They do not sing much. When one does, the others fall silent for a second, and then the drip-texture closes over it.

**In the air:** midges, but only in slabs. They breed in the iron broth and emerge in coordinated pulses, and for four or five minutes the space above the third terrace is a solid column of them, and then it is nothing again. The old flywheel arcs are furred with their shed skins, decades deep, a grey pelt on the metal that is soft to touch and smells of nothing.

**Under the standing gable:** bats, a small colony, maybe sixty. They leave at dusk — you can tell dusk here only by their leaving — and hunt somewhere else, and come back with the outside world inside them, and the guano they drop is the single largest import of energy into the basin. Beetles work it. Mites work the beetles. Half the visible animal life in this hollow is, at two removes, funded by moths from another valley.

**Predation runs continuously and quietly.** Nothing hurries. Everything here is cold, and cold makes the pace of hunting deliberate: a gutter eel may spend nine minutes closing a distance of thirty centimetres.

## Rules of the place

1. Water enters as seep and leaves as breath. Nothing is flushed. What arrives, stays, in some form, in some layer.
2. Light is never enough to be a resource. Energy comes from below (chemistry) and from outside (bats).
3. The basin has finished changing shape. New material is added — a millimetre of flowstone in a decade — but the *form* is complete and self-maintaining. Break something and it will be re-cemented in place rather than removed.
4. All schedules are hung on the eleven-minute breath. Snails, eels, the burp of the pipe, the density of the fog. If the breath ever stopped, this would be a different world by morning.
5. The machinery is part of the geology now, and part of the food web, and it is still, in a small way, working. It requires nothing from anyone. It will not be turned off.

## Standing there

Cool, not cold — you would want a coat but not gloves. Dim enough that colour is uncertain and you'd swear the ochre steps are grey until you break one open. Wet enough that you can taste the air. Quiet enough that you can hear an individual snail move.

And crowded. That is what stays with you afterwards: how *full* a place with nothing growing in it can be. Every square hand's-breadth of that brick oval is occupied — by film, by shell, by cricket, by shed skin, by lime, by rust, by droplet — layered on layer, near-touching, all of it moving slightly, all of it breathing on an eleven-minute cycle in the almost-dark, and none of it needing you to be there at all.
