## The Sump

Begin with the water, because everything here begins with the water. It stands nine or ten metres deep and it is cold in the way that stone is cold — not biting, just utterly indifferent to your body's opinion. Eleven degrees. It has been eleven degrees for a very long time. Look up from the floor of it and the surface is a shivering grey membrane, and in one quarter of that membrane there is a paler patch, a bruise of daylight coming down through a grating four metres above the water's face, so that the whole chamber is lit like the inside of a bottle of weak tea.

The chamber is a room. That's the scale of the place — one room, twenty-two metres across, octagonal in plan, roofed with a shallow brick dome that meets the walls in eight groins. You could shout across it, if shouting were of any use here, which it isn't. It is a room and it is the entire world, and there is no natural surface anywhere in it. Not one. Under the silt is brick; under the brick is more brick; under that, engineering blue clay puddled by hand a very long time ago and rammed until it forgot it had ever been earth. Nothing in this place grew, was deposited, or was uplifted. It was all laid, keyed, bolted, and set — and then it was left, and the water came up through the floor drains and did not go away.

The bricks have gone soft. That's the first thing you notice once your eyes settle. Every arris rounded, every corner nursed down to a curve. The mortar has receded a full centimetre so that each brick sits alone in its shallow socket like a tooth in a shrunken gum, and the bricks themselves have lost their edges to a hundred and forty thousand cycles of slow flow and slower chemistry, until the wall reads as a field of low domes, a cobbled thing, a wall pretending to be a riverbed. Run a hand across it and it is like stroking the flank of something large and asleep. It will not wear much further. It has arrived. The corners have found their radius; the arches have found their sag; the iron has found its final form as rounded orange loaves that hold their shape but crumble at pressure, like biscuit. This is a landscape that has finished happening. Everything that could fall has fallen. Everything that could settle has settled. What remains is the stable residue, and it will look exactly like this in another thousand years.

And yet the place is loud.

That is the contradiction at the heart of it. The sump roars. Sound arrives at the ear as a single mass and then, with patience, separates into layers.

Underneath everything: a sixty-cycle hum, structural, felt in the sternum more than heard, the note of a large volume of water being pushed.

Above that: the falling. On the north-east face, three metres above the waterline, a culvert mouth two metres wide vomits an unbroken column of cold water into the chamber. It has done this without pause for longer than anyone has been alive. The column is the colour of milk where it enters, a white root driving down into the dark, and it drags a curtain of bubbles four metres deep, and the bubbles come apart into a rising fizz that never entirely stops. The noise of it is not a crash but a sustained tearing, like canvas ripping in a wind that never falls.

Above *that*: the machine.

Beneath the falling column, sunk to its axle in silt and black water, is an overshot wheel of cast iron, five metres in diameter, twenty-eight buckets, and it turns. It turns at about nine revolutions a minute and it has been turning at about nine revolutions a minute since it was commissioned. The buckets are worn thin as wafers at their lips and every one of them has been repaired at least once with a plate riveted crookedly on. The bearings sing — a two-tone shriek, rising and falling, that arrives every six and a half seconds. Off the axle, a train of gears the size of millstones carries the motion up through the west wall into an Archimedean screw in a trough, and the screw lifts water out of the sump and away into a drain that leads somewhere nobody in the chamber has ever needed to know about. The screw makes a rhythmic slop and gulp. The gears make a grinding conversation of iron on iron with a stone or two in it.

And off a second, smaller take-off, a belt slaps around a dynamo the size of a beer keg, and the dynamo, which is rusted into a single amorphous object except for the parts that must move, feeds one lamp.

The lamp hangs from the crown of the dome inside a wire cage, and it burns a sick amber that flickers with the wheel. Nine flickers a minute. It is not enough light to read by. It is enough that the dome above the waterline is visible, a soot-brown vault, and that the water beneath the lamp shows a slowly rotating disc of gold, and that everything else in the chamber has a shadow that pulses.

So the room has weather. That is the honest word for it. Twice a day, something far away breathes and the water in the sump rises and falls by forty centimetres over six hours, and when it rises, the culvert's column shortens and the wheel labours; when it falls, the column lengthens and the wheel gains half a revolution a minute and the lamp brightens perceptibly. There is a bright tide and a dim tide. There is a loud tide and a slightly less loud tide. That is the day here, and the creatures keep it.

Because there are a great many creatures.

Where the falling column tears air into the water, it makes the whole chamber breathable, and where there is oxygen in cold clean water there is life in numbers that surprise people who expected a dead place. The silt floor crawls. Water lice — *Asellus* — in their thousands, grey and armoured and grazing the film off every brick, so thickly in the aeration zone that the floor there seems to move like a nap of fur. Amphipods flickering sideways through the light-shaft, so many that the shaft looks dusty. Blind white shrimp, thread-thin, in the seams where mortar has gone. A crayfish the colour of a fingernail, missing a claw, living under a fallen keystone with the patience of furniture.

And eels. Eight or nine of them, big, older than the lamp perhaps, holding station in the slack water behind the wheel with their heads out of crevices and their bodies inside the wall. They are the top of the food chain in a room the size of a chapel and they know it. There are bullhead in the rubble, stone loach nosing the silt in short dashes, a small dark shoal of something minnow-like that has learned to hold in the bubble curtain where the eels won't hunt. A rat swims the surface once or twice a tide, from the grating ledge to the culvert mouth, purposeful as a ferryman. There is a wren, sometimes, that comes down through the grating and works the dry brickwork above the waterline for spiders, and the spiders are there because midges hatch out of the sump in slow continuous numbers and drift up the light shaft in a thin column, and that column is the only thing in this room that ever leaves.

The plants are almost an afterthought, and that's correct. Life here does not need them. Directly under the grating, in the one square where daylight lands on masonry, there is a hand's-breadth of moss so dark green it reads as black, and a liverwort like spilled wax, and above the highest waterline a single hart's-tongue fern, pale and etiolated, two fronds, one broken. Under the electric lamp, where the amber falls on wet brick, there is a beard of stringy algae that the water lice keep cropped to stubble. Everything else — the other twenty-one metres of the room — has only the invisible vegetable film, the diatoms, that make the brick slick and the lice fat.

Space is the other resource here, and there is plenty. That's the feel of the place, once the noise stops being an event and becomes a condition: it is not crowded. It is a mostly empty cold room with an enormous machine in one corner, a waterfall in another, a lamp overhead, some scattered fallen stone, and a great deal of nothing in between. Long open water. Ten metres between one presence and the next. You can hover in the middle of the sump and be touching nothing, seeing three things, hearing everything.

The rules, insofar as a room has rules:

Nothing decays quickly. At eleven degrees, a drowned rat takes a season to go, and it goes by being eaten, not by rotting. Bones accumulate in the low ring of the floor where the wheel's back-eddy drops them, and they stay white, and that ring is the closest thing the chamber has to a graveyard or a library.

Nothing new is made. The chamber has no builders. Every element in it was placed by hands that have been gone a very long time, and the only construction still occurring is caddis larvae gluing grains of hundred-and-forty-year-old brick dust into tubes the size of a match.

Nothing stops. The water falls, the wheel turns, the screw lifts, the lamp flickers, the lice graze, the eels wait, and the tide comes twice. It is not a ruin. A ruin has stopped. This is something rarer — a made place that outlived its purpose and kept its habits, worn down to its final smooth shape and still, faithfully, nine times a minute, going round.
