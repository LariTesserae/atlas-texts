## The Long Sink

Begin at the rim, because the rim is where the world explains itself.

You stand on a limestone plateau in a cold latitude — dwarf birch to the ankle, reindeer lichen, wind, a sky the colour of skimmed milk. The ground is pale, fissured, ordinary. And then it stops. It stops along a line as clean as a broken plate, and beyond that line there is nothing but air and, far below, green.

The roof of the country fell in here, and not long ago. You can tell by the rock: the cliff faces are still bone-white where they sheared, unweathered, sharp enough to cut a palm. Lichen has only begun to freckle the upper metres. The talus at the bottom is angular, unrounded, the blocks still fitting one another like the pieces of something recently broken. Whole slabs of the old ceiling remain in place as natural bridges, spanning the gap in three or four places, hundreds of metres long, thin as ribs. They are being eaten from beneath by water and time and they will go, but not yet.

The collapse did not happen in one place. It happened in a chain — sink after sink after sink, following the drowned river that hollowed the plateau — so that what lies below is not a hole but a valley with a lid mostly off it. Eight kilometres of it, wandering, branching, roofed in patches. From the rim you see three sinks and the shadowed arches between them, and the daylight going down in shafts and pooling on the floor sixty, eighty metres beneath.

## The floor

Down there it is a different climate, and the difference is the whole reason for everything else.

Cold air is heavy. It slides off the plateau in the small hours and drains into the sinks and cannot get out, so the floor sits in a permanent pool of chill — frost under the north-facing boulders in July, ice lenses in the talus that never fully go. But the walls stop the wind entirely. And the light comes down almost vertically at midday, full strength, unfiltered.

Shelter, cold, and light, with water everywhere: the result is a lushness that has no business at this latitude. Moss grows here in pillows a metre thick, deep enough to sink an arm into, so saturated that stepping on them presses out water like a sponge. Liverworts curtain the seep-lines in overlapping green scales. Filmy ferns — plants normally the size of a thumbnail — grow here in sheets the size of doors, because the air below the drip-line never falls below saturation. Hart's-tongue ferns spring straight out of the rock joints. On the talus, alder and grey willow have taken hold, ten metres tall, leaning toward the light, their trunks furred to the top with more moss. Higher on the walls, where the ledges catch sun, there are cushions of saxifrage and moss campion and a few contorted birches rooted in nothing at all.

Water is not an event here; it is the condition. Every wall weeps. The whole plateau drains through this ceiling, and it arrives as ten thousand separate threads — not waterfalls but a constant, fine, chandelier drip, catching the light, hanging the air with mist. The floor of each sink holds a river that is only intermittently a river; mostly it is a stepped chain of dark green pools, and it goes under the rock at the downstream end of each sink and comes out at the top of the next.

## The city

Now look closer, because the walls are not walls.

The undercut faces — the great overhangs where the collapse left the rock leaning out like a wave — are plastered from the talus to the rim with mud nests. Millions. A colonial martin, small and sooty, builds a rounded pot of river clay and saliva, and where there is no room for a new pot it builds on the roof of an old one, and its offspring build on that, until the overhang carries a crust of habitation a metre and a half deep in places, honeycombed, layered like a reef. Old nests fill with silt and grow moss and become planters; ferns hang out of them; the whole cliff face is a vertical marsh of dead architecture with the living generation sealed to the outer surface of it. Where sections have fallen away you can read the strata: pale new clay, then grey, then dark, then something that is essentially soil, forty or fifty generations of building down.

Below the martins, in the talus, live the diggers. Call them that. Something between a marmot and a beaver, thick-furred, slow, sociable, working in family lines. They do not simply burrow — the talus is too coarse and too wet for that. They *build*: they wall the gaps between boulders with clay and chewed root, they terrace, they cut drainage channels so that the seepage runs past their chambers instead of through them, they roof the terraces with woven willow and pack it with moss. A digger colony is a stepped town of small dark doorways occupying the whole slope of a talus fan, with paths worn shining into the stone between them. In late summer they harvest — sedge and willow-leaf, cut and stacked into standing haycocks on the flat rocks to dry, then carried in. The haycocks are the most orderly thing in the valley and they look, from the rim, exactly like a farmed field.

The water is managed too, though no one intends it. The dams are the work of larger animals in the lowest pools, stepping the river into a staircase of ponds and holding the water table high across the whole sink floor; the fine work is done by caddis larvae, whose gravel cases, cemented side by side in their thousands along the pool lips, build low weirs a hand's breadth high. The weirs silt up. The silt grows moss. The moss holds water. Everything here builds by accident and the accidents accumulate.

And it is *packed*. That is the thing you cannot convey from the rim. There is no bare surface anywhere below the rim-line. Every stone is mossed; every moss carries snails and springtails; every nest carries a plant; every plant carries an insect; every crevice is somebody's door. Roots run through the martin-crust and hold it together. Snails graze the algae film on the outside of occupied nests. Bumblebees — big, furred, cold-adapted — nest inside abandoned digger chambers and work the saxifrage on the sunlit ledges. Spiders sheet the gaps that nobody else wants. Where a slab fell last spring and left ten square metres of clean white rock, that rock is already blue-green with algae and stitched with the first moss protonema, and there are three martin nests on the underside of it. The valley does not tolerate emptiness for a season.

## The quiet

Here is what surprises anyone who arrives expecting a rookery.

It is almost silent.

A stone bowl this size is a resonator. Every sound made on the floor comes back off the walls, doubled and delayed, and comes back again off the roof-arches. In a place where a hawk-owl hunts the shafts at dusk and a lithe brown mustelid works the talus at all hours, noise is a liability that compounds. So the valley has gone quiet the way a forest goes tall — under pressure, over a long time.

The martins do not chatter; they communicate at the very edge of hearing, and mostly by touch at the nest-hole. The diggers use a whistle so brief and high it registers as an absence rather than a note. When a predator crosses the sink, the whole colony does not shriek — it simply stops, and the stopping travels across the slope faster than anything could fly, and for ninety seconds there is a silence with a shape to it.

What you hear instead is water. The drip from ten thousand seeps, out of phase, so continuous it becomes a texture rather than a sound — a hush, like blood in the ear. Under it, the pools moving over the weir-lips. Occasionally the deep gulp of the river going under rock. Once or twice a day, somewhere, a small stone falls, and the sound of it goes around the valley three times before it dies.

## The year

Nothing here is finished, and that is the last thing to understand.

In spring the plateau's snow arrives all at once. For ten days the seeps become jets, the pools brown and rise, the weirs are overtopped and some are destroyed, and the martins wait it out on the rim. Blocks come down. Every year the walls give up a little more, the bridges thin, the sinks lengthen toward one another. The valley is getting longer at both ends and no one alive in it will see it stop.

In summer, fog fills the sinks each dawn — the cold pool meeting the wet air — and burns off from the top down, so that for an hour the arches float above white and the floor is invisible, and then the light comes back down its shafts and hits the moss and the whole bottom of the world lights up green.

In autumn the diggers stack their hay and seal their doors with clay and the martins go, all at once, in a single quiet morning, leaving the cliff-crust standing empty and dripping.

In winter the shafts admit a thin blue light for four hours a day. Ice grows out of the seeps in ribbed columns, and the moss goes on photosynthesising underneath it, and the valley — cold-floored, wind-proof, water-fed, roofed in places, crowded past all reason — holds.
