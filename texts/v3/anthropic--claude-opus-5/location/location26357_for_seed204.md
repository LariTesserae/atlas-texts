## The Room

There is a mountain in the last of a young range, still rising, that has a slot in its summit ridge. From below — from any of the four valleys, from the air, from orbit — the slot does not exist. The ridge reads as an unbroken blade of grey-blue rock with a snow cornice on the leeward side. This is not concealment in any exotic sense. Someone bridged the opening long ago with a lid of the same stone, quarried from the same seam, matched grain to grain, and the weather has been working on it for two centuries now with the same indifference it shows the rest of the ridge. There is a joint you could find with your hands if you knew where to put them. Nobody outside has ever put their hands there.

Underneath the lid is a space forty-one metres long, between nine and fourteen wide, and ninety-two deep. The people who live in it call it the Room. That is the whole of the world under discussion. Everything else is elsewhere.

---

**The walls.** Blueschist and a coarse pale gneiss, folded against each other at a hard angle, both of them *new* in the way that matters: unweathered, unrounded, still holding the fracture faces they were born with. Run a light along them and the surfaces flash — mica, garnet, a glassy dark mineral nobody has bothered to name because naming it would be a formality. The edges are sharp enough to open skin. Down at the fifth level there is a slab that came off the east wall in 74 and it still has a conchoidal scar on it like broken bottle glass, unblunted, because in the Room the only erosion agents are water and frost, and frost is managed.

Everything in the Room slopes wrong. The floor is not a floor; it is talus, being sorted. The mountain is growing at just under three millimetres a year, and it does not do this smoothly. It does it in ticks. A tick is a compression and release somewhere in the root of the range, and it arrives in the Room as a sound below hearing and a sensation in the soles of the feet, and afterwards a few dozen stones have moved and something in the architecture needs shimming.

**The water.** The Room sweats. Warm saturated air rises off the eastern valley all afternoon, finds the ridge, gets pulled through the throat below the lid, and meets rock at two degrees. What happens then is the central fact of the place. Condensation forms on ninety thousand square metres of cold fresh mineral surface, and it does not sheet — it beads, and the beads walk down the wall along crystal boundaries, and where a hundred boundaries converge you get a seep, and where seeps converge you get a thread of water with an audible voice. There are four hundred and six named threads. They are named because they are the clock.

The east wall weeps steadily. The west wall weeps in the six hours after the fog tide and then stops. The overhang under the lid grows an ice chandelier every winter, five metres of it, and in the third week of the thaw the whole assembly lets go at once with a noise like a building falling, into a catch-net of woven filament strung at level three, which sags and rings and holds. This event is scheduled. People come out onto the stairs to watch it, drinking something hot, saying very little.

Nothing here is dry. The stone is dark with wet and the wet has a smell — cold rock, ozone from the field braces, a faint iron. Cloth left out does not dry, it merely gets colder. Everyone owns two of everything.

**The light.** A slot of sky runs the length of the Room, north-northeast, six metres wide at the narrowest. Direct sun crosses it for fifty-one minutes near midsummer and not at all between the equinox and the far side of winter. But the Room is bright. The upper eighteen metres of both walls are coated to a depth of a few molecules with something that takes light in and gives it back slowly and evenly, so that the whole upper Room functions as one soft window and the light arriving at level five is ordinary daylight, slightly blue, without a source you can point at. It brightens and dims with the day outside on a lag of about forty minutes. On overcast days the Room is the colour of a well-lit office. Nobody finds this remarkable. It is simply what walls do.

---

**The town.** Seven levels, pinned to the two long walls, facing each other across a gap of nine to fourteen metres. Stairs on the outside of everything, in switchbacks. Nine bridges. Roughly three hundred and forty rooms, of which perhaps a hundred and ten are in use. Forty-three people.

That is the shape of it: a city's amount of construction, layered and infrastructural and dense in every direction — plumbing, drainage, load paths, the Survey's instrument runs, the cold-sink flues, three cisterns of eleven thousand litres each, a foundry that hasn't been fired in years, a market consisting of four stone tables — occupied by almost nobody. You can stand on the bridge at level four in the afternoon and see the whole settlement at once and count two people in it. One of them will be a lamplighter's apprentice checking dripline gauges. The other will be sitting somewhere, not doing anything. The emptiness is not a wound. It is the correct ratio. Forty-three people in three hundred rooms means everyone has space to be quiet in.

**Construction rules.** Nothing in the Room rests on the ground. This is the first law and every visible feature descends from it. The mountain grows and the walls diverge and converge by fractions of a millimetre a season, unevenly, and any structure keyed rigidly into both walls would tear itself apart inside a decade. So: every building sits on jack-plates. Every plate has a screw and a reading. Every bridge has a slip joint at one end, greased, with a shim stack beside it in a labelled box. The Survey walks the levels on a nine-day cycle with a machine the size of a lunchbox that reads the geometry of the entire Room to a few nanometres, and posts the numbers, and crews turn screws. Two turns here. Half a turn there. Take a shim out of bridge six.

The load-bearing is not honest. The struts are far too slender for what they carry: finger-thick columns holding four storeys of stone. They work because of the braces, which are not material in any load-bearing sense — a brace is a cylinder of stiffened space between two anchors, and what it stiffens is the geometry, not the steel. When one fails it does not bend; it simply stops being there, and the column above it fails in the ordinary way, catastrophically, which is why brace anchors are inspected more often than anything else in the Room. The braces hum. It's a very low hum, and you only hear it in the hour after a tick, when the whole Room is settling.

**Heat.** The Room holds at about two degrees and the residences hold at nineteen, and the difference is not made by burning anything. There are sinks in the walls that move heat against its inclination, quietly, and dump it up the flue above the lid, where it emerges into the outside wind as a faint plume that mimics the cornice's own vapour and has never been noticed. The cost of this is paid in something the engineers describe with a shrug and a gesture at the floor.

---

**The living things.** Vegetation: there is none. Not a lichen, not a crust, not a smear of black film. The walls are scrubbed by hand, level by level, on rotation, because anything with a hold-fast will crack the rock and cracked rock is water in the wrong place. The scrubbing is the oldest job and the least popular.

There is one plant in the Room. It lives in a stoppered glass cup on a shelf at level two: a knot of moss about the size of a thumbnail, kept in a few drops of cistern water, brought in by someone's great-grandmother from a valley that may or may not still have moss in it. It is watered on the ninth day. It has been alive longer than anyone currently in the Room. Nobody has decided whether it is a pet, a hostage, or a shrine.

Animals: in the cisterns, a population of small blind pale amphipods, three or four thousand of them, ancestry unknown, tolerated because they eat the biofilm that would otherwise need scrubbing. Children fish for them with wire and get told off. In the water films on the north wall, a rotifer and two tardigrade species, present but not observed. And in the summer months, on average nine days a year, a chough comes down through the slot.

The chough is the only large animal that ever enters. It arrives from nowhere, black and glossy with a red bill, walks about on the level-five railing with total unearned confidence, steals something, and leaves. There is an unwritten agreement that it is not to be interfered with. Its visits are logged in the same ledger as the ice-fall and the ticks. In one hundred and eighty years the ledger records four thousand and six chough-days. There is no reason to think it is the same bird. There is no reason to think it isn't.

---

**Sound.** Water, mostly. Four hundred threads at four hundred pitches, and residents can hear when one of them changes. Footsteps on wet stone. The clink of a shim box. The brace-hum after a tick. A door. Occasionally a voice, but low — people here talk quietly out of a habit older than any of them, because the Room is a hard-walled resonator and because loud sustained noise loosens ice, and everyone learns as a child what the chandelier weighs.

Otherwise: nothing. Long stretches of nothing. The wind that scours the ridge outside at seventy knots is audible as a pressure, a distant grain, but it does not come in. That is what the lid is for.

---

**What the place is doing.** It is not still, for all the quiet. In any given hour: the fog tide is coming up or going down; the west wall is starting or stopping its weep; the cisterns are filling; the sinks are pushing heat uphill; the Survey is walking; the talus is sorting itself by a stone or two; the mountain is rising by a fraction of a hair; forty-three people are turning screws to keep a town level on a floor that will not stop growing. The Room is a machine for staying the same shape inside something that refuses to.

And above all of it, six metres of raw sky, weeping down the walls, going grey, going blue, going grey.
