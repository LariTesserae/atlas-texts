## The Sill

The ground here is younger than the plants growing on it, and both are younger than the machines, and none of them make any noise.

Begin with the rock, because the rock is the newest thing. The Sill is a tableland about the size of a small county, tilted perhaps four degrees to the northwest, standing somewhere near twenty-six hundred metres. It is pale — a bone-coloured welded tuff, shot through with quartz seams that catch the light like wet salt — and it has not yet been worn. That is the first thing you notice with your body rather than your eyes: nothing is rounded. The joint blocks have the edges of broken porcelain. The scree at the foot of every blade of rock is angular, unsorted, still ringing-fresh, and it shifts under your boot with a dry clicking that is the loudest sound you will hear all day.

There are no valleys yet, only *splits* — fissures the width of a road and the depth of a cathedral, opened along the cooling joints, running parallel across the plateau like the lines of a ruled page. Where water gets into them it comes out the far side as a fall, and the falls have no plunge pools. They have not been here long enough to dig one. The water simply arrives at the bottom, shatters on unbroken stone, and goes up again as mist. Every cliff on the Sill wears a permanent standing veil at its base, a hem of returning water, and in that hem the light does something that takes getting used to.

## Light

The light is nearly the whole of the weather. The Sill stands above the cloud deck for most of the year, and the cloud deck is white, level, and vast — a floor of light extending to every horizon, brighter in places than the sky above it. So illumination comes from below as well as above. This means, in practice, that nothing on the Sill has a proper shadow. Objects have a faint doubled greyness on either side, a smudge, an apology for a shadow, and that is all.

You cannot look downward for long. The plateau's own pale rock throws back what the cloud sends up, and by mid-morning the air itself seems to be the source, so that the shortage of shade becomes a physical problem, a thing you plan your day around. Skin burns in twenty minutes. Unglazed glass yellows in a season. The few structures here have their windows on the *floor*, small and smoked, to admit the useful light from beneath and refuse the rest.

The plants have solved it their own way, and their solution is the strangest-looking thing about the place.

## The vegetation

It is lush — absurdly, upholstered-ly lush, given that there is no soil. There is instead moss: cushions of it a metre deep in the lee of every blade of rock, packed so tight you can stand on them and leave a print that stays for a week. Under the cushions the rock is bare and unweathered. The moss has not made soil; it has simply moved in and taken up residence on stone, a tenant with no interest in renovation.

Out of the moss, and out of the splits, comes the scrub: dwarf woody plants at knee height, wind-flattened, with leaves like small dark mirrors. Their colour is not green so much as an oiled near-black, and they are glossy on both faces, top and bottom, because both faces receive full sun. This is the double-lit flora. Nothing here has an underside. Turn over a leaf and find another leaf. Break open a stem and find the pith pigmented dark as a bruise, defending itself from light that comes through the plant's own body.

In the deeper splits, where the air holds still and the mist pools, the vegetation stacks itself in layers: a floor of liverworts, a storey of ferns whose crosiers are permanently half-open, a canopy of twisted dwarf trees at four metres, and then — the layer that unnerves visitors — beards of pale moss hanging from the *undersides* of the canopy and from the overhangs of the cliff, thriving on reflected light, growing downward toward the brightness below. Density here is total. You do not walk in a split; you insert yourself, and the vegetation closes behind you without a sound.

Water arrives almost never as rain. It arrives as fog, driven up the escarpment twice a day on the thermal turn, and as night condensation, and — three or four times a year — as a genuine soaking storm that everyone talks about afterward. The plants are built to drink from air. Every leaf is a collector, hair-fringed and drip-tipped; every moss cushion is a sponge that hoards its litre and releases it over a fortnight. Dawn on the Sill is a slow general dripping, the sound of ten thousand separate small events, none of them individually audible at more than an arm's length.

## The industry

And this, of course, is exactly what the machines are for.

Across the northwest third of the plateau, arranged along the contour of the prevailing upslope wind, stand the Strings: fog harps. Vertical wire arrays, each panel three metres tall and thirty long, wires spaced a finger's width apart, hung in ranks a hundred deep. From a distance they are invisible. From the side, at the right angle, they flare into being all at once — a mile of hairline fire — and then vanish as you keep walking. There are, by the surveyor's plate at the lift-house, four thousand and some panels. Nobody has recounted in a long time.

They have no moving parts. Fog blows through, water beads on wire, beads run down wire into a gutter, gutters run to a header, headers run to the penstocks at the lip of the escarpment, and the whole harvest of the plateau — some hundreds of thousands of litres on a good foggy day — drops two thousand metres through pipe to the pump-halls cut into the cliff below the cloud. Its fall powers the turbines that power the plateau. The place runs on nothing but the weight of its own collected mist.

So this is a fully industrial landscape: electrified, mechanised, scaled, purposeful, with an output measured in megalitres and a client somewhere down in the dry country that nobody up here has seen. And it is silent. Turbines are two kilometres below and inside rock. The harps are static. The air is thin and carries badly, and between the moss cushions and the wire ranks and the vegetation-packed splits, the whole plateau is anechoic — a landscape that swallows. Speak at a normal volume and the person four metres away sees your mouth move and hears a suggestion. Shout and you get almost nothing back: no echo, no carry, just a flat dead exertion, faintly embarrassing. Tenders here use hands. Their sign language has drifted so far from any other that it is effectively a local dialect, and its most-used gesture means *look, over there, slowly.*

## The Combers

There *are* moving machines. You have to watch to believe it.

Between the ranks of harps run gantries — the Combers — self-tracking, solar-plated, four metres wide, whose job is to strip fouled wire, re-tension sagging wire, and re-string broken wire. They travel at eleven millimetres a minute. This is not a limitation; it is the design. Fast machinery makes vibration, vibration knocks the beads off the wire before they run, and a harp that has been shaken yields nothing. So the Combers move at the pace of a growing thing. You can stand beside one and watch its manipulator arm select a wire and feel along it, and the motion is genuinely, physically imperceptible; the arm has simply moved, some minutes later, when you look back. Their bearings are wetted with a wax that keeps them from squeaking. Their contactors are magnetic and mute. Moss grows on their upper plates in the summer, and they are not cleaned, because the moss shades their casings and keeps their electronics cool.

The Combers also, quietly and without instruction, re-level the whole installation. The Sill is still rising — the field notes at the lift-house say thirty-one millimetres a year, uneven, tilting the plateau by a hair each decade. The gutters must stay true to a tenth of a degree or they will not run. So the machines spend most of their operating life correcting for geology, jacking a footing here, shimming a header there, in a slow argument with the mountain that neither side will win. The Sill pushes up; the Combers push back down; the water keeps running.

## Wireweed

In the older panels, something has happened that the original engineers did not plan for. There is a plant here — locally *wireweed* — a rush-relative that grows in straight unbranched dark filaments, a metre and a half tall, vertical, and almost exactly as thick as harp wire. It came up between the panels, then within them, and it collects fog with a fair fraction of the wires' efficiency. The Combers, whose sensors judge a string by tension and drip-rate rather than by material, service the wireweed alongside the steel: strip its fouling, cut its dead, and — this is the part that stops people — *string it*, weaving new filaments into gaps where wire has failed, because in the panels' logic a working element is a working element.

There are panels now, out at the northwest edge, that are entirely vegetable. They yield well. Nobody has decided whether to correct this. The question has been open for something like forty years, which is a normal length of time for a question here.

## Fauna

Almost nothing lives on the Sill, and what does lives quietly.

Grey wolf spiders in the moss, hunting by touch. A mute grouse-like bird, heavy and reluctant, that walks rather than flies and whose alarm response is to stand perfectly still against a lichen crust for up to an hour. Beetles in the pump-hall drainways whose larvae have never experienced darkness. Once or twice a year, high overhead, a skein of migrating geese crosses — visible as specks, inaudible.

The absence of birdsong is the thing that visitors comment on last, having spent their whole visit not knowing what was missing.

## The built

The structures are few and small and all of them are partial.

The lift-house at the lip: stone below, riveted plate above, with the winch drum and the surveyor's brass plate and a stove that is the only source of smoke on the plateau. A line of eleven cable pylons marching down into the cloud, of which the seventh is bent and has been bent for as long as records exist. Four tender-huts, of which two are roofed. A concrete pad and footings for something ambitious that was never built, now under a metre of moss and reading, to the eye, as a low green plinth. Cisterns. Cairns of angular waste rock. A wooden bench, silvered to the colour of the tuff, sited to face down-cloud at the place where, on the clear evenings, the light comes level across the top of the deck and the whole white floor turns to hammered copper for eleven minutes.

## Rules of the place

Nothing is old, and nothing wears out; the rock is too new, the erosion too slow, and the machines too gentle. Things here fail by being *colonised* instead. Leave a tool on the ground and the moss will have its handle by autumn.

Water never rests. There is no lake, no pond, no stream you could put a hand in. All the water on the Sill is either in the air, on a wire, in a pipe, inside a plant, or falling.

Sound does not travel, so nothing here is announced. Weather arrives as a change of brightness. Machines arrive as having-already-arrived. People arrive by being seen.

And once or twice a month, the cloud deck rises. It comes up over the escarpment in the space of an hour, and then the Sill is inside the light instead of above it — a white so total and so evenly sourced that there is no up, no down, no distance, and no visible outline to your own hands. The tenders call these the white hours. Nothing stops. The harps run their best yields of the year in that hour, streaming, every wire a thread of water, and somewhere in the whiteness the Combers go on at eleven millimetres a minute, and the only sound in the entire county is the collected drip of a plateau's worth of fog going quietly down a pipe toward a country that has none.
