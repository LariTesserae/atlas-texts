## The Warm Room

You come down to it through a shaft that was once a stair and is now a slope of its own rubble — brick, mostly, glazed brick, the edges of every piece rounded off like river stone though no river has ever run here. The rounding is the work of a hundred and forty years of condensate, one drop at a time, in the dark. Put your hand on the slope and it is warm as a flank. Twelve steps down you stop needing to guess where the room is, because you can hear it.

The sound arrives before the space does: a bass note somewhere near the bottom of hearing, not loud yet but occupying, the kind of sound that displaces thought the way water displaces air in a bottle. Six steps further and it has acquired an upper structure — a metallic shear, a hiss, and a long irregular pattering like applause heard through a wall. By the time the shaft opens out, the sound is loud enough that you would have to shout, and there is nobody to shout to, and you will not shout.

The room is not large. Nave-sized. You could throw a stone the length of it if you could see the far end, which you can't, quite. Barrel-vaulted in brick, thirty feet to the crown, the vault stained in three horizontal bands that record three separate centuries of waterline, though there has never been standing water above the ankle here except in the sump. The floor slopes four degrees toward that sump. Cast-iron grating over most of it, and over the grating a silt of rust flour, spore dust, and dead frond — a black-red loam, ankle-deep in the low corners, which the whole place has made out of itself, from itself, with nothing brought in and nothing taken out.

---

**The machine.** At the room's western third stands the reason for everything: a horizontal turbine on a cast bed, coupled to a generator whose windings were wrapped by hand by somebody paid by the hour. It is still turning. It has been turning since before the shaft collapsed. It draws its steam from a fissure below the floor — a geothermal tongue that the builders tapped and never had to feed, and it will go on turning until the fissure closes or the bearings gall, which the evidence suggests is a question of centuries rather than years. It loses about one revolution per minute per decade. There is no maintenance and no maintainer. There is only the long, slow, absolutely reliable running-down.

The machine is being buried where it stands. Calcite-rich condensate has been draping it at something under a millimetre a century, so that it now wears a hooded flowstone cape, translucent amber at the edges, with the original casting visible only in the places where heat keeps the stone from forming: two hot patches on the housing, polished bare and faintly gold, like the toe of a saint's statue where pilgrims touch. Everything about the machine's silhouette is now geology except those two spots of still-working metal.

**The weeping pipe.** All the water in the Warm Room comes from a single six-inch return line that runs the length of the vault at spring height, carrying something cold from somewhere deeper. It sweats. It has sweated forever. It is white with mineral to twice its diameter and hung with soda-straw stalactites the length of a forearm, and it drips at a rate governed by the machine's load cycle — which is where the applause comes from. When the turbine surges, the pipe cools, and for forty minutes it rains indoors: a hard, uneven, delicious drumming on the grating and on ten thousand leaves. Then it eases back to a drizzle. The full cycle takes about thirty-three hours. Nothing in the room does anything that is not tuned to those thirty-three hours.

**The light.** Dim, and orange, and old. Nine carbon-filament lamps in wire cages, wired to the generator, still burning at maybe a fifth of their design brightness because the voltage has drifted and nobody adjusted the rheostat. They give the room the colour of weak tea. Four are dead. One flickers on the surge. Add to this the phosphorescence of the gauge faces — radium paint on a bank of dials, its zinc sulphide long since burnt out to a corpse-green murk that no longer marks numbers but still, unmistakably, glows. And in high summer, for perhaps eleven days, a blade of true daylight enters through a broken flue at the east end and crosses the floor. The moss knows those eleven days. You can read them in the sward: a pale scorched stripe, an annual scar, the calendar of the room written in injury.

---

**The garden.** The air here is sour — thick with carbon dioxide off the fissure, laced with hydrogen sulphide, and hot, thirty-eight or forty degrees, humid to the point where sweat simply stops working. It is an atmosphere in which no animal can persist for long, and none does. There is no beetle in the loam, no midge in the light, no bat, no spider, not one worm. The absence is total and it is the most conspicuous fact about the place once you notice it: a jungle with no motion in it that is not water or machine.

And it *is* a jungle, of the older kind. Nothing here flowers, because nothing here could ever be pollinated. The Warm Room is a cryptogam kingdom — spores and runners, division and dispersal by shaking. Ferns dominate: a great coarse tongue-fern rooted in the silt over the grating, and above it a hanging species that grows *downward* from the vault joints in curtains eight feet long, fronds turned inward toward the machine rather than outward toward the lamps, because down here warmth is a better guide than light and the plants have long since agreed on this. Between them: liverworts in overlapping shingles, mosses in six or seven textures from bristle to velvet, and on the flowstone itself a black-green algal skin that squeaks faintly underfoot.

Densely packed, layer on layer, near-touching, so that walking the length of the room means parting things constantly with your forearms and getting a soaking from the stored rain in their crowns. Nothing bites you. Nothing scatters. That's the part that gets under your skin.

**The strange part, stated plainly.** The ferns release their spores on the bass note. Not incidentally — the sori split at a particular frequency, and the machine's fundamental is that frequency, and has been for a hundred and forty years, which is long enough for selection to do its work. The turbine is the garden's wind. It is also, functionally, its pollinator, its season, its clock, and its sun. Every spore in this room was shaken loose by a bearing turning in oil that has not been changed since the last war but one.

Which means the running-down is not neutral. As the machine sheds its revolution per decade, the fundamental drops. It has already fallen out of resonance with one species — you can find it, a fine bronze-coloured fern, still growing in vigorous sterile mats near the hot patches, producing nothing, reproducing only by creeping, having lost its future somewhere around 1970 without any change in its appearance whatsoever. Give the room four hundred more years and the rest will follow, one voice at a time, until the garden is entirely clonal, ancient, immense, and finished. It will look exactly as it looks now. That is the local definition of catastrophe: nothing visible happens.

---

**The marks.** People were here. Not many and not long. A foreman's cabin of riveted steel plate stands against the north wall, its door gone, its interior stripped to a bench, a boot-scraper, and a shelf on which one white porcelain cup sits welded into its own saucer by six inches of flowstone. Stencilled on the plate beside the doorway, still legible under the mineral glaze: **NO ENTRY WHILE ROLLING.** On the wall inside, a slate with a tally on it — chalk strokes in groups of five, forty-one groups, and a forty-second group of three.

The chalk is gone from the older strokes; they are grooves now, etched where the calcium of the chalk changed how the slate weathered. The last three strokes are also grooves. Everything in this room is dated by the flowstone, and by the flowstone the last three strokes are not old. They are perhaps thirty years old. The rest are a hundred and thirty.

There is a rational explanation. Dissolution patterns imitate a great many things. The room is full of coincidences of that sort — a moss cushion in the exact shape of the pressure gradient across the housing, a hanging fern that has grown into a passable profile of a man at the sump edge, the flickering lamp that flickers on the surge and therefore appears, if you have been down here nine or ten hours, to be answering you.

**How to be here.** The rules are few. Do not go into the sump; the gas pools there and the water is at 71 degrees. Do not linger past the second rain; the CO₂ takes your judgement before it takes your breath and it takes your judgement gently, as a kindness. Do not touch the two gold patches on the machine, though you will want to, badly, because they are the only part of the world down here that is being kept clean by work. Do not expect anything to move. Do not mistake the loudness for company.

And when you climb the rubble slope again and the bass note thins out behind you into a hum and then a pressure and then nothing, understand that it did not stop. It has not stopped since before your grandmother. It is down there now, in the dark, at forty degrees, making rain on a hundred thousand leaves that no creature will ever eat, shaking loose spores in a shrinking chord, one revolution slower every ten years, patient as stone, and entirely uninterested in whether you were ever in the room.
