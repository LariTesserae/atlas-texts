## The Shelf at Aunhal

There is a wall of grey-blue schist that runs for a distance nobody has measured, and about two-thirds of the way up it, where the rock has spalled and slumped over some unthinkable span of time, there is a single green ledge.

It is not large. Sixty paces long, twenty at its widest, tapering at both ends into wet ribs of stone where nothing can stand. A person walking it slowly, stopping to look at things, uses up an afternoon. You could shout from one end and be heard at the other — though you would have to shout, and you would hear yourself come back four seconds later off the buttress across the gulf, thinned and stripped of consonants, as if the mountain had considered your words and returned only their vowels.

Everything about the place is small except what surrounds it, which is nothing at all.

---

**The rock.** Schist, foliated, splitting in wet plates the colour of a mussel shell, with mica in it that flares white when the sun comes at a low slant so that for ten minutes each morning the whole cliff appears to be very slowly boiling. The ledge itself is a fault-terrace: a bed of harder stone that resisted while everything above it went, over uncountable centuries, downward into the cloud. Its floor is broken, not flat — steps, basins, folded lips of rock, all of it rounded now, the sharpness long gone out of it. This is a landscape that finished becoming itself a long time ago and has been maintaining that condition ever since, quietly, with water.

Over the schist, in the wet places, a second stone has grown. The spring water here is lime-heavy, and where it seeps and drips it leaves calcite behind: pale tufa in curtains, in fluted organ-pipes, in bulbous swags along the back wall like melted candle-fat. Moss grows into the tufa and the tufa grows over the moss, so that whole cushions have turned to stone with their green still living on the outside. Put your hand against the back wall and you touch something between a cave formation and a hedge. It is cold, and it gives slightly, and it is wet.

**The water.** A fall arrives from above — you cannot see its source, only that at some point up there in the brightness a river leaves the mountain. It does not land on the ledge. It passes fifteen paces out from the lip, a white rope forty feet across that has already been half-shredded by its own descent, and it goes on down past the ledge into the cloud floor without ever, so far as anyone here has witnessed, reaching a bottom. That is the first strangeness of Aunhal, and it is not really a strangeness, only a fact of scale: the fall becomes mist before it becomes a river again. Two miles of falling and it simply stops being water.

What the ledge gets is the shred. Spray drifts in on every eddy, so the air is always faintly wet, and the rock always dark, and every horizontal surface holds its own small sky in a puddle. There are also true springs: the mountain's water table cut open by the terrace, weeping from a seam along the back wall in a dozen places, one of them strong enough to be called a stream for the eleven paces it exists before going over the edge.

And there is the second water, which goes the wrong way. In late morning the sun heats the cliff face and the air begins to climb it, and everything loose in the mist column reverses — drizzle rises past you, seed-fluff rises, spider-silk rises, whole veils of droplets ascending the wall at walking speed, so that you can stand at the lip in what any body would call rain and feel it on the *underside* of your chin. The tufa curtains on the back wall have a faint upward flare at their bottom edges. They have been recording this for ten thousand years.

**The sound.** It is loud here. Not intermittently — continuously, structurally loud, the way a forge or a weir is loud. The fall is the floor of it, a broadband roar that varies with the wind but never drops out. Above that, and this is the surprise, the birds.

Aunhal is a bird city. The tufa pockets and the schist splits hold a colony of some ten thousand pairs of a grey bird with rust under the wing — the same bird that swings in screaming ribbons around the buttresses at dusk, whose voice is a thin metallic shriek and which produces, ten thousand at once, a sound like a nail being drawn very slowly across slate, sustained for hours. Swifts thread through them, higher, faster, silent by comparison. Choughs work the ledge itself, walking about on red legs with an air of proprietorship, making a call exactly like a stone dropped into a bucket.

And at dusk, out of the moss cushions, the frogs. They are the size of a thumbnail, brown, with a pale line down the spine, and they live their whole lives in the water held between moss stems, in a wet that never dries because the mist never stops. Each one produces a click. Fifty thousand of them produce a sheet of sound with no gaps in it, a shimmering seethe that sits on top of the waterfall's roar without mixing, and continues until the temperature drops through some threshold near midnight, at which point they all stop within about a minute, and the silence that isn't silence — just the fall, just the wind in the notch — feels like an enormous door closing.

The wind in the notch: at the eastern taper there is a slot in the rib, a hand's-breadth wide and twelve feet deep, which sounds a low tone when the westerly runs. It is not always in tune with itself. It has two tones and they beat against each other.

**The green.** Cool and constantly wet and constantly lit — nothing here grows tall, but everything grows thick. Moss in mounded cushions a foot deep, twenty species of it, some of them soft as fur and some of them like wet coral. Ferns out of every crack, filmy ones in the shade of the tufa whose fronds are one cell thick and translucent. A low woody shrub with small leathery leaves and a flower like a purple bell, forming an impenetrable knee-high thicket over the ledge's middle third — the only thing here that could be called a canopy, and you could look over it. Sedges. Sundews on the seep-flushes, catching the mites. Lichen on everything the moss has not taken: crusts, scabs, grey rosettes, and long hair-lichens that hang from the shrub twigs and stream sideways all day in the updraft.

Also one tree. A single stunted juniper-ish thing in the wind-shadow at the western end, trunk thicker than its height, bark ropy and silvered, maybe fifteen hundred years old, maybe four hundred, nobody can tell with these. It is dead on the windward third and alive on the rest, which is a normal condition for such a tree and not a tragedy.

**Nothing large.** This is the ledge's fundamental rule, and it explains almost everything about the ecology. Nothing large can *arrive*. There is no path down, no path up; the cliff above overhangs, the cliff below is smooth for a thousand feet. Everything living on Aunhal either flew here, was carried here as a seed or a spore or an egg on a bird's foot, or is descended from something that did. So: a hundred and forty species of invertebrate, thirty-one of bird, the frogs, one shrew-like thing that came up nobody knows how and hunts the frogs and is found nowhere else, a pair of falcons who kill in the void and eat on the eastern rib and have made the rib white. No grazing animal. No large predator. Nothing bigger than a raven with its feet on the ground.

Hence the emptiness, which is the strangest quality of the place and the hardest to convey. The ledge is *crowded* — biomass everywhere, sound everywhere, motion everywhere. And three paces out from the lip there is nothing whatsoever, for a vertical distance and a horizontal distance both so large that they read as the same distance, which is to say: void. The nearest other green ledge is visible on clear afternoons on the far buttress, and it might as well be a star. Two dense small things and a gulf. The density of Aunhal, measured over any honest volume, is zero.

**The marks of hands.** People came. Not many and not recently.

There is a rope. Or the tail of one: forty feet of three-strand cord of twisted plant fibre, grey and hard, fused into the tufa at the back wall where the drip has been running over it for long enough to make it part of the rock. It goes up into the overhang and stops. It cannot bear anything now.

There is a shelter, if you can call it that: a dry-laid wall of schist plates, chest-high, built into the angle of a boulder, roofed once with something that is gone. Inside, a hearth-scoop with charcoal in it — a lens of black three inches thick, which means a great many fires — and the charcoal is juniper and bird-bone. There are four of these shelters along the ledge, all of the same build, all facing away from the fall.

There is a cistern, cut into a basin in the schist to deepen and square it, the pick-marks still legible under the calcite glaze, with a lip and a channel to lead the seep in and a smaller channel to lead the overflow out. It works perfectly. It is full of clean cold water and three species of frog.

There are fowling poles: two of them, wedged in a crack, ash or something like it, each twelve feet long, one still carrying a scrap of net at the tip. There is a coil of gut line. There are bone gorges — the simplest fish-hook there is, a splinter sharpened at both ends — though there are no fish within two miles vertically, so they were for birds. There are stone flakes everywhere in the hearth-middens, and no metal anywhere at all.

And there are the stacks. Nine of them, on the eastern rib among the falcon-whitened stone: columns of flat schist plates, waist-high, carefully balanced, each with a bird's skull set in a gap near the top. They are weathered into their own stability; the mosses have welded the lower plates. They face out over the void, and they are placed with a regularity that says the ninth was built to answer the eighth. Whether they were a count, a calendar, a grave-marker, a prayer, or a boundary is not recoverable. The birds land on them.

**The day.** Dawn is cold and clear and comes up from below as much as above, because the cloud floor lights before the sky does, and for a while the underside of every overhang is glowing and every drop of water on every moss-stem is a small lamp. Then the mica boils. Then the colony wakes, all at once, and the noise goes up by an order of magnitude and stays there. Mid-morning the updraft starts and the rain reverses. Around noon the cloud floor begins to rise — it comes up the cliff like a tide, and for two or three hours Aunhal is inside a white room with a roaring in it and visibility of nine feet, everything dripping, the frogs briefly encouraged into daylight singing. Mid-afternoon it clears from the top down. Long gold light, hair-lichen streaming, the falcons hunting the swift-ribbons. Then dusk, the shriek-storm at its peak, the frogs coming on underneath it. Then the frogs stopping. Then frost, most nights of the year, a thin one, gone by the second hour of sun, and the tufa growing another few molecules thicker.

**The last rule.** Anything that goes off the edge is gone. Not destroyed — gone, removed from the account, transferred to the void, which does not return things. Bones, seeds, plates of stone, fledglings that misjudge, whole moss cushions when the frost undercuts them. Aunhal loses a little of itself every day and has done for as long as it has existed. It stays because the water keeps bringing lime, and the birds keep bringing nitrogen and seeds and eggs and mites and dirt on their feet, and the mountain keeps weeping, and the balance of these two — the falling-away and the flying-in — has been almost exactly even for a very long time.

That is what "established" means, here. Not that nothing happens. Everything happens, constantly, loudly. Only that the total is unchanged.
