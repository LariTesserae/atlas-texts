## The Loud Garden

### Terrace Nine, west shoulder of the mountain called Adze

You come up onto it out of the scree and the first thing you notice is that nothing is making any noise at all.

The sky is nearly black at the zenith, a bruised violet down toward the horizon, and the sun is a small hard white coin that throws shadows with edges like cut paper. The air — there is air, technically, a breath of nitrogen and argon at about the pressure of a good vacuum flask — carries nothing. Not the crunch of your own boots, not your breathing, not the tick of the cooling regulator at your hip. You walk into an absolute hush across a floor of pale glittering sand, and then you kneel and put your palm flat on the rock, and the mountain screams into your wrist bones so hard you fall over.

That is the introduction. Everyone gets it. It's in the training and it happens anyway.

### The engine, presumed

Somewhere under the terrace — eleven hundred metres down, judging by the phase lag, though the surveys disagree and always have — something old is still running. It is not a reactor in any sense the word usually carries. It processes something, and like every processor it must shed the waste of processing, and on a world with no ocean, no weather worth the name, and an atmosphere too thin to convect a candle flame, there is only one channel left open: it can radiate, or it can vibrate.

It vibrates. It has been vibrating for a length of time that the shard-drifts, laid down in fatigue laminae like tree rings, put at somewhere over nine million years.

So the mountain is a bell that never stops. The energy pours out through the rock as compression, shear, surface waves; it arrives at the terrace as a chaotic standing field, one hundred and sixty to two hundred and ten decibels in the stone, restructuring itself continuously as the deep source drifts and stutters. The upshot is that the ground here is loud beyond any reasonable meaning of the word, and the terrace is *cold* — measurably colder than the plains below, a persistent minus-forty, because the engine is exporting its heat as noise and the noise is going somewhere else. This place is the outside of a refrigerator. The rock hums because it is being cooled.

### The garden

Nothing here drinks. There is no liquid on the terrace, no ice, no dew, no chemistry of the wet sort at all. What grows here grows by being shaken.

The fronds — the standard term, though they are nothing like leaves — are silica. They are grown, not deposited; the growth tips are piezoelectric, and they take dust from the sand and stack it molecule by molecule wherever the local strain field tells them to, which means they grow *toward loudness*, up the acoustic gradient, into the antinodes. A mature stand reaches four or five metres. The stems are hollow, drawn thin as a wineglass rim, ribbed at intervals for stiffness, and they fan at the top into sheets a few hundred nanometres thick that break the white sunlight into oil-slick colours: peacock, bronze, a green that goes to magenta as you move your head. There are thousands of them. They stand in crowds and thickets, tier upon tier up the terrace's back wall, and the light comes through them layered and shivering, and the word for it is *lush*. It is a rainforest made of drinking glasses, in a vacuum, at forty below.

They are also constantly dying. This is the other half of the terrace and the more important half. A frond grows into the loudest place it can find, and the loudest place it can find eventually exceeds its fatigue limit, and it fails — usually at a rib, usually all at once. On any given afternoon you will see a dozen collapses. There is no sound. A five-metre tower of iridescent glass simply becomes a slow-motion chandelier falling apart in the low gravity, sheets peeling and turning and catching the sun, and it goes down into the drifts and the drifts take it, and within nine or ten hours new tips are up through the wreck of it, feeding on the changed field the collapse left behind. The whole garden turns over roughly twice a season. Nothing here is old except the sand and the machine.

The sand is the garden's ancestors, ground finer by nine million years of shaking. It is knee-deep at the terrace lip and forty metres deep at the back wall, and it moves — not in wind, there is no wind, but in acoustic creep, whole dunes migrating upslope against gravity toward the antinodes, which is one of the several things about this place that visitors take badly.

### The empty part

Here is the thing that photographs never convey: the terrace is *hollow*. Three hundred and some metres across, bounded east and west by drop-offs into thinner rock where the ringing doesn't carry and nothing grows, and inside those boundaries the volume is essentially unoccupied. The stands of glass are widely spaced — twenty, thirty metres between thickets — because the antinodes are widely spaced, and between them is nothing. Not bare ground: *nothing*. Vacuum, sunlight, and the enormous silence of the air. You can stand in the middle of the Loud Garden with your arms out and touch nothing at all in any direction, and hear nothing, and if you keep your feet still you could believe the whole place is a photograph.

Then you touch a stem, and the stem is playing the engine directly into your skeleton, and you understand that the emptiness is the emptiness of the inside of an instrument.

### Fauna

Abundant. Absurdly so, once you learn to look at the right scale.

**Nodelings.** Palm-sized, armoured in the same drawn silica as the fronds, six legs, no eyes worth the name and an exquisite sense for strain. They live in the nulls — the moving quiet spots in the standing field — and because the field is turbulent the nulls slide and vanish and reopen, so a nodeling spends its entire existence sprinting. You see them going flat-out across open sand for no visible reason, stopping dead, holding, then bolting again. They are running from loudness. In the nulls they graze the sand for organics. When a null closes on one it dies instantly and messily, and the sand around the loudest stones is littered with them, and this is the terrace's principal soil-forming process.

**Sifters.** Dog-sized, low, with paired hammer-limbs and a mouthfield of ceramic combs. They eat glass. They walk up to a mature frond, brace, and break it — deliberately, at the ribs — and then process the fragments. They are the destruction the garden depends on; where sifters are absent the stands grow into monstrous overloaded cathedrals and come down all at once in cascades that take out half a hectare. Sifters have no interest in you and will lean on you like furniture.

**The chandelier.** Above the three or four hottest stones, where the amplitude is highest, the thin air is driven so hard that it can hold things up. There you find a shifting cloud of levitated grains, seed-tips, dust, moulted nodeling shell, and a species of animal the size of a lentil that has abandoned the ground entirely — thousands of them, hovering in nested layers at the pressure nodes, spinning slowly, catching the light. It is the single most beautiful thing on the mountain and it exists because the air is too thin to be anything but violent.

**Hushes.** Rare. Larger. Nobody has a good image. A hush is an animal that produces cancellation: it generates an anti-field with its own body and drags a pocket of silence with it. Plants inside the pocket lose the strain that feeds them and die within minutes; nodelings inside it lose all sense of where the world is and simply stop. Hushes eat both. You do not detect a hush coming. You detect a hush by the fact that the ground has gone quiet, and if you have any sense at all, you leave.

### Marks and remains

Nothing large was ever built here. What's on the terrace:

Nine pylons, spaced irregularly along the back wall, four metres, tapered, of a grey material that has not weathered in nine million years while the rock around it has retreated half a metre. They are not part of the engine. They are older than the engine's ecology and younger than the engine. They are extraordinarily silent — the acoustic field goes *around* them, and each pylon casts a null-shadow a few metres long which the nodelings use as a permanent refuge and highway. There are seven distinct nodeling species that exist nowhere but in the shadows of the pylons.

One dome, low, buried to its shoulders in migrating shard-sand, three metres across, no seam, no door. It rings at a frequency well below the engine's, on its own schedule, roughly every twenty-two hours. Nobody knows.

A cairn of thirty-one stones on the eastern lip, tuned — each stone dressed until it took a specific pitch. Human work, four centuries old, a survey team's joke that turned into a memorial and then into a shrine. You put your forehead to the top stone. You get a chord.

A single boot, upright, glassed over by frond-growth into a solid iridescent lump the size of a bread loaf.

A rectangle of dead sand forty metres by forty at the terrace's centre where nothing has grown for as long as records go back, and the field reads flat and normal, and the reason is unknown, and the sifters walk around it.

### Rules

Never touch two stands at once. The phase difference between two antinodes is enough to break a wrist, and has.

Ground contact is cumulative. Twelve minutes total per day, boots included. There are worse mountains and none that will deafen you through your feet.

Do not stand still in a null. The null is moving; you are not.

Do not shout. Not because it carries — nothing carries — but because the fronds will find you. Anything that puts energy into the rock is a growth gradient, and a warm body kneeling on stone is a small, slow, delicious anomaly. Camp overnight in the same spot and you will wake ringed by new tips, three centimetres high, all leaning inward.

Take nothing. Not because of the usual reasons. Because a frond removed from the field stops being fed and begins, within about an hour, to come apart into powder, and the powder is very fine, and it does not stop being piezoelectric, and you will be finding the Loud Garden in your equipment and your lungs and your quiet rooms for the rest of your life.
