## The Rise

### 1. What it is, from far enough away to be wrong about it

From the low country — if there is low country; nobody has walked out of the interior to confirm it — the range shows as a bank of dark against a sky the colour of a banked fire. It does not have a skyline so much as a raggedness. You watch it for an hour and the raggedness changes. Not with cloud. The teeth themselves come and go.

It is continental. It has its own weather, its own tides of warm air, its own seasons that do not correspond to any orbit. But saying *continental* undersells it, because a continent is a fixed quantity of ground, and this is not. The interior manufactures more interior. Walk east for eleven days at a steady pace and you are further from the eastern edge than when you began, and you feel this not as a horror but as a mild administrative disappointment, the way you'd feel about a queue.

The whole mass is going up. Not slowly.

### 2. The rock, which is new

Everything here is fresh out of the mold, and you can see the mold's grain on it.

The ridges are sharp in a way natural ridges are not. Arêtes with edges like a struck flake of obsidian, running for forty kilometres without a single rounded shoulder. Faces that are flat — genuinely flat, ruled-surface flat, with faint parallel striations across them like the marks a broach leaves in a bore. There are seams. Long raised welts of rock run up the mountainsides where two halves of something were brought together and the excess squeezed out and never trimmed. Flashing, a machinist would call it. There is flashing on these mountains three metres high and a kilometre long, brittle, translucent at the crest, ringing faintly if a stone taps it.

The scree is unspeakable to walk on. Every fragment is a fresh edge. Nothing has had time to be tumbled. You do not slide on this scree; you are filleted by it.

And in the rock, everywhere once you learn to see them: the inclusions.

### 3. The works

They are not buildings. Nothing here was built. That is the first thing to be clear about, because the eye keeps insisting otherwise.

Set into the young stone, at all scales, are mechanisms. A flywheel eight metres across, half-emerged from a cliff face, its rim polished, its spokes still bedded in matrix. A column of pistons ascending a gully like a vertebral column, each one rising and falling on a stroke of about ninety seconds. Gear teeth outcropping along a ridge — you take them for a natural crest of upturned strata until you count them and find the spacing exact. Bearings the size of ponds, seated in their races, holding a slow turn. Bundles of what can only be cable, mineral cable, thousands of stranded filaments of a grey metallic fibre, running out of one hillside and into another, taut, faintly warm to the touch.

None of them are attached to anything you could call an apparatus. There is no factory. There is no floor plan. They are distributed through the rock the way garnets are distributed through schist, and like garnets they seem to be a natural product of the conditions — a mineral phase that this world falls into under pressure. Where you find one you will find more, in the same orientation, at the same grade.

They are working. That is the part that takes some sitting with. They are turning, stroking, tensioning, drawing current — there is current; the cable bundles have a field on them that makes the hair on your forearm stand up when you pass within a metre — and what they are producing is the mountain range.

This is not uplift. Nothing is folding. Nothing is being thrust over anything. Material is being *extruded*. On the high shoulders, where the vegetation thins, you can find the working faces: a die-slot a hundred metres wide from which new ridge is emerging at the speed of a slow escalator, still soft at the lip, hardening within a body-length of daylight, already carrying the striations of its passage. You can put your palm on new mountain and feel it move under you. It is warm, of course. Everything here is warm — blood-warm at the surface, uncomfortably warmer in the seams and the ducts, so that the whole range breathes a moist thermal upward and generates its own ceiling.

### 4. Light, air, water

The ceiling is why it is dim.

Cloud sits on the Rise permanently, a metre or a thousand metres above the canopy, lit from above and behind by whatever the sun is. The light that gets through is orange-brown and even, a light with no direction in it, the light inside a kiln that is cooling. Shadows are weak and multiple. Noon and evening are matters of small degree. Nothing here glitters, because nothing is lit brightly enough from any one side to glitter.

The air is thick with water that will not become water. This is the strangest deprivation of the place: you are constantly wet and constantly thirsty. Everything is beaded. The undersides of leaves carry droplets, cables carry droplets, your eyelashes carry droplets, and none of it runs. Surface tension is stronger here, or evaporation is more eager, or the world has simply not yet worked out drainage — there are no rivers. There are no streams. There are, in the folds and low seats of the terrain, occasional pockets: pools of standing condensate, brown-clear, held in stone basins that they did nothing to carve, and around each pool the ecology thickens into a rioting collar of green and the animals come in shifts, silently, in numbers.

Mist moves upward here more often than down. It is a heated range; the convection is all inhalation. Standing on a ridge you watch the cloud pouring *up* the valleys towards its ceiling, and the effect is of the world quietly refilling its own lungs.

### 5. The green

The vegetation is a finish coat.

New rock is bare for perhaps a day. By the second day it has a felt of something like liverwort. By the fourth there are stems. By the tenth it is closed canopy, and the canopy is twenty metres deep and layered five or six tiers thick, and every tier is growing on the tier below it rather than on the ground, because there is no ground. Root mats span the gaps between ridges. You walk on a woven floor with nothing beneath it, and here and there a gap shows you a hundred metres of dim green space going down, hung with more of everything.

It is lush but it is not diverse in the way an old forest is diverse. It is lush the way a fever is hot. Everything is soft-tissued, fast, provisional, running on condensate and warmth and the mineral flash off the new stone. Leaves are enormous and thin and hang limp with the weight of their own dew. Colours run to dark bottle-green, liver-red, a bruised violet in the lower tiers where the light is more idea than fact. Fruiting is continuous and casual; things drop and rot and are replaced within the week.

Nothing here is old. If you find a great trunk, three metres through, it will be nine months old and hollow and full of water and insects.

### 6. The abundance, and its silence

There is an immense amount of animal here, and it is nearly all quiet.

The dense air swallows sound; the moss and the leaf-felt swallow what's left; the machinery runs at frequencies below hearing, so that the dominant acoustic experience of the Rise is a pressure in the sinuses and a faint buzz in the fillings of the teeth. Above that floor there is almost nothing. Wingbeat. The tick of dew concentrating and dropping onto a leaf below. A snap, far off, of flashing breaking under its own weight. Long minutes of literally nothing at all, in a forest where fifty animals are within thirty metres of you.

They have not needed calls. Nothing here has been around long enough to invent a call. They signal by scent, by canopy-shake, by the electrical field they carry off the cable-seams, and they move through the tiers in silent, coordinated masses like weather.

Two guilds are worth naming, because they organise everything else.

**The trimmers.** Low, broad, armoured, moving in herds of thousands across the bare high shoulders. Their mouthparts are grinding plates. They eat flashing. They eat burr, mold-line, the sharp lip of new extrusion, the brittle overspill at a die-slot. They are the erosion of this world — there is no other. Wind does not have time here; water will not run; frost never comes to a range this warm. So all the rounding that ever happens to these mountains happens in the guts of a slow shuffling animal the size of a cow. Where the herds have passed, a ridge has a soft edge and a dusting of white grit and looks, briefly, like it belongs to an older planet. Behind them the extruders keep pushing out fresh sharpness and they turn around and start again.

**The gaunts.** Tall, thin, four-legged, hairless, moving alone through the mid-canopy with a careful high step. They are duplicate-eaters. This requires explanation.

### 7. The rules

The Rise is being drafted, and drafts have revisions.

Because the range is manufactured rather than grown, the process makes mistakes and does not always clear them. The commonest artefact is the **twin**: two identical ridges, side by side, parallel, matching down to the individual lichen and the individual sleeping animal. One of them is the accepted version. The other has not yet been withdrawn. You may camp on either. If you camp on the wrong one, you will wake in the same place on the other, with your gear correctly arranged and one small item — a buckle, a tooth — slightly differently worn, and you will not be able to say which of you woke.

The gaunts eat the surplus. They go along the discarded ridge, delicately, and take things out of it, and the ridge thins and greys and by evening the valley between the twins has closed and there was only ever one. They are performing a service. They are also, unmistakably, feeding.

Other rules, offered practically:

**Elevation is negotiated, not measured.** The Rise gains height while you ascend it. A summit approached is a summit deferred. You reach summits only by not intending to; the range appears to be sensitive to being climbed, and much less sensitive to being wandered.

**Objects have no back-history.** Pick up a stone here and hold it and try to imagine where it was last year. You will fail, and the failure has a specific taste to it, like biting foil. It was not anywhere last year. It was made at the moment your hand closed, to spec, complete with plausible wear. This is fine. Everything is fine. Do not follow the thought further than three steps or you will find that it applies to your hand.

**Repetition pockets.** In certain hollows the same span of afternoon runs again, four or five times, before releasing. Animals in these hollows are fat and calm. The dew never quite falls. You will notice you are in one only around the third pass, when you recognise the exact shape of a cloud you have already resented.

**Nothing has been built.** No wall, no cairn, no shaped stick, no worn path other than the ones the trimmer herds grind out and abandon. The mechanisms are not artefacts; they are geology. There is no evidence anywhere on the Rise that anything ever intended anything. This is either enormously restful or the single most disturbing fact about the place, and which one it is for you will change three or four times a day.

### 8. Standing there

Late in what functions as the day, on a shoulder at maybe four thousand metres, warm rain-that-isn't hanging in the air and beading on everything, the orange ceiling low enough to feel like a lid:

Below you the canopy goes out for as far as the murk allows, tier on tier, dark and glistening, moving here and there with animals you cannot see and will not hear. Ahead of you a die-slot is running, a hundred metres of new ridge easing out of the rock at walking-pace-divided-by-a-thousand, its lip still soft, and the trimmer herd is already up against it, thousands of grinding plates working in complete silence, and behind them a fuzz of green is closing over the ground they have worked, so fast you can almost watch it.

Under your boots, through the stone, the pistons are going. You can't hear them. You can feel them in the joints of your jaw, a patient reciprocating pressure, ninety seconds a stroke, and there is no chimney, no smoke, no operator, no purpose stated anywhere.

The mountains are being made. They are being made faster than anything can wear them down, faster than anything can settle on them, faster than the world can decide whether it meant them. Nothing is old. Nothing is finished. It is warm, and it is quiet, and it is not going to stop.
