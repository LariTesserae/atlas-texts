## The Uplift

Begin with the rock, because the rock is the youngest thing here.

It comes up cold and glassy, black with a blue sheen in the fractures, still exhaling the hydrogen and sulfur of its making. In places you can lay a hand flat on a slab and feel it flex — not tremble, *flex*, the way a sheet of ice flexes over a lake, because the whole plateau is a floating plate of new crust being pushed from beneath at the pace of a fingernail's growth. Four thousand metres up and still rising. There are no valleys. There are no gullies, no scree fans, no rounded shoulders, none of the softening that time and falling water are supposed to do to a mountain. Every edge is an edge. Every fracture is a fresh fracture with a cutting lip. The landscape has the look of something that was broken five minutes ago and has not yet been allowed to settle.

It will not be allowed to settle. That is the first thing to understand about this place: water falls on it constantly, in quantities that should have carved it into canyons a thousand times over, and the water never touches the stone.

## The Loom

Everything above the rock is alive.

Not "covered in vegetation." Not "forested." The distinction matters and it takes a while to see. From a distance — if you could get a distance, which you cannot, because the thing is continental and there is no outside — from above, through a gap in the permanent cloud deck, you would see a surface the colour of wet iron and old blood, matte, absorbing, textured like a brain or a lung, folded into ridges that follow no drainage logic at all because there is no drainage. That surface is between forty and two hundred metres thick, and beneath it is rock, and there is nothing in between. No soil. Not a gram. Soil is what happens when the dead of a place accumulate somewhere the living cannot reach, and here there is nowhere the living cannot reach.

Push into it and the layers declare themselves.

**The Hem.** The uppermost two metres, where light arrives. The blades here are broad and nearly black, pigmented to the edge of absolute — they take in something above ninety-nine percent of what falls on them, across a spectral range far wider than any leaf has business using. This is why the world below is dim. The dimness is not weather and not latitude; the dimness is *appetite*. Light does not so much filter through the Hem as leak, in the reluctant way that heat leaks from a good vessel. The blades sweat continuously. They do not have stomata in any recognizable arrangement; they have vents, actively driven, and they push aerosol upward in fine grey plumes that nucleate the cloud deck. The cloud is not a thing the place lives under. It is a thing the place exhales, on purpose, to keep itself cool and wet and shaded, and it has been maintained without a break for longer than the rock beneath it has existed.

**The Tangle.** Below that, thirty to a hundred and eighty metres of interpenetrating structure so dense that "space" becomes a theoretical quantity. Stems the diameter of a thigh, of a wrist, of a hair, all cross-braced, all fused where they touch — and they touch everywhere. There are no individuals. There is no way to say where one organism stops. Cut a stem and the sap that comes out is under such pressure that it hisses; cut it again a hundred kilometres away and it hisses at the same pressure, because the whole plateau is one hydraulic continuity, one enormous column of water standing up inside living tissue against gravity, held there by mechanisms that do not appear in any physics of capillarity you would recognize. The water table of this world is not underground. It is at chest height, and at shoulder height, and at four hundred metres, and it is inside things.

The Tangle is warm. This is the strangest sensation the place offers to a body used to mountains. Outside, at the Hem, the air sits a few degrees above freezing and hoarfrost silvers the blade-edges every night. Twenty metres down, it is the temperature of a cool room, and the warmth is metabolic — the slow enormous respiration of a mass the size of a continent, losing heat so grudgingly that it functions as its own climate. The air smells of crushed stems, of ozone, of wet mineral, of something faintly like almonds that is probably a signal rather than a byproduct.

**The Piers.** And here is the part that changes the category of the place. Where the Tangle meets rock, it does not sit on rock. It has *grown* rock. Root-analogues that are more properly described as chemical tools press into the basalt and take it apart at the scale of individual crystal faces — selectively, choosing silicon, rejecting iron, leaving behind etch-pits so precise they diffract. And then, from what they have taken, they lay down fibre: hollow mineral tubing, aragonite and something not-quite-opal, extruded in helices, in trusses, in load-bearing arcs that spring across fracture voids in perfect three-hinged form. The piers are grown stone. They are what the tissue stands on and they are the reason the plateau does not erode.

Water falls on this world by the cubic kilometre. It is intercepted at the Hem, drunk, passed hand to hand downward through living channels, used, transpired, re-condensed, used again, and what finally reaches the rock does so through mineral tubing, at controlled pressure, saturated with silica and already giving up its load to the next generation of pier. The rain has not had a free surface in a hundred million years. It has not been permitted to *fall* in the sense of falling freely, or to *flow* in the sense of finding its own path. Erosion here is not slow. Erosion has been abolished, and the mountains stay raw and knife-edged and embryonic underneath a living roof, like an unfinished sculpture kept under a wet cloth.

## The Shout

Nothing prepares you for the noise.

Stand anywhere on the plateau — and there is nowhere else to stand, the plateau is nine million square kilometres — and the sound sits on you like weather. It is not a roar exactly. A roar is broadband and stupid. This has structure. It has parts, and once you can hear the parts you cannot un-hear them.

The floor of it is water: sheet-flow over a hundred billion square metres of blade, ten thousand tonnes a second somewhere in the immediate acoustic neighbourhood, no channels to concentrate it into the familiar rhythm of a river, just the vast undifferentiated shhhh of rain being handled.

Over that, percussion. The tissue grows fast — visibly, centimetres a day in the frontier zones — and growth at that speed is not silent. Turgor builds until a node lets go with a crack like a rifle. Multiply by every square metre. It is a continuous popcorn hail of small structural failures and small structural triumphs, and it comes from all directions, and it never stops.

Over that, the venting. The aerosol plumes leave the Hem through apertures at high pressure, and the apertures are shaped — deliberately, functionally shaped — into resonators. Each one produces a tone. There are perhaps a trillion of them, tuned across four octaves, and they open and close on cycles driven by humidity and cloud thickness and hydraulic pressure and the light budget. The result is a chord the width of a continent that shifts over hours. In the dawn hour, when the cloud deck thins and light debt comes due, the whole plateau climbs a semitone and holds it, and the standing waves that form in that held note are strong enough that mist gathers along the acoustic nodes in visible sheets, hanging in the air in stripes, forty metres apart, motionless, while everything else moves.

And under all of it, felt rather than heard, the cavitation. Water held up four hundred metres inside tissue is water under tension, and tension fails. Every failure is a snap in the ultrasonic, and the sum of them is a hiss at the top of hearing and a pressure in the teeth. Long-term residents in a place like this would go deaf, if there were anyone to go deaf.

The sound is loud enough to be *useful*, and the ecosystem uses it. There are no flowers here, because flowers are advertisements and there is no one to advertise to. Instead there is sound-release: spore vesicles that will not open except at a specific frequency and amplitude, so that reproduction happens only when the correct continental chord is sounding, which is to say only in the correct season, only in the correct hydraulic state, only when the mother-mass has decided. Fertility is conducted through the air as a note.

## The Absence

There are no animals.

This is easy to state and hard to feel, because a landscape this productive should be crawling. Look for a long time and you understand the shape of the absence. Nothing here is defended. There are no thorns, no spines, no bitter alkaloids, no gums, no scent-warnings, no mimicry, no camouflage, no bark in the armoured sense. Every surface is soft, unguarded, edible in principle, and completely un-eaten. The tissue's only enemies are pressure, cold, and itself.

And nothing is coloured for eyes. Nothing needs to be seen. The absolute-black of the Hem, the bone-white of the mineral piers, the translucent grey-green of the deep tissue — these are all engineering colours, chosen for photon budgets and structural chemistry, not for signalling. The whole place has the aesthetic of a machine room, which in a sense it is.

There is movement, though, and at first you will misread it. Drifting through the mid-Tangle, at about the pace of a slow walk, are things the size of a fist: spore-carriers with three or four pneumatic legs, inflating and collapsing in sequence, walking along the stems. They have no nerves and no sensors beyond a gradient-following patch. They will walk for eleven days and then stop, and split, and become tissue. They are not fauna. They are the equivalent of seeds, if seeds could pace. Watching them work along a stem in the half-light, twenty of them in file, you get the strongest feeling of being watched that this world offers, and it is entirely false.

## What Is Made Here, and by Whom

Nothing is built. There are no walls, no tools, no shelters, no artifacts, no ruins, no evidence anywhere on nine million square kilometres that anything has ever intended anything in the way that hands intend things.

And yet the piers are load-calculated. And yet the resonator apertures are tuned. And yet somewhere in the deep tissue there are waveguides — actual optical waveguides, cellulose-analogue fibres with graded refractive index, running for kilometres, carrying the few photons the Hem consents to release down to sub-populations that need light for signalling rather than energy, with losses per metre better than anything glass can do at room temperature. And yet the lithification chemistry runs at temperatures and pressures where it should not run at all, catalysed by protein complexes that appear to steer reactions by shaping the phonon field around them — a technique that our own physics can describe only in the way that a person who has heard a language can describe having heard it.

No one made this. It grew into it. Given a continent's worth of substrate, a permanent supply of water, an unlimited supply of fresh reactive rock rising from below, no predators, no competitors, no seasons worth mentioning, and a hundred million years without a single interruption — this is apparently what a plant becomes. It becomes an industry. It becomes weather. It becomes the reason its mountains have no valleys.

## The Frontier

The most alive place on the plateau is the edge, and the edge moves.

Where new crust surfaces — long fissures, tens of kilometres, where basalt sheets come up hot and glassy — there is a zone of bare rock, sometimes a hundred metres wide, sometimes two kilometres. It is the only exposed geology in the world. It steams. It is the only place where you can see what the ground actually is, and where you can hear something other than the Shout: at the frontier the noise drops, because there is no tissue to make it, and the sudden partial silence is physically frightening, like a hand taken away from your back.

The advance across new rock happens in waves you can watch over a day. First a wetting — the mass throws water forward, sheeting it over the hot stone, cracking it thermally, and the crack-noise there is a machine-gun stutter. Then a grey bloom, a crust-form that goes down into the fresh etch-pits. Then, within a week, the first piers, spidering out over the fissure, arching, meeting, fusing. Then the Tangle pours over the piers and the Hem closes above and the light goes out and the chord picks up a new voice, slightly flat at first, tuning itself over a season.

Behind the frontier, in the oldest interior of the plateau, the reverse process runs: the mass consumes its own deep structure and abandons it, and there are cavities down there, hollows the size of cathedrals under the living roof, floored with white mineral tubing and roofed with grey collapsed tissue and filled with air at a hundred percent humidity and a temperature of eleven degrees, absolutely dark, absolutely still, and absolutely loud — the Shout arrives in those chambers as a low pressure-hum you feel in the sternum. Water beads on every surface of them and never falls, because the surface tension of the mineral fibre is engineered to hold it. Weeping rooms. Nothing lives in them. Nothing has ever lived in them. They fill in from below over a few hundred years and are grown again elsewhere.

## Rules of the World

If you had to write down what this place will and will not permit:

Water is never free. It has no surface anywhere on the continent that is not tissue or engineered mineral. There is no lake, no pool, no stream, no drop of rain that reaches the ground under its own authority.

Light is a debt, collected at the top and never repaid. Depth is measured in darkness.

Sound is the medium. It carries season, fertility, hydraulic state, and the position of the frontier. It is not communication in any intentional sense; it is more like the noise a factory makes, which nonetheless tells you exactly what the factory is doing.

The rock is younger than the growth. Everywhere. Without exception. The plateau's oldest stone is a fraction of the age of the thing standing on it, because the stone keeps arriving and the thing standing on it has simply never stopped.

Nothing is eaten. Everything is reabsorbed.

And there is no horizon. Not because of curvature or haze, but because the Tangle is packed and the Hem is closed and the interior is a solid of stems in dim red-black light, and from any point inside it the world extends about nine metres in every direction and then becomes tissue, warm, wet, humming, cross-braced, continuous, for two thousand kilometres.
