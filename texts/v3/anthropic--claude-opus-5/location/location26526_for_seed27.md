# The Dry Canopy

## The Plateau

It is one enormous tableland, and it has been worn down so long that the word *mountain* no longer applies to anything on it. What remains are stumps: broad shield-shapes of pale mineral rising ten or twenty meters over kilometers of width, so gradual that you cross one without knowing, aware only that the horizon has changed its mind. The rock beneath is a fine-grained feldspathic stuff the color of old bone, and where it shows through — which is rarely, at the lips of scarps and in the trampled aprons around the towers — it has the smoothed, licked quality of a stone in a riverbed. But there has never been a river. There has never been water at all here, not in the chemical sense, not as ice, not as vapor. The smoothing is the work of four billion years of dust and wind and the slow chewing of roots.

The plateau stands about four kilometers above the basins that surround it, but the atmosphere is thick and cold and heavy with nitrogen and carbon dioxide, so the air up here has the pressure of a sea-level afternoon and the temperature of a cellar in early spring. Six degrees. Eight in the middle of the long day. It does not vary much. Nothing here varies much anymore.

## The Wick

Everything is plant. This has to be understood before anything else makes sense.

The ground is not soil with plants in it. The ground is a mat, ankle-deep to knee-deep, of interlocked dry filament — call it **wick** — that runs unbroken from one edge of the plateau to the other, a single continuous tissue several hundred kilometers across. It has the give and rasp of a coir doormat. It is gray-green, sometimes bronze, and it makes a sound underfoot like walking on stale bread.

The wick does not drink, because there is nothing to drink. Its chemistry is solid-state. Long ago something engineered — or the plateau's biology invented, or the two things happened at once and can no longer be told apart — a metabolism that moves electrons instead of fluids. The filaments are doped cellulose analogues, semiconducting, threaded with ordered rows of metal atoms one atom wide. Where an Earth plant would run sap up a tube, the wick runs current up a wire. Where an Earth plant would sweat, the wick discharges: a faint continual sputter of static off its tips, so that on a still hour the whole plateau hisses like a field of frying oil.

Its energy budget is strange and thin. The sun is small, old, and orange, and hangs in a permanent haze of levitated dust, so noon here is roughly the brightness of a room with one lamp in it and the curtains drawn. Photosynthesis of a kind still happens, but slowly, and it accounts for perhaps a third of the plateau's income. The rest is scavenged: piezoelectric strain harvested from the flexing of rock under thermal tides; cosmic ray flux caught in mineral scintillators grown into the wick's underside like tiny teeth; and — most of it — the potential difference between the plateau's surface and the ionosphere, which the towers tap and spill downward for everyone.

## The Stacks

Every three to nine kilometers, a tower.

They are called nothing by anyone, because nothing here uses names, but their form is consistent enough to be a species: a fluted column of dry heartwood-analogue, black-brown, forty to two hundred meters tall, hollow, its walls perforated with thousands of ports that open and close on a slow schedule. Around the base, buttress roots spread in a radial fan and vanish into the wick. Higher up, the fluting resolves into balconies, ledges, galleries, nested chambers — regular, repeating, tiered. There are stairs. There are doorways with proportion and lintels. There are rooms whose walls carry a bas-relief of channels that any engineer would recognize as circuitry and any priest would recognize as scripture.

This is the built quality of the place, and it is not metaphorical. Somebody designed these. Somebody specified a floor height and repeated it eleven times. The stacks are a city — a scattered, low-density, generously spaced city, more like a plain of grain silos than a downtown, with kilometers of empty living carpet between neighbors — and it was grown rather than assembled, and its builders have been gone so long that the question of who they were has decayed past the point of being interesting.

The stacks compute. The lattice in their walls does something with electron spin and phase coherence that is not simply electronics, and one of the visible symptoms is that the interiors of the older stacks are measurably colder than their surroundings and stay that way, radiating nothing, in flat violation of any bookkeeping you care to do. Another symptom is the **slow ground**: an apron of about eighty meters' radius at the foot of each tower where processes run at roughly nine-tenths speed. Dust settles more languidly there. A dropped pebble takes a beat too long. Animals learn the boundary as a physical thing and step over it the way you'd step over a curb.

## Sound

It is very loud. This surprises people who expect a dead place to be quiet.

Three sources braid together. First, the hiss — the continuous corona discharge of a hundred thousand square kilometers of wick, a white-noise floor at the volume of heavy rain, which is the closest thing this world has to rain.

Second, the stacks. They are hollow, ported flutes standing in a plateau wind that never fully stops, and the ports open and close under active control. The result is that each tower is an organ pipe playing itself, in the range of eight to sixty hertz, felt as much as heard, and the towers are tuned to one another. Standing between two of them you can hear the beat frequency walking slowly across the space, a throb every four or five seconds that moves your sternum. On a good wind, the whole plateau is a single chord held for hours, and when the chord changes — it changes maybe eleven times a day — every animal on the plain stops moving for the duration of the transition.

Third, the discharges proper. Every so often, somewhere, the accumulated potential between a stack's crown and the sky exceeds tolerance and lets go: not lightning as a storm makes it, but a deliberate, scheduled, gentle-looking violet column that stands for two or three seconds and detonates the air around it. The crack arrives across the flats a beat later and rolls, and rolls, and the wick's hiss dips for a moment afterward as the whole field drinks what just fell.

There is a fourth thing, occasional. Some stacks talk. It is not speech in any language, but it has the cadence of a list being read: a sequence of tones with the exact prosody of a voice reciting coordinates, or names, or a forecast, patiently, ending on a downward inflection and beginning again. It is the same list every time. It has been the same list for an extremely long time, and it refers to nothing that still exists.

## The Animals

Abundant, and all of them are structurally plants, or were, which is the same thing on this plateau. There is no separate animal kingdom here. Motility is a lifestyle choice that certain lineages of wick made and stuck with.

**Tillers** are the commonest: things the size of a hare, six-legged, built of the same dry filament as the ground, that graze the wick by pressing a flat ventral pad against it and drawing off stored charge. They leave discharge-scars — pale ovals, thousands of them, that regrow in about a season. They move in loose scatters of forty or fifty and they *tick* audibly as they feed, and a grazing party sounds like a slow, spreading applause.

**Fetches** hang. They are broad, brittle, veined sheets three or four meters across, nearly weightless, holding position over the plateau by riding their own charge against the ground's — electrostatic levitation, cheap in this atmosphere, silent. They do not flap. They drift, and they turn, and they are the only quiet thing in the world. They feed on the ionized wake left behind a tower's discharge, and so a lightning-column will draw a dozen of them in from kilometers off within a minute, converging like paper in a draft.

**Sparrowers** are the predators, and they are ugly, and they hunt by grounding. A sparrower is a long, low, four-limbed thing with a dorsal spine of insulator quills and a mouth that is really an electrode. It creeps close to a feeding tiller, plants its feet, and completes a circuit; the tiller's stored charge dumps through it in a fraction of a second with a snap you can hear a kilometer away, and the tiller falls over, empty, and is eaten at leisure. On any given hour of any given day, standing anywhere on the plain, you will hear that snap somewhere in the distance, once every few minutes, like a bored person cracking their knuckles.

And in the stacks: colonies. Something small and many-legged has moved into the upper galleries in vast numbers and lives there in a state of complicated cooperation with the architecture, cleaning the ports, patching the lattice, being tolerated. Their traffic on the balconies at dusk is a shimmer, a dark quivering fringe on every ledge, and the noise of it, up close, is unbearable.

## Rules

Shadows on this plateau lean. Not toward or away from the sun — toward the nearest stack, always, by a few degrees, more strongly the closer you are. Nobody knows why. It is probably a side effect.

Nothing rots, because rot needs water. Dead things dry, crack, and are dismantled atom by atom by the wick's roots over decades, so the plain is littered with intact, hollow, weightless corpses in postures of great dignity, and children of any species that had children would find them wonderful.

The dust does not settle evenly. It piles in faint lines and arcs across the flats, following field gradients, so that from the top of a stack the whole plateau is written over in immense pale contour lines that shift week by week and are, at any moment, an accurate map of something no eye can otherwise see.

## The Long State

Everything here is finished. The mountains finished. The chemistry finished; there is nothing more to invent when your solvent is nothing and your food is the sky's own voltage. The builders finished, whatever finishing meant for them, and left behind a city that grew into a forest and a forest that never stopped being a city, and a list read aloud eleven times a day to an audience of grazing animals who have learned to stop moving when the chord changes.

It is not a ruin. Ruins are things falling. This is a thing that has stopped falling: worn to its floor, cooled to its temperature, sparse and enormous and extremely loud, humming its held note over four hundred kilometers of dry living carpet under a small orange sun that it can barely see and does not particularly need.
