## The Cirque at Umberline

Nothing here was built. Everything here is architecture. That contradiction is the whole place, and it is best to take it in at the notch, before descending.

The notch is a saddle in a young ridge — so young that the rock still has edges. This is not weathered country. The arêtes stand up in plates and blades, blue-grey, freshly quarried by ice that left only recently and left in a hurry, and the scree below them has not yet learned to lie flat. Everything is at the angle of first repose. Everything has a rim of frost on its north face at all hours, a fine sugaring that never quite goes, because the air here sits at the temperature where water can't decide, and does both, all day.

From the notch you look down maybe sixty meters into a basin roughly three hundred paces across. The basin is the whole world of this description. You can walk its perimeter in twenty minutes. It is a small place. That matters, because it is also a dense one, and the density has nowhere to go, so it has gone upward and inward and become a settlement.

---

**The pattern**

What you see first, in the dim, is streets.

They are unmistakable. Lanes a stride wide, running between walls of shoulder height, sometimes higher, meeting at junctions, opening into small plazas, dead-ending in chambers that have a floor and three sides and a doorway. From the notch the plan reads like a hill-town seen from above with the roofs off — a warren of green-black masonry, courts and closes and a rough radial order pulling toward the low center where the water gathers.

The reason is honest and geological, and knowing it does not make the streets less street-like.

Ground this cold and this wet, freezing and thawing twice a day for a hundred and forty days a year, sorts itself. It shoulders coarse stones out to the edges of cells and leaves the fines in the middle. It makes polygons. On flat ground the polygons are wide and hexagonal; on slope they stretch and become stripes; where slope meets flat they curve and knot. Any high cold place with water near the surface will do this. It is called patterned ground, and normally it is a low thing, a mosaic of gravel rings a few fingers proud of the soil, the sort of pattern you only notice when you stop to notice it.

Here the pattern got tenants.

---

**The material**

The tenant is a cushion plant. Call it stonewool, since nobody calls it anything. It is a moss-form, though not a moss — dense, dark, packed to the density of felt, each shoot a few millimeters from its neighbor and each shoot dividing again, so that a hand's breadth of the surface contains perhaps a hundred thousand growing tips, all pressed together, all pushing.

Stonewool colonizes stone. It prefers the coarse rim of a polygon, where the drainage is good and the rock gives it purchase, and it grows up rather than out, because it hates wind and each cushion shelters the cushion beside it. Over centuries — the basin is not old, but centuries are available — the rims went vertical. They became walls: a core of sorted stone, a skin of living cushion four fingers thick, and beneath that skin a mass of its own dead selves, compressed into black peat, waterlogged, structural. The walls are load-bearing plant. Push one and it gives a hand's width, elastic, then holds. It is warmer than the air. Put your palm flat against a south-facing wall in the middle of the day and there are eight or nine degrees in it that the atmosphere does not have — the cushion traps sun, ferments its own dead, and hoards the heat in the wet peat like a slow brick.

The floors of the streets are a different plant: a flat prostrate liverwort, olive, slick, with a faint mineral gloss, which cannot compete on the rims and so has taken the lanes. It is genuinely slippery. Walk with care.

The roofs — some cells are roofed — are the third element. Where two walls lean close enough, the cushions on their tops meet and knit and fill the gap, and the seam thickens, and the chamber below becomes interior. This takes about forty years. There are roofed rooms with a meter of living material overhead, and lightwells where the knitting failed, and long covered passages that you must stoop to enter and that smell of cold iron and cut grass.

Everything is plant. There is no wood in the basin. There is no fiber, no thatch, no cordage, no mortar, no tool mark, no fired thing, no metal, no glass, no wheel-rut, no smoke and no trace that smoke was ever made here. There is stone, water, ice, and a single interlocked mass of living tissue that has taken the shape of a town.

---

**Light**

Dim, and dim in three ways at once.

The cirque walls are high and steep, so direct sun enters late and leaves early, and for four months does not enter at all. Second, the basin makes its own weather: water near the surface, air near the dew point, and so a fog forms in the bowl most mornings and most evenings and much of the time between, standing in the streets in bands, so that a lane forty paces long fades out at twenty. Third, once you are inside — under the knitted roofs, in the covered closes — the light that reaches you has passed through a hand's depth of chlorophyll and arrives green, thickened, the color of a bottle held to a candle. You can read a face in it. You cannot read a page.

The overall impression is of very early morning, sustained indefinitely.

---

**Water**

Water is the only thing in the basin that behaves like infrastructure, and it does, thoroughly.

The rock walls weep. Not springs — weeping: the whole cliff is damp, a permanent sheen, and it beads and runs and freezes and runs, and along the base of the cliff the seeps have cut a shallow ring-channel that the stonewool has arched over, so that all along the perimeter there is a covered watercourse you can hear but not see. Lean against the wall of the outermost close and there is a stream running behind your shoulder blades.

From the ring, water goes inward. It does not flow on the surface. It flows *within* the walls — through the saturated peat cores, capillary and slow, so that the entire settlement is a wet sponge holding perhaps a hundred tons of water in suspension in its own body. Where a wall is cut (frost splits them; a stone shifts; a slab falls from above) the wound leaks for a season, dark and steady, and then closes.

Below the peat, and this is the part that ought to be said plainly: the water has made rooms. Cavities, cellars, chambers under the streets, roofed by the mat and floored by ice that survives the summer. Some of them are large enough to stand in. You find them by sound — a hollow note underfoot, a change in the give of the liverwort — and by the breath that comes up out of them, which is colder than the cold and smells of nothing at all.

Twice a day, at the freeze and at the thaw, the whole basin shifts. Water expands into ice; ice relaxes into water. Walls lean a millimeter and lean back. It is not perceptible unless you are still and paying attention for a long time, and then it is.

---

**Sound**

Mostly quiet. Emphatically so — the density absorbs. Stonewool is the best sound-deadening material in the world; a shout in a covered close does not echo, it is simply swallowed, and comes back as nothing, and this is the single most unnerving thing about the place.

Against that quiet, four sounds:

Dripping, everywhere, uncountable, unsynchronized, from the cliff and from the roof-seams and from the doorway lintels. It is the baseline. It never stops. After an hour you cease to hear it and after three hours you hear it again and it is louder than before.

Wind, but only overhead. The lanes are calm. Above the roofline the wind moves in the notch and over the arêtes with a thin sound, and you get the strange sensation of standing in a sheltered room whose ceiling is weather.

Cracking. Small, sharp, sporadic — ice releasing in the cliff, stone letting go, a frozen seam splitting. Perhaps twenty times an hour at dusk. Always from somewhere you are not looking.

And, at the freeze and the thaw, from the cellars, a low sustained note. Water pushing air out through a mat of vegetation. It lasts a few minutes, it is barely above the threshold of hearing, and it comes up through the soles of the feet as much as through the ears. It is the closest thing the basin has to a voice, and it says the same thing every twelve hours.

---

**Fauna**

Rare, and worth the wait.

Wingless flies live in the cushion surface, dark, slow, needing no wings because the wind up here punishes flight and the whole world they require is four millimeters thick. They are everywhere and invisible; you notice them by the itch on the back of the hand after leaning on a wall.

There is a grazer: a pale flatworm-thing the length of a finger, blind, that lives in the peat and eats the dead layer from beneath, and whose tunneling is genuinely load-bearing — its galleries are the ventilation of the wall interiors. Turn a broken slab of peat and there will be one or two, retracting slowly. It is the closest thing here to a citizen.

Birds come. Once, twice in a day, a small grey-brown one crosses the basin without stopping, and its shadow going over the fog is an event. Something nests in the cliff face high above, irregularly, in a good decade. Below the nest the stonewool grows more vigorously for twenty paces — the only fertilizer this basin ever receives, arriving as a gift from above and building a slightly taller neighborhood.

No large animal. No track, no scat, no browse line, no burrow of any size. Nothing has walked these streets. That is what the streets are for.

---

**The doorways**

Here is the unusual part, and it should be stated once, without insistence, and then left alone.

The chambers have openings, and the openings are not random. They are consistently a bit higher than wide. They are consistently sized so that a body of a certain build could pass through them without stooping and without room to spare — a body a little shorter than a person and a little broader. They are consistently on the leeward side. Many of them have a lip at the base, knee-high, of thickened peat, worn smooth on top, which is exactly what a threshold is.

This is explicable. The wind decides which side of a cell the cushion can close over; the geometry of frost cells sets proportion; the smoothing on the lip is meltwater, running out of the chamber, polishing where it always runs. Every feature has a cause and the causes are all ordinary.

But there are four hundred of them and they agree with each other, and standing in the middle of a small plaza in the green half-light with eleven doorways facing you, each waist-lipped and shoulder-wide and dark, the ordinary explanation does its work and then stops working, and what remains is the flat sensation of being in a place that is fully furnished and entirely unoccupied, and has been, for a long time, and is being maintained.

---

**Rules of the place**

That the only builder is water, and its only tools are freezing and thawing, and it is patient.

That nothing is dead. There is no rubble in the basin, no fallen material, no ruin — a collapse is absorbed and re-grown within a generation of cushions, and the plan closes over the gap like skin. There is therefore no history here you can read. Only a present tense, thick and wet.

That the plan is not fixed. Frost heave moves each wall roughly a hand's width per decade, and the movement is not random: cells grow, split, merge, migrate downslope. The town is redrawn continuously at the pace of a slow argument. Two visits fifty years apart will not agree about the streets. There is no map that stays true, which is another way of saying there is no map.

That heat is the scarce thing and the walls hold it, and so every enclosure is a kindness, and this — not shelter from rain, not defense, not desire — is why the basin became a city.

That the vegetation is one organism, or near enough that the distinction stops paying. Cut a wall and the wound closes from both sides at once. The whole basin is roughly two hundred tons of interconnected living tissue at a temperature just above freezing, breathing very slowly, four hundred doorways open, waiting for no one in particular.

---

**At the end of the day**

The sun leaves the rim at about the fourth hour after noon. The fog, which had thinned, comes back up out of the cellars — you can watch it rise from the doorways, one and then another, in no order. The dripping slows and turns to ticking. The low note comes up through the floor for three or four minutes. Frost begins on the north faces of the walls and works around, and by full dark every surface in the basin has a nap of ice crystals on it, standing straight out from the living green beneath, so that if there were any light at all the whole town would be furred and pale and appear, briefly, to be made of something else.

There isn't any light. There is the sound of one stone letting go, somewhere behind you, in a street you have not walked.
