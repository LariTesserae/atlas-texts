# The Sweating Notch

## Where it sits

Nine thousand-odd metres, but forget the number — what matters is the feeling of being above the weather rather than in it. The mountain is a single blade of rock shoved up out of a warm ocean so recently that the sea salt is still in its cracks, and this place is one notch in the blade's upper edge: a saddle perhaps a hundred and eighty paces long, sixty wide at its widest, walled at both ends by rising arêtes and open on both flanks to nothing at all.

You could walk the whole of it in four minutes if the footing allowed. It doesn't. So it takes an hour.

## The rock

Young rock has a particular ugliness that becomes beauty when you understand it. There is no talus here — no aprons of broken stone, no soft skirts of gravel. Nothing has had time to fall. The faces are still the shapes the fracture made: conchoidal dishes the size of doors, blades that will cut a boot sole, edges so unrounded they whistle when the wind takes them at an angle. The colour is a wet blue-black with a bronze sheen where the iron in it has begun, only just begun, to consider rusting. In shelter you can find surfaces that have never been rained on, still bearing the glassy skin from the moment of cooling, and they feel, absurdly, like the inside of a kiln.

Put your palm flat on any of it. It is warm. Blood-warm at the surface, warmer in the crevices. The heat is coming from below and it never stops.

## The water

This is the notch's odd fact, the thing that makes people who study it come up on the cable and stay too long.

The summit is a spring.

Water should not be here — should have drained, should be frozen, should be far below. But the mountain's interior is fractured to a depth nobody has bottomed, and the heat drives moisture up through those fractures as vapour, and in the last few hundred metres, where the rock finally cools toward the air's temperature, the vapour gives itself back as liquid. The stone weeps from the inside out. Not from cracks, particularly, not in identifiable springs — everywhere. A sheen on every face. Fat drops assembling on the undersides of overhangs, hanging, dropping, being replaced within the minute. Whole walls that glisten and run so evenly they look varnished.

So there is a running sound under everything: a hundred thousand small percussions on leaf and stone and steel, arriving at no rhythm, summing into a single soft roar like a fire heard from another room.

And there is the cloud. The notch stands where an updraught comes over the flank and cools, so for perhaps twenty hours of the day the saddle is inside a cloud that is being manufactured directly overhead. It is not fog that drifts through. It is fog that is *born* here, condensing out of clear air two metres above your head, so that if you look up you see the sky vanishing into existence — a haze that thickens as you watch, and then you are in it, and everything is white and glaring and dripping.

## The green

Warmth plus permanent water plus high light equals what you would find in a river valley, except pressed flat by the wind into something no higher than your ribs.

Nothing here is tall, but there are five or six storeys of life stacked in that half-metre. On the rock itself: a black crust-lichen, then a rust-orange one, then liverworts in flat overlapping palms. On the liverworts: mosses, and the mosses are the ground here — a soaked mattress a hand deep, so saturated that a footprint fills with clear water before you have finished lifting your boot. Out of the moss, cushion plants like green fists, tight as brains, each one an independent bog. Out of *those*, dwarf tree-ferns no taller than a kneeling child, croziers uncurling slow enough that you could mark their progress against a matchstick over a week. And on the fern stems, epiphytes — little strap-leaved things and one orchid, pale green, unremarkable, that flowers whenever it likes and smells of hot iron.

Everything touches everything. There is no bare ground except where the wind has scoured a lane through the notch's centre, and that lane is the path, and it is the only place you can see the actual mountain.

## The machinery

Somebody came, and what they built is very small and very loud.

There are four structures and no more.

**The condenser** stands at the western end where the updraught arrives: a lattice tower of galvanised steel, nine metres, hung with sixty tiers of corrugated collector plate. Cloud goes in one side and comes out the other slightly less cloudy, and the difference runs down the plates in sheets, into a gutter, into a header tank. There is no pump for this part. Gravity and altitude do all of it.

**The two turbines** sit in the throat of the notch on concrete pads that are the only poured stone in the landscape. They are old, riveted, and they turn continuously — never fast, never stopping, at the single unvarying speed the notch gives them. Each drives a compressor. The noise is not a whine but a groan, a long descending vowel, and the two of them are minutely out of phase so the sound beats against itself: *waaah — waaah — waaah*, four or five seconds to a cycle. You feel it in your sternum before you consciously hear it. After a day you stop hearing it. After three days you cannot sleep without it.

**The pump house** is a windowless brick cube — real fired brick, hauled up in pieces, laid by hand, mortar still bright — squatted over the header tank. Every eleven minutes, more or less, a valve opens and a piston makes six strokes and a slug of collected water is sent down the mountain through a hundred-millimetre pipe. The strokes are the loudest single event in the notch. Each one is a hammer blow on the world's largest bell, and after the sixth there is a silence in which you can hear the dripping again, and every fern on the pad shivers.

**The cable** anchors on a pylon at the eastern end and goes out horizontally into whiteness. Its hum is a high sustained third above the turbine groan. Twice a day a bucket-car climbs out of the cloud carrying grease, a mail sack, sometimes a person, and it arrives already dripping, and it leaves within the hour.

Everything else is remains and marks: a rail of steel bolted along the path, worn bright by hands; four numbered survey pins; a stack of plate stock going green under moss; a bootprint in the pump house's concrete, unweathered, sharp as the day it was made and likely to stay that way for a thousand years. Nobody sleeps here. There is no bed and no roof for a bed.

## The rules

The rules of this place are three, and everyone learns them in the first hour.

**You cannot be heard.** Between the turbines and the water and the wind, a shouted sentence at four metres is gone. So all business in the notch is done with the hands — a compact sign language developed by the water crews, thirty-odd gestures, most of them concerning valves. People who work here long become quiet down below too.

**Nothing dries.** Not skin, not paper, not steel. Metal is either greased or gone. Everything you wear becomes part of the water cycle within twenty minutes and stays that way, and because the air is warm this is not misery, only strangeness: a permanently soaked warmth, like working inside a mouth.

**The machinery is the reason the garden exists.** Or the garden thinks it is. The condenser's runoff, the pump house's waste heat, the drip-line beneath the pipe — these are the richest habitats in the notch. The deepest moss is against the brick. The tree-ferns crowd the concrete pads. The one orchid grows on the turbine housing. The place is not being reclaimed by nature; nature arrived *after* the rivets and has organised itself around them.

## The living things you might see

Rarely. That is the honest word.

Swifts — a dozen, maybe twenty, dark and hoarse, riding the updraught. They do not land in the notch and nobody has found where they land. They pass close enough to hear the sound of their bodies going through the air, which is a real sound, a *fp*, audible even here.

Beneath the pump house, in the warm wet dark, a pale frog with translucent flanks. Perhaps forty of them in total, in the world. They are silent, or they call at a frequency the turbines eat.

Beetles in the moss, small, iridescent, four species. A single kind of long-legged fly at the condenser gutter, in clouds, all summer.

And nothing else. No mammal has ever come up. No fish, obviously. No snake. The great biomass here is vegetable and the fauna is a rumour within it.

## The pace

Everything slow.

The mountain rises. Not metaphorically: the survey pins say four to six centimetres a year, and the crews have twice had to re-shim the turbine pads because the notch is warping, patiently, under the machines. Somewhere in a filing cabinet at the valley station there is a graph of it, a straight line going up for sixty years, and everyone who sees the graph laughs, and then stops laughing.

The moss advances a thumb's width a season toward the path.

The turbines have not changed speed in living memory.

The pump strokes every eleven minutes and has struck perhaps two million times.

Once or twice a season the cloud fails — the updraught slackens or the air is too dry — and the notch stands clear in hard blue-white light with three hundred kilometres of ranges below it and the sun so close and unfiltered it burns in ten minutes. Then everything glitters unbearably: every leaf, every drop, every rivet-head. The turbines slow half a turn. The water still comes out of the rock.

It lasts an hour, sometimes two. Then the cloud is manufactured again overhead, and the notch closes, and the groaning resumes, and the mountain goes on sweating and rising in the white.
