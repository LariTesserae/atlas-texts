## The Basin Under the One Leaf

You do not descend into it. There is no shaft, no mouth, no throat of dark to pass through. You walk down a slope of pale rock in ordinary daylight and at some point you notice that the light has gone green-gold, that the wind has stopped, that the sky has veins in it.

The roof is a leaf. One leaf, spanning the whole subsided basin — some eleven kilometres at its widest, less across the narrows — grown out from a stem-mass on the northern rim and laid over the depression the way a hand is laid over a bowl. From beneath, it is a ceiling of translucent tissue perhaps two metres thick, held aloft on its own ribbing: primary veins as broad as roads, arching; secondaries braided beneath them; a fine reticulation between, so that the light arriving is divided and divided again into a mesh of brightness. Noon sun comes through it at maybe a third of its strength and still it is *bright* — brighter than any forest floor, a flat scouring brightness with no shadows worth the name, because the source is not a point but the entire sky. You cannot look up for long. Your own shadow is a faint quadruple smear, four ghosts at slightly different angles, cast by four different regions of glowing membrane.

Everything in the basin is the colour of held light: chartreuse, olive, brass, the yellow-green of a lamp seen through a hand.

## Heat and the water that never leaves

Sealed under a leaf, the basin cooks. It is a steady thirty-eight to forty-one degrees at the floor and hotter under the ceiling, and it has been that temperature for as long as there has been a ceiling. The air is thick, still, and just short of saturated — humidity high enough that your skin never quite dries and never quite runs.

There is no rain. There is *condensation*, which is more reliable than rain and less generous. The underside of the roof-leaf sweats continuously; droplets gather along the veins, migrate to the low points of the arches, and hang there. They do not fall. Surface tension and stillness conspire: with no wind and no vibration, a drop can hang for weeks, growing, until it touches its neighbour and the two merge, and eventually a bead the size of a plum detaches and drops eighty metres into the canopy below, where it is caught, held, wicked away. Almost nothing reaches the floor as rain. Walking the terraces you will be struck by a warm falling drop perhaps twice an hour, always from directly above, always a small shock.

What water there is on the floor lives in pockets: shallow pans in the rock, pools the size of a room, a slow shining sheet in the deepest part of the basin that could be called a lake if it moved. It does not move. It is skinned over with something between algae and film. Around the pans, the stone is wet and dark; two metres away it is bone-dry and dusty. The basin is damp, not wet — a dry world with water in it, the way a body is dry with blood inside.

## Raw rock

The floor is new. This matters more than anything else about it.

The basin dropped — a roof of limestone over a dissolved void gave way, once, cleanly — and the collapse debris lies where it landed. The blocks are angular. Their fracture faces are *glassy*, unfrosted, unpitted; you can see the original crystal grain and the fine chatter-marks of the break. Nothing has rounded them. There is no scree apron, no fan of fines at their bases, no rubble skirt, because rubble requires weathering and here nothing weathers. No frost. No wind-blast. No running water. No lichen — the plants have not colonised bare stone in that way. No roots prying. The blocks are as they fell, propped at the angles at which they came to rest, and a hand-span of grit under the largest of them is the total sediment production of the entire basin.

Where the seep-pans are, travertine is precipitating out — and this is the only thing in the landscape that could be called progress. Terraces build outward in lobed rims, milk-white, each rim a few centimetres high, each holding a still film of water. The newest lobes are soft, the texture of wet sugar; they will take a thumbprint. The older ones are hard and glossy and *unscuffed*. You can walk across a hundred metres of travertine and look back and see your footprints as slight compressions in the soft margins and nothing at all on the hard, and by any normal reckoning of stone you have crossed something that was laid down yesterday and will be crossed tomorrow — except that it is not laid down yesterday. It is only ever laid down. The basin is permanently one instant old.

## The plant, which is the architecture

Vegetation is not a covering here. It is the volume.

From the underside of the roof-leaf, stems descend — not vines exactly; they are the leaf's own supports, come down to find the floor, hundreds of them, thickening as they go, meeting the rock in flanged pads like the feet of tables. These are the columns. Between and around them the mass has filled in: sheets of flattened stalk grown edge-to-edge into walls, spiralling out from the columns; horizontal decks of interlaced cane at four or five distinct levels, each thick enough to walk on and each supporting the standing growth of the level above; hanging curtains of strap-shaped leaves, motionless, spaced a hand apart, layer behind layer behind layer until sightlines end in green.

Walk here and you are indoors. Not metaphorically — you pass through doorways where two stem-walls have grown not-quite-touching, along corridors, into chambers ten metres high with column-trunks at the corners and a deck for a ceiling and one bright slot of roof-leaf visible far above through a gap. Then a chamber that narrows into a passage you must crouch through. Then a hall a hundred metres long. The plant has produced a city without producing a single symmetry: everything is structural, nothing is designed, and the result is more coherent than either.

There are perhaps four kinds of plant in the whole basin, and it is possible there is only one. Nobody has established whether the columns, the decks, the curtains and the pale mats on the floor are separate organisms or organs. There is no other kingdom present at any scale you can see. No fungi. No bacterial slick. No rot.

**Nothing decays here.** This is the central rule, and everything follows from it. In the sealed heat, under the constant light, with no decomposers ever having arrived, dead plant matter simply dries and stays. Every cane the growth ever put out is still where it was put. Dead leaves that fell fifty years ago — a century, a thousand years, there is no way to tell — are held in the lattice where they lodged, brittle and pale and intact, and the lattice is largely *made* of them: dry structure with a skin of living tissue over it, thickening outward, the whole basin a coral of its own history. There is no soil. There is no leaf litter. There is no humus smell — the air smells of hot green sap and mineral water and, faintly, of paper.

And because nothing decays, nothing needs replacing, and because nothing needs replacing, growth long ago reached the form it wanted and stopped. Nothing here is growing now. There are no buds, no shoots, no dying fronds, no seasons, no flowers, no seed. The plant has finished. It holds.

## Sound

Mostly silence, and it is the silence of a room, not of a wilderness: enclosed, dead, absorbent. No wind means no leaf-noise. No flow means no water-noise. Your footfalls on travertine are shockingly loud and your footfalls on cane-deck are a low woody drumming you can feel in your knees.

What sounds there are:

The drops striking the canopy, irregular, soft.

A tick, occasionally, from a cane bearing load — a fibre going, taking up slack. It never comes twice from the same place.

And once or twice a day, far off, a long groaning crack as one of the great primary veins of the roof-leaf flexes under the accumulated weight of its own condensation. It rolls the length of the basin and takes eight or nine seconds to die. It is the only event.

## The few animals

There are animals, and you may spend three days without seeing one.

They are all one sort: a long-limbed climber the length of your forearm, moss-green with a paler belly, with hands on all four limbs and eyes set forward, that lives entirely on the decks and curtains and drinks hanging condensation off the veins. It moves as though the air were syrup. It will cross a chamber in twenty minutes and you will only notice it has moved by comparing memories. It does not call. It has nothing to call to; the nearest other one is a kilometre off. There cannot be more than a few hundred in the basin, and they are not increasing, and they are not decreasing.

No insects. No worms. No birds — birds would need a way in.

## The structures

Scattered through the middle terraces, mostly near the water-pans, there are shelters: low domes about three metres across, built of dry canes taken from the lattice, bent and lashed at the crossings with split fibre. Twenty-two of them have been counted along the eastern pans; there are others.

They are not ruined. They cannot be. The knots have not slackened, the canes have not greyed, the lashings hold at tension. Each has a swept floor of travertine chips, a stone at the centre with a shallow pecked basin in it, and nothing else. No hearth — there is no fire here and never has been; no smoke-stain anywhere on the ceiling of the world. No tools, no cordage stores, no marks on the walls. The doorways all face the same direction, which is nothing in particular: no north, no prevailing weather, no sunrise.

They were made by hands with opposed thumbs, working patiently, without metal, without heat. They are as new as everything else. Whether that means they were built last week or before the collapse is not a question the basin will answer, because the basin has thrown out its clocks: no wear, no rot, no weather, no season, no night, no growth, no death that leaves a trace.

## Leaving

You will get hungry, and that will be the only reliable measure of time you have carried in with you, and it will start to feel like a private eccentricity — a rhythm you brought from outside, running in a place that has none.

Walk toward any margin. The columns thin. The roof-leaf lowers, thickens at its edge into a rolled rim, and there is a gap of a few metres between rim and rock where the true light comes in, white and violent, and with it the first movement of air you will have felt in days. It smells of dust and distance. It has weather in it, and consequence.

Behind you the basin holds exactly as you found it, and will, and does.
