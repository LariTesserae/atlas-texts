## Bay Nine

You come down to it by a stair of blue engineering brick, thirty-one treads, each worn concave in the middle by boots that no longer come. The handrail is galvanized pipe, and where your hand would fall it is furred with a green so fine it feels like felt and comes away on the skin. Warm air rises past you as you descend, the way warm air rises out of a laundry — not a draught but a slow, continuous offering. By the fourth tread your glasses are gone; by the tenth your shirt has stopped being a separate thing from you.

The stair ends in a doorway with a brass plate polished by shoulders: **BAY 9 — RECIRCULATION.** Beyond it the sound arrives all at once, the way water arrives when you step off a ledge.

---

It is one room. That is the first thing to understand: not a system of caverns, not a city, one room and its outbuildings, sixty paces by forty, brick barrel vaults springing in four bays from cast-iron columns with acanthus capitals that somebody bothered to cast in 1889 and nobody has looked at since. The crown of the vault is nine metres up. You could put a small church in here and still have room for its graveyard. Everything the world contains, in this place, is inside this volume, and the volume is entirely full.

Full is not a figure of speech. There is no air-space in Bay Nine that is not also leaf, root, pipe, water, or vapour. The columns are columns of ivy-fig, *Ficus pumila*, laid on so thick in overlapping shingles of leaf that the iron beneath is only inferred — you find it by knocking, and the knock comes back dull and low. The vaults are hung with lagging-ferns rooted in the crumbled asbestos wrap of the old steam mains, their fronds two metres long, hanging down in green combs that touch your head as you pass and shed a spatter of collected water down your collar. Between the ferns, the brick shows in patches the colour of weak tea, and from the brick grow liverworts in flat overlapping rosettes, and out of the liverworts grow mosses, and out of the mosses grow the smallest ferns of all, no bigger than a thumbnail, in the millions. Substrate all the way down. The brick is only the last, deepest layer of soil.

You do not walk on the floor. The floor is somewhere beneath you, tiled, probably, in the quarry tile the works used everywhere; but the floor has two metres of root mat on it now, a woven brown-and-white mass of fig root and sedge root and the tough black cords of something that came down through a duct decades ago and has not been identified because nobody has come to identify it. The mat gives underfoot like a mattress and springs back. Where you stand, water rises around the print of your boot and then drains away in about a minute. Everything here is at the water table's exact lip. The mat *is* the water table, colonized.

---

The machines are still running. That is the second thing.

At the north end, in a brick housing of its own, the ram works — two cylinders, a flywheel taller than a man, a beat at about ninety to the minute that is not a sound so much as a condition of the room. You feel it in the sternum and in the fillings of your teeth. Every fourth or fifth stroke it finds a resonance in the vaults and the whole bay says a syllable back. Over that: the belt-slap of the fan drives, four of them, leather belts that have been dressed so many times they have gone black and soft as tongue; the fans themselves in cages of expanded steel, turning slowly, pushing the warm wet air in a circuit that has not changed direction since the works was commissioned. Over that: water. Water in the leats, jade-green with algae, sliding at walking pace along channels cut into the platform edges. Water in the pipes, hissing at a steam trap that has been failing politely for years. Water falling from the vault crown as a fine indoor rain, continuous, five or six drops a second per square metre, the room condensing itself back onto itself. And under all of it, so low you take it for your own pulse, the sump.

You cannot hold a conversation in Bay Nine. You can shout a word and have it received; two words will not survive. The people who worked here used hand signs and a chalkboard, and the chalkboard is on the wall by the door, and the last thing written on it in a careful engineer's capitals is **LAMP ROW 3 OUT — REQ. BALLASTS ×6**, and the chalk has been there long enough to have gone soft and grown a bloom of grey mould in the shape of the letters.

---

Light comes from three sources, none of them generous.

There are the pavement lights: forty-odd cast-iron frames set into the street eight metres overhead, each holding a grid of prismatic glass lenses meant to throw daylight down here. They still throw it. It arrives grey-green, filtered through the moss that has sealed each lens into a little window of pond, and lands in soft rectangles on the canopy, never reaching the mat. In these rectangles the plants are different — tighter, harder-leaved, faintly reddened. You can map the street above by looking up: the two rows of lights, the gap where the tram rails run, the one lens someone broke in 1953 that now has a rope of fig hanging out of it, seven metres of it, hanging in the daylight and not going any further, because it has found nothing to hold.

There are the lamps: sodium and old mercury-vapour in wire cages, six rows of twelve. Row 3 is out. Row 5 is out. Of the remaining, perhaps thirty still burn, and each one burns inside a globe of its own vegetation — the fig has grown up over the cages and made a green lantern of each, so the light leaves them already coloured, already softened, and the room is lit the way an aquarium is lit: enough to move by, never enough to read.

And there is the water. Not light of its own, but the leats catch what there is and hold it, and the room's brightest surfaces are those long moving ribbons at ankle height, going nowhere, coming back around.

---

Nothing here has ever eaten anything.

This is the strangest fact about Bay Nine and it takes people a while to notice, and when they notice, they usually stop and stand still for a moment. There are no animals. Not a spider in the corners of the vault. Not a woodlouse in the rotting timber of the shoring. Not a fly at the sump, not a midge in the vapour, not a worm in two metres of root mat that any garden would have made an empire of. Bay Nine is sealed at every join, filtered at every intake, held at thirty-one degrees and ninety-eight percent humidity, and it was built that way for reasons of product hygiene by a company that no longer exists, and the seal has held.

So the ecology runs on one engine only: growth, and rot, and growth again. Leaves fall and the mat takes them. Fungi do the work that in any other world is shared out among a thousand mouths — brackets on the shoring timbers, fine white mycelial felts through the mat that you can lift in sheets, a scatter of pale gilled things on the wetter beams that live four days and go to slime. There is no browsing. Nothing is nibbled. Every leaf in Bay Nine reaches its full designed size and then ages honestly and dies of age. This gives the whole room an oddly formal look, like a plant collection in an old engraving — nothing torn, nothing skeletonized, every frond complete.

And nothing is pollinated. The figs flower inside their own fruit, as figs do, and wait for a wasp two millimetres long that has never once come down the stair, and so the fruits swell and go hard and green and stay hard and green for a season and then drop into the mat unseeded. The sedges make pollen and shed it into the fans and the fans distribute it evenly over everything, a yellow dust in the leats each spring, and no seed sets. Everything in Bay Nine that lives here now got here by being a piece of something else. Runners. Layerings. Spore. A cutting in a technician's pocket. The whole cathedral of green is, genetically, about eleven individuals, endlessly extended — one fig that is the columns and the lanterns and the rope in the broken lens, one sedge that is the leats' whole margin, one fern that is the ceiling. It is less a forest than a very large single organism wearing a building.

---

It changes, but you would need a life to see it.

The mat rises about three millimetres a year; there is a datum mark scratched on a column and the mat has taken thirty centimetres of it since the mark was made. In the third bay a lime stalactite hangs from a bad joint in the vault, the length of a forearm, and lime grows at the speed lime grows. One arch, the second on the east side, has bowed by perhaps forty millimetres and has been bowed by perhaps forty millimetres for as long as anyone recorded, and the shoring under it — pit props, dogged and wedged — is soft on the outside and sound in the core. When a lamp fails, the vegetation under it browns over the following three seasons, and then the mosses come in and take the space, and the brown goes green again at a lower altitude, and that is what an event looks like here.

There is a logbook on a shelf by the door in a steel box that did not keep the wet out. It has swollen into a solid brick of grey pulp with a spine. You can see the shape of writing on the outermost page, and no words.

At the far south end, past the fans, where the ram's exhaust water comes back warmest and the mat is deepest and the air is thick enough to lean on, one root of the fig has gone up the wall, into a hairline crack in the brick, through the crack, and into the packed clay under the street. You can see where it entered. You cannot see where it is going. It is going up at a few centimetres a year, into the dark and the cold, toward a pavement that it has no way of knowing is there, and it has been doing this, by the look of it, since roughly the war.

The ram keeps its ninety to the minute. The rain comes off the crown. The green lanterns hold their light in.
