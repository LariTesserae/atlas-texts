## The Rising Floor

Begin with the fact that there is no horizon in the usual sense. The land goes on for the distance of a continent at nearly one altitude, and so what you see at the edge of sight is not a line but a slow whitening, a place where the air itself becomes the object. Two thousand kilometres of plateau, held up at four kilometres, cold, mostly dry, mostly quiet, and thick with life in the seams.

The rim mountains stand around it like something recently broken. They have not been worn. Their ridges are blades; their faces still hold the pale unweathered colour of rock that was underground a geological instant ago, and their talus is angular — you can hear it clatter, sheet by sheet, in the afternoons when the frost lets go. There are no gorges cutting inward. This is the first thing a traveller from lower country fails to understand. Rivers have not yet had time to saw their way in, and so nothing drains off the plateau. Every drop of water that falls here stays here until it evaporates. The interior is a mosaic of thousands of closed basins, each one a shallow pan floored with grey silt and rimmed with a bathtub-ring of salt, each one separated from the next by low, sharp, unrounded ridges of the same grey rock. From above it would look like the surface of something that had cooled while boiling.

### The nightly tide

The water comes from the air, and it comes on a schedule.

Below the plateau, unseen, there is a warm moist ocean of lower atmosphere. Each night, as the rock loses its heat to a sky as clean as any sky gets, cold air slides down the inner slopes and the moist layer is pushed up over the rim and spills. Fog enters the basins the way water enters a flooded field: silently, unevenly, finding the low ground first. By the fourth hour of dark the basins are full of it and the ridges stand out as islands. Anyone on a ridge at that hour is standing above a white sea with a black sky over it and can see, at the edge of things, the fog surface stirring in slow lobes.

What the fog does is condense. It condenses on everything: on gravel, on lichen, on wool, on eyelashes, on the woven hair screens the people put up on purpose. By the coldest hour it is not water but rime — a fur of white ice on the windward side of every object, feathered, sometimes as long as a finger. In the morning the sun comes over the rim with the ferocity that only thin air permits, the rime lets go, and for perhaps ninety minutes the whole plateau drips. Then it is dry again, hard-lit, dark blue overhead, and stays dry until night.

Rain proper is rare — perhaps eleven or twelve times a year, in a season when something larger passes overhead — and it arrives as a violent gift, brief hail, then flat sheets of it, and for two days afterward the basins hold real standing water, mirror-shallow, wading depth at most, full of things that hatch fast.

### What grows

Almost nothing, and it is arranged with terrible precision. There is no soil to speak of; the rock is too young for it. What there is instead is a graph of the drip-lines.

On the downwind side of every ridge crest, where the rime-melt runs off in the morning, there are lines of cushion plants — dense grey-green hemispheres, hard as bread, some of them centuries old, growing at the rate of a coin's thickness per year. Between the cushions: crustose lichen in map-patterns, orange and black and a mineral pale green, the actual dominant vegetation of the continent by area. In the shadow of overhangs, moss, blackened and desiccated by day, swelling within minutes of the fog's arrival. In the silt of the pans, salt-tolerant rosettes with roots twenty times the length of their leaves. And along the meltwater grooves, a single woody thing, waist-high at most, cork-barked, growing horizontally: the only source of anything you could call wood, and protected by rules so old that nobody remembers there being a time before them.

### What lives

Far more than the plants would suggest, because the plants are not the base of it. The base of it is the fog and the lichen and the insects.

There are colonies. This is the word for the plateau's ecology. A single ridge holds a burrow-city of stout grazing rodents at densities that make the ground sound hollow — thousands per ridge, whistling, clipping cushion-plants, cropping lichen with specialised teeth, and it is their whistles, more than anything, that constitute the sound of the world. There are hare-things with translucent ears. There are herds of a horned ungulate the size of a small pony, blue-grey, that move across the basins in files of two and three hundred, following the fog line, cropping the salt-rosettes; they move almost soundlessly, because the pan silt is soft and their hooves are broad, and a herd can be a kilometre off and utterly inaudible. There are the predators that follow them: a long-legged cat that hunts at the rime hour, a wolf-analogue that is small and works in pairs, and a soaring bird with a two-metre wingspan that does nothing at all until the sun is high enough to make thermals off the dark ridges.

And twice a year, for perhaps ten days, the sky fills. Migratory waterfowl cross the plateau in numbers that whiten the fog. They come down onto whatever standing water there is, foul it, eat everything in it, and leave. The plateau's largest single deposit of nitrogen arrives on wings and departs. Everything here — every crop terrace, every fishless pan, every child's chore — is arranged around this.

### The city, and the cities under it

There is a settlement in the basin called by its people something that translates as *the Ninth Sill*, and it holds perhaps forty thousand people, and it is entirely made of stone, bone, dung, felt, and hair.

It is dense because heat is expensive. Buildings share every wall they can. The standard unit is a half-sunken room, three metres by four, floor dug down into the gravel, walls of drystone corbelled inward, roofed with rafters of ungulate legbone and rib lashed with hair rope, then reed matting, then turf, then a course of flat stones. Rooms accrete against rooms; a family's holding is a chain of five or six of them, and above and beside it another family's chain, and the roofs are the streets. You walk over the town on flagstone paths at roof level, past smoke-holes plugged with felt, down ladders into the dark, and the whole mass of it holds warmth the way a loaf holds warmth, so that the deepest rooms in the oldest blocks never freeze even in the long cold.

The infrastructure is water infrastructure and it dominates the skyline. Along every ridge above the town run the combs: frames of split bone and cork-wood, taller than a person, strung with a close vertical warp of black hair rope, thousands of them, kilometres of them, standing in ranks like a crop. Fog passes through and leaves itself behind. The rope drips into troughs cut in stone, the troughs feed channels, the channels feed cisterns, and the cisterns — corbelled domes, plastered with a burnt-gypsum lime, sunk into the rock — are the actual wealth of the place. A family's standing is the volume of its cistern. A marriage is, in the legal documents (scratched on slate; there is no paper), a merger of catchment.

Below the combs are the terraces: not for grain — there is no grain here — but for the cushion-plants, which are farmed, and for a cultivated salt-rosette whose taproot is the staple starch, and for the dung heaps, which are managed like orchards. Dung is fuel, and fuel is the second wealth. The herds are driven in each evening to sleep in walled folds so that the fuel does not walk away.

Tools are stone, bone, horn, fired clay, and a small amount of copper, cold-hammered from nodules found in a particular seam in the rim and worth roughly its weight in cistern-space. There is no wheel here — nothing to roll on, and every load moves on backs, human and animal, in wicker frames. There are no bells. There is almost no ringing sound in the entire city, which is part of why it is so quiet: no metal, no wheels, no sawmills, felt underfoot, wool everywhere, and the thin air itself swallowing volume, so that a voice at fifty metres is a thread. People speak quietly by habit and consider loudness a kind of clumsiness. The loudest things in town are dogs, children on the roof-paths, and the flat wooden clack of the fog-comb inspectors testing rope tension.

### The slow thing

Here is what governs everything, and what no single life is long enough to see.

The plateau is still rising. Something like the width of a thumbnail per year — a metre in three or four generations. The fog, however, does not rise. The fog arrives at whatever altitude the moist layer sits at, which is set by the ocean far away and does not care about local uplift. So the fog line, measured against the rock, creeps *downward* — which is to say the land climbs slowly up out of its own water.

The consequence is that this city is the ninth of its name, and the previous eight are still there, uphill, dry.

Above the current terraces the slope is covered in them: courses of drystone with their roofs collapsed inward, cisterns full of dust, comb-frames rotted to their post-holes, and above those, older ones half-buried, and above those, only rectangles legible in the lichen — because lichen grows differently on worked stone. The city quarries its ancestors. It is cheaper to carry a dressed lintel down two hundred metres than to cut a new one, and so every wall in the living town contains stones from the dead towns, some of them recognisably shaped for purposes nobody now uses. The bone-yards are up there too, the ossuaries left behind, and it is understood — this is not religion so much as bookkeeping — that the dead stay at the altitude where they died, and that the dead are therefore always uphill, and that the direction of the past is *up*, and the direction of the future is *down and inward*, toward the centre of the basin, toward the wet.

Nobody in the Ninth Sill will move. Their grandchildren's grandchildren will begin the Tenth, forty metres lower and eight hundred metres inward, on a plan already surveyed and marked with cairns, in an argument that has been running for two hundred years about whether the lower catchment will actually hold.

### At the rime hour

Stand on a roof-path before dawn. The fog has come up to the level of the fourth terrace; you are just above it, and it is entirely white and entirely still, and the ridge across the basin is an island in it. Everything within reach of your hand is furred with ice — the rope railing, the felt roof-plug, the rim of a clay pot someone left out, your own sleeve. Somewhere below in the white, invisible, a herd is being let out of a fold: you can hear the wooden gate-bar and then nothing.

The sun comes over the rim and hits the combs first, and ten thousand hair ropes go from white to black in a wave that takes about a minute to cross the town. Then the dripping starts, everywhere at once, a sound like a very large soft animal drinking, and the channels begin to talk, and the marmot-colonies on the sunlit ridge start their whistling, and the fog in the basin thins from the top down and is gone by the second hour, leaving the pan glossy and dark and the salt-ring blazing.

By midday it will be dry, hard-shadowed, dark blue overhead, and cold, and the loudest thing in a hundred kilometres will be a rock falling off the rim.
