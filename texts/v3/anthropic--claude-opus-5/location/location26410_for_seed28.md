## The Throat and Its Forty-One Quiets

### Approach

You hear it two days before you find it, and for those two days you walk in the wrong direction.

The plain is gypsum, flat as a poured floor, white enough that the sun arrives twice — once from above, once from underfoot — and the horizon dissolves in a band of glare. Nothing grows here. Nothing crosses. There is no water anywhere in this country and has not been for a very long time; what fell as rain in some earlier age went into the rock as sulfate and stayed.

The sound comes across that emptiness as a low pressure in the chest before it becomes a noise in the ear. A fundamental somewhere near sixty cycles, with a sour stack of harmonics riding on it, and above those a thin continuous shriek like a wet finger on glass, never resolving, never pausing for breath. Hot air over hot ground bends it. Walk toward the loudness and the loudness moves; the plain throws the roar back off its own polished surface so that at midday it seems to fall from directly overhead, from the whole white sky at once. Guides find the place by walking a slow spiral with their heads turning steadily side to side, tasting the gradient with both ears, distrusting it, correcting. They keep resin plugs in their ears and pull them out for a count of three, once every hundred paces, and by evening their nostrils are bleeding and they are perfectly certain of the way.

Then the ground simply stops.

### The Pit

There is no rim of upthrown rock, no hill, no warning. The plain runs level to a lip and drops sixty paces into a shaft that widens as it goes, an inverted bell of pale limestone and gypsum, terraced and slumped and honeycombed, perhaps ninety paces across at its belly. At the bottom, in a floor of black scoured stone, is the Throat: an opening no wider than a threshing floor, exhaling.

What it exhales is hot and dry and does not stop. Carbon dioxide mostly, with hydrogen sulfide, a little ammonia, free hydrogen, a smell of struck matches and turpentine and hot iron. The gas comes up at a speed you can lean against. It has been coming up long enough to have polished the flue smooth, long enough for the whole bell of the pit to have found its resonance and settled into it, the way an old instrument settles. The roar is the Throat's own note, sung into a stone bell, sustained without a single breath for however many thousand years.

The heat at the floor is enough to bake dough on a flat rock. The heat at the lip is merely the heat of an oven's outside.

### The Lattice

Now the thing that makes this place a city rather than a hole.

A standing wave in a bell has nodes: surfaces and points where the pressure cancels itself, where two halves of the same sound arrive opposed and annihilate. In the Throat's bell these nodes lie about three paces apart, arranged in nested shells and struts, an invisible architecture of quiet hanging in the loud air.

The city is built on them. Only on them.

Forty-one cells at present. Each is a small drum of stone and lashed reed-stem no larger than a person kneeling with arms out, plastered smooth, shuttered with plates of selenite that let the white light through in slabs, and each is suspended in the general emptiness — cantilevered off a terrace, propped on a single carved column, slung on a bridge of laid stems, or simply sunk into the wall where the wall happened to intersect a good null. Between them: nothing. Air. Three paces of unbearable noise on every side of every room. From the lip the city looks like a scattering of pale seed-pods in a vast empty jar, connected by threads. The density of the built world here is almost nothing per volume; the volume is the loud part, and no one lives in it.

Step across a threshold and the roar does not diminish. It *ceases* — not muffles, ceases, as if a hand closed over the world. Your ears ring in the vacuum it leaves. Then, after a minute, you begin to hear the inside of your own skull: the surf of blood, the grind of the jaw, a high tone that belongs to the ear itself. People come out of a deep still with their expressions rearranged. Children are not left in them alone.

Speech in a still stays in the still. Nothing said inside carries out. This is understood to be the reason the place was settled, and the reason it is worth the noise.

### Drift

The nodes move.

As the day heats, the air in the bell thins, the speed of sound in it climbs, the wavelength stretches, and every node in the lattice slides outward and upward by a hand's breadth, then two, then a forearm. By noon a room built for the cool hour is no longer in its quiet; the null has migrated into the empty air outside its window, and the room is full of thunder.

So the city is occupied in shifts, and the shifts are astronomical rather than social. A given cell is habitable for a stretch of the day and uninhabitable for the rest, and its neighbor three paces down and out inherits the quiet as it leaves. People move all day, carrying their bedding, following the silence around the inner face of the pit like beads of mercury tilting on a plate. Some cells are good at dawn. Some are good only in the hour before the wind of the Throat shifts at dusk. Two cells, built long ago on a node of the seventh harmonic, come into quiet twice a year for about nine days, and are the most valuable property in the world, and are used for dying in.

By every bed there is a shallow drum with a pinch of sulfur sand on the skin. In a null the sand lies dead still. When the node begins to leave, the sand walks — creeps, then dances, then leaps — and a sleeper who does not wake to the sight of it wakes instead into the roar, which has been known to be fatal, or worse, permanent.

The same instrument is the surveyor's. Sanded drums on jointed sticks, plumb bobs, knotted strings, tuned rods of baked clay: this is the whole technology of the place, that and copper chisels soft enough to sharpen with a stone, and lashings, and lime plaster. They have no metal worth the name and no need of it. What they have instead is a two-century-deep tradition of measuring the invisible geometry of a sound with sand and patience, and building into it by hand.

The young survey, because they can still hear, and hearing is the only way to know that the sand is not lying. The old build, because by fifty everyone here is stone deaf, and a deaf mason can work all day out in the loud where a hearing one would come apart. Both are necessary. Between them, the work of the city is a conversation across a gap that no one can speak across, conducted in hand signs and in scratched marks on plaster, and in the fact that the buildings stand.

### The Garden

Nothing here drinks. The pit is lush anyway.

Out of every crack in the terraces grows the reedwood: hollow stems, silica-stiffened, waxed against the heat, growing in glossy thickets of purple-black and liver-red and a deep bruise-violet, with fleshy blades that turn edge-on to the noon and flat to the morning. It does not use water. It splits hydrogen sulfide instead, taking the hydrogen, laying the sulfur down as fine yellow granules that sift out of the leaf-joints and collect in drifts along every ledge like pollen. Sunlight does the work; the Throat provides the reagent. Its sap is a clear terpene oil, fragrant, and so flammable that fire is the single absolute prohibition of the city. There is no water to fight it with. Nothing is ever cooked over a flame here — dough goes onto the hot black floor-stones near the Throat, and the bakers work in leather masks in the sour wind, and the bread comes up with a faint taste of matches.

The reedwood is also the loud part. Every hollow stem is an organ pipe standing in a gale, and the shriek above the fundamental is the collective voice of the garden. Which means that gardening, here, is tuning. Cut a thicket on the fourth terrace and the whole harmonic stack shifts, the nodes slide, and eleven houses go bad at once. The pruning of the reedwood is regulated more strictly than marriage. Old men who have not heard anything in thirty years argue in signs about the length of a stem, and are right.

### What Lives Here

Almost nothing. A place this loud is a place with almost no animals in it.

There are the borers: soft flightless moth-things, pale as candle-wax, that lay in the reedwood joints and whose grubs eat the pith and open new holes in the stems. Every hole is a new voice. Every new voice detunes a house. The borers are the great enemy, hunted with hooked sticks and with fingers, and their grubs are the only meat in the city, roasted on the floor-stones, tasting of resin and salt.

And there is the one thing that hunts the borers: a lean six-legged animal the length of a forearm, blind, translucent, that lives out in the loud where no one goes, and hunts by pressing itself to a stem and feeling for the wrong vibration. It never enters a still; a still is, to it, a blank space with nothing in it, a room made of death. People see one perhaps twice a season, gripping a stem out in the thunder, patient, and consider it lucky, and leave it alone.

### The Long Slump

The pit is widening. A finger's breadth a century, the gypsum sweating and shedding, the terraces slumping. As the bell grows, its note flattens, and the lattice of nodes swells with it and walks slowly outward across the walls.

So the honeycomb: every terrace is pocked with the shells of stills whose quiet left them two hundred, four hundred, eight hundred years ago and never came back. Plastered drums standing open and full of roar, sulfur silting up in the corners, reedwood growing out through their selenite windows. Nobody demolishes them. They are structure now, and shade, and a record of where the silence used to be, and the masons build the new cells with their backs against the old ones, laying course on course of a city that has been in perfect balance with its own dissolution for longer than anyone's genealogy runs.

Forty-one rooms. Forty-one people, more or less, spaced three paces apart in a hole in a white plain, in a bath of light, in a noise you could feel from a day's walk away, each one sitting in a small round pocket of absolute silence, listening to their own blood, waiting for the sand to move.
