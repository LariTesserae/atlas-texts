## The Chamber Called Second Growth

You will hear it long before you can say anything about it, because there is no light here and there never has been. Not dimness. Not the almost-light of caves where a distant shaft leaks blue. Zero. The eye gives up its office within a hundred breaths and hands everything over to the ears and the fingertips and the roof of the mouth, and after that the place begins.

The sound is a drone in the low register, loud enough to press on the sternum, loud enough that two people standing a pace apart would have to lean in and shout. It comes from everywhere and slightly from above. It does not stop. It has not stopped. Within it, if you hold still, you can pick out the grain: a fundamental like a bowed cable, a beating just off-unison as though two enormous reeds were tuned a hair apart, and above that a fine dry rattle, like seed in a husk, which is in fact seed in a husk, many thousands of them, shaken by the same pressure that drives the drone.

The chamber is small. That is the first surprise. Cross it in forty paces if you go slowly and keep one hand out. It is a hollow inside something living — a gall, a swelling, a chambered lung in the flank of an organism whose body you will never see the edges of. You are inside the plant. There is no outside available to you.

## The Floor and the Fabric

The floor is warm-cool, about the temperature of a stone kept in a shaded room, and it is not soil. Run a palm over it: dense, close-grained, faintly waxed, with a nap to it in one direction like the pile of a rug. It is wood, if wood can be a floor that grew as a floor. It has been walked and worn and rounded by nothing but its own slow rubbing against itself, for so long that all its sharp features have gone. Every ridge here is a low swell. Every corner is a fillet. The whole interior has been sanded down by age the way a riverbed is sanded by water, except that there is no water and never was, and the abrasive was time and a faint constant vibration and the dust of the plant's own shedding.

The dust is important. Ankle-deep in the low places, it is the consistency of flour milled from bone, and it is entirely plant: powdered cellulose, spent resin, the sloughed inner skins of a body that has been renewing itself very slightly, very slowly, for an unreckonable while. It does not stick to you, because nothing here is damp. It falls off. It has no ambitions.

## Dry Chemistry

There is no water. This is a fact you can taste — the air is so dry it strips the back of the throat, and it smells of turpentine and cold pepper and something like burnt sugar left overnight. The plant does not run on water. It runs on oil: a clear, thin, non-freezing terpene sap that moves in its walls through channels you can feel as raised cords, and if you press one, it gives like a full vein and returns.

Once in the chamber, at the base of the north wall, there is a single node that weeps. Not water — a bead of the oil, forming over a span of perhaps a hundred days, hanging, falling. It makes a sound when it lands. You will not hear it over the drone; you would have to put your ear to the floor and wait, and know when to wait, and no one knows.

Cool, dry, dark, oiled. This is the whole weather of the place. There is no wind except the pressure-breath of the host, which comes in a long cycle: the drone rises a quarter-tone and swells for something like nine days, then sags back over eleven. That is the only season. It has had that season a great many times.

## The Town

Now the strange part, which you will find with your hands.

The chamber is organized. Not chaotically vaulted, not lumpy as a cave — laid out. Radiating from the center are seven lanes, low-walled, each about a shoulder's width, each running to a cluster of cells. The cells have doorways. They have thresholds, raised and worn to a shine. They have shelves in their walls at exactly the height a hand reaches at rest, and small round flues in their ceilings, and in the larger ones a bench along the inner curve. Some of the cells are stacked: a second course above the first, reached by treads that are unmistakably treads — nine of them, each dished in the middle where feet would have fallen, if there had ever been feet.

There have never been feet.

Nothing lives here and nothing has. The town is grown. The host makes architecture the way an oak makes a burl or a wasp-gall makes its own little cathedral of tissue — as a byproduct of its own logic, following rules it does not know it has. The lanes are not streets; they are the plant's way of distributing pressure and sound so that the chamber does not rupture at its thin places. The shelves are lignified braces. The flues vent gas. The stairs are simply what happens when a growth ring meets an internal fold at a certain angle, over and over, for a very long time.

But knowing that does not stop it from being a town. Layered, infrastructural, organized, hollow. And ancient beyond the point where the distinction matters. The oldest quarter, at the chamber's low western end, has slumped and softened until it is only a suggestion of thresholds under the dust, a topography of former doorways, a flattened field of civic memory. Nine-tenths of the volume in here is empty air. That is the character of the place more than any wall: enormous quiet space between the few standing presences, and the drone filling all of it, and nobody at all.

## The False Tools

In the cells you will find tools. This is the thing that undoes people.

Adzes. Awls. A blade the length of a finger with a tang and a wrapped grip. Needles in a graded set, thickest to finest. Wedges. A small mallet with a bound head. They are all of one material — the same dense oiled wood as the walls, or a harder resinous version, amber-black and heavy — and they are all perfectly, plainly, hand-shaped, worn where a hand would wear them, blunted where use would blunt them.

They grew. There are nodes along the base of every cell wall where they form, one every few years, dropping when ripe like fruit that has decided to be a chisel. The plant has been iterating a set of forms since before there was anyone to have a use for them, and it has refined them, and it will keep refining them. It has no hands. It has never seen a hand.

You may pick one up. It will fit.

## The One Moving Thing

There is one thing here that goes about. It is the size of a loaf and shaped like a curled tongue, dry, chitinous with hardened sap, and it is not an animal — it is a piece of the plant that detached and kept its own small hunger. It crawls the floor, grazing dust and spent resin, at a rate of perhaps a body-length a day. Its track behind it is a shallow polished groove. There are old grooves everywhere, crossing and recrossing the town, generations of them, worn into the flooring like the wheel-ruts of a road; that is much of what has flattened the old western quarter.

It makes no sound you can separate from the drone. It has no eyes to lose in the dark. If you set your hand in front of it, it will detour without any sign of having noticed, and if you set your hand in front of it every day for a season, its track will bend around that place permanently.

## Rules

- Nothing here is wet. Anything wet that arrives will be dried, cracked, powdered, and swept to a low place.
- The dark is not a condition; it is the medium. There is nothing here that light would improve, and the host has no tolerance for heat.
- Sound is the only weather. Learn its cycle and you have a calendar. That calendar is the only clock in existence within these walls.
- The town was not built and cannot be finished. It also cannot be ruined; it can only be worn down, and it is being worn down, and it is renewed at very nearly the same rate. It has been at that balance for a long, long time. This is what the end of a landscape's life looks like when the ending is stable: no ruin, no growth, just the flattest possible version of itself, held.
- Anything you leave behind will be incorporated. Slowly. It will be domed over in resin, then walled in, then, in some number of centuries, it will appear as a shelf.

## The Long View

Stand at the center where the seven lanes meet, ankle-deep in flour, one hand out to nothing, and let the drone do the describing. It maps the space for you if you turn your head: the flutter where the sound spills into the open lanes, the dead pockets in the doorways, the ringing overhead where the ceiling thins to a membrane no thicker than a fingernail. Beyond that membrane the host's pressure works, and the reeds sound, and the seeds rattle, and none of it is for you or about you.

It is a small room. You could carry the whole town away in a wheelbarrow, if you did not mind killing it. And it is a city, entire, hollow, cool, dark, oiled, worn down to its final form, playing one enormous note that changes twice a month by a quarter-tone, with a set of perfect hand tools laid out on every shelf, waiting to be picked up by nobody, forever.
