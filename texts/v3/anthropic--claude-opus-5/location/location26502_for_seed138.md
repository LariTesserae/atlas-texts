## Kettle

The land here is younger than the buildings on it.

Nine years ago — or ninety, the story does not vary in its shape, only its numbers — a shrug somewhere under the seabed lifted a shelf of mud and shell out of the water, and the water ran off it and did not come back. What is left is a flat about the size of a large farm: grey-brown, glossy, wrinkled the way the bottom of a bucket is wrinkled. Nothing has cut into it yet. There are no gullies, no channels, no rounded edges. The rim of the flat is a lip of clay standing a hand's height above the lagoon, and that lip is *sharp* — you can see the layers in it, bands of shell and silt still in the order the sea laid them down, the topmost band still bright with dead cockles that have not lost their gloss. Put a boot through it and the print stays. The prints from the first survey party are still there, a line of them going out toward the west, filled with clear water, holding.

The flat is low. It is so low that the horizon is nothing but a change in colour: pewter below, pearl above, and where they meet a smear of steam. Standing on the highest thing in Kettle — the coal-crane gantry, ten metres of riveted lattice — you can see the whole world, and the whole world is about four kilometres across.

It is hot. Not the hot of sun; there is no sun, or there is one somewhere behind the vapour ceiling, delivering a flat white glare that never localizes into a disc. The heat comes up out of the ground and sideways off the pans and out of the boiler house doors, and it has no daily curve. The steam ceiling holds it in and diffuses the light down to a permanent late-afternoon dimness, so the electric lamps burn all hours: carbon-filament bulbs in wire cages, strung on poles down the boardwalks, running orange-dim off a generator that was never sized for the job. They do not brighten. They do not flicker. Each one has its own halo of midges, a fixed sphere of them, and the spheres are the same size in what would be morning and what would be night.

Everything wet, nothing raining. That is the first rule to learn. The sky never breaks. The water comes from below, where the table sits two fingers under the crust and rises into any hole you dig, warm and faintly bitter; and it comes out of the air onto anything cooler than the air, and the only things cooler than the air are the intake pipes bringing lagoon water to the works. So the pipes weep. They run on trestles above the boardwalks, six-inch iron, furred with a green-black crust, and beneath each joint there is a small permanent pool, and beneath each pool a small permanent stain, and the stains do not spread. The rust on the corrugated iron of the sheds is the deep red of wet brick and it has stopped. It reached the depth it wanted and quit. You can scratch it with a nail and find bright metal a millimetre down.

The works themselves: twenty-two evaporation pans, laid out in a shallow arc, each about the size of a tennis court, bermed with the same clay the flat is made of, and only nine of them are working. The rest were dug and left. The nine hold brine at three colours — the far ones green, the middle ones the pale of skimmed milk, the near ones almost mauve from whatever lives in salt that concentrated — and their level never changes. Evaporation and seepage have found each other and shaken hands. On the berms the salt crust grows in plates that look exactly like frost, and to walk on them in that heat is a small permanent joke of the senses: the crunch says winter, the air says stokehold.

The town proper is thirty-one structures on pilings. Not many, but they are *built*: framed, braced, sheathed, plumbed, wired — a real settlement's worth of construction, thinly spread. Between them there is a great deal of nothing. That is the character of the place, more than the heat even: infrastructure at wide intervals, the density of a village with the apparatus of a city. A boiler house with three riveted boilers and a stack of black brick that leans four degrees off plumb and has leaned exactly four degrees for as long as anyone has cared. A pump shed. A crystallizer shed with a floor of packed salt polished to marble. A tramway on wooden rails, gauge about sixty centimetres, running from the pans to the tipple, with four tubs on it — always four, always two loaded and two empty, at the same points on the line, which is one of the things visitors ask about and get no answer to. A machine shop that smells of hot oil. A bathhouse. A shrine the size of a wardrobe with a tin roof and a lamp in it that is the only light in Kettle noticeably brighter than the rest. A dormitory row of eleven identical rooms, of which seven are occupied. A chandler's with stock on the shelves and dust on none of it.

And it is *loud*. This is the thing that gets into your teeth. The pumps run on a two-stroke thud, offbeat with each other, a slow arrhythmic pounding that comes up through the pilings and into the soles of your feet and stays in your body after you leave. Steam sighs from six or seven relief valves in a chord that never resolves. The tramway tubs creak on their axles without moving. Somewhere a whistle blows for no shift change and never at an hour you can predict; and the birds — the birds are constant, a roaring shingle of sound off the salt banks, ten thousand terns and stilts and a nation of small waders, screaming over ground that has no predators on it yet because the flat is too new for anything four-legged to have found a way across.

The fauna is the wealth here. Fiddler crabs work the whole surface of the untaken flat, one to every hand's breadth, pale as candle wax with one red claw each, and when you walk they go down and the plain seems to blink. Mudskippers hold the shallow margins, propped on their fins, mouths open. Egrets stand in the pans up to their knees in brine that ought to kill them, and don't die. Kites turn overhead in the vapour, four of them, always four, at four heights. In the boiler house there are rats grown enormous and slow and unafraid, and eight dogs live under the dormitory and have divided the boardwalks between them by treaty. Everything eats and nothing seems to increase.

The vegetation is a list, not a landscape. A hundred and some mangrove propagules have rooted in the lagoon margin, and every one of them is knee-high, and every one of them has been knee-high since it took. Two clumps of grey succulent on the west berm. A fig, planted, in a kerosene tin outside the bathhouse, holding eleven leaves. A green film of algae on the north face of everything. That is all. There is water everywhere and nothing grows tall, because growing is a kind of change, and change is the resource this place lacks.

That is the last rule and the biggest one. Kettle is at equilibrium. Not dead — very much not dead; it is noisy and hot and crawling with life and its machinery is under load. But nothing here is going anywhere. The mud has stopped compacting; the buildings have finished settling and hold their tilts. The salt production matches the salt shipped. The stack leans four degrees. The lamps hold their dimness. The pans hold their levels, the crabs hold their spacing, the kites hold their altitudes, and the footprints of the first surveyors sit out on the new ground full of clear warm water, holding their edges, sharp as the day the sea left.

People come to Kettle to work, and stay, and do not particularly age, and eventually walk out along that line of prints toward the west and are not missed, because the dormitory row keeps its seven.
