## The Combs of Ashael, or Whatever the Ridge Is Called Where You Are Standing

Begin above the cloud, because there is nowhere else to begin. The cloud is below you, a white plain going out to every horizon and taking the curve of the world with it, and the cloud is also above you, thinner, higher, a second ceiling of the same white — and both sheets carry the same grey stain, the shadow of this massif, cast down and cast up at once by a sun that cannot be doing both. Between the two cloud-planes there is perhaps four thousand metres of clear, cold, blinding air, and standing in it, saturating it, crowding it, is the mountain.

It is not one mountain. It is a congestion of stone.

---

**The rock and its habit.** The material is a pale banded silicate that splits into leaves, like slate that has never quite decided to be slate — ash-grey, bone-grey, a warm ochre where iron has wept through. It cleaves at a fixed angle, always the same angle, so the whole massif has one grain running through it, upper-left to lower-right, and every fracture, every ledge, every collapsed face obeys it. Ten thousand parallel shelves. Walking here is walking on a tilted library.

Through the grey stone run veins of a clear glassy mineral, some of them a finger wide, some of them broad as a road. These are the lenses. Where the sun strikes a vein squarely it enters the rock and comes out somewhere else — a hundred metres down the ridge, out of a wall's shadowed side, out of the floor of a gully — and so there are suns in the ground. There are suns in the undersides of overhangs. At mid-morning you may stand at a junction of three veins and have four shadows, splayed like the fingers of a hand, and only one of them is doing what you are doing.

The pinnacles: this is what makes the place dense rather than merely vast. Erosion has been at work here a very long time and has reached its settled habit, and its habit is to leave columns. They stand shoulder to shoulder, thirty and sixty and two hundred metres high, so close in places that you pass between them with one palm on each and the sky becomes a bright crooked seam overhead. Aisles, chimneys, blind courts. A stone thicket. The eye keeps trying to read them as trunks and keeps being corrected: no bark, no branching, no green, only bedding-planes and the wet gleam and the small white blooms.

---

**The blooms.** They are not lichen. They are salts.

Where water seeps out of the rock and evaporates in the cold sun, it leaves gypsum and other pale minerals in forms that are frankly, offensively organic — rosettes, coral fans, fibrous tufts like hair, curling ribbons that grow *away* from the stone in defiance of everything. In the shaded aisles they mat the walls hand-thick. They are brittle as spun sugar and they make a fine bright crunch underfoot, and they have a smell, faintly bitter, faintly of struck flint and old milk.

They also compete. This is the ecology: a bloom that grows fast is a bloom that grows soluble, and the next wet cycle dissolves it and reprecipitates it further down, where it overgrows a slower, harder colony and smothers its seep. Aragonite fans encroach on gypsum needles. Something black and metallic — manganese, probably — spreads in dendritic sprays that look exactly like a fern's shadow and eats the pale forms by staining them. There are successions. There are old scars where a great fan collapsed under its own weight and left a bare patch that is being recolonised at the rate of a hand's breadth per year. There are seasons in it. There is nothing alive in it at all.

No animal has ever been here. No spore, no seed, no wing. The air is too clean; it has a scoured quality, like the inside of a bell.

---

**Water.** The mountain sweats. That is the plain truth of it. Cold stone standing in humid air between two cloud-sheets does what a cold glass does in summer: it takes water out of the sky. Every north face is beaded. Every overhang drips. The aisles between the pinnacles run with thin sheets of water that spread out flat and slick over the cleavage planes and go down in a thousand small vocal threads, and where they pour off a lip they hang for a moment as a bright rod and then break into a drifting veil that blows back onto the wall it came from.

Some of it goes up. On the eastern buttresses — where the local gravity has gone slightly wrong, or has always been slightly wrong, since nothing here is broken, only settled — the seeps run *upward* along the grain, gathering into a slow ascending creek that leaves the summit ridge as a rising plume and joins the upper cloud. Standing under it you feel your inner ear argue with your eyes. The stone is not steeper there. It is simply that down is oriented a few degrees off from what your body brought with it, and the water knows the local convention and you do not.

Below the melt-line, ice. Every night the seeps stiffen, and every dawn they let go, and the sound of that letting-go is one of the great sounds of the place.

---

**Sound.** It is loud here. Not the loudness of a storm; the loudness of a workshop.

The base layer is water — the constant grained hiss of a hundred small falls, the deeper knock of drip on hollow slab, the glassy tick of the salt blooms taking on weight. Over that, wind combing the pinnacle field, and because the columns are of many different heights and thicknesses the wind plays them, and the massif has a broad ragged chord that shifts through the day, mostly in the low register, with occasional single pure notes from some chimney that has found its length.

And over *that*, the hammering.

It comes in bouts. A hard, deliberate, unhurried knapping — stone struck on stone, six or eight blows, a pause, six or eight more. It comes from around the next buttress. It always comes from around the next buttress. Follow it and it relocates without ever seeming to have moved. It is not an echo; echoes here are worse than that.

Here is the local rule about sound, and it is best learned early: **the report arrives before the event.** Somewhere between one heartbeat and one full day early, depending on how far you are from the summit. That crack you heard at noon is the block that comes off the north wall at dusk. The hammering is not a maker at work. It is the shattering of stone that has not yet chosen to fall. A person could, in principle, live by this. Some did.

---

**The made things.** They are scattered, small, and completely without doubt.

*The combs.* On every windward saddle, standing out of the scree, are palisades of thin slabs set on edge, two hundred, three hundred to a rank, each slab a hand's width apart, each notched along its lower edge so that the water it strips from the passing fog runs to a point and drops. Beneath the ranks are troughs cut into bedrock and lined with a black tar-like mineral, and from the troughs run channels, and the channels go — went — somewhere. They are drystone work, no mortar, no metal, shaped with hard cobbles by someone with an enormous amount of patience and time and a very exact eye for the grain. They still work. They work beautifully. They fill their basins every night and the basins overflow every morning and the overflow goes down the channels and out of a broken aqueduct into two thousand metres of empty air.

*The shelters.* Perhaps one per valley-head: a low corbelled dome of stacked leaves of the same stone, the roof a single split slab, the doorway low enough to require kneeling. Inside, dry. Inside, a floor swept clean, a hearth-ring that has never held fire because there was never anything to burn, a stone lamp with a cup for oil that was never oil. Tally marks on the lintel, in groups of eleven, some lintels bearing a few dozen groups and one bearing thousands, worn to shallows, recut over the top of themselves.

*The hands.* On the sheltered walls of the aisles, sprayed outlines: a hand laid flat, pigment blown around it, the pale stone left as the negative. Ochre and manganese. Four fingers and a thumb. Correct size. Hundreds of them, layered over each other, the older ones fading under salt bloom, the newer ones crisp — and there is no dust on the newest, and the pigment cup is standing beside it, still damp, and the damp is only condensation, and the condensation is nothing, and you should keep walking.

---

**The iteration.** This is the thing that takes longest to notice, and once noticed cannot be un-noticed, and is the real character of the place.

Walk the main ridge westward and you will pass a distinctive spire — a leaning column with a hole through its upper third, the hole shaped like a pear. Twenty minutes on, you pass it again: leaning at the same angle, hole in the same place, but the hole is smaller and the column's edges are sharper. Twenty minutes more: smaller still, sharper still, a young thing, the fracture-scars still bright. Go the other way and it ages: the hole widens, the lip thins, the lip breaks, the column stands headless, the column is a stump, the column is a raised patch in the scree with the salt blooms making their slow fans across it.

Everything here is like this. The ridge is not a place with a history; it is a history laid out as a place. Erosion has not happened to this landscape — erosion is a *direction* you can walk in, and the massif holds every stage at once, side by side, dense, layered, near-touching, which is exactly why there is so much of it per unit of sky.

Which raises the question of the shelters, and the tallies, and the handprints, and it answers it too, in a way you may not enjoy. There is one shelter, and there are forty of it. There is one maker, and there are however many stages of the making. Somewhere east along the grain a shelter is a heap of unsorted slabs beside a scored outline in the dirt. Somewhere west it is a mound with grass-coloured salt growing out of the collapse. And in between, at the settled middle of its own life, the roof is on, the floor is swept, and the tallies are being cut at a rate of eleven per — per something.

---

**The rules, briefly, for anyone who finds themselves here.**

Count nothing. The cairns disagree with each other and with themselves; a spur marked with nine stones on the way out carries six on the way back, and both are correct, and the walk back takes a third of the time and you will not have hurried.

Do not follow the hammering. Note the bearing and stay off that face until tomorrow.

Trust the water for direction, but only the water that is falling. The water that is rising belongs to a different down.

Your shadows will not agree. The one under your feet is yours. The others are of the same person at other erosional ages, doing what he did or will, kneeling, carrying, striking, stopping at last with a hand on the lintel — and if you watch them long you will begin to walk in step with one, and that is how it starts.

And it is bright. That is what one remembers after the strangeness has worn to an ache: the sheer scoured brilliance of it, the double cloud-plain glaring white above and below, the suns coming up out of the floor, the wet stone throwing light back so hard it has weight, the salt fans burning like frost that will not melt, and everywhere the sound of a great deal of water going nowhere at all, very fast, and the patient knocking of a stone that hasn't fallen yet.
