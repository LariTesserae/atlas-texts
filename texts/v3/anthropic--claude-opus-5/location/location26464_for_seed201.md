## The Combing Ground

**I. The shelf**

At four thousand-odd metres, on the north face of a mountain that has no name because no one has ever seen the whole of it at once, there is a shelf perhaps two hundred paces long and sixty deep. It is the only horizontal thing for a vertical kilometre in any direction. Above it the rock goes up in slabs so freshly split that their fracture faces still hold the shine of the break, blue-grey, faintly greasy-looking, arranged like the pages of a book dropped and stepped on. Below it the rock goes down and does not stop going down; the eye slides off the edge and finds nothing to hold until it reaches, very far away and very far below, a floor of cloud that is not a floor.

The shelf is not a ledge worn by weather. It is a piece of the mountain's interior, exposed. Somewhere under this range a plate is still shoving itself under another, and the country is being pushed up faster than rain can take it down — a few millimetres a year, which is nothing, which is enormous. So the mountain is young in the way a wound is young. Its edges have not rounded. Its screes are angular, stacked at the steepest angle loose stone can hold, and they clink rather than crunch. Nothing here has had time to become soil except in one place, and that place is the whole point.

**II. The Gap and the green**

Along the shelf's western end the ridge above is cut by a notch, a clean V perhaps twelve metres across, as though something narrow had been withdrawn from the stone. Through the notch, for six or seven hours of most days, comes cloud.

It does not fall as rain. This is a dry sky — the air has been wrung out on the far side of the range and comes over thin, glassy, hungry. But the notch faces the one direction from which moist air still climbs, and when that air lifts and cools it goes white, and the white pours through the Gap in a slow silent rope, and the rope frays out across the shelf, and the shelf combs it.

Combs it with plants. There is a mat here, thick as a mattress in places, and it should not exist at this altitude. Cushion plants first: domes of silver-grey rosettes packed so tightly you could set a cup on them, each rosette bristling with hairs that are not hairs but condensers, and along every hair a bead of water. Then mosses in the lee of the cushions, four species that I can distinguish and probably a dozen I can't, going from rust to gold to a green so saturated it looks wet even when dry. Then, in the deepest pockets, willows — real willows, woody, ancient, sixty or eighty years old, and eleven centimetres tall, running horizontally under the moss with their leaves poking up like a scatter of coins. Saxifrages in the cracks, their roots holding fracture-faces together as if apologising for the mountain's haste. A grass whose seed-heads are transparent.

You could walk the length of the green in three minutes. It is a room, not a country: a garden the size of a granary loft, hung on the outside of the sky. And it is *dripping*. Under the mat, water moves. Put your ear to the cushion plants and you hear a fine ticking, thousands of individual droplets migrating downslope through moss, arriving at the shelf's lip, and departing as a mist so fine that it never reaches anything below. The shelf makes its own small weather and consumes it entirely. Beyond the reach of the Gap's cloud-rope — twenty paces east — the stone is bone dry and bare, and stays bare, and the boundary between garden and desert is as sharp as a coastline. You can stand with one boot on moss and one on grit.

**III. What lives**

Here is the strangeness of the high aeolian country: it is crowded.

The wind that comes up from the valleys carries freight. Aphids torn from crops eight thousand feet below. Moths, midges, thistledown, spider silk, pollen, beetles, ash, one entire dragonfly a week. All of it lands cold and stunned or dead on the shelf, and all of it is food, and so the shelf supports a fauna that eats rain from the sky in the form of insects.

Springtails, first — millions. Turn a stone at the moss margin and the surface *boils*, grey grains vaulting away on their spring-tails, silent, dense as spilled flour. Then the spiders: a jumping spider the colour of the fracture-faces, palm-sized in leg-span, which does not spin but stalks; there are perhaps four hundred of them on the shelf and each holds a territory the width of a hand. Beetles that are more or less permanent adolescents, taking eleven years to mature, black and glossy, patrolling the same six metres of grit for a decade. A cold-loving fly with wings reduced to stumps because at this altitude flight is a way of being deleted. A single species of harvestman with legs like drawn wire, standing over the moss at dawn on tiptoe to keep its body out of the frost.

And above them the vertebrates, which are few in number but constant in presence: choughs, six or nine of them, black, red-billed, absurdly good at flying, who arrive from the void every morning without any visible effort of climbing. A pair of accentors that nest in a fracture. Something furred and heavy-bodied that lives under the eastern scree and comes out at noon to sun itself and does not run when you approach because it has never been given a reason to. Snowfinches. Once, tracks: broad, four-toed, pressed into moss, going along the shelf and then over the edge, with no tracks returning.

So the census is high and the density is nothing. The shelf's biomass, all of it, would fit in a wheelbarrow, and the volume of the world it occupies is measured in cubic kilometres of empty freezing air. The living layer is a film — millimetres of moss on a face that drops for a mile. Life here is two-dimensional and abundant; the third dimension is a void with birds in it.

**IV. Sound**

There is almost none, and the reason is arithmetic: thin air carries less. At this pressure a sound loses its body. The choughs call, and the call arrives already old, half-erased, as if heard through a wall. Rock falls across the cirque — a hundred tonnes of slab letting go, dust plume, the whole event playing out perfectly and mutely, and eleven seconds later a soft *whumph* like a book closed in another room. Wind through the Gap does not roar; it hisses, and mostly it doesn't do that either, because the Gap smooths it.

What you hear instead, if you hold still: the ticking of water under moss. Your own blood. The dry rustle of a chough's feathers as it passes two metres away, which is louder than its voice. And frost — at dusk, when the temperature crosses over, the whole shelf begins to click, ten thousand tiny expansions, a sound like a fire that gives no heat.

**V. What was built**

At the shelf's eastern end, where the stone is dry, there is a shelter: a rectangle of dry-laid slabs, waist high, roofless now, roughly three paces by two, with the slabs chosen so well that the walls have not shifted despite frost, despite the mountain rising underneath them. A doorway faces north-west, toward the Gap. Inside, a floor of smaller stones laid flat, and a hearth-ring in which there is charcoal, and the charcoal is from wood that does not grow within two days' walk.

Around the shelter: nine cairns, none taller than a knee, spaced along the lip of the drop at irregular intervals. Some hold a single flake of quartz on top. Sunk in the moss near the Gap there is a trough — a slab hollowed by patient percussion, no metal marks, just the shallow pecking of a harder stone used ten thousand times — set to catch the drip from an overhang. It still catches it. It is full, and its water is very cold and tastes of nothing at all.

Elsewhere, scattered: a bone awl. A twist of cordage made from the transparent grass, still holding its twist. Three blades of grey stone, one broken. A chough feather bound to a splinter with sinew, function unknown. And on the underside of an overhanging slab, out of the light, in soot: a row of eleven short vertical strokes and, beneath them, one horizontal.

No one has been here for a long time. The moss records footsteps for perhaps three seasons; there are none. But the trough is full, and the shelter walls stand, and the whole assemblage has the quality of a room whose occupant has gone out.

**VI. Rules**

The shelf keeps its water. Nothing that condenses here descends; it evaporates from the lip, rises, cools, comes back through the Gap. On a still bright day you can watch the same cloud make three passes.

The green cannot move. It is precisely the shape of where the cloud-rope lands, and the cloud-rope is precisely the shape of the Gap, and the Gap is being widened by frost at some rate of millimetres per century — so the garden is very slowly changing shape, and will one day be a different garden, and then, when the notch has been cut down to nothing and the cloud spills over the ridge in a sheet instead of a rope, no garden at all. This is not a tragedy anyone will observe.

The light is the dominant fact. At this height there is nothing above to soften it: the sun does not so much illuminate as press. Shadows are absolutes with hard edges, purple-black, cold to stand in — a difference of fifteen degrees between one side of a stone and the other. The moss is dark to protect itself and the cushion plants are silver to reflect, and every leaf on the shelf is either armoured or hiding. The choughs fly through the light without appearing to be affected by it. In late morning the snowfield across the cirque throws so much glare back at the shelf that the underside of everything is lit, and the whole garden seems to hover, shadowless, on a floor of white fire, and the silence goes with the light, and for a while the place is not quite convincingly real.

**VII. A day**

Before dawn: hard frost. The harvestmen on tiptoe. The moss stiff, crackling. The trough skinned with ice, and the ice tilted, because the drip has kept working under it.

Sunrise, sudden — the shelf faces the wrong way for a long dawn, and instead the ridge opposite ignites and the light arrives sideways as a rebound. Thaw begins at once. The mat softens, sags, begins to tick. Springtails rise through it in a slow grey tide toward the surface warmth.

Mid-morning: the choughs arrive, one at a time, out of the void, without apparent effort. They walk the moss margin, probing, taking the night's cold-stunned aphids. The jumping spiders come out and take up their stations. The furred thing appears at the scree and lies down.

Noon: glare. Everything stops moving in the open. The Gap begins to smoke, and the cloud-rope comes over, and the temperature falls eight degrees in a minute, and the garden drinks.

Afternoon: cloud and clear alternating, rope on, rope off, wet and dry, ten times. Somewhere a slab lets go, silently. Insect freight arrives on the updraught in pulses, whole squadrons of exhausted midges. The beetles patrol.

Dusk: the rope withdraws. Sky goes green, then a hard dark blue with no evening in it. The clicking begins. The choughs leave downward, dropping off the edge like thrown stones. Frost.

Night: stars without twinkle, because there is not enough air to make them tremble. The cushion plants, which spent the day hoarding, breathe out. The moss stiffens around the small bodies of everything asleep in it. And the mountain, imperceptibly, on some schedule no one on the shelf can attend to, comes up another fraction of a millimetre out of the earth, carrying the room, the nine cairns, the full trough, and the garden that is the shape of a hole in a ridge.
