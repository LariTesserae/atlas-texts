## The Cellar Garden

Four metres of gravel, silt, and frost-shattered stone lie between this room and the weather. Above, an upland of dwarf willow and grey lichen, wind-scoured, treeless, the kind of country where the ground stays hard until midsummer. There is nothing up there to mark the place except a fold in the hillside where the turf has slumped over a lip of poured stone, and a single step, and then the dark going down at a shallow angle, more a burrow than a stair.

You go down eleven paces and the wind stops. That is the first thing: the abrupt end of moving air. The second thing is the smell — wet stone, cold iron, and under it something startlingly green, sappy and bruised, like a crushed stem, though nothing green grows here in the sense you mean.

---

The room is perhaps nine metres by five. Low ceiling, arched, the vault cast in one piece from a grey material that has weathered to the texture of soap. Where water has run down it for a very long time, the surface has gone velvet with mineral. The floor slopes half a degree toward the far corner, where a basin the size of a washtub has been cut into the rock and never once, in all the seasons, run dry or overflowed.

Everything else is plants.

Not green ones. The moss carpet underfoot is the colour of old ivory and oxblood, ankle-deep and springy, so saturated that each step presses out water which then wicks back in behind your heel. Above it stands a mid-storey of stiff fronds — a fern-shape, but leathery, felted on the underside, in shades of bruise-purple and translucent bone. They grow shoulder to shoulder, near enough that walking means parting them with your forearms, and they are cold to touch, colder than the air. From the vault hang curtains of thread-plant, metres of it, fine as wet hair, that brush your face and then cling.

There is no light. Not dim light — none. Your eyes will manufacture grey shapes and drifting sparks for the first ten minutes and then give up. The garden is known by hand and by nose and by the soles of the feet.

---

**What feeds it.** Run your fingers into the wall behind the moss and you will find the threads: a lattice of filaments finer than hair, grey-white, set into the cast stone in a mesh that follows no pattern the hand can read. The plants are rooted to it. Their roots are pale, thick, and clasping — nothing like feeder roots, more like the grip of a hand — and where root meets filament there is a slight tackiness, a wetness that is not quite water.

The lattice runs on the difference between here and the surface. Four metres of soil is a very good thermometer that lags very badly: the heat of July arrives down here around November, the cold of January around May, each wave flattened and smoothed and months late. Between the buried rock, which barely changes, and the strata above, which do, there is always a gradient — a trickle of temperature falling perpetually downhill. The lattice takes it. It takes hardly anything, a whisper of power per square metre, and hands it to the roots as electrons, and the plants breathe carbon out of the damp air and build themselves from that. Photosynthesis without photons. Slow as hell. A frond takes eleven years to unroll.

This is why they have no chlorophyll and no need of it, and why the pigments they *do* have — the ivory, the oxblood, the purple — are not for catching light but for shedding the last useless traces of it. Which is why a torch is a violence here. A single hour of white light bleaches a square metre to the colour of paper, and the paper takes a decade to be green-black again.

**What waters it.** Nobody had to plan it. Meltwater finds the seams every spring and comes through the vault in three or four reliable places, drop by drop, and the drops keep a rhythm you can set a heartbeat to. Everything else is condensation. The air in here sits at the dew point almost permanently; the ceiling sweats; the fronds sweat; you sweat, and your breath comes back to you as a faint fog you can feel on your own cheek. In deep winter, when the surface cold finally arrives, the entry passage grows a beard of frost crystals, feathered, and the basin skins over, and for six weeks the garden runs dry and holds still. Then the wave passes and it drinks again.

Nothing is truly wet and nothing is ever dry. Damp is the whole climate.

---

**The made things.** Someone built this and left. Not dramatically — there is no sign of hurry. Set into one wall is a shelf of the same cast stone, and on it eleven ceramic trays, crazed glaze, stacked in two leaning piles, the bottom ones fused to the shelf by two centuries of mineral. A wooden handle, gone to sponge, lies where it fell; the tool it belonged to has left only a rust-shadow in the moss, a russet outline like a leaf print. A drain grille at the low corner, iron, half-eaten, entirely overgrown, still draining. A cast bench near the entry, cold enough to hurt.

And a tab of metal at eye height by the door, stamped with lettering that the mineral has filled in and smoothed over so completely that reading it is a matter of running a thumb across a bump and knowing that once there was a word there.

That is all the building. Nobody has come to tend the place in a very long time, and the place has not required tending. It found its balance and settled into it — the drip rate, the moss line, the eleven-year frond, the frost beard, the basin's level. Established. In its own state. If the makers walked back in they would find their garden intact and no longer theirs.

---

**Sound.** Mostly quiet, and the quiet has an edge to it, because there is a note. A very low hum from the lattice, too low for the ear, felt in the molars and the sinuses, steady as long as the gradient holds and dropping away entirely for a few days at the turns of the year, when the surface temperature crosses the deep temperature and there is nothing for the mesh to lean on. Those are silent days. Old visitors' notes call them the *slack*, and say the plants shed water on those days, and the room smells more strongly of sap.

Otherwise: four drips, out of phase. The creep and tick of the fronds expanding as your body heat reaches them. A faint sucking under your boot. Far off, muffled through metres of dirt, the wind — not heard, exactly, but present as a pressure that comes and goes.

**No animals.** No wingbeat, no scuttle, no web across the passage, no gnat spiralling in the dark. Nothing here needed pollinating; these plants divide, fragment, and sporulate, and have never made a flower. The absence is loud. You notice it as a wrongness before you can name it, the way a stopped clock is noticed.

There is animal life, technically. Squeeze a handful of the moss and the water that comes out is thick with rotifers and water bears and nematodes, thousands of them, a whole invisible pasture. They dry to dust in the slack and swell back to life in the thaw. Nothing here has eyes.

---

**The rules, if you want them.** Heat is the currency, and you have brought some, and the room will spend the next several hours metabolising you: fronds turning very slightly, moss exhaling. Stand still long enough and you become weather. Anything left behind that holds warmth — a coat, a hand pressed flat — will be grown over, slowly, from beneath. Nothing rots at speed; a footprint in the moss stays legible for eight or nine years, softening at the edges like a memory. Light is a wound. Silence is the resting condition. And the whole place is patient in a way that is difficult to be near: it is currently doing something that began before you and will be finished long after, and the thing is only this — the July heat, buried, walking down through the dirt toward a room where nothing can see, arriving in November, and being turned into leaves.
