## The Bowl

You find it by going downhill, which is easy, because everything here goes downhill. This is a floodplain country — silt, ox-bow scars, warm rain three afternoons out of five — and the land lies so flat that a drop of ten meters counts as a landscape event. The Bowl is that event. A shallow depression maybe four hundred paces across, rimmed with a lip of packed clay and old flood-wrack, and inside it, filling it to the brim, something that is at first hard to read as either a settlement or a plant.

The first thing you notice is not the shape. It is that the air over the rim is trembling.

Not visibly. You feel it in the soft parts of your face. Stand with your chest above the clay lip and your knees below it and you are in two worlds: from the waist up, an ordinary damp afternoon, cattle egrets, the click of grasshoppers, a distant river. From the waist down, a roar.

The sound pools in the Bowl like water. It has a surface. It has a level, and the level rises and falls a little through the day, and where it lies against your body you can feel the exact line, a cold shimmering band across your ribs. Ducking below it is like ducking under water: a pressure, a closing-over, and then you are inside and the world is only noise.

## What is in the Bowl

A town. Eleven streets, if you count generously; the plan is roughly radial with a large paved plaza slightly off-center. Walls of two or three storeys, doorways, lintels, stairs with worn treads, window openings, a colonnade along the north arc, low arcades whose ceilings sweat. Kerbstones. A drainage channel running along each street to a sink in the plaza. It has the settled, unsurprised look of a place that has been the way it is for a very long time, and has been rained on for all of it.

Nothing in it was built. It was grown, and it is still growing, at the pace at which a beech thickens — which is to say, the town is a fraction of a hair wider than it was when you came over the rim.

The material is a wood that goes green under the surface and brown at the face, waxy, close-grained, warm to the palm as a horse's flank. It takes a polish. Everything here has taken a polish, not from use but from vibration: the sound scours its own architecture smooth. Run a hand along a doorjamb and it feels like the inside of a shell. Where the flow of sound is fastest, at corners and thresholds, the wood is glassy and slightly translucent, and you can see, an inch or two down, the pale ropes of the vessels going by.

The walls are not solid. Tap one — you will not hear the tap — and you can feel that it is a bundle of tubes. Every column is a reed. Every room is a hollow. The plaza's paving is a laminate of leaf pressed into slate-like sheets, and beneath it there is a void the size of a barn.

## Why it is empty

There is nothing in the rooms.

This is the strangest thing about the Bowl and the last thing you understand. Forty-some interiors, dry-ish, warm, undamaged, and each of them contains nothing but stacked air. No drifts of leaf-litter in the corners. No nests. No dust — dust cannot settle here; it is held permanently in suspension, a faint mineral haze at knee height that never comes down. Set your bag on a floor and within an hour it will have walked itself to the doorway. Within three it will be outside in the street. The floors are minutely, endlessly convulsed, and they sort. Anything with mass migrates out.

The emptiness is the point. The town is an instrument, and an instrument must be hollow.

Up in the highest galleries, in the third-storey rooms nobody can reach because there are no stairs to them, the plant flowers: dull greenish panicles, hundreds of thousands of them, dry as paper, each holding a pinch of pollen too heavy for wind. The pollen comes out only when the structure rings. So the structure rings, continuously, and has for however long this has gone on — the growth grew rooms to be a resonating body, grew streets to be its ducts, grew a plaza floor over a void because it needed the drum, and grew the shapes of doorways and lintels not in imitation of any town but because those are the shapes that give the note.

That the result looks like a town is either coincidence or a fact about what shapes want to be. Standing in the plaza, you will not be able to decide. Nobody has ever decided.

## The sawyers

The noise is not made by the plant. The plant is only the body.

Inside the walls live the sawyers: a small dark insect, thumb-joint-sized, with a ribbed plate on each flank and no interest whatsoever in the outside. They are born in the wood, live in the wood, and die in the wood; the whole species is interior. There are, by any reasonable estimate, several hundred million of them in the Bowl at any hour. You will not see one. You will feel them as a granular texture in the surfaces, and in the mornings, when a brood matures somewhere behind the north colonnade, you will feel the wall you are leaning on go suddenly hot.

They do not chorus. They saturate. There is no rhythm in it, no pulse to hold on to, no rise, nothing to count. It is a flat continent of sound with a shape rather than a beat, and different streets are different pitches — Third Street a low grinding, the arcades a high thin scream you feel in your teeth, the plaza a note so deep that it isn't a note but a kind of nausea and joy.

At full level the loudness passes through *deafening* and out the other side. Ears stop reporting. What remains is not silence and not noise: it is a total presence, like being held. Some people weep. Nearly everyone, sooner or later, sits down in the middle of a street with their palms flat on the ground for an hour they will not remember spending.

## Abundance you cannot hear

For fauna the Bowl is rich past belief. It is only that sound is not available here, so nothing uses it.

Frogs the color of old wax sit in every window opening, throats pumping, entirely mute — they signal by inflating a lobe on the throat that is white on one side and black on the other, flick, flick, a semaphore going all afternoon down a whole colonnade. Birds here have gone showy and silent, flaring wing-bars to say what elsewhere would be sung; the local flycatchers have evolved a stiff, absurd, formal little bow. There are ants in vast quantity, running the drainage channels, farming a fungus on the sink-edge in the plaza. There is a soft-mouthed browser the size of a pig, six or seven of them, that eat leaf-laminate off the low walls and navigate by whisker; they are patient and they lean against people. There are spiders that hunt by feeling the wood, and a long green snake that hunts the spiders by feeling the same wood, and something larger and unphotographed that empties the highest galleries of sawyers once a season and leaves a smell of cut grass.

All of this occupies almost none of the volume. The Bowl is prodigiously alive and near-perfectly hollow: living things clinging to the surfaces of enormous empty rooms, the way barnacles occupy a harbor. Ninety-nine parts of the Bowl by volume is warm, humming, unoccupied air.

## Water, and the rain-hour

Water arrives three ways.

By condensation: the arcade ceilings sweat, because vibrating wood is warm wood and the Bowl's air is thick. The drops do not fall. Held by the shaking, they crawl sideways along the ceiling in beaded lines, gather at the arcade mouths, and are flung. Standing at an arcade mouth in the late afternoon you will be sprayed, gently, from a doorway with nothing behind it.

By seep: the Bowl is low, the water table is high, and the plaza sink is always brimming with a black, clean, faintly bitter water that shivers so hard it looks furred. Animals drink at it constantly and never seem to touch its surface.

And by rain — which is the important one, because rain is the only quiet.

When a warm front comes across the floodplain and the rain actually starts, the sawyers stop. All of them, within about ten seconds, for reasons that are theirs. And the Bowl empties of sound the way a bath empties, from the top down: the surface of the loud sinks below the rim, below the second storey, below your head — you feel it go past your face — down to ankle height, and then out.

What is left is a small warm town of green-brown wood in the rain, dripping, ordinary, the frogs suddenly all calling at once in a racket that seems, after the other thing, delicate as chimes.

The rain-hour is when the Bowl is real. This is not a metaphor and it is the one hard rule of the place.

## The rule

Under the sound, sequence does not hold.

Nothing dramatic happens; that is what makes it difficult. You walk up Third Street and arrive in the plaza. You cross the plaza and enter the colonnade and come out on Third Street, walking up. You have done this before. You will find your own footprints in the silt of the drainage channel ahead of you, going the way you are going, and behind you the channel will be smooth. You will meet the browsers again and they will lean against you again, and there will be, in the wood-dust on their flank, the shape of your hand from a previous meeting that has not happened.

The reason people give — and they give it during the rain-hour, when they can speak, sitting on the plaza kerb with their ears ringing — is that the loudness has no events in it. There is nothing in that sound to mark before from after. Memory needs edges. And so under the roar the Bowl becomes a single thickened moment several hundred paces wide, in which everything that occurs occurs at the same time, in eleven streets, forever, and you move through it as through a room rather than as along a line. You do not get lost. There is no *lost*: the town is small, you can see the rim from anywhere. You simply cannot get *out*, because getting out is a sequence, and sequence is what the Bowl does not have.

You leave in the rain. Everyone leaves in the rain.

The clay lip is slick then and you go up it on hands and knees, and at the top you turn around, and there it is below you: forty empty rooms full of stacked air, eleven streets with their drains running, a plaza with a black sink at the middle of it, all of it steaming faintly, all of it very old and entirely settled and needing nothing.

Then the rain thins. And you feel it start again down there — not hear, feel, in the clay under your palms — the level rising, the first galleries filling, the surface climbing the walls toward the rim, and the pollen going up off the high flowers in a fine dull cloud, over the floodplain, going wherever it goes.
