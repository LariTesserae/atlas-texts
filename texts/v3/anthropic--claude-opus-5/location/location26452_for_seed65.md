## The Plateau of the Long Note

**The rim**

You come up out of cloud, and then the cloud is beneath you and stays there.

The tableland begins at the top of a wall of pale welded ash some two thousand metres high, and once you are over the lip there is no more climbing worth the name — only forty kilometres of gently tilted stone going away from you in every direction, tipped a degree or two toward a northern horizon that shows nothing but more of the same. The cloud sea presses against the wall all day and never rises above a line you could mark with chalk. It is not weather. It is a permanent boundary, like a coastline, and the plateau is what stands out of it.

Because of that white floor below, the light comes from two directions at once. Overhead the sky is nearly black-blue at the zenith, and underfoot the cloud throws the sun back up at you, so that shadows are pale and filled in from below, and everything — stones, animals, your own hands — looks slightly unattached to the ground, as if lifted a finger's width. There is more light here than any surface knows what to do with. Pale ignimbrite, ninety percent of the ground, reflecting like fired clay. You squint from the moment you arrive. Bare skin burns in an hour, and the same hour it burns, the wind keeps you cool enough to shiver.

**The sound**

The wind comes from one quarter and does not change. Not seasonally, not diurnally, not in living memory or the memory of the memory. Somewhere out over the lowlands a pressure gradient has locked itself and the air pours across the tableland at a fixed forty knots, and it has done so long enough that the land has grown into the shape of the fact.

Twelve kilometres in from the rim the wind reaches the Flue Field, and this is where the sound is made. The ash-rock there is honeycombed — tafoni weathering, cavities the size of a fist and cavities the size of a room, pinnacles standing forty and sixty metres, every one of them pierced through and through like bone gone spongy. The air goes into that and comes out as a roar. It is not a howl and not a whistle; it is a broad low chord with a hard edge on it, somewhere near a hundred decibels at the base of the pinnacles and never below eighty anywhere on the plateau. You feel it under the sternum before you register it as noise.

Shouting is useless. A hand cupped to an ear against a mouth conveys perhaps one word in four. After three days something in the head gives up and reclassifies the roar as background, and then a strange thing happens: the plateau seems silent. A white silence, with a weight to it. People who come down from here go deaf for a week and hear the note in their skulls for a month, and some of them never quite stop.

The note itself does not vary. That is the difficult part to convey. It has no gusts, no lulls, no rising toward evening. It has been the same chord for as long as anyone has stood here to hear it, and the pinnacles that make it are eroding at precisely the rate at which the wind packs new ash into their windward flanks, so even the pitch holds.

**Ground and water**

The surface is stone stripes: sorted lines of cobbles alternating with fine grit, running dead downwind, kilometre after kilometre, ruler-straight. They were made once, by frost and time, and they are finished now. Walking across them is like walking over the corduroy of a vast combed field. Nothing disturbs the pattern. Even the animal trails run along the stripes rather than across.

Water arrives sideways. Each day the cloud sea leans a little further onto the rim and the wind drives fog through the first few kilometres of the plateau, and everything on the leeward side of everything grows rime — horizontal feathers of white ice, all pointing the same way, all roughly the same length, because they sublime in the sun at the rate they accrete in the fog. You can break one off and come back in a year and find its replacement the same size.

Beyond the fog belt the plateau is dry. There is one stream, the width of a stride, fed by seeps at the base of the pinnacles where condensation collects in the deep cavities and runs. It goes four kilometres and falls into a shaft in the rock — the Sink — and does not come out anywhere the valley people have found. Around the Sink is the only green worth the word: a fringe of moss and hair-grass perhaps thirty paces wide, humming with midges, absurd in all that pallor, like a coin dropped on a floor.

**What grows**

Almost nothing, and it takes a long time to do it. Cushion plants, hard as bone, tapered to leeward like little hulls; you can stand on one without marking it. Split one and you will find growth increments so fine you need water and good eyes to count them, and the answer will be four or five hundred. Lichen occupies only the lee faces — the windward faces are polished bare by grit, permanently, and the lichen line on every boulder points the same direction, so you can navigate by stones as reliably as by sun.

The vegetation is not what feeds this place. That is the strangeness of it, and once you see it you cannot unsee it.

**What lives**

The plateau is fed by the wind. Every day the moving air carries up out of the forests four thousand metres below a fine, unceasing traffic of insects: aphids, midges, small moths, mayflies, thrips, spiderlings on their silk. They rise, they chill, they stop flying. They come down over the tableland as a thin invisible snow, and in the lee of every stone stripe, every boulder, every cavity in the Flue Field, they accumulate in drifts — grey-brown, soft, faintly iridescent where the moth wings are, ankle-deep in the best-sheltered corners.

An entire ecology stands on that drift and touches no plant at all.

Jumping spiders live in the crevices in absurd numbers, thousands per hectare, hunting the fallout. Flightless beetles — the wind took their wings away, generation by generation, until only stubs remain — plough the drifts. Springtails in millions. A wingless cricket the length of a thumb-joint, which has no song and no ears, and which signals to its mates by drumming its abdomen against stone: the whole reproductive life of the species conducted in vibration, because up here sound is a wall and nothing can be said through it. A small pale fox that eats spiders and beetles and, in a good week, nothing else. Hares in the fog belt, cropping cushion plants down to nubs that regrow over decades. Wild sheep in scattered bands of nine or eleven, spread so thin across the tableland that you can walk a full day and see one band, twice.

Overhead, always, the swifts.

They are the plateau's great fact after the wind. Some millions of them, riding the standing wave off the rim, a slow river of black scythes turning above the Flue Field from first light to last. They feed on the same rising insect traffic, but higher up, before it dies. They do not land except to nest in the tafoni cavities, and their feet have gone nearly to nothing; birds knocked down by hail are found unable to stand. They sleep on the wing, up in the black-blue. And they fall, constantly, in ones and twos and hundreds — the cold takes them, the walls take them — so that beneath the colony there is a second drift, of feathers and small dry bodies, and beneath that a thin greyish crust of guano that has been fertilising the same forty hectares for so long that the moss there grows a hand deep and springy, the softest thing for thirty kilometres.

The choughs work that ground, and the lammergeier-thing works the sheep bones, and the fox works the edges, and none of them can hear each other, and all of them turn their heads constantly, because in this place vision is the only sense that carries.

**People**

Nobody lives here. Three or four times in a generation, parties come up the Feather Road from the valleys — six days of climbing — with bone hooks, sinew line, obsidian blades from an old vent on the western shoulder, and empty skins.

They come for two things. Feathers from below the colony, for arrows and for trade. And the drift: they scoop the insect fall out of the leeward pockets with wooden paddles and press it into cakes, which keep for years and which are worth, down in the valleys, a great deal. It is the strangest harvest in the world — a food gathered out of nothing, from a place that grows nothing, brought to the surface by air.

They cannot speak while they work, so they sign, and the sign language of the high valleys is full of gestures that only make sense up here: the flat hand tilted to mean *the wind is unchanged*, which serves as a greeting; the two-fingered tap on the breastbone that means *I hear you*, though nobody hears anything.

They build nothing. Partly because the wind takes anything smaller than a boulder within a season. Partly because the plateau already has all the shelter that is needed — the pinnacles' lee cavities are dry, out of the blast, and warm enough — and partly because of a rule that is not quite a taboo and not quite an opinion: that this is a place which has finished happening, and it is bad practice to start it again. So they leave what they always leave. Flakes. A scatter of ash from a dung fire. A worn paddle. Within twenty years the paddle is gone and the flakes are polished on one side and dull on the other, like everything else.

**Equilibrium**

That is the last thing to say about it. The plateau does not change.

The cloud arrives at the same height. The rime grows to the same length. The stripes lie where they lay. The pinnacles lose and gain and hold their pitch. The swifts wheel in the same direction over the same forty hectares. The drift falls, is eaten, falls again. The sheep number what they numbered. Nobody's grandfather described a different place, and the descriptions are unusually detailed, because when you cannot talk you look very hard at things.

Stand out on the stone stripes in the middle of it, in that shadowless doubled light, in a hundred decibels of unvarying chord, with nothing within a kilometre of you but a fox somewhere and a hectare of spiders under the rocks and a river of birds turning overhead, and you will notice that the sensation is not emptiness exactly.

It is more like being inside something that is already complete.
