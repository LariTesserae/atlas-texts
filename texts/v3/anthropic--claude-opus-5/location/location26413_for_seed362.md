## The Extent

### 1. What the ground is

There is no ground. There is the Deck: a laminate of lignin and pressed dust, laid down leaf-on-leaf, sheet-on-sheet, for something like four kilometers of depth before it reaches anything that could be called a floor — and what it reaches there is only the earliest laminae, the same material, older, harder, ringing like porcelain when the deep shafts tap it.

The Deck is honey-white and faintly translucent, the color of a fingernail held to a lamp. It is warm to look at and cool to touch. Walk on it and it answers with a dry tick, the sound of a beetle's back, once per step, no echo. It does not dent. It has been walked on by nothing, mostly, for a very long time, and the wear it shows is not the wear of feet or water — it is polish, an all-over satin sheen where the shed dust of the world has been dragged across it by wind for so many centuries that the surface has become a kind of lens itself.

Nothing here is natural substrate. There is no rock. There is no soil. If you dig, you find manufacture all the way down: fiber, glue, ducting, buried colonnades, spent flywheels of compressed wood the size of villages, part numbers still legible in the grain.

### 2. Light without heat

The sky is a ceiling and the ceiling is alive.

Three hundred meters up, the canopy closes — not a shade but an optic. The leaves of the Extent are transparent, structured in concentric ridges like the pressed-glass lenses of old lighthouses, and they are stacked in five or six lamina with a slow independent tilt. Light comes down through them focused, split, re-collimated. It arrives everywhere at once, from every angle, so that nothing in the Extent casts a shadow. You stand in a shadowless brightness like the interior of a snowfield, or a photographer's tent, or a well-lit workshop with no windows and no lamps.

But it is cool. The canopy takes the heat. Everything below the red end of the spectrum is combed out, absorbed, and pumped downward as work; what passes through is a thin blue-white radiance with no warmth in it at all. The temperature at Deck level holds near eight degrees, day and night, continent-wide, with a variance of less than one. You can stand in blazing light for a week and never sweat. You would not sweat here anyway. There is nothing to sweat with.

### 3. The dry chemistry

Water exists in the Extent as a currency, not a medium. It is sealed inside the tissues in tiny quantities, hoarded, ledgered, never allowed to become free liquid; if a vessel is breached, the surrounding tissue crystallizes shut within seconds, and a small dull thump is the only sign — the loudest sound many regions ever produce.

Everything else moves as powder.

The vascular system of the Extent is pneumatic. Hollow trunks the diameter of grain silos run in aisled ranks, and inside them, sugar-dust and resin flour and graphite fines travel on slow currents of air, pushed by valve-tissue that closes and opens with the softness of an eyelid. Sap here is a suspension. Nutrition is a freight schedule. Put your hand on a duct-trunk and you feel a granular hush passing, like sand in a hourglass at the scale of a river.

Because the world is dry, it is also charged. Static accumulates in the leaf-lamina until the canopy crawls with corona: violet fringes at every ridge and tip, a slow brushfire of discharge that goes on for hours and makes no thunder. Silent lightning is the only lightning there is.

### 4. What the factories make

The trunks are not merely trunks. Split one lengthwise and you find drive shafts of laminated fiber, dry-running on graphite bearings, turning at a hundred and eighty revolutions a minute with no more noise than a cat's breath. You find bellows the size of chapels. You find crystal stacks in cellulose racks, generating potential from the pressure gradient between root and crown, feeding filament glands that could arc-light a city — except there is no city; the light is used internally, for chemistry, in sealed bulbs of clear pith.

The Extent is an industrial plant in the full double sense. The distinction between grown and made was not lost here — it never obtained. There is one process, and it has one verb, and the products come off it warm and numbered.

And what it makes, chiefly, is *more Extent*.

Along the great fronts — the Extruders — new Deck comes out at a rate of two or three meters an hour, spooling from mouths a kilometer wide, hardening in the cool bright air, ringing very softly as it sets. New colonnades erect themselves behind it in surveyed ranks. This is why the Extent is vast: the vastness is a product. It has been in production for a long time and the schedule has not been revised.

This is also why the elevation stays low. The surface is always new. Nothing has been standing long enough anywhere to become a mountain, and the Extruders lay level, level, level, out to the horizon and past it.

### 5. The rule about copies

Here is where the world stops being merely strange.

Nothing in the Extent casts a shadow. What things cast instead is a **double** — a lower-resolution copy of themselves, offset by half a meter, appearing over the course of a few minutes, dry and light and structurally faithful for perhaps a day before it powders.

Everything doubles. A leaf casts a leaf. A shaft casts a shaft, non-functional, hollow. A dropped tool casts a tool that cannot cut. The Extent's entire dust economy — every gram of powder in the pneumatic trunks, every sift of resin snow, the whole polish on the Deck — is composed of collapsed doubles, swept up and reprocessed. The Sweepers are low, wide, plant-things of stiff bristle that graze the aisles on turgor-driven feet, harvesting the day's shed selves. They are the most numerous large organism in the world and among the quietest: a brushing sound like a page turning, repeated to the horizon.

From this one rule, the rest of the Extent's physics follows:

**Distance is not conserved.** Walk a straight aisle and you will pass junction 4-4-9. Walk further and you will pass junction 4-4-9 again, and it will be fresh, unweathered, and its double will be lying beside it looking older. Not the same junction. Not a different one, either. The Extruders make extent; they do not make it *unique*.

**Counting is a manufacturing operation.** Below about forty, arithmetic behaves. Above it, the act of enumerating produces what it enumerates. Count the leaves on a limb and you will get a number, and the number will be correct, and it will be correct because you said it. Surveyors who worked here — if any did — left instruments with the counting wheels deliberately broken off.

**Names are stored in tissue.** There are Archivist stands, dense low thickets of black-brown scale-leaf, each scale holding one fact or one designation. The part numbers legible in the bark all over the Extent are read out of these stands, continuously, by contact. Where an Archivist stand has burned or powdered, the region around it goes anonymous: the numbers in the grain are still there, still crisp, and cannot be read by anything, including the machinery that needs them. Those districts stall. They stand in their bright light, fully functional, indefinitely idle, waiting for a word.

### 6. Fauna, and why there is so little of it

Animals double too.

A copy of a machine is a dead machine. A copy of an animal is an animal. Within a season, one grazer becomes a herd; within a year, a herd becomes a weather system with teeth. The Extent solved this a long time ago, and the solution is still in place: the **Wardens**, which are not predators in any usual sense but immune bodies — tall, pale, jointed structures like standing looms that unfold at the approach of vertebrate motion and take the excess back into the dust economy. They are perfectly silent. They are also perfectly indifferent, and will take the original as readily as the copy, having no way to tell and no reason to care.

So there is very little fauna, and what there is has adapted to the rule rather than against it:

**The Fret.** A grey, six-limbed browser the size of a hare, which moves only in bursts, and never in a straight line for longer than four seconds. Its doubles, being faithful, do the same thing — but a moment later, in the wrong place. A Fret travels inside a small cloud of stale versions of itself, and the Wardens, unable to resolve the cloud into a single target, mostly let it pass. Where a Fret has been feeding, the Deck is scattered with dozens of crumbling grey hares, all facing slightly different directions.

**The Several.** A bird-analogue with a wingspan of a meter and a body of dry brown vane. There is no correct number of them. They exist above the counting threshold, permanently, and any attempt to establish a population produces one. Locals — if there had ever been locals — would have called them a mercy: they do the only thing in this world that resembles song. It is a two-note contact call, very soft, a fifth apart, and it is the reason that anyone standing in the Extent for a week will begin listening for the sky.

**The Tinker.** Palm-sized, flat, armored, a beetle in every way that matters. It eats doubles exclusively — walks up to a fresh copy of a leaf and mines it. It is therefore useful and therefore unmolested by the Wardens, and it is the only animal in the Extent whose life is easy.

### 7. Sound

The Extent is the quietest large place there is.

Everything in it is fibrous, hollow, and dry, which is to say: acoustic foam, four kilometers deep and a continent wide. Sound goes into it and does not come back. There are no hard flat surfaces except the polish, and the polish is convex. There is no water to slap or drip. All the machinery transmits power by tension and shear rather than by impact, and all of it runs in graphite, so its noise floor is a whisper.

The full inventory of ambient sound, taken at any point on the Deck:

- the granular hush of powder in the ducts, continuous, felt as much as heard;
- the tick of one's own steps, unreturned;
- the brushing of Sweepers, near or far, indistinguishable;
- the click of a canopy lens re-tilting to follow the light — sharp, singular, startling, perhaps four times an hour;
- the soft dull thump of a sealed water-vessel somewhere going into crystal;
- the whisper of resin snow arriving, when it arrives;
- the two notes of the Several.

Corona storms make no sound at all. Neither do the Wardens. Neither does the arrival of a new front of Deck, two hundred kilometers wide, ringing so faintly as it sets that the ringing registers in the teeth rather than the ear.

### 8. Weather

There are seasons, of a kind, and they are production schedules.

**The Broadcast.** Twice a year the canopy releases dry pollen at industrial volume — not for reproduction, exactly, but for specification: each grain carries a template, a revision, an updated part number. The air goes gold, the light thickens, the shadowless brightness turns the color of beer, and visibility falls to thirty meters for eleven days. This is the only period in which the Extent is opaque, and the only period in which it is warm — the pollen holds the infrared that the canopy usually strips, and the temperature climbs to a giddy nineteen degrees. Afterward, everything is very slightly different, and no one can say what has changed, because the Archivists have already updated.

**The Sift.** Resin snow. Fine, sweet-smelling, non-melting; it lies in drifts, and the Sweepers work double shifts, and for a month the Deck is soft underfoot and the steps make no tick, and the silence becomes total and slightly unbearable.

**Static Season.** Cool, clear, brilliant; the canopy crawls violet for weeks; every filament in the world stands on end; doubles form faster and coarser than usual, so that the aisles fill with rough grey approximations of things, half-made, crumbling, and the whole continent looks briefly like a warehouse of rejected drafts.

### 9. The condition it is in

Established. Balanced. Old.

The Extent is not decaying, because decay requires water and there is none; it is not eroding, because there is nothing to carve; it is not growing wild, because it is not wild. It is running. It has been running long enough that the running has become its own landform, and long enough that the question of what it was originally for has been shed, doubled, powdered, swept, and pumped through the ducts as freight.

There are seams, still, in the deepest laminae, where the geometry changes: an older module, different standards, part numbers running on a different scheme. Someone specified this. Something arrived here dry and cool and bright and began to make extent, and it never stopped, and it never will, and every hour it lays down two more meters of shadowless world and the Sweepers brush away what the world's own light has left behind.

If you stood in it — in the level colonnaded brightness, in the eight-degree air, in the silence, with a grey powdered copy of yourself standing half a meter to your left and beginning to slump — you would understand very quickly that you were not the subject of the place, and that nothing was, and that this was not a cruelty but simply the schedule.

And then, far off, over the aisles, in no particular number: two notes, a fifth apart.
