## The Dry Green Plain

Stand anywhere on it and the horizon is the same in every direction: a low grey-black floor, and on that floor a forest the color of old copper and verdigris, packed so tight it looks solid, and rising out of the forest at intervals of a few hundred paces the pale spires and buttresses of the mounds. The plain goes on like that for the width of a continent. It has no mountains. Its highest point is a shield volcano so broad and so gentle that you can walk up it for six days without ever feeling that you are climbing, and from the summit you see only more of the same, receding into a dust-colored haze, and in the far distance one horizon that glows faintly at night.

That glow is where the land is still being made.

**The floor.** The rock is new — new in the geological sense, which here means that some of it is still warm to the touch a hand's depth down. It came out as sheet lava, thin and fast, and it cooled into the shapes that fast lava makes: ropes, coils, gut-like folds, whole acres of it wrinkled like skin pushed by a thumb. Where gas collected under the crust, the sheet lifted into blisters and low domes, some of them the size of a cathedral, hollow, roofed in glass a finger thick. Where the flow drained out from under its own hardened lid, it left tubes: corridors running for tens of miles beneath everything, cool, still, absolutely dark.

Nothing has worn this landscape down. There are no gullies, no fans of gravel at the base of the pressure ridges, no soil in the ordinary sense. The broken edges of the flows are still sharp enough to open a hand. What little sand exists was not made by weather; it was made by mandibles and by root-acid, ground and secreted, and it is not lying loose on the ground — it has all been collected. This is a landscape being built rather than destroyed, and the builders got to the raw material before erosion could.

**The rule about water.** It never rains here. There is no river, no pond, no puddle, no morning dew, no wet stone, no mud. The air is so dry that a cut heals into a rind. And yet the forest is dense enough to stop a body from walking through it, and the animals are fat.

The water is underneath, and it is young. The cooling rock exhales it — steam wrung out of the magma, rising through joints and cracks, cooling as it climbs, condensing in the ceilings of the lava tubes as a film so thin it never gathers into a drop. That film is the entire hydrology of the continent. Everything living here is a device for finding it and holding it.

So the roots go down, not out. Each succulent sends a single taproot straight through the crust, sometimes forty feet, into a tube, and there the root frays into a white fan that spreads along the damp ceiling like frost. Above ground the plant is a reservoir with leaves: barrel trunks, candelabra arms, blue-green skin thickened into something between rind and cartilage, coated in wax that flakes off when you scrape it. There is no undergrowth because there is no light left over, and no leaf litter because nothing is ever wasted; the dead stems are cut up and carried away within a day. Nothing rots. Without free water there is no rot. Anything overlooked simply mummifies, standing where it died, going grey, and eventually goes into a mound as building stone.

Break a stem and you get not sap but a cool, faintly bitter jelly that you could cut with a knife. Everything on this plain that drinks, drinks by biting.

**The city.** The mounds are the work of an insect — a mason, pale, blind, half an inch long, working entirely by touch and smell, of which there are perhaps a hundred trillion here. There is no technology on this plain. There is no fire, no wheel, no worked tool, no wire. There is only saliva, ground rock, chewed plant paste, and about two million years of continuous construction, and that has been enough to build something a visitor would have no other word for than a city.

A single mound is a chimney complex thirty or forty feet high, ribbed and fluted, ivory where it is new and grey-lichened where it is old, with a base that spreads out in buttresses like the roots of a fig. But the mounds are not separate. They have grown into each other. Ridges of masonry run between them, arcades where two colonies met and neither surrendered, blind walls, fused pillars, and long low vaults thrown right over the tops of the succulents so that the plants grow up inside a stone room with an open ceiling of their own leaves. Under all of it the galleries run in three or four superimposed layers: transport tunnels wide enough to put an arm into, sealed and plastered airtight, descending to the tube systems; fungus chambers; nurseries; and the enormous slow-breathing lungs of the ventilation shafts, which take the heat of the day and use it to pull cooler air up from below.

There is no ground level here. There is only the most recent one. Dig down through the plaster and you find older mounds entire, roofed over and built upon, still holding their shape, filled with the fine dust of abandoned rooms. In places the accumulated masonry is eighty feet thick and the living lava is a memory beneath it. The masons' material, mixed and set and mixed again, is harder than the basalt it came from. They are the only force on this plain making stone faster than the volcano.

Because moisture is the only real currency, all the traffic is roofed. This is the single most important fact about the look of the place: an enormous population, and an empty surface. The foragers travel in covered ways, cutting the succulents from inside their own arcades, carrying jelly and rind down into the dark. The surface is left to the sun, the wax-bloom, the lichen, and the animals that can afford to be out in it.

**The animals.** Abundant, and mostly quiet. The great grazers are low, broad, armored things that move along the avenues between mounds at the pace of a slow walk, shearing off succulent arms with flat teeth and chewing for hours; they do not need to hurry and nothing hunts an adult. Their young are hunted, by long-legged runners with dark hides and enormous eyes that hunt at dusk and dawn and lie up all noon in the shade of the buttresses, panting silently, mouths open. In the crowns of the forest there are climbers with prehensile tails and drinking-tongues that bore neat holes in the thickest stems; the holes scar over into knots, and an old succulent is knuckled all over with them like a tree full of nail heads. Wasps make grey paper lanterns in the forks. Small lizards live in the fluting of the chimneys and go in and out of the ventilation slots, tolerated because they eat the flies. There are birds — heavy, bad fliers, walkers really — that nest on top of the mounds where the updraft keeps their eggs warm, and stalk the arcades eating masons by the thousand, and are the reason the arcades are roofed.

And in the lava tubes, in absolute darkness, on the frayed white root-fans and the fungus beds, there is a whole second ecology: pale shrimp-like things that have never seen open air, blind hunters with legs like wire, and colonies of masons that have not come to the surface in a hundred generations.

**Sound.** It is a quiet world, and the quiet is the strangest thing about it, given how full it is. The leaves are too thick to rustle; in wind they knock together softly, a sound like distant applause heard through a wall. The mounds hum — a low, broad, tuneless note from the ventilation shafts, more felt in the chest than heard. When a colony is alarmed, tens of thousands of soldiers bang their heads against the gallery walls at once, and the whole mound gives out a dry hiss like sand poured on paper, and then stops. At night the rock ticks as it contracts. That is most of it. No frogs, no streams, no rain on leaves — the absence of those three sounds is what makes the silence feel deliberate.

**Weather, and the pace of things.** The plain is big enough to make its own weather and it makes weather without water. By late morning the black rock has loaded the air with heat and thermals rise in columns you can see by the dust in them; by afternoon there are dust devils walking the avenues, ten or twelve visible at once, tall and slow, going nowhere. Twice a year the forest releases pollen and for a week the light turns the color of weak tea and everything, mounds included, is dusted ochre; the masons harvest the fallen pollen off their own roofs. There are false clouds — steam and sulfur haze standing over the vent fields — which never once in the history of the world have produced rain. Nights are cold enough to make you understand why the animals sleep against the mounds.

And along that one glowing horizon, the flow front comes on at the pace of a slow walk, five or six miles a year, burying the forest and the city under fresh sheet lava. Ahead of it the succulents seed; behind it, on rock still too hot to stand on, the lichen has already started. The masons abandon a mound the year before the flow reaches it and begin the next one out on the raw glass, and the city moves, entire, at the speed of the land being made.
