## The Bole Room

You do not arrive at it. You get inside it.

From the outside there is only the swamp: warm brown water lying still between the stilted trunks, a foot deep, two feet in the channels, the whole plain of it so low that the horizon is a smear of leaf and haze with nothing rising anywhere. Somewhere in that flatness one trunk has thickened at the base into a swollen boll, waist-high at first glance, then chest-high as you wade closer, then taller than you — a green-grey drum of living tissue with a vertical slit down one side, no wider than a hand's span, edged and beaded like the lip of a healed wound.

You turn sideways and press through. The slit gives, then closes behind you with a soft valvular sigh, and the swamp goes away.

---

Inside is a room. Four paces across at the widest, oval, the ceiling a low vault you can touch if you straighten your arm. That is the entire extent of this place. Everything else — the water plain, the canopy a hundred feet up, the whole warm continent of foliage — is elsewhere, and does not matter here, and will not be discussed again.

The walls are not wood. They are the pale flesh of a stalk, the colour of an unripe plum where the light is thin and of wet green glass where it is not, and they are cool to the palm in a way that the air is not. They give slightly. Under the give there is turgor, the resistance of a filled thing. A film of condensation stands on every surface in beads too fine to run, so that the room sweats without dripping, and your own skin cannot tell where your sweat stops and the room's begins.

The floor is a woven mat of aerial rootlets, fibrous, springy, worn to felt-smoothness along the two or three paths that traffic has taken for a long time. At the low end it dips into a sump the size of a washbasin — black water, ankle-deep, with a slow lens of oil turning on it. The sump breathes. Every ninety seconds or so it swells a half-inch and subsides, and the movement is not tidal, because there are no tides at this scale, and not respiratory in any way you can locate, and it has been doing this for longer than the swamp has had its present shape.

---

### The noise

The room is loud. This is the first fact of it and the last.

Along the eastward wall runs the trunkline: a translucent pipe of tissue as thick as your thigh, fused into the wall for most of its length but bulging free in three places where the tissue has thinned to a window. Inside it the water column is climbing. You can see it as a shudder in the light, a rapid strobing of pale interference, and you can hear it as a hydraulic hammer — a low, wet, industrial *thud-thud-thud*, faster than a pulse, absolutely regular, coming up out of the floor and into the arches of your feet before it reaches your ears. Interleaved with the hammer are clicks. Sharp, small, bright, dozens a second, like ball bearings dropped into a tin dish: air seeding into the water column and being crushed out again, cavitation and repair, ten thousand tiny failures a minute, all of them survived.

High on the opposite wall, above head height, there is a puckered aperture ringed with stiff hairs. This is the vent. It exhales, constantly, a warm gas-rich draught that smells of rising bread and cut grass and something faintly sour underneath, and as it exhales it plays the hairs like a reed. One note. It has been one note for centuries: a held organ chord, somewhere below middle, with a beat in it where two of the hairs disagree by a fraction. Nothing about the swamp outside affects the pitch. Storm, drought, midnight, noon — the note.

And behind everything, under the hammer and the reed, there is a hiss so continuous you stop hearing it and then hear it again with a small shock — the sound of traffic on the far side of the walls. The tissue is galleried. There are passages threading the whole thickness of the bole, and something is moving through all of them at once, in both directions, always, and the sound of that is rain on a tent from a great distance.

You cannot speak here at conversational volume. You could shout and not be answered. The room does not permit talk and does not need it.

---

### Warmth, and why it never varies

It is thirty degrees in the bole room. It is thirty degrees at dawn and at four in the morning and in whatever the swamp has instead of winter. The room makes its own heat: in a crypt behind the sump, sealed off by a membrane you can see the shadows through, the plant is running a slow fermentation of leaf litter it drew down through its own roots, and the fermentation runs warm, and the walls modulate it. Put your palm flat against the crypt membrane and you feel the heat swell into your hand, and then — after a second or two — feel the tissue under your palm cool, deliberately, compensating. It is thermoregulating against you. You are a thermal insult and you are being managed.

The light does the same thing. Three thinned patches in the upper wall admit a filtered green-gold daylight, enough to read a face by, not enough to cast a hard shadow. When cloud crosses far above, the patches thin further; when the sun finds a gap in the canopy, they cloud. The room has an iris. The illumination in here has not measurably changed in the lifetime of anything that lives in it, and this is the single most disorienting thing about the place — not the noise, not the heat, but the fact that after an hour, or six, you have no idea which it has been.

---

### Who is here

Abundant life; almost nothing visible.

A frog the size of a thumbnail, translucent-flanked, sits on the rim of the sump with its throat going. It is inaudible. It has been calling into this racket for its whole species' history and has evolved to call in the one narrow silence between the reed-note and the hammer, and other frogs, somewhere in the galleries, hear it.

Gnats stand in a loose column in the light from the middle window, holding station in the draught, spaced far apart, thousands of them across the whole gallery-system and eleven of them here.

One long-legged carrier ant crosses the floor on the polished path, alone, going somewhere. Another follows forty seconds later. This is what abundance looks like at this scale: a queue stretched so thin by the size of the room that you only ever meet it one member at a time. In the walls there are perhaps four hundred thousand of them.

A bird lands at the entrance slit and does not come in. It looks. It leaves. The slit is the width of a hand and the room owes the outside nothing.

---

### Worn things that look made

Erosion has been at work in here for a very long time, and erosion in living tissue takes the form of callus.

There is a ledge along the western curve, waist-height, flattened and glossed by whatever has rested on it, with a lip that turns up at the front exactly as a shelf's lip would. There is a shallow bowl in the floor beside the sump, smooth as a spoon, with a channel running out of it toward the water. There is a ring of hard corky scar-tissue near the ceiling, perfectly circular, a hand's breadth, where a branch was shed so long ago that the wound is now simply a feature. The entrance slit's edges are beaded and polished to the finish of a doorknob in an old house.

Nobody made any of this. Nothing here was ever built, carried in, cut, or fastened. Every surface in the room is one continuous organism, and every surface that looks like carpentry looks that way because use and healing, running against each other for centuries, arrive at the same forms that carpenters do. It is very hard, sitting on the ledge, to keep believing that.

---

### Rules

The room does not change. Cut the wall with a knife and by the time you have eaten and slept the cut is a raised white line, and by the second sleep it is the same faint seam it would have been anyway. Foul the sump and the sump clears. Bring in a metal object and leave it on the ledge; come back and it is where you left it, warm, beaded with condensation, neither rusted nor cleaned — filed. The bole room does not preserve things and does not destroy them. It absorbs them into its equilibrium and continues.

There is no night here, no season, no weather, and consequently no way to be early or late. The hammer keeps a tempo but not a time.

And it is not a shelter, whatever it feels like. Nothing in the room's design — and design is the wrong word, and the only word — has anything to do with you. The heat is metabolic. The pumping is transport. The reed-note is exhaust. The room is the working interior of a machine that grew, running at one setting, at full volume, in a warm shallow water-world, and has been running that way since before the channels outside took their present course.

You go out through the slit and the tissue beads close behind you, and the swamp — the whole open steaming plain of it, its herons, its dripping, its wind in a hundred feet of leaf — is *quieter than the room you just left.*
