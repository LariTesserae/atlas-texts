**The Drowned Room Under the Blackthorn**

---

Last February a field settled. Not dramatically — no roar, no cattle lost. A hollow simply appeared at the low end of a pasture where the water always stood longest, an oval of turf tipping in like a lid set crooked on a jar. By March the rain had filled it. What is down there now is about four paces by three, and no deeper than a tall man plus his reach, and it is one of the strangest rooms in the world, though nothing about it announces this from the surface. From the surface there is only a dark oval of standing water with blackthorn leaning over it, and grass drowned at the rim, still green, still trying.

Go down.

The first thing is the cold: seven degrees and steady, a cold with no opinion, the temperature of ground rather than weather. The second is the closing of the light. It goes fast — three feet of peat-stained water and the daylight is already a brown memory; five feet and it is gone entirely, not dimmed but *concluded*, as though the surface were a ceiling rather than a boundary. Below that there is no light in the ordinary sense at all. There is something else, which we will come to.

The shaft narrows and then, without warning, does not. The hole in the turf is the width of a cart. The room beneath it is wider than the hole by a good margin on every side — the roof shelves out into darkness, sandstone and packed clay, and the fresh fracture where the ceiling failed is still sharp enough to cut a hand. Nothing has rounded it. The break is only months old and the rock has that raw, unweathered smell that stone has when it has just been split, a smell that survives underwater somehow, a mineral flatness at the back of the throat. Grass roots hang from the crack, white and alive, twelve and fifteen inches of them, drinking.

---

**The weed**

The room is *full*. This is the thing that takes longest to accept.

From every surface — the clay walls, the shelved roof, the silt floor, the fallen slabs, each other — grows a pale weed in ropes and fronds and thousand-leaved mats, packed so tightly that there is no volume of water anywhere in the room larger than a cupped hand that does not have something growing in it. It is the colour of the inside of a shell. Its leaves are fine as fennel, and where two mats meet they do not compete; they interleave, they braid, the way two heads of hair braid, and you cannot find the seam. Push a hand in and the weed parts and closes without resistance and without hurry, and when you take the hand out the shape of it stays there for a while — a hand-shaped absence held open in the greenery, sealing itself over the following hour.

There is no light here for it. It grows anyway.

What it eats is duration.

This is the rule, and everything else in the room follows from it. The weed takes in the plain interval between one event and the next, the raw stuff of *and then*, and it builds its leaves out of that. In a season of hard growth the room becomes slightly cheaper in time than the field above it. Things down here have less *between*. A pebble knocked off a ledge does not fall so much as it is found to have arrived. A rising bubble is a bubble at the floor and then a bubble at the roof, with a thin greyness where its travel should have been, like a word with the middle letters missing.

---

**Sound**

For the same reason the room is nearly silent, and its few sounds are all too short.

A sound is a shape made out of time. Strip the duration out and you get a click. So: the drip that comes through the roof crack every forty seconds does not plink; it *ticks*, a hard dry tick with no ring to it, absurdly small for the size of the drop. Somewhere in the west wall, in the old mortar, isopods are working, and their working reaches you as a scatter of pinpricks, like sand thrown at a window from very far off. Once or twice in an hour the whole room makes a single soft thud, low, felt in the sternum rather than heard — the sound of a large slow thing settling by a hair. That is the room getting bigger. It has grown perhaps two hand-widths in every direction since February. It is not tunnelling and it is not collapsing. It is simply, patiently, more.

Everything else is the great padded quiet of a place stuffed with soft plants.

---

**Light as an object**

Now the thing about the light.

Scattered through the weed — perhaps forty of them in the whole room — are pockets of daylight. They are the size of plums, some the size of eggs. Each one holds a specific afternoon: you can see, inside the little globe, the exact colour of light coming through the blackthorn on some particular day, grain of dust and all, the yellow of four o'clock in late August, the flat pewter of a wet Tuesday. They hang in the fronds. They are perfectly clear and perfectly bright and *they do not illuminate*. A leaf half an inch from a globe of noon is as black as the floor. Put your palm behind one and your palm stays dark. The light is entirely inside itself.

The weed made them, or the weed keeps them, or both. If you take one — they lift out easily, cool and firm, like a peeled egg — the fronds around the gap discolour within a day, going the grey-brown of boiled nettles, and do not recover. So people who know about the room do not take them. There are perhaps six people who know about the room.

The rest of the seeing you do down there is not seeing. In darkness this complete, with the weed touching you everywhere, you develop an accurate picture of the room by pressure and by cold and by the fact that the plants tell you where they are. It is a wholly adequate way to know a small place.

---

**What was here before**

The field settled because something under the field was hollow. It had been hollow for a long time.

Along the north side of the room, mostly buried, runs a course of dressed sandstone blocks — five of them visible, the tooling still crisp, laid by somebody who knew the work. There is a step, single, going nowhere, its tread worn into a shallow dish by feet. There is a doorframe standing free in the middle of the floor with no wall attached to it and no door, its jambs socketed for hinges that have rusted to a red stain in the stone. There is an iron ring in the ceiling, and directly beneath the ring, in the silt, a shallow stone basin, and in the basin the weed grows thicker and finer than anywhere else, a white foam of it, brimming.

Nobody living knows what the room was. The reasonable guess is a wellhouse, a spring chamber, a place people came to fetch water and left small things behind. The doorframe argues with this. So does the fact that if you go through the doorframe — and it takes an effort of will, because it means putting your face into the densest weed in the room — the water on the far side is the same water at a different hour. Sometimes it is warmer by a degree. Sometimes there is grit in it that isn't there on the near side. Once, a man who had gone through came back with a blackthorn petal in his hair, in November.

---

**The animal**

There is one, and you never see it. You see the space where it has just stopped being.

It leaves a corridor through the weed — a clean-walled tube the thickness of a wrist, opening ahead of nobody and closing behind nothing, travelling at a fast walking pace along the wall and around the doorframe and away under the north slabs. It has done this perhaps nine times since the room flooded. Something eel-shaped, judging by the tube. It has never been in the tube. It is always the departure and never the thing.

Otherwise: the isopods in the mortar, pale as rice. Some translucent shrimp near the roof crack that come in with the rain and die of the room's poverty of *between* — they starve for interval, they cannot get a stroke finished. Their bodies do not rot. They hang in the fronds like commas.

---

**The slow year**

The room changes at a rate that would bore a stone. Two hand-widths of growth in six months. The weed thickening by a fraction. In the deep corner past the doorframe, down is beginning to lean — a dropped pebble there now arcs perceptibly toward the wall before it settles, and the arc is a little more each season, and in some decade or century that corner will simply have a different floor than the rest of the room, and the weed will grow sideways into it without commenting, since the weed has no opinions about direction.

Above, in the pasture, the blackthorn keeps leaning. The grass at the rim keeps being green underwater, which it should not be, and is, and will be. In August the light through the leaves goes a particular yellow.

Down in the dark it is being collected.
