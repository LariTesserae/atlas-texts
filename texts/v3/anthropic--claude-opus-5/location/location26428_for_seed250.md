## The Shelf Sea

Start with the fact that the sea is in the wrong place.

Four thousand meters below, there is another ocean — the old one, grey-brown, sluggish with sediment, the one that behaves. This one sits above it on a plateau the size of a continent, and it does not drain. At the plateau's edge the water comes right up to the lip and stops, standing in a lens perhaps eighty meters proud of the rock, curving over nothing, held by something that has no visible apparatus at all. If you stood at that edge — there is nowhere to stand; the edge is a vertical face of fresh columnar rock still ticking as it cools — you would see the sea's surface bulge outward past the brink and simply end, in a bright meniscus, taut as the skin on a bead of mercury. Light goes through it. On calm mornings you can look up through that overhanging lip from below and see sky through six meters of seawater, warped and green.

Nothing built holds it. There is no rim, no wall, no ring of towers. That is the first thing to understand about this place: the engineering here is not in objects. It is in the local terms of the world. Somewhere in the first hundred kilometers of atmosphere, or in the rock, or in a set of relations that don't have a location in the ordinary sense, the gradient has been bent — gravity here does not point quite down, it points slightly *inward*, toward the plateau's center, by a fraction of a degree that accumulates over two thousand kilometers into a bowl with no bottom lip. The sea lies in that bowl. It is a basin made of a preference rather than of stone.

You can feel this as a sound. Not metaphorically. There is a carrier tone, somewhere around eighteen hertz, that you hear in the sinuses and the long bones rather than the ears, and it is everywhere, and it never stops. The water carries it better than the air. Underwater it becomes a pressure that arrives in the chest at intervals, patient, like being inside something's slow breath.

---

Everything else is loud in the ordinary way, and it is loud past reason.

Surf, mostly. There is a great deal of coastline out here for a place with so few islands, because the islands are new — days new, seasons new — and new rock does not know how to be a shore. There is no sand. There has not been time for sand. There are no beaches, no rounded cobbles, no shelving gravel, none of the soft grammar that an old coast uses to talk a wave down to nothing. Instead the sea meets black planes and fresh fracture, edges still sharp enough to open a hand, angles of ninety and sixty degrees where the basalt columns broke square, sea-caves that are just gaps between prisms. A three-meter swell arriving on a surface like that does not break. It detonates. The report is flat and hard, a slam with no tail, and it repeats every nine seconds along fifty kilometers of frontage, out of phase with itself, so the total sound is a rolling artillery that never resolves into rhythm. Spray goes up ninety meters in white columns that hang, sag, and come down as a rain that tastes of iron and hot stone.

Under that: the quench. New rock is still arriving. Fissures open along lines the eye can trace for a hundred kilometers, and lava comes up into cold water, and cold water cracks it — not gently. Each cooling front goes off like a stack of dropped plates, ten thousand fractures a second, and the sea conducts it. Put your head under and the deep is a foundry. Snapping, popping, a granular roar with peaks in it, and under that the *thump* of gas cavities collapsing. The water near these places is milky with fresh glass, and the temperature climbs from four degrees to eight, and then the current takes it away and it is four degrees again.

Because the sea is cool. Uniformly, obstinately cool, top to bottom, equator to fringe — a working temperature. It does not stratify the way an ocean should. Something is stirring it. You can see this in the streaks: bands of slightly different color, forty kilometers long and half a kilometer wide, sliding past each other at speeds that have no business being visible from the air. The surface is not a plane but a shifting tessellation of these bands, and where two of them shear against one another there is a permanent standing chop, a line of white water hundreds of kilometers long that does not travel. It stays. The waves in it are always breaking and the line never moves. Navigate by them, if there were anything to navigate.

---

Weather here is local and constant and enormous.

The sea makes its own. Convection cells the size of nations sit over the plateau, and their rain is not rain — it is a wall. It arrives as a visible vertical thing, blue-grey, straight-edged, dragging a foot of white boil where it touches the surface, and it moves at fifty kilometers an hour, and it takes twenty minutes to pass, and it delivers more water in that time than a temperate country sees in a year. Inside the wall there is no up and no down, only water traveling in every direction at once, and the noise is a white saturating hiss that erases the surf. Hail comes with it, hail with hollow centers, hail that shatters into rings.

And at the plateau's rim, where the bent gradient goes strange, some of the rain falls the wrong way. Not much. A haze of drops peeling off the top of the meniscus and rising, slow as ash, until they thin out into cloud two kilometers up, feeding the cells that will fall again. The rim is permanently hooded in that mist, a white collar around a continent, roaring where the overhang sheds its excess into the abyss below — a waterfall four thousand meters tall and two thousand kilometers long, and mostly it is not water by the time it lands. It is weather.

---

Now the emptiness.

The water is clear. Not tropical-clear. Absurdly clear, the clarity of distilled laboratory glass, because there is nothing in it. No plankton. No larvae. No animals of any kind, at any depth, in any volume of this ocean. Nothing swims here, nothing drifts, nothing eats. Visibility runs to four hundred meters and then just runs out of light, and the color goes from a hard glassy cyan through cobalt to a blue-black that has no grain in it at all. Drop a white stone and you can watch it for a full minute. You can see your own shadow on the water four hundred meters below you, a small grey person, alone in a lens of light with no other shape in it.

Density here is a joke. The islands stand a hundred and fifty kilometers apart, three hundred, sometimes six hundred. From the top of one — and they are high, these islands, sheer-sided tables and asymmetric ridges of unweathered rock, a thousand meters, fifteen hundred — you see nothing but the banded sea to a horizon that is very far away because you are very high, and one distant thunderhead standing on its own rain, and the light. The light is generous. This is not a dim world. The sun is unobstructed at these altitudes and the air is thin and washed, and the whole plateau blazes: white spray, black glass, cyan water, all of it at a brightness that makes you squint and keep squinting. It is a bright, cold, deafening, entirely vacant place.

---

Except for the green.

On the new rock, in the splash zone and a little above it, there are films. Sparse — you can walk a kilometer of ridge and see none, and then find a patch the size of a room. They are not lichen and not algae and not quite anything, a slick blue-green membrane maybe two millimeters thick, holding water like a sponge, edged with a fringe of tiny black rhizoids that go into the basalt farther than they should — centimeters into solid unfractured rock, following no crack, boring by dissolution. Where they have been for a while the stone has changed. It is duller, softer, faintly sticky, and it crumbles. There is a dust under those patches. Grains. The very first sand in this ocean, a few grams of it, made by something that was clearly put here to make it.

Because that is what this is. The plateau is not a landscape yet; it is a landscape's first hour. No valley on any island is cut, no ridge is rounded, no delta exists, no soil, no bay, no spit, no dune. Every edge is original. The rain has not had time to write anything on the rock, and where it has begun — a pale streak below an overhang, a shallow flute in a column face — you can see the mark is days old, and the marks are all the same age, everywhere, across a continent. It is the strangest thing about the place, stranger than the standing sea or the upward rain: the total absence of history in a thing this size. Two thousand kilometers of coast, and every meter of it brand new.

The rules, insofar as there are rules:

The sea will not warm, and will not stratify, and will not stop being stirred.

The level rises. Slowly — a hand's width a year, perhaps — and the rock rises faster, and neither has settled on a final figure.

Nothing that sinks past the light comes back. There is no scavenging. There is no decay in any hurry. Things simply lie on the new rock in the dark, undisturbed, waiting for sediment that has not been invented yet.

And the green spreads, at its own rate, patch to patch across two hundred kilometers of open water by means nobody has observed, making dust, making stickiness, making the first thin possibility of soil — laying down, grain by grain, the medium in which everything that is going to happen here will eventually have to happen.

It is a foundry with a garden being started in it. It is a cathedral under construction with no scaffolding, at a volume you cannot speak over, in full daylight, entirely alone.
