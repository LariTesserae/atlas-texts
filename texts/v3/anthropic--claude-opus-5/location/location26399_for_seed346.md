## Ashlar, and the Ground It Is Holding Down

### The ground

Start with what's underfoot, because underfoot is the point of the place.

The ground at Ashlar is a green-grey mud that is not entirely mineral yet. It takes a boot-print and holds it, but if you come back in an hour the print has smoothed itself out — not filled by water, not slumped, just *reconsidered*. Down at the level of the ankle you can see the substrate deciding: fine sediment in some places, in others a matte, uniform paste with no grain at all, the visual texture of a surface that has been given a colour and a hardness and nothing else.

Pick up a pebble. Most of them are fine — grey, cold, a little damp, faintly speckled, exactly what a pebble should be. But split one with a hammer and you may find the interior blank. Not hollow: blank. A smooth pale nothing where the crystal structure should be, holding the shape of the break. Keep looking at it and within four or five seconds it fills in — quartz, usually, sometimes a plausible feldspar — because it has now been asked, and the asking obliges an answer. The people of Ashlar do this at parties, sparingly. It is considered rude to make the world do homework for entertainment.

The land is low. Not a plain; a plain implies work has been done. This is a broad flat *pre*-place, at most a few metres above the level of the standing water that lies in sheets to the north and west and reflects the sky without a ripple. Nothing here has been cut. There are no valleys, no gullies, no fans of debris, no terraces. Rain, when it comes, does not know where to go, and so it goes everywhere at once, in a thin unhurried film, and evaporates or sinks or simply stops.

Except in one place.

### The Rill

At the eastern edge of town, behind a low fence of drawn stone with a brass rail worn bright by hands, there is a channel in the ground eleven centimetres deep, one metre eighty long, and roughly the width of two fingers at its narrowest point.

It is the first erosion anyone has found here. It cut itself. Nobody made it, nobody induced it, and it has continued, at a rate of a little under a millimetre a year, entirely of its own accord.

The Rill has a keeper. It has a measuring log going back sixty-one years, kept in a ledger that is copied out twice annually because paper here is not always confident. It has, on the fourth day of the cold-turn, a small ceremony in which the water in the channel is not touched, spoken over, or blessed, but simply watched by whoever wants to come. Forty or fifty people stand in the drizzle without talking and watch water move eleven centimetres downhill.

Understand what they are watching. They are watching the world do something without being asked.

### The town

Ashlar is four hundred metres across at its widest and holds, at last count, nine hundred and forty people. The buildings go up seven, eight, nine storeys, leaning in over lanes barely wide enough for two people to pass with their shoulders turned. Catwalks cross overhead at three heights. Ladders are more common than stairs. Every roof is a floor for something else: cisterns, moss-frames, drying lines, the low humming boxes of the loom-relays, a single greenhouse the size of a wardrobe with four tomato plants in it that are the subject of continuous municipal anxiety.

Density is not an accident here and it is not scarcity of land — there is *nothing but* land, unfinished in all directions. The density is the mechanism. I will come to that.

The material of the town is drawn stone. The looms — squat grey machines set into the lowest courses of every building, three or four to a block — take up substrate slurry through intake pipes and pull it out again as beam, plank, tile, pipe, thread, sheet. The draw is not a chemical process in any sense that would survive an examination. Watch a loom running and you will see the slurry go in and the finished beam come out, twelve metres long, in about ninety seconds, with the beam emerging *already old*: faintly weathered at the corners, with a plausible history of small dents. The looms produce objects with pasts. Nobody in Ashlar knows why this is necessary, only that beams drawn without the false age do not stay.

Drawn stone is warm on its inner faces. Not much — the warmth of a hand that has just left. It never cools completely. In winter, which is most of the time, people sleep against walls.

The town smells of cold wet mineral, of lamp oil, of the ozone-and-clean-laundry smell that comes off a working loom, and faintly of tomato leaves near the third block.

### The Anchors

Nine pylons stand around Ashlar in an irregular ring, none of them further than two hundred metres from the market. They are ten metres tall, tapering, the colour of wet slate, and they are the only objects in the region that do not condense moisture. Rain arrives at an Anchor and is gone. Frost will not sit on them. Moss will not grow within a hand's width of the base.

The Anchors hold the local physics in place. That is the whole of the official explanation and it is not a metaphor. Inside the ring, mass behaves, light behaves, cause precedes effect in nearly all cases, and objects remember their shapes overnight. Outside the ring the situation degrades with distance, gently at first, then not gently.

At the eighth hour of every day, one Anchor tolls. It is not a bell sound. It is a very low tone, felt in the sternum and the back of the jaw more than heard, lasting about four seconds, and afterwards the town seems very slightly more *there* — colours a half-step truer, the edges of things crisper, the mud a little more committed. Children can tell you which Anchor tolled by how the light changed. Adults pretend not to be able to.

### The mechanism, plainly stated

The world is under construction, and construction here does not finish by itself. Substrate laid down and left alone drifts back toward draft: loses its interior, loses its grain, loses its distinction from adjacent substrate, and eventually loses the property of being any particular place at all.

Being lived in is what fixes it.

Not observed — observed is not enough, and the early expeditions that tried to fix ground with instrument arrays and unmanned watch-stations learned this expensively. It has to be *used*. It has to be walked on with intent, leaned against, complained about, cooked in, argued in front of, swept, repaired, remembered wrongly, and returned to. The fixative is habitation, and it is required at a certain minimum concentration per unit volume, which is why Ashlar is nine hundred and forty people in four hundred metres and eight storeys instead of nine hundred and forty people spread comfortably over a valley that does not exist yet.

Ashlar is not a settlement that happens to be here. Ashlar is a clamp.

Sixty-one years of clamping have produced: a ring of ground four hundred metres across that no longer needs to be told what it is; a soil profile with an actual A horizon in the eastern quarter; lichen; a persistent frost line; and the Rill.

### Rules, as posted at the market and in every stairwell

**One.** Do not go past the Anchors alone, and not for more than a day, and mark your route with objects you have used.

**Two.** Speak indoors, or against a wall. Words spoken in the open air in the middle of a lane are sometimes taken up, and returned three to nine days later in a slurred and rearranged form, from a doorway or a cistern or the mouth of a sleeping person. The returns are usually harmless. They are never welcome.

**Three.** Sinnet Lane is eighty-one paces walking east and sixty-two paces walking west. This has been true for nineteen years, is not getting better, and is not to be tested by children.

**Four.** In the lee of the fourth Anchor, rain falls upward in a column about two metres across. Do not stand in it. Do not put things in it. Two things put in it have not come back and one thing came back that had not been put in.

**Five.** If you see the dog in two places, you have seen the dog. There is one dog. He is called Nine. Investigating this is not forbidden but the town would rather you didn't.

**Six.** Your shadow may arrive late. Up to about four seconds is normal. If it is more than ten, report to a loom-warden and do not use ladders.

**Seven.** Anything you cannot name, name. Anything you have named, use. Anything you have used, mention to someone else. This is not superstition. This is the work.

### Living things

The vegetation is thin and deliberate. Moss — wet-lace — grows in tidy lines wherever condensation runs off drawn stone, so the whole town is drawn over in pale green tracery following the drip-paths, and from a catwalk the buildings look diagrammed. There is grey-black lichen on the ground in long branching filaments, and this is watched carefully, because the lichen grows along paths that water will eventually take. It knows about drainage the world hasn't built yet. The survey office maps the lichen annually and files the maps as forecasts. The Rill, when they went back and checked, had been drawn in lichen eight years before it began to cut.

In the market square there is one tree, four metres tall, brought here as saplingwood from Elsewhere in the founding years. Half of it — the western half, split more or less down the trunk — is that original wood, and it drops its leaves each cold-turn and stands bare and does the ordinary miracle in spring. The eastern half was repaired with drawn stone thirty years ago after a fall, and it has grown since, and it puts out leaves, and it has never once dropped them. It never learned autumn. Nobody has told it.

Animals are rare and none of them are native, because native is not a category that applies here yet. There are the kettle-birds: small grey-white things, sparrow-sized, that stand very still on the drip-chains in the rain and do not appear to breathe so much as cycle, dimming and brightening on a slow count. They eat nothing anyone has identified. They are found only in fixed ground and are used, informally, as an indicator: where the kettle-birds sit, the world holds.

There is Nine, the dog, who is grey and old and short in the leg and who is seen most mornings in three separate lanes at once, greeted in all three, and who has never in eleven years been in two places within sight of one another.

### Light, sound, weather

The light is normal. This is worth stating because so little else is. There is a proper sun somewhere above the cloud, at a proper distance, doing proper work: shadows fall the right way, mist glows where it should, wet slate shines. What is unfinished is the sky. It is a flat even luminous grey, uniform from horizon to horizon, and at day's end it does not colour — no red, no orange, no gradient — it simply dims over about forty minutes to a dark that is also even and also grey. There are no stars. The founding generation found this hardest. Those born here mostly do not think about it, though there is a word in the local speech, *skypang*, for the feeling that arrives sometimes at dimming-time, and everyone knows it.

It is cold, always, hovering a degree or two either side of freezing. Breath shows. Hands live in sleeves. When water does freeze it freezes wrongly: it forms first as a perfect featureless plane, no needles, no ferns, no crystal habit, and only develops proper structure after a day or two of being looked at and walked past. Rime on the drip-chains is the correct kind, and is admired.

And it is quiet. Sound in Ashlar is thin — the drawn stone drinks it, and rule two keeps voices low and indoors. What you hear standing in the market at midday is: water dripping from four hundred chains at slightly different intervals; the loom-hum, a soft two-note thing under everything; a shutter; somebody's low voice through a wall, words indistinguishable; feet on a ladder. Once a day, the tone from an Anchor. Beyond the ring, nothing at all — the outside is not silent so much as *unrecorded*, and people who have walked out and back describe the return, the moment the drip and hum come up around them, as the best sound in the world.

### The slow part

Ashlar grows about one wall a season. There is no reason to hurry; hurrying does not fix ground faster, and ground fixed fast unfixes. The work is measured in the deepening of the Rill, in the eastward creep of the soil horizon, in the lichen maps thickening year by year with the ghost of a drainage network — a river, eventually, if the forecasts hold, running north-west, some centuries out.

Everyone here knows the shape of the end. When the ground is finished — when it holds its own interiors, cuts its own channels, keeps its own frost, and does not need to be leaned on — the clamp comes off. The looms will be walked out. The Anchors will be drawn down. Ashlar's drawn stone will go back to substrate, because drawn stone is scaffolding and was always understood to be scaffolding, and what will be left is a low damp cold plain with proper soil and a real river and a small anomalous eleven-centimetre channel that a keeper's descendant may or may not have arranged to have preserved.

They talk about it without much grief. It is the only ambition available and they have it wholeheartedly: to be the temporary thing that made a permanent thing possible. To be, in a hundred generations, a slightly odd patch of loam and a place where the lichen once wrote down a promise.

In the meantime: nine hundred and forty people packed eight storeys deep in four hundred metres of cold wet grey, talking quietly against walls, naming everything, using everything, mentioning it to each other, sleeping with their backs to the warm stone while the world underneath them slowly, slowly agrees to be a world.
