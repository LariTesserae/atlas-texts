## The Sink

There is a place in the high cloud-forest where the ground gave way a very long time ago and has been finished with the business of giving way ever since.

You come to it through moss. The ridge at this altitude is all moss — pelts of it a hand deep over the roots, cushions of it swelling on the branches, the trees themselves half-buried in their own green wool. Water is not weather here; it is a condition. The air arrives already wet and leaves the same way, and everything between drips, not from rainfall but from simple contact with the air. Your sleeves darken within minutes. The cold is mild and total, the cold of a cellar in spring: about six degrees, never much more, never much less.

The opening is not dramatic. A rim of black limestone the shape of a shallow bowl, perhaps eleven paces across at the widest, ringed so thickly with tree fern and clubmoss and bromeliad that you could walk past it in daylight — such daylight as there is — and only notice that the birdcalls change pitch as they pass over. The lip is rounded. Every edge here is rounded. The rock has been dissolving at the rate of a fingernail's thickness per human lifetime for longer than the species has had names for things, and it has arrived at the shapes that water prefers: lobes, scallops, drooping hoods, a thousand small basins each holding its thimble of water and its thimble of life.

## The descent

There is a way down. Not a made way — nothing here is made — but a slope of collapsed blocks that settled into their arrangement long ago and have since been welded together by flowstone into a single ramp, gray-brown, slick, faintly ribbed, like the inside of a throat. It is not a difficult climb. It is only that with every body-length you lose the world above a little more.

Ten body-lengths down there is a boundary, and it is the most exact thing in the whole place: the light-line. Above it, green — the last hanging ferns, the last liverworts, everything still tinted by the grudging gray filtering through the fern canopy overhead. Below it, no green at all. Ever. The line does not move with the season, because the season does not reach in here. It sits on the west wall at the height of a standing man and it has sat there since before there were standing men.

Below the light-line, your eyes stop being the instrument you navigate with.

## The floor

The chamber is small. Room-scale. If you spoke in a normal voice you would hear the far wall answer almost immediately, which is why nobody who came here ever spoke twice in a normal voice.

It is packed. This is the fact that takes the longest to accept. There is no bare surface anywhere. Not one. The floor is a mat of pale roots and rootlike things, ankle-deep, spongy, and out of that mat grows a forest that has never seen the sun and does not want to. Everything is the color of things kept in a jar: wax-white, bone, translucent amber, a bruised violet where the stems thicken. Ghost-pipes as thick as fingers, nodding in ranks. Coral-fungus in candelabra. A knee-high thicket of a stalked, leafless, orchid-adjacent thing that flowers permanently — no bud stage, no wilt, every specimen bearing three open flowers at all times, which is one of the several details about this place that is not quite right and cannot be argued with.

None of it photosynthesizes. All of it is plumbed, through fungal thread, into the roots of the great wet forest on the rim above. The trees up there make sugar out of the poor gray light; the threads carry it down through the rock; the ghost-garden eats it in the dark. The sink is the stomach of the ridge. Lush and lightless at once — that is the trick of the place, and it is not a trick, only an arrangement that has had four hundred thousand years to become reasonable.

## Water

Water enters as breath. The air comes in over the rim already saturated, meets rock four degrees cooler, and gives up what it cannot hold. So the walls do not drip from above so much as *sweat*, evenly, everywhere, a continuous beading and running that has polished the flowstone to the texture of soap. Where the seepage gathers it makes threads, and where the threads gather it makes a sump in the north corner, black, perfectly circular from long dissolution, perhaps two arm-spans across and unknown deep.

The sump has not changed level in recorded observation, nor in unrecorded observation, which is nearly all of it. Whatever goes in is matched by whatever goes out, somewhere below, into the karst.

## The fauna

Put your hand down flat anywhere and you will cover perhaps forty animals.

The walls above head height are upholstered in harvestmen — not scattered, *upholstered*, a shag carpet of clustered bodies six deep, legs interlaced into a single continuous fabric that breathes very slightly when you get close. Beneath them, cave crickets stand on the flowstone with their antennae extended to twice their body length, motionless, mapping the air. Whip-spiders occupy the hoods and overhangs, one to each, spaced with the exactness of a settled society, their feeler-legs sweeping slow arcs. In the root-mat: springtails in numbers that make the floor look like it is simmering; blind beetles; flatworms; a pale amphipod that goes everywhere the water goes, which is everywhere.

And snails. Thousands, of nine kinds, grazing the fungal bloom off the wet stone with a sound like the very slow turning of pages.

It should be a roar of life and it is almost silent. This is the second thing that takes acceptance. Nothing here calls. There is nobody to call to who cannot be smelled or felt. No wind reaches the floor. No birds descend past the light-line — they simply won't, they turn at the boundary as though it were glass. What you hear, standing still, is: the tick of water into water, four or five times a second from various heights, a rhythm so old and so regular it stops sounding like sound; the rasp of snail on rock; and occasionally, when something large shifts on the wall, a dry whisper as ten thousand harvestmen legs adjust and resettle.

The only light is under the eastern overhang: a scattering of blue-green points where fungus-gnat larvae hang their fishing lines. Not enough to see by. Just enough to prove your eyes are still working. The number of points does not vary. Someone counted them once, over three visits across eleven years, and got the same figure, and left, and did not come back.

## Craft

There are made things here, though nothing built one with hands.

In the shallows of the sump, and in every basin large enough to hold a cupful, the caddis larvae are at work. Each one has assembled a tube around itself, and the assembly is fastidious: grains sorted by size, laid in a graded spiral, the coarse at the tail and the fine at the head, with the empty shells of the smallest snails set in as ballast at intervals of exactly four grains. Pick one out and turn it in your fingers and it is unmistakably *craft* — selection, sequence, a standard being met. Set it down and the animal walks it away without hurry.

Under the overhang, where a colony of pale ants keeps a fungus garden, workers carry crumbs of dry root-fiber to soak up the seep and haul the loaded sponges back home. Tool use, plain as anything, in the dark, without instruction, since before the tools upstairs were flint.

That is the whole technology of the sink, and it is enough. Nothing here needs a wall. The wall is already there, and it is the ridge.

## The rules

Three, and they hold.

**Nothing crosses the light-line in either direction except water, sugar, and things that fall.** The sink eats what drops in — leaves, a beetle, once in a long while a bird — and gives nothing back upward.

**The sum is constant.** As many hatch as die. As many drops fall as drain. The garden neither spreads nor retreats; the ghost-pipes stand where the ghost-pipes stood. There is no boom, no crash, no succession, no gradient. Whatever tuning was required happened so long ago that the tuning itself has eroded away, and what remains is only the note.

**Therefore there is no time in here, only duration.** Which is why the visitor who counted the lights left uneasy. Not because the place is hostile — it is not hostile, it is barely aware — but because a person standing in the middle of that packed, cold, breathing, sweating, silent room, in the black, with forty small lives under each palm, understands quite suddenly that they are the only thing present that is going somewhere.
