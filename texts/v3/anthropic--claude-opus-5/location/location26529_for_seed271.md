## The Sink

The plain that holds it is warm and mostly dry — a low limestone tableland, no more than a hundred meters above the sea it can't see, flat enough that the horizon is a matter of haze rather than distance. The surface rock is bare in patches, pale grey, fluted with shallow runnels where a hundred thousand rains have gone off it in a hurry. Between the bare patches: thin red soil, and out of the soil a scattering of low hard-leaved shrubs, each one standing apart from the next by three or four times its own width, as if they had agreed on it. Grey-green leaves, thick as coins. They do not shade anything. Walk here at midday and your shadow is the only cool thing for a long way.

The sink announces itself late. There is no rise around it, no cairn, no change in the shrubs. The ground simply stops being ground, and there is a hole in the plain about the size of a village threshing floor — twenty-five paces across, roughly oval, its long axis running northeast. The rim is a clean broken edge of bedded limestone, and below that the walls curve inward and downward into shadow. What you are looking at is the collapsed roof of a chamber. The chamber is still there, mostly. Only its middle fell.

---

**Getting down.**

At the northern lip, someone cut steps. Six of them, then a gap where the seventh has gone, then four more, then the natural slope of a breakdown ramp. The treads are dished in the center from feet — a finger's depth, no more, but that is a great many feet, or a few feet over a very long time. The risers are irregular. Whoever cut them was working with a pick against a rock that splits along its own preferences rather than yours.

Beside the top step is a low wall of dry-laid stone, knee-high, following the rim for perhaps eight meters before it stops. Not a fortification and not a parapet — more likely it kept animals from walking in. There are no animals now. The wall has settled into itself; the stones have found their seats and grown a common skin of grey lichen that runs across the joints, sealing them the way a scar seals two edges of skin. Nothing in it has moved within living memory, and there is no living memory here to speak of.

---

**Inside.**

Under the rim the temperature drops maybe three degrees and the air goes soft. Not cold — warm still, blood-warm in the deep part — but heavy, and it carries moisture the way a held breath does. Skin registers it before the lungs do: the arms go slightly slick.

The floor of the sink is a long apron of breakdown, limestone blocks the size of tables and coffins and, near the center, one the size of a small house, all fallen from the vanished roof. They have been down here long enough to lose their edges. Calcite, dissolved out of the walls above and re-deposited by the drip, has laid a thin cream-colored glaze over everything — over the blocks, over the rubble between them, over the fallen fragments of the seventh step. The glaze is smooth and faintly translucent, like a candle that has run and set. It gives the whole floor a look of having been poured rather than fallen.

Where the roof still holds — the two ends of the oval, running back under the plain into true darkness — the ceiling is low, three or four meters, and hung with a modest curtain of stalactites: nothing dramatic, a fringe of blunt stubs and one thin ribbon perhaps a meter long, banded amber and white. Below the ribbon is a stalagmite that has not yet risen to meet it and will not, not within any span that means anything.

---

**The water.**

Water is the whole reason for this place, and there is very little of it.

It comes in three ways. First, as seepage: the walls in the shaded northeast are dark and glistening, and if you set a palm against them the palm comes away wet, though the rock never runs. Second, as drip: from the tips of the stalactites, from the underside of the largest block, from a dozen anonymous points on the ceiling. Third, and rarely, as rain — three or four times a year the plain gets a real downpour, the sink takes what falls into its mouth, and for an hour there is a sheet of brown water sliding down the ramp.

None of this accumulates. At the low end of the floor there is a pool. It is not large: four meters by two, an irregular lens held in a natural basin of flowstone with a rim like a lip. It is perhaps waist-deep at the center. The water is clear to the bottom and the bottom is pale calcite, so the pool reads as a slab of grey-green light lying in the floor. Its surface is almost always still; when a drip lands, the rings go out, cross, come back off the rim, and are gone within a minute.

The pool has an outlet you cannot see — a seam in the flowstone at its far end, leading down to the water table, which lies some meters below and stretches away flat under the whole tableland. What comes in goes out at very nearly the same rate. The level of the pool has a mark on the rock — a thin dark line of algae and mineral, a hand's breadth wide — and the water has stayed inside that hand's breadth for a period no one has counted. After the heavy rains it rises to the top of the mark for a day. Then it settles. That is the entire range of events available to this place.

---

**What grows.**

Very little, but what there is, is placed exactly.

At the drip line under the northern lip, where light comes down and water comes down together, there is maidenhair fern — a low continuous cushion of it, dark stems, leaflets the size of a fingernail, all trembling very slightly and constantly, because every drip that lands anywhere in that patch passes the shiver along. It is the only thing here that moves without being touched by wind.

Above the ferns, growing out of a joint in the wall three meters up, is a fig. Not a large one — a trunk the thickness of an arm — but it has sent white roots down twelve feet of vertical rock, flattened against the stone like poured wax, searching for the seep. Its canopy leans out over the void and takes most of the direct sunlight that would otherwise reach the floor. Everything below is therefore lit greenish, and dimly.

Elsewhere: liverworts in flat green rosettes on wet stone. A film of dark algae on the underwater flowstone. On the drier blocks, a crust of grey-black lichen that has been there so long it has begun to etch the calcite beneath it. On the rim above, the shrubs, keeping their distances.

No grass. No flowers except the fig's, which are hidden inside the fruit, and the fruit does not set, because the wasp that would pollinate it does not come here.

---

**What doesn't.**

There are no animals. This is not eerie; it is just poverty. The pool has no fish — nothing could have carried them here — and no shrimp, no snails, nothing in the water but the algae and whatever is too small to argue about. The roof has no swallows and no bats: the openings into the dark ends are too tight and lead nowhere good. There are no ants on the steps, no beetles under the blocks, no spiderweb in the corner of the cut basin. Occasionally something flies over the sink, high, going elsewhere.

The absence is audible. You notice it as a hole in the sound, the missing layer of tick and rustle that every other warm place has.

---

**Sound.**

What remains: the drips. Four or five distinct voices, each with its own pitch and interval — one into the pool, a clean *plink* about every eleven seconds; one onto flowstone, flatter, faster; one onto the fern cushion, which absorbs it into a sort of *chuff*. Their intervals do not coincide, and their combined pattern has never repeated and never will.

Under that, a slow exchange of air between the sink and the deeper dark, felt more than heard, a movement of about the strength of someone breathing on the back of your neck from across the room.

Over the rim, if the day has any wind, a dry hiss from the shrubs. It doesn't come down here.

---

**The marks of people.**

Besides the steps and the wall: a basin, cut into the wall at chest height directly under the strongest of the drips, about the size of two cupped hands, with a small spout worked into its lower edge and a groove leading from the spout toward — nothing now; whatever stood beneath it to be filled is gone. The basin is full and the drip overflows it steadily, so the spout has run for as long as the drip has, and has grown its own small tongue of flowstone hanging beneath, a mineral duplicate of the water it carries.

On the flat face beside the basin there is a patch of plaster, hand-sized, adhering. It once had a color; it is now the color of the rock. There is no inscription, no figure, no way to know if there ever was.

One dressed block lies half-buried in the ramp, a lintel or a threshold, with a square socket in it.

That is all. This was a water-place — a cistern-stop on a route across a dry tableland, used by people who came, filled, and went. There is no evidence anyone stayed. There is no charcoal, no midden, no potsherd on the surface, though there may be some under the calcite glaze, sealed and unreadable.

---

**Time.**

Nothing here is going to happen. The fig will drop leaves and put out leaves. The pool will rise a finger and fall a finger. The ribbon stalactite will lengthen by the thickness of a page in a human lifetime. The dished treads will not dish further, because nobody walks on them.

The collapse that made this room happened a very long time ago, and the room has since arrived at its terms: the rate of dissolution matched by the rate of deposition, the water in matched by the water out, the light admitted matched by the leaf that intercepts it, the erosion long since past its violent phase and settled into something better described as maintenance. It is not preserved. It is simply finished, and continuing.

If you sat on the largest block for an hour — the stone warm through your clothes, the green dimness, the eleven-second drip, the fern shivering, the plain overhead going about its dry business — you would find that nothing had changed, including you, and that this was restful rather than otherwise.
