## The Sunk Crown

At the height where the air would be thin and cold, there is no air. There is water, and it is warm.

The thing rises out of a saddle between two grey ranges: a low dome, wider than it is tall, the shape a sandcastle takes after three tides. From a distance you would call it a hill and be wrong. It is a heap of set stone — every block placed, every joint packed with clay that has long since turned to something like stone itself. Nobody cut into a mountain here. They *built* the mountain, course by course, over a span of time nobody now can hold in mind, and then the weather spent an equal or greater span taking the edges off it. The result is a hill with no geology. Break it open anywhere and you find courses. Split a block and you find the pockmarks of a hand-held pick, each one the width of a thumbnail, thousands upon thousands of them, still legible after all this because nothing here freezes and nothing here grows.

The dome is full of water to within a hand's breadth of its crown. The water came from below and never left.

---

Enter by the one aperture the erosion left open — a mouth in the north flank that was once a doorway with a lintel and is now a rounded hole, the way a bar of soap has a hole where the string went. Slip in. The water takes you immediately and holds you at a particular depth, because it is layered like a thing baked in a pan: cool and nearly fresh at the top, a hand's span of shimmer, then a heavier warm brine below that will hold a body up as if on a shelf. The shimmer-layer is the floor of the upper world and the ceiling of the lower one. Push through it and it closes behind you with a sensation like drawing a curtain across your skin.

Below that curtain the temperature climbs to something like blood. Not the scalding of a spring at its source — this is the far end of a long journey, water that came up through the packed foundations from somewhere deep and gave most of its heat to the stone on the way. It arrives tired and mild. It rises through the chambers at the speed of a shadow moving on a wall. A full turn of the cycle — up through the central shaft, out along the galleries, down the flanking wells, back into the packed floor — takes something between a day and three days. Nothing about this place happens faster than that.

---

Inside, the built-ness is total and slightly overwhelming. There is no room here, no cavern, no volume of empty water larger than a small chapel. Instead there are *many* small volumes, stacked and interlocked and near-touching: cells the size of a crouching person, galleries where two could swim abreast, corbelled vaults where the stones step inward to meet at a knot-shaped keystone. Everything is close. Everything has a wall a stroke away. The whole interior is honeycomb — perhaps four hundred chambers, perhaps five hundred, all within a dome you could walk around in six minutes if you walked on the outside and were minded to.

The walls were carved once. You can tell by touch better than by any other sense: broad shallow depressions in patterns, spirals that begin crisply and dissolve, rows of hollows that might have been figures, a band running at chest height through every gallery that was certainly *something* and is now a smooth trough worn into the stone like the sill of a much-used window. The carving is not weathered away. It is grazed away. That distinction matters here and I will come back to it.

The stone itself is a pale tufa-like rock, soft enough that a person with an antler pick and no metal at all could work it, hard enough to last. In the niches — and there are niches everywhere, at the joins of vaults, at the base of stairways that now go nowhere but up into blank ceilings — the tools are still lying where they were set down. Picks of antler. Wedges of a harder dark stone brought from elsewhere. Mallets of the same dark stone, their working faces bruised white. Coils of cord that mineralized in place and now look like grey intestines, still knotted, the knots still tight. No metal anywhere in the building. Not a scrap, not a stain. They did all of this with their hands and with animal bone and with patience of a kind we don't have a word for.

---

It is dark. Not the dark of a closed eye — there is a residue.

Directly over the central shaft, a round hole in the crown lets a coin of grey down into the water for perhaps forty minutes near midday, and it does not reach the second gallery, let alone the fourth. Below that, the light in the Sunk Crown is made by the animals, and it is very poor light: a faint bluish-green fog that clings to the bacterial mats, brightening slowly when something disturbs them and fading over a quarter-hour. Enough to see that there is a wall. Not enough to see the wall.

The mats are the base of everything. They are not plants. Nothing here is a plant; nothing here has ever been a plant. The mats are the simplest thing that can be called alive, and they live on the chemistry the warm water carries up out of the foundations — sulfur, iron, the slow leaching of the mortar. They coat the stone in a film like wet felt, white and rust-orange and, in the hotter cells near the shaft, a black that is very slightly warm to the palm.

And the fauna are *abundant*. This is the surprise of the place, and it takes a while to register, because they are all quiet and most of them are pale.

Shrimp, first — translucent, eyeless, in numbers that make the water feel grainy. They hang in the galleries at every depth like dust in a sunbeam, and you cannot pass without displacing hundreds. Then the snails: a fingernail-sized gastropod with a flattened shell, grazing the mats, in densities of maybe forty to a hand's breadth of wall. Then blind eels, white as candle wax, the length of a forearm, that lie in the corbelled joints with only their heads out and drift free at intervals no one has ever timed. Then a soft-bodied thing like a flat pale slug, palm-sized, that lives only on the ceilings. Then, in the deepest wells where the brine is heaviest and hottest, colonies of little tube-dwellers with feeding fans, packed shoulder to shoulder over whole walls, so that a chamber down there feels furred.

They are all descendants of something that came up the same way the water did, and they have been here long enough to lose their eyes, their pigment, and any hurry.

---

Here is the thing about the carvings.

Where the builders cut a groove, the groove held more surface, and more surface held more mat, and more mat drew more snails, and the snails rasp stone as they graze — a few microns each, a whole population, every day, for an interval so long that the number stops meaning anything. So the deepest and most careful carving was the most nourishing, and being the most nourishing it was the most attended, and being the most attended it went first. The plainest walls are the best preserved. Everything anyone bothered to say here has been eaten off the stone by animals that were only doing what they do, and they did it evenly, patiently, without malice, and the record is not defaced but *polished* — every edge rounded into every other edge until the galleries have the smooth-worn feel of a riverbed, which is exactly what they are not.

Run your hand along the chest-height band and you can feel where the meaning was. It is a very particular sensation. The stone is softest there.

---

It is quiet in a way that has to be described carefully, because water is not silent — it is *dull*. Sound arrives here without edges. The snails make a rasping that would be audible in air and is here only a texture at the base of hearing, a granular hush, like sand settling in a jar. The convection sighs through the shaft doorways once in a long while with a sound like breath under a blanket. The shimmer-layer, when something crosses it, gives a soft thick note, a swallowed *thop*. Occasionally, somewhere in the structure, a mortar joint two thousand years past its patience lets go, and a fist-sized piece of the dome settles a finger's width, and the whole hill makes a single low knock that you feel in the sternum and hear a moment later as almost nothing.

That knock happens, perhaps, four or five times a year. It is the loudest thing that ever happens here.

---

The rules, as far as they can be stated:

Nothing accelerates. Every process in the Sunk Crown runs at the pace of the water cycle or slower — the grazing, the settling, the deposition of new mineral crust over the old tool marks, the collapse. A change begun today is finished in a century.

Nothing is natural. There is no substrate under this. Dig down through the floor of the lowest well and you find more courses, more clay, more thumbnail pick-marks, going down past where anyone has bothered to go. The builders raised this out of a saddle of loose scree and the scree is somewhere down there, packed and forgotten, load-bearing.

Nothing sees. Light is a rumour here, and the only creature ever to enter with eyes is the one reading this.

And the place is small. That is the last thing, and the one that stays with you. For all the density of it — the four hundred chambers, the numberless animals, the ten thousand years of grazed-off speech — the entire world fits inside a dome you could throw a stone across. It is not vast. It is not a wilderness. It is one warm, dark, crowded, patient object at the top of a cold range, full of life that has never wanted anything, holding the shape a great many hands gave it, and losing that shape at a rate of a few microns a day, forever.
