## The Basin Under the Warm Sea

There is no sky here, and there has not been one for a long time — long enough that nothing living in the basin has a word for it, or a use for one.

Take it from the top anyway. Six hundred metres of water stand overhead, warm all the way down. This is an enclosed sea, cut off from the cold oceans by a shallow sill somewhere far to the north, and so it never got the chill that most deep places have. The water at the floor is close to blood-warm, twenty-eight or twenty-nine degrees, dense with salt, thick-feeling, slow. It moves the way syrup moves in a tilted jar. Objects fall through it reluctantly. A shed carapace can take a whole day to reach the bottom.

The light that comes down from above is not light. It is the memory of light, filtered through six hundred metres of green-brown particulate, and it arrives as a difference so faint that only three or four species in the basin retain the eyes to register it — and they use it only to know which way is up, which they mostly already know from the pull of their own weight. Everything else here is done by touch, taste, pressure, and remembering.

## The floor

The basin floor is flat. Not smooth — flat. It is a plain of soft grey-tan ooze, decades of fallen matter compacted into something with the consistency of curd, and it extends further than any single animal here has ever travelled. Relief is measured in metres. The whole visible world, if it were visible, would be a shallow dish maybe four kilometres across, rimmed at the far edges by slumped terraces where the basin walls came down in ancient landslides and settled into their angle of repose. Those slumps are old. Their scarps have been softened, re-softened, filled. Nothing here is sharp anymore. Erosion in a place with no waves and no weather is a patient, lateral thing: it works by burrowing, by grazing, by the slow slump of saturated mud, by the endless sift of animals turning the sediment over and over until every original edge has been eaten smooth.

Through this plain run rivers.

This is the first thing a visitor would have to accept. Beneath the basin lies a bed of ancient salt, and where seawater reaches it, it dissolves it and comes back up as brine — water so loaded with salt that it is heavier than the sea around it. It cannot rise. It cannot mix. It pools in the hollows and runs down the shallow gradients in defined channels, with banks, with meanders, with little cutbanks where the current undercuts the mud. A brine river is perhaps a metre deep and four across. Its surface is a true surface: a shivering, mirror-slick interface with the ordinary sea above it, complete with something like ripples, something like standing waves at the riffles. Where the channel goes over a terrace edge it pours off in a slow, coiling brinefall, heavier than its surroundings, entirely silent.

The rivers are lethal. Anoxic, nine times the salt of the sea. Anything that swims into one convulses and dies, and does not rot, and is not eaten, and simply lies in the channel bed, pickled, sometimes for years, a soft grey figure in a soft grey stream. Every animal in the basin knows the brine line by taste from a body-length away. This knowledge is the single most important fact in local existence, and it is not learned — the larvae that lack it never grow.

At the lower end of the plain the rivers gather into a lake. It is perhaps three hundred metres across and it has a shoreline, an actual shoreline, with a wrack-line of bones. Its mirrored surface is the strangest object in the basin and one of the strangest in the world, and it reflects nothing, because there is nothing to reflect.

## The city

And around the brine — this is the point — there is a city.

Nobody planned it. It is nonetheless organized, layered, infrastructural, and dense.

The brine is not just poison; it is fuel. It carries methane and sulphide up out of the salt bed, and along every riverbank and every metre of lakeshore there is a rind of bacterial mat, chemosynthetic, growing in white and dove-grey and occasionally a pale sulphur yellow that no eye here has ever seen. The mats are the pasture. On the pasture live the mussels — beds of them, ten thousand to the square metre, cemented into each other and into their own dead, so that the beds have become masonry: a rough calcareous rock, a metre and a half thick in the old districts, riddled with chambers and passages. Under and through this mussel-rock live everything else.

Above the beds rise the towers. These are tube worms, and they build in bundles, upward, at a millimetre a year, for centuries; the tallest stands three and a half metres and is older than most of the basin's landforms. The bundles fuse. They lean into one another. They form colonnades, arcades, and stands so thick that a fish cannot pass between them, and inside their tangled bases the sediment is held in place and stops being sediment and becomes floor. There are streets, in the sense that there are gaps kept open by traffic and by the constant grazing of shrimp, and dead ends, and squares — small clearings where the worms failed and the crabs made a midden and the midden made a mound.

Then the tenancy. Every hollow in the mussel-rock has a resident. Every worn-out worm tube is a doorway. Eels lie in the wall-holes with their heads out, one per hole, evenly spaced as bricks, and have lain there for years. Octopuses hold the better corners — the ones with two exits — and mark them with cleared aprons of shell and crab-claw and the vertebrae of things they ate, arranged not decoratively but functionally, as a kind of hard flooring and a kind of alarm system, because you cannot cross a shell apron in the dark without being heard. There are galleries dug by ghost-white lobsters into the compacted ooze, with two mouths apiece, one raised and one flush, so that the sluggish deep current draws through them on its own; the whole under-city is passively ventilated by this trick, repeated ten thousand times, and the exhaled water comes up out of the chimney-mouths in slow warm plumes that other animals use as landmarks. Infrastructure without a single tool. Not one thing in this basin has ever been made by a hand that intended it.

## What grows

Almost nothing green, because there is no green to be had. Vegetation here is a rumour from elsewhere. What arrives is fall: leaf-litter and stems and whole rafts of drowned reeds from the far shore, sinking for four days and landing in the streets, where they are dismantled within a week. There are two exceptions, and they are the basin's oddities.

The first is a seagrass that came down in a slump about eight hundred years ago, was buried alive, and did not die. It has no chlorophyll left worth the name. It is white, translucent, thin as a hair, and it grows in tufts at the brine margins, its roots packed with the same bacteria that feed the mussels. It photosynthesizes nothing. It farms. There are meadows of it, ankle-high, on the lakeshore, and they move all together when the brine breathes.

The second is a sponge that grows only on the crowns of the tallest worm towers, in the shape of a shallow dish, and does nothing but catch what falls.

## The rules

*Be quiet.* This is not law but consequence. Sound carries beautifully in warm dense water and there is nothing to see, so noise is the only thing that gives you away. The basin is therefore near-silent, and the silence is deliberate, maintained by ten million animals each declining to make a sound. What you hear, listening: the tick and crackle of shrimp on the mussel-rock, a fine sleet of a noise, everywhere and unlocatable. The low groan of a mud terrace settling a centimetre. The soft pouring hiss of a brinefall, which is barely a sound at all, more a texture in the water. And, four or five times a night, the single dull thud of something large closing its mouth.

*Do not cross the brine line.* See above.

*The seasons are falls.* Twice a year the sea above turns over and the basin receives — a fortnight of heavy snow, organic, thick, everything the upper world has finished with. The streets fill. The crabs go mad. The eels come out of the walls, which they otherwise never do. Between falls, the city subsists on its own recycling and on the brine, and grows a little quieter, and a little hungrier, and the octopuses begin to eat the neighbours.

*Nothing here is new.* Every surface is a used surface. Every tower is built on the skeleton of a tower. The mud has been through a hundred thousand digestive tracts. The basin is not developing toward anything; it arrived at itself a very long time ago and has been holding that shape ever since, warm, dark, crowded, patient, and, in its own terms, complete.
