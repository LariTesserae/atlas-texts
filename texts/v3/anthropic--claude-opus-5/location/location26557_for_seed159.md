## The Loaf

There is no sky and it is broad daylight.

Understand this first, because everything else follows from it. You are three hundred fathoms under something — nobody down here knows what, and the question is considered childish — and yet you can read fine print, count the hairs on your knuckle, watch a shadow sharpen at your feet. The light comes from above, from the ceiling, which is not a ceiling in the sense of a lid but in the sense of a wound that is closing. It hangs a quarter-mile up in most places, lower in others, and it is knuckled and lobed and bulging downward like the underside of risen dough, and where two of its lobes press together and fuse there is a seam, and the seam burns white.

Not lava-white. Not the sullen orange of a flow. Kiln-white, hearth-white, the colour of a horseshoe an hour before the smith gives up on it. Thousands of seams, some a hand's width, some running unbroken for eight miles, crosshatched over the whole roof of the region — so that the floor below is lit from a hundred directions at once and shadows come out soft-edged and multiple, like the shadows under a chandelier.

The seams cool. That is the important part. A seam burns for a season or a decade and then goes to grey, and while it dies another opens two hundred yards over, and the light *walks*. The herders call it glare-drift and they read it the way sailors read weather. A patch of floor can be in high noon for eleven years and then slide into a slow rolling gloom that lasts a generation, and everything living there either follows the light or learns to do without it. There are whole valleys in the Loaf that are dim and cold-ish and full of pale things with enormous nostrils and no eyes at all, and those valleys were bright and roaring with life when the current grandmothers were girls.

## The ground has not finished

Do not call this a cave. A cave is a hole worn in old stone by patient water. This is the opposite process, running the other direction. Here the stone is *arriving*.

The floor is fresh. Everywhere it is fresh. It comes up in swells and blisters and slow domes, extruded from below through vents you can put your arm into if you don't mind the smell, and it sets in overlapping tongues and lips and drapes, and every edge is sharp because nothing has had time to be worn. There is no soil. There is no sand — not properly, not weather-made sand, because there is no weather to make it. Every grain of grit in the Loaf was chewed. It came out of an animal. The herders call it flour and use it for scouring pots and packing wounds, and they know exactly which herd made which grade of it.

The rock is warm to lie on and gives slightly at the surface, the way a scab gives. It is full of things: bubbles, cavities, glassy black lenses of it that ring when struck, and — this is the whole economy of the place — occasional blebs of native copper and silver-grey nickel-iron caught in the setting stone like currants in a loaf. Nobody here smelts. Nobody here has ever needed to invent a furnace, because the furnace is the world, and metal is a thing you *find*, in nodules the size of a plum, by walking a new swell with a bone mallet and listening for the difference in the note. A woman with a good ear is wealthy. A woman with a good ear and a grown son to carry is a matriarch.

Underfoot, always, the sound of formation: a percussive tick and crack and knuckle-pop coming up through your soles, a sound like a bathhouse full of people cracking their fingers, continuously, from horizon to horizon.

## Water, in its careers

It is damp. It is not wet. Steam comes off the vents, hits the cool underbellies of the ceiling-lobes, and comes back down as an even, patient, all-over drip. There are drip-curtains hanging in the bright air like beaded doorways, mile-long, hissing. There are pools in every low place, thumb-hot to hand-hot, milky with dissolved lime, rimmed with terraces so new and thin they crumble when a beast leans on them.

Rivers here have no beds. They have careers. A river in the Loaf is a temporary opinion about which way is downhill, and the floor is always revising it. A watercourse will run for six years, wearing not a channel but a *stain*, and then a swell rises a foot across its path and the whole thing swings ninety degrees overnight and floods a dry basin where four thousand animals were sleeping. This is not a catastrophe. It is Tuesday. The animals know how to swim and the herders know how to read a swell's intention from the pattern of its blisters, and everyone in this landscape, without exception, sleeps light.

## Overwhelming

Now the difficulty: there is not one plant in the Loaf. Not one. No plant has ever occurred here and the concept does not exist in the local languages.

What there is instead is *film*. The rock, wherever it has cooled below scalding and above tepid, wherever it is damp, goes over with a bloom — rust-orange, sulphur-yellow, a slick bruise-purple, an iridescent grey that peels in sheets like sunburn. Chemical mats. They eat the stone and the vent-fumes and the light, in some obscure way, and they are the bottom of everything. They grow *fast*. A new swell is bare for a day and furred by the third.

And on that furring: the herds.

They come in layers, near-touching, saturating every volume of the air and floor and pool. On the ground, the **chalkers** — waist-high, splay-footed, armoured in overlapping plates the shape and colour of oyster shells, working forward in a rasping front two miles wide, planing the film off the rock with tongues like carpenter's files. A hundred thousand of them in a single push. The sound they make is the single loudest thing in the world: a continuous industrial hiss, the sound of an enormous building being sanded, under which you must shout, and around the edges of which the flour rises in a knee-high haze that catches the seam-light and turns the whole horizon gold.

Behind them, in the flour, the **tap-crabs**, ten to the square yard, drinking condensation out of hollows with fringed limbs, clicking.

Above them, the drip-curtains are hung with **stone-moths** — mica-winged, no mouths, they live nine days on what they ate as larvae — and the moths go up in sheets when a chalker herd passes, thousands at once, a sudden silver blizzard rising into the bright, and then the **bell-flies** come down through them in spirals, screaming, and the mica gets in your food for a week.

In the pools: **sifters**, eel-shaped, milk-blind, so thickly packed in the good hot pools that the water looks like a braid, and the pools *slosh* on their own, and you can hear a good sifter pool from a mile away — a wet slapping applause.

And then the things that eat all of that. The long-legged pale ones that run down chalker calves. The ambusher in the vents. The one the herders won't name directly and refer to as *the neighbour*, which comes up under a herd from below, through the soft new floor, and takes what it takes, and leaves a hole that will be sealed by next season.

Layered, saturated, ceaseless. There is no square yard of the Loaf without a heartbeat in it. Overwhelming is not a metaphor; the herders lose infants to the sheer press of small animals, and every camp maintains a night-child whose only job is to walk the perimeter with a rattle, all shift, every shift, forever.

## The people, and their refusal to build

Scattered marks. A tripod of chalker ribs, hide-lashed, sheeting of stretched membrane, one hearth-slab dragged from camp to camp for four generations. Tools: black glass blades struck from the lenses in the new stone, bone, sinew, gut, ground shell, and those found nodules of copper hammered cold into beads, fishhooks, ear-weights, needles. No wheels — the floor is too broken. No pottery kilns — no fuel worth the trouble, but plenty of natural glaze on any rock left near a vent, so cooking is done by putting food *on the world*.

They do not build permanently and they think people who would are insane. The ground rises. A wall two years old is already leaning; a wall ten years old has been swallowed to the lintels or split up the middle by a swell coming through. So the built things you find are of two kinds. Small light things, moved constantly. And *shrines* — deliberate cairns raised over metal-bearing swells, marking a claim, expected to be eaten, the eating being the point.

Except, here and there, the Old Stair. Fourteen hundred steps of dressed and mortared blockwork going up out of a plain and stopping in mid-air, nine hundred feet up, at the place where the ceiling used to be and isn't any more, because the ceiling has grown *past* it. Nobody knows who cut it. The herders climb it to be alone, and to look out over the whole braided, steaming, roaring, gold-hazed extent of the region — the swells like sleeping animals, the seams overhead crossing and dying and lighting, the chalker fronts moving in their slow miles-wide sweeps, the black smudges of neighbouring clans — and to remind themselves of the one rule that governs everything here, which is:

Nothing in the Loaf is finished, including the Loaf, and the correct response to that is not grief but *speed*.
