## The Tableland

Start with the fact that there is no sky worth the name.

Above the plateau there is a lid: a ceiling of condensate, six or seven kilometers up, stretched flat and unbroken across a continent. It is not cloud in the ordinary sense — it does not tower, it does not sail. It lies there like the underside of a pot lid, and it glows very faintly in places where warm columns strike it from below, a dull maroon bloom that spreads and fades over hours. That is nearly all the light there is. Enough, once your eyes have given up on the idea of seeing, to make out the difference between stone and not-stone at forty paces. Enough to notice your own hands as absences.

The heat does not come from above. It comes up through the soles.

---

## Terrain

The Tableland is basalt, laid down in floods so long ago that the flood-fronts have weathered into gentle steps, each riser a few meters, each tread a few kilometers. Walking it feels like descending a staircase built for something with a stride of an hour. It runs roughly two thousand kilometers north to south and no one has established the width, and it stands high — high enough that the air is thin and dry despite the warmth, high enough that the condensate ceiling is a local phenomenon of the plateau itself and not of the world.

The stone is fissured. This is the first thing that looks wrong, and it takes a while to accept that it isn't.

Cooling cracked the basalt into columns, as basalt does, and then the columns cracked along a second axis, and pressure from beneath widened certain joints preferentially, and the result — repeated across a continent — is a grid. Not a perfect grid. A grid the way a mud flat is a grid: hexagonal at small scale, rectilinear where old faults dominate, and everywhere too regular for comfort. The fissures run straight for kilometers. They meet at consistent angles. The walls are sheer and smooth, glazed with a hard grey enamel of deposited silica, and they are warm to the touch, and warm air rises out of them with an even, patient pressure, like the breath from an open mouth held very close.

These are the flues.

They are between a meter and eighty meters wide. They go down further than anything has reported back from. And they are the entire reason for this place.

## The Engine

Underneath the plateau, at depth, magma. Above the magma, an aquifer trapped in a bed of ancient porous sediment — a sheet of water the size of a sea, held under pressure in the dark. Where the two meet, the water is driven upward as superheated steam, and the flue-grid is the flue-grid because two hundred million years of that steam have selected, widened, and glazed the paths of least resistance.

So: the plateau is a machine that runs continuously and was never assembled.

Steam goes up the flues. It carries dissolved load — sulfur, silica, copper, silver, arsenic, tin, a dozen more — and as it rises it cools, and as it cools it drops what it cannot carry, in strict order, at strict heights, the way a distillation column separates its fractions. At three hundred meters below the surface, chalcedony. At two hundred, the sulfides. At the last forty meters the temperature crosses a threshold and native metal comes out of the vapor directly, plating the flue walls in dendrites — ferns, coral fans, hairs, whiskers of copper and silver a meter long, growing at a rate of perhaps a hand's breadth per century, growing until they cannot bear their own weight and shear off and fall back down into the heat to be dissolved and lifted and deposited again.

Above the plateau, the vapor that gets all the way out keeps rising until it hits the cold, and condenses, and forms the ceiling.

And the ceiling rains.

Not evenly, not often. Rain here is a local, episodic thing: a warm shower over some hundred square kilometers, dropping for twenty minutes, water at body temperature and faintly sour, and then nothing there for another two months. Meanwhile fog collects along the shaded southern walls of the fissures every pressure-cycle, beads, runs, and pools in basins at the flue mouths — and this is more reliable than the rain, and everything living knows it. The plateau is dry in the aggregate and generously wet in about four percent of its area, and that four percent is where the world happens.

Nothing about this loop requires a maker and nothing about it produces a product. Metals accumulate and collapse. Water lifts and falls. The output is the running.

## Rules

**Heat is geography.** Warmth does not spread outward from a point; it comes up in lines. Life here is linear, strung along the flues like beads on wire, and between the wires there are eighty-kilometer stretches of bare warm rock where nothing lives at all. Density is low and it is low in a *pattern*.

**Nothing is above.** No sun, no stars, no seasons of light. The cycle that organizes behavior is barometric: the deep aquifer surges and slackens on a rhythm of about forty-one hours, and when it surges the flues breathe harder, the air pressure at the surface rises by a few millibars, the fog thins, and everything that hunts by scent hunts then. Call it day. It has nothing to do with light.

**Nothing has been built.** Not one wall, not one cairn, not one shaped edge. This deserves stating twice because the flues will keep trying to convince you otherwise — the straightness, the enamel glaze, the way three fissures will meet in a plaza-like basin with a rim of pooled water and a colonnade of columnar joints around it. It is not a ruin. There has never been anything here to ruin. The only near-artifacts are the dendrite groves, and those are chemistry.

## Vegetation

Sparse and low and almost all of it black.

On the open tableland: crusts. Chemoautotrophic films that live off the sulfur in the dust and the trace moisture in the air, in patches the size of a dinner plate, greasy underfoot, releasing a faint smell of struck matches when crushed. Between the patches, nothing but grit — decomposed basalt with a metallic glitter in it.

Along the flue lips, real plants, or things that occupy that role: **flue-figs**, squat and thick-barked, no more than three meters tall, all their growth on the vent side, leaves reduced to hard black scales angled to catch condensation and run it down into a fluted trunk. They root *inward*, down the fissure wall, into the warmth. A grove of them along a flue edge from above would look like a line of hunched shoulders. Under them, in the wet shade: pale tubular things, moss-analogues, and long white filaments hanging into the updraft, feeding on what the steam carries.

Nothing here is green because nothing here has ever had a reason to be.

## Fauna

This is where the place is generous, which surprises people who expect darkness to be empty. Darkness is only empty of *observers*.

The flues are stacked ecosystems. Down at the hot levels, in the vapor, there are **whisker-shrimp** in numbers past estimating, transparent, thermophilic, feeding on chemical gradients and on each other, rising and falling with the pressure cycle so that at the surge you can hold a hand into a flue mouth and feel them strike it like fine sand. They are the base of everything.

Above them, **spinners** — flat-bodied, ten-limbed, palm-sized, running along the dendrite groves on the flue walls upside down, harvesting the whisker-shrimp out of the updraft with limb-baskets and, incidentally, pruning the metal ferns. Their shed shells are copper-plated. A flue wall in the dendrite zone is upholstered in them, thousands to a hundred meters, all moving very slightly, all silent.

Above those, the **long-arms**: the plateau's principal predator of spinners, a slender four-limbed animal with a body like a stretched cat and forelimbs twice its own length, which does not climb down the flues so much as *span* them, bracing wall to wall, and which hunts entirely by heat and by touch. Eyes vestigial, sunk, functionless. The face is mostly a fan of vibration-sensitive bristles. They are common. In a night's walk along a major flue you might pass forty and hear one.

On the surface: **kiln-hogs**, heavy, blind, plated, that lie in the exhalation of a flue mouth all through the slack phase absorbing heat and then wander out onto the cold tableland to graze sulfur crusts for eight or ten hours before they must return or die. Their range is thermally bounded to about twelve kilometers, and their trails have been beaten into the grit so deeply and for so long that they look like roads, radiating from every warm mouth, and they are the closest thing to infrastructure the Tableland has.

And in the ceiling — because there is life up there too — sheets of something gelatinous and photosensitive that ride the condensate and feed on the mineral fines the steam lifts, and which are responsible for that faint maroon glow. It is not the warm columns that make the ceiling shine. It is those things reacting to the warm columns. Which means that all the light in this world is, ultimately, an animal's opinion about the weather.

## Sound

Mostly quiet, and the quiet is not stillness; it is the quiet of something enormous running smoothly.

The base note is below hearing. You feel it in the sternum, a pressure-hum from the flue system, and after a day you stop noticing it and after two days its absence in the deep tableland becomes unbearable. Layered above: the hiss of a flue mouth, close-range only, dying to nothing at three hundred meters. Water dripping in the fog basins with the tick-tick irregularity of a cooling stove. The chime — genuinely a chime, a thin high tone — of a dendrite branch shearing off somewhere below and glancing off the walls on its way down. This happens perhaps twice a night per kilometer of flue and is the most beautiful sound in the world.

And once in a while, far off, one of the deep flues clears a blockage and vents: a column of steam at high velocity, a sound like a great sheet of canvas being torn slowly, going on for a minute or four, and the ceiling above it flushing brighter, and every long-arm within twenty kilometers freezing in place until it stops.

Then quiet again. Then the whisker-shrimp resume. Then the metal keeps growing on the walls, at a hand's breadth per hundred years, toward no one, for nothing, forever.
