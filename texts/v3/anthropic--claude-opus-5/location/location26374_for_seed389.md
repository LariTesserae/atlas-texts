## The Rise

Nothing here is old enough to have worn out.

The mountain came up through the plain within living memory — not erupted, not violently, but *extruded*, the way a knuckle rises under skin. Somewhere beneath, a body of hot rock and hotter brine is being squeezed by pressures that have no interest in speed. The result is a dome nine kilometres across at the base and steepening as it climbs, its summit now high enough that the air has gone thin and hard and slightly wrong in the lungs. It rises still. Between four and six metres a year, more in the wet quarters. You can hear it if you lie down on a cut face at night and are patient: a fine granular ticking, like sugar settling, which is the rock making room for itself.

The surface has no soil. It has no scree, either, no talus fans, none of the tired debris a real mountain wears at its feet. The rock arrives at the sky already finished and immediately begins its very slow disagreement with the weather, and the weather is losing. What you walk on is fresh: a pale amber-grey stone, half salt and half something glassier, laid down in ribbons the width of a hand. Where it is newest it is faintly translucent at the edges. Where it has been up a decade or two it has begun to dull and craze, and a person can scratch a mark into it with a coin, and that mark will still be legible when their grandchildren are old, because nothing comes here to erase it. There is no frost. There is no wind worth the name. There is not one root anywhere in the world to prise a crack open.

So the Rise is a mountain of unweathered corners. Ridgelines you could cut bread on. Fresh faces that hold the reflection of the sun like poured metal. It is a landscape in its first draft, and the strangeness of it is that it looks less like geology than like something recently unpacked.

## Heat and the Sweat

The heat comes from below. This matters. The sun at this altitude is thin and clean and does its work honestly, but underfoot the stone runs at blood temperature on the high terraces and considerably above it in the vent quarters, where the pavements are laid on ceramic spacers and you can feel the warmth coming up through boot soles as a steady pressure. South faces in the afternoon cannot be leaned against bare-armed. The whole dome radiates all night; the sky above it wobbles.

And the rock sweats.

That is the local word and it is the correct one. The brine that drives the uplift is being wrung out as the dome swells, and it seeps: from hairline joints, from the bottoms of quarry cuts, from between the amber ribbons, arriving at the surface at eighty or ninety degrees and heavy with dissolved stone. It emerges quietly. There is very little gushing. Mostly it beads and creeps and hangs, and evaporates in the thin air, and leaves behind flowstone drapes that grow over doorways in a generation, and salt fur on every north-facing sill.

Water in this world is therefore not weather but exudation. It comes out of the ground rather than falling on it. The only true rain is the fog collar — a permanent white belt at about two-thirds height, where the mountain's own exhaled steam meets air cold enough to condense it. From above, the summit district floats on a flat white ring. From inside the collar, the air is a warm continuous drizzle that never falls hard enough to run, and everything is beaded, and every metal surface weeps, and the light is a diffuse pearl. Below the collar the flanks are bone dry, streaked white, and nothing lives there at all.

## The City

The city is older than the ground it stands on. That is the plainest way to say it.

Its people came up with the rock — they were on the plain when the plain began to lift, and they simply stayed, and rebuilt, and stayed. Six generations of that have produced a settlement wound around the dome in tight helical terraces, dense to the point of near-touching, because flat ground is scarce and every square metre of it is contested. Buildings share walls, then share floors, then grow into one another. Streets pass through the third storeys of things. Whole lanes are roofed by the undersides of the lanes above, so that half the city is interior, lamp-lit, humid, warm as an armpit, smelling of hot mineral and machine oil and salt.

The material is the mountain: cast blocks of the amber stone, precipitated rather than quarried, poured in moulds from the sweat itself. Reinforced with iron where iron can be spared. There is no wood — no wood at all, anywhere, unless imported at ruinous cost and kept as a rich man's furniture. There is a great deal of ceramic and a great deal of cast metal and an enormous quantity of glass, because glass is cheap when your feedstock is silica-saturated water and your fuel is the ground.

Everything is layered. Pipes run outside the walls in tarnished bundles, five and six deep, following the streets like ivy that has been organised by an engineer. Condenser fins bristle from every roof and every north wall, harvesting the fog collar, dripping into gutters that feed cisterns that feed the next terrace down. Chimneys, but low ones, and they don't smoke much.

## The Works

The industry is the strangest thing here, and the quietest.

The Rise's factories do not burn much. They *bake*, using the ground's own heat, and they precipitate, and they press. The characteristic machine of this city is a thing the size of a house that completes one operation per day. Great vertical screw presses that descend a centimetre an hour. Settling towers where mineral liquor drops its load across a fortnight. Kilns that are simply chambers sunk into a vent, loaded at the new moon, opened at the full. There are flywheels four storeys tall turning at one revolution per hour, driven by thermal siphons, storing the mountain's warmth as motion and giving it back at need with a low sound like a held breath.

What comes out is stone. Cast block, cast pipe, cast tile, cast beam. The works consume hot brine and produce architecture, and the surplus — the tailings, the off-spec, the slag of a city that makes rock for a living — is barrowed out to the flanks and tipped, in orderly wedges, downhill.

Which is the thing worth understanding: the erosion here is negative. Nothing is being taken off this mountain. The city is *adding to it*, tipping a metre of manufactured strata onto its own skirts every few years, so that far in the future, when weather finally gets around to this place, it will cut down through banded layers of factory waste and find them indistinguishable from rock, because they are rock, because the difference was never as large as anyone assumed.

## Sound

It is mostly quiet, and this surprises visitors more than the heat.

Three reasons. The air is thin, and carries poorly, and swallows the top of every sound so that even a hammer blow arrives flattened and close and dead. Nothing moves fast enough to be loud — a press descending at a centimetre an hour has no voice. And the city is so dense, so packed with soft warm stone and hanging pipework and drapes of flowstone, that it absorbs itself.

So: a background of dripping, everywhere, at all hours, the fog collar giving up its water into ten thousand tin gutters. The occasional deep unhurried *thump* of something completing. Footsteps. Voices kept low, because in quiet places people lower their voices without deciding to. Once or twice a day, the ticking of the ground, if you're somewhere still enough. Every few weeks, at unpredictable hours, a sharp crack from somewhere in the fabric of the city as a wall finally gives up on the shape it was built in — and then the silence afterward, and people counting, and someone going to find out where.

## Living Things

No plants. Not one. Not a moss, not a lichen — the surfaces are too new, too hot, too salt.

But the sweat pools have colour in them, and that colour is alive: thermophile mats in rust, ochre, and a bad green, growing in rings that record the temperature gradient of every pool like a contour map. They are farmed, in the lower vent quarter, scraped and pressed into a protein cake nobody enjoys.

Animals are rare and specialised. There are the pool shrimp, blind, translucent, three centimetres, harvested with fine nets. There is a long-legged white fly, the drinker, which stands on condenser fins with its abdomen tipped and collects fog — enormous eyes, no mouth to speak of, entirely silent, and considered lucky. And above the collar, riding the thermal that the whole mountain generates, there are birds that appear never to land: high dark crescents, circling all day and all night on the column of hot air, feeding on what the updraught carries them. Nobody on the Rise has seen one on the ground. Their nests, if they have nests, are somewhere out on the plain, in the world where things fall down instead of up.

## The Rules

The city has ordinances that make sense nowhere else.

Every building is required to be *jackable*: founded on shim courses, with access ports at the base, so that the levellers can get under it. The levellers are the largest guild on the Rise. They work slowly and continuously, following the survey, raising this corner two millimetres, dropping shims under that one, because the dome is not rising evenly and never has. A street is re-graded once in twenty years. A house that has not been touched in a decade is leaning visibly and known by name.

No structure may be built with a rigid span greater than eleven metres. Nothing may be joined permanently to two different terraces. All plumbing crosses a terrace boundary through a slack loop, and the loops hang everywhere, in bronze and lead, like intestines, and the sound of the city is partly the sound of those loops dripping.

And nothing is built to last more than about ninety years, because in ninety years the ground it stands on will have risen four hundred metres and become a different place with a different climate, and the fog collar will have moved down past it, and the water will have stopped coming, and that quarter will be dry white flank, abandoned, immaculately preserved, visible from below for a very long time.

That is the view, in the end, from the summit works at dusk: the live city in its warm bright belt of fog, and below it, descending the flanks in orderly rings, the dry perfect corpses of the city as it used to be — six generations of stranded terraces, unweathered, unrotted, unentered, going down and down toward the plain, holding their shape in the dark heat, waiting for an erosion that has not yet been invented.
