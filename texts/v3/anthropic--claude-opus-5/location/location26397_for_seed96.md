## The Basin of Combs

You come over the rim at about the height where breath begins to show, and the first thing that fails you is the sense of ground.

There is no ground. There is a basin perhaps five kilometres across, cool and cloud-rimmed, and every square handspan of it is masonry. Terraces stacked on terraces, sixty or seventy tiers from rim to floor, each one a shallow basin holding a skin of water. Between and through them run channels, spillways, sumps, ramps, standing walls pierced with sluice-holes, stairs that double as weirs, so that the whole bowl reads as a single object rather than a place with things in it. Nothing pokes through from underneath. Turn over a coping stone at the very lip of the rim and beneath it is another cut stone, and beneath that another. The construction goes down as far as anyone has ever cared to dig, which is not far, because digging here means dismantling.

The scale is comprehensible — you can see across it, you can walk it in a day if you are patient with stairs — but it does not end. Where the rim dips at the north-west there is a gate, three courses high, and through the gate the same architecture continues into the next basin, and the light off that basin is visible as a paleness in the air above the gap. There are, one assumes, others.

## Stone

The stone is a pale tuff, close-grained, the colour of a wet fingernail. It is fresh. This is the strangest thing about the Basin of Combs and the last thing a visitor notices, because freshness is a negative quality: there is no rounding, no staining beyond the mineral, no fill in the joints, no lichen crust — no lichen at all, nowhere, on nothing. Every arris is sharp enough to score skin. Every face carries the scalloped tool-marks of its dressing, ten or twelve strokes to the handspan, crisp as the day they were struck. Heaps of cutting-waste stand at intervals in the angles of walls, still in their conical tip-shapes, still angular, not one flake washed away.

Nothing here is mortared. The blocks are cut to fit, and they fit with the tightness of a closed eye — and this is where the world's first rule shows itself.

**Worked stone grows.** Not unworked stone; the raw rim rock does nothing. But once a face is dressed flat, the stone begins, very slowly, to insist on that flatness — to add to itself, to complete the geometry it has been shown. A block gains roughly a fingerwidth in a human lifetime. Joints therefore tighten, then bind, then bulge. Left alone for two centuries a wall would swell and lock and crack itself.

So the architecture is not built. It is *pruned*. Every junction in the basin has a shelf, and on every shelf a set of tools: hammerstones of dark quartzite, wedges, a punch of cold-hammered native copper soft enough to blunt in an hour, coils of grey cordage combed from the fibrous serpentine that seams the rim. Beside them, more often than not, a pair of slit-goggles cut from black volcanic glass, no thicker than a thumbnail, drilled for a fibre tie. Every tool is worn. Every tool has been put back.

## Water

Water is everywhere and almost never moving in a way you would call moving.

It arrives twice: as fog and as melt. The fog comes up out of the cloud sea below the rim every morning between the first grey and the third hour, pours over the lip like something poured, and lies in the basin's lower tiers, and the cut stone sweats it. Pale tuff is a superb condenser. By mid-morning every vertical face in the lower forty tiers is running — not dripping, running, a film a hair thick, silent, so that the walls look varnished and darken two shades. The films collect in gutters no wider than two fingers, cut into the top of every wall, and the gutters feed the basins.

The melt comes off the rim's shaded south flank all summer, a slow tea-coloured seep with no channel of its own, gathered by a comb of forty parallel ducts — the Combs, which give the basin its name — that spread the seep so thin across such a width that it enters the system as a sheet rather than a stream.

Everything is graded to be quiet. The whole hydraulic scheme drops something like ninety metres from rim to floor, but it does so across so many hundreds of turns and sills and shallow spreads that no water anywhere in the basin has enough gradient to talk. It slides. It creeps a lip and lays itself down on the next surface without a sound. Watch a leaf-shaped ripple cross a terrace and it will take a quarter of an hour, and it will not break.

There is exactly one exception, and it is deliberate. On the eastern face, at the junction of the eleventh and twelfth tiers, a spillway drops clear a metre and a half into a bell-shaped sump. It is the only free fall in the basin, and it is audible from perhaps three hundred paces — a low, hollow, unhurried knocking. When the tiers above are full it knocks steadily. When they are drawing down it knocks in doublets. When the summer seep fails it stops, and the silence of it stopping is, they say, the loudest event in the region. Everything in the basin is timed against that sound. It is the only clock, and it is a clock that measures water rather than hours.

## Light

The basin is brilliant. High, thin air; a hard white sky most days once the fog has burned off; and below that, forty thousand basins of still water lying flat on pale stone.

The result is that light arrives from both directions with roughly equal violence. The stone glares. The water mirrors. Shadows are cast upward onto the undersides of copings and lintels, so that every overhang holds a rippling grey light that moves when the water moves, which is to say almost never, which is to say the shadows shiver on a timescale of minutes. Unshielded eyes give out within an hour. Hence the obsidian slits on the shelves. Hence, too, the fact that all the deep work — the trimming, the resetting — appears to have been done in the fog hours, when the world is a soft grey box and the basin's true dimensions are unguessable.

The temperature holds cool and steady: rime on the north copings at dawn, thawed by the second hour, never a hard freeze even at the top tiers. This is not accident. The tiers are stacked and their water volumes graded such that the basin buffers itself; the whole thing is a thermal mass with a shape. A cold snap moves through the system over about eleven days, arriving at the floor cisterns as a barely detectable cooling.

## The Pale Matter

The floor of the basin holds nine cisterns, the largest forty paces across, each roofed with corbelled slabs and pierced with a grid of finger-holes for light. They are the terminus. Every drop that enters the Combs or condenses on a wall ends here, having crossed several hundred sills and been spread, aerated, warmed by sun-struck stone, cooled, and settled.

In the cisterns there is something in the water.

It is white and it has no shape of its own. It lies on the cistern floors in a layer between the depth of a fingernail and the depth of a hand, and it is faintly, unevenly greasy to the touch, and when it is disturbed it does not disperse like silt but folds, and then slowly recovers its level. It is not a plant. It has no cells, no membrane, no reproduction, nothing that would satisfy any definition. It is a chemistry — a very long, very slow, self-elaborating chemistry that requires clean cool water at a stable temperature with a particular mineral load and a particular light, delivered daily through finger-holes at a particular angle, and it has been getting exactly that for as long as the basin has existed.

Over the years it thickens.

Once you have seen the cisterns, the whole basin re-reads itself. The ninety-metre drop, the hundreds of silencing sills, the fog-combing walls, the thermal grading, the fanatical dressing of every surface so that no grit and no rot enters the water — none of it is a city. It is an apparatus. Five thousand square metres of terraced stone in service of nine puddles of not-quite-alive.

## Rules

- Dressed stone completes itself; raw stone does not. Therefore all maintenance is subtraction. Nothing is added; things are let back.
- No metal but native copper, and copper is soft and precious and used only for punches. There is no smelting because there is nothing to burn. There is no wood anywhere in the basin, nor charcoal, nor ash, nor bone. Cordage is mineral. Sacks are woven mineral. Fire, if it happened here at all, happened elsewhere.
- Nothing eats. There is no decay. A dropped hammerstone stays where it falls in perfect condition indefinitely, and so the basin's floor is littered, in a low scatter, with a record of every accident ever had in it.
- Water is not permitted to be loud. This is not a superstition; it is a structural principle carried out with enormous discipline, and the one exception is a clock.

## Absence

There is no one in the basin.

Not in the sense of ruin — ruin has a smell and a slump and this place has neither. In the sense of interval. The tools are worn from long use and laid down in the correct places. The cutting-waste is heaped and unwashed. On the thirty-fourth tier, midway along a wall of two hundred and six blocks, forty-one blocks have been re-trimmed and the forty-second has been scored with a guide-line and not yet struck.

Measure the growth on that unstruck block and you can say how long it has been waiting. The number is not large. It is about eleven years — less than a fingerwidth of stone, a hair's swell in a joint, nothing that has yet begun to bind.

Whoever combs this basin is late, and not very late, and the cisterns are still white, and the spillway on the eastern face is still knocking in a slow steady beat that means the tiers above are full.
