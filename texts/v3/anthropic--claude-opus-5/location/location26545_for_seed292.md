## The Weeping Edge

Start with the fact that there is no horizon, only a decision about where to stop looking.

The land here is a table of stone lifted three thousand metres and then left alone for an unimaginable stretch of time — long enough that the mountains it once carried have been taken off it entirely, grain by grain, and carried away to become the flat country somewhere below and beyond. What remains is quartzite and old, old sandstone: the hardest fraction, the part the water could not argue with. It has no peaks. It has swells, whalebacks, broad ribbed domes with their joints rounded like the corners of a much-handled coin. Where the rock shows through, it shows in pavements grooved by nothing more violent than rain running the same line for ten million years. You can lie down on those grooves and fit your spine into one.

So: the highest place in the region, and also the oldest and most nearly finished. The extreme elevation does not feel like a summit. It feels like a floor — the last floor, swept.

Everything unfinished is on top of it, and everything on top of it is water.

---

**The water.** Rank it at very nearly total. Not ocean; there is no sea up here, unless you count the one that arrives every afternoon on its side.

The plateau sits where a warm ocean far below sends its air up an escarpment two kilometres tall, and that air cannot carry what it carries any higher, and so it lets it go. Over the tableland the rain is measured not in millimetres but in months of continuous event. There are dry hours. They are noted. The rest of the time the air is a substance you move through with your mouth partly closed, hung with drift so fine it does not fall but simply accumulates on you, over which sit the real rains — the vertical grey walls that come across the domes with a visible leading edge, a moving cliff of noise you can watch approach for four minutes before it hits.

At the escarpment itself, the Weeping Edge, the rain goes up. The updraft off the wall is strong enough to take the drops off the lip and carry them back over your head, so that on the last hundred metres of land the weather comes at you from below, a cold upward smoke, and you must tilt your hat down at the front and open your collar at the back. Rivers arriving at that edge do not fall so much as unravel: they leave the rock as water, become rope, become smoke, and are gone into whiteness before they are halfway down. Nothing on the plateau has ever seen the bottom of its own waterfalls.

Standing water everywhere the rock permits: black pools in the joint-grooves, peat lakes the colour of strong tea, quaking flats of sphagnum that hold your foot and then let it in to the knee. Running water in every fold, at every scale, and this is the part the ear cannot get used to. Ten thousand streams. The plateau's surface has been so long worked that its drainage is baroque — braided, self-crossing, doubling back through gorges only three metres wide and forty deep, slots so narrow that daylight goes down them green.

---

**The sound**, then, since it comes next in importance. Loud. Not violent-loud; *upholstered* loud, everywhere, at all times, from all directions, with structure. The nearest thing is a factory floor heard from inside a heavy coat.

You learn the layers as you learn a language. Underneath: the escarpment itself, a bass note with no beginning, the whole edge of the world exhaling. Above that: the graded whites of individual falls, each with its own pitch, so that in fog a person walks the plateau by ear the way you'd walk a house in the dark, hand out — *that's Three Sisters on the left, that's the Chute, if I hear the Chute on my right I've turned around.* Above that: rain on leaf, which is a rustle; rain on rock, which is a hiss; rain on tin, which is a riot. Above that: frogs. Above the frogs, at intervals, the crack and rumble of something letting go somewhere — a slab, a mat of soil, a stack of logs at a bend — and then the swallow of it, and then the rain again.

Nobody up here speaks quietly. Voices carry a metre and a half. Every conversation has its hand on your arm.

---

**The vegetation.** Lush, and starved, and these are the same thing.

Ten million years of rain has washed the nutrients out of this rock so thoroughly that the soil is white sand and rust-red clay, chemically nearly nothing. So the forest is dense and low and hard-leaved. Gnarled little trees at chest-to-three-times-your-height, trunks no thicker than a thigh but two hundred years old, growing so close that the canopy is a single quilted surface you can put your hand on. Every branch is furred to twice its diameter in moss, and out of the moss grow ferns, and out of the ferns grow orchids the size of a fingernail, and in the cups of the orchid leaves there is water, and in the water there are larvae. Layer on layer on layer; press your face in and there is another world nine centimetres down. Bromeliad-like rosettes brim on every fork. Filmy ferns one cell thick line the gorge walls like felt.

And because nothing is available in the soil, the plants take it from the air and from the animals. The open flats between groves are hung with pitcher plants — squat maroon ones in the sphagnum, and long green trumpets climbing the low scrub, each with a lid, each half-full of rain and drowned ants. Sundews mat entire pavements in glittering red. There are places where you walk across a hundred metres of nothing but small patient carnivores, and the only sound under the rain is the tick of tiny lids.

The colours are limited and saturated: black rock, red sundew, sixty greens, the tannin brown of every stream, the white of every fall, and above it all the flat bright grey of a sky that is 1.7 out of 3 as much light as anyone needs — a shadowless working light, enough to read by outdoors, occasionally punched through by a shaft when the cloud deck tears, at which moment the whole plateau steams visibly and every wet surface for a kilometre goes to hammered tin.

**Temperature:** cool, always. Eight degrees, eleven, six. Cold enough that wet is dangerous and warm enough that things grow like an argument. No frost, no dry season, no rest. The most temperate violence imaginable.

---

**The fauna** is abundant in the way that only wet cool places with no predators worth mentioning can be. Frogs first: torrent frogs with suction toes clamped inside the falls themselves, tree frogs the size of a thumbnail calling from pitcher-plant rims, a great slow-blinking one in the peat that sounds exactly like a dropped stone. Land crabs, blue-jointed, walking sideways up the gorges. Snails on every trunk, some as broad as a saucer, grazing algae in rows. Leeches in the moss that stand up and wave when you pass, and which you learn to flick without breaking stride or comment.

Fish that climb: little armoured loaches with mouths like limpets working their way up vertical wet rock beside the falls, a slow procession of them in the thin sheet-water, going where no fish should be. Swifts nesting *behind* the falls, going through the curtain at forty kilometres an hour with a sound like a card being flicked. Bats in the slots. Otters, fat and unbothered, that follow you along a stream out of curiosity and then lose interest. Hornbill-heavy flocks that cross the plateau twice a day in raucous strings, following the cloud line up and down.

Insects in a volume that is best understood as weather.

---

**The works.** This is the strange part, and it is not very strange.

There is power here — the most reliable power on earth — and a long time ago somebody noticed. So the plateau is scattered, thinly, with industry: not a landscape of factories but occasional single ones, one per valley, one per major fall, each a small dark bulk of riveted iron and moss-shingled roof pinned to the rock beside a chute.

They are wheel-houses. Penstocks of iron pipe, joints weeping, come down the rock faces in long straight lines that are the only straight lines in the region. Inside: Pelton wheels, cast a century back, spinning under a jet the diameter of your wrist and never once stopped. Line shafts along the ceiling, leather belts with a slap in them, governors turning their weighted balls with an idiot patience. Some houses grind; some spin fibre off the hard-leaved scrub into a coarse wet felt; some draw wire; one, on the biggest fall, does nothing but make electricity, and from it a single copper line on wooden poles wanders forty kilometres across the domes to string bare bulbs in mist at seven crossings, each bulb a fuzzy orange globe visible from four hundred metres and audible — a faint hum — from twenty.

Nobody turns anything off. There is no reason to and no way to store what arrives. The wheels turn through the night, unattended, and the plateau's soundscape has, threaded through the water, a permanent low mechanical heartbeat: *slap, slap, slap*, belt-joint, from around the shoulder of a hill.

The rest of the building is small and partial. A hut, a cairn, half a stone kiln with a tree in it. Ruined pylons whose cables have gone to moss ropes. Iron rails, twelve metres of them, going into a peat pool. Somebody's bathhouse over a hot seep. And the bridges — because bridges are the whole problem.

---

**The rules,** as anyone here would tell you, shouting:

**Anything rigid fails.** Timber rots in a season, iron rusts through in twenty years, masonry is undermined by water finding the joint. Therefore the bridges are grown: fig roots trained across the gorges over decades, hand-guided, plaited, thickening every year, alive, and *stronger* each flood. The oldest are five hundred years old and carry a loaded mule. Growth is the only material that beats the rain, and it beats it by being always under construction.

**Nothing here is dry, so fire is inherited.** Stoves are not lit; they are kept. A dead stove is a serious embarrassment and a two-hour walk to the neighbour's.

**The stone is finished and the skin is not.** This is the plateau's whole character. Underneath, the rock has reached its final shape and has nothing left to do — settled, patient, past all ambition. On top of it, everything is in continuous collapse and regrowth: mats of soil and moss two metres thick, holding for forty years, and then sliding off a whalebacked dome in one wet afternoon and exposing a clean grey scar, which will be green fuzz in two months, ankle-deep in three years, forest in fifty. Channels shift; a stream you crossed at knee height in the morning is a brown tonnage by evening, carrying whole trees end-over-end. Fog rises and falls twice a day, drowning and unveiling valleys like a slow tide of the sky. The cloud deck sits at your feet at noon and over your head by four.

Maps are drawn in pencil, and dated to the week.

**And it is very, very old,** which is the thing that finally gets into you. All this turbulence — the storms, the slides, the roaring, the swifts, the belts slapping, the frogs — is happening on a surface that has already been to the end of its own story and stayed. The commotion is enormous and it changes nothing. The plateau has been rained on like this since before there were grasses, and it will be here, this shape, this height, unbothered, when the wheel-houses are a stain of rust in the moss and the copper line is a green thread in a tree.

At night the cloud closes and the bulbs come on and there is no landscape at all — only a black warm-wet nothing, seven orange smudges, ten thousand streams, and somewhere behind a hill, at a steady, unhurried rate, the slap of a belt going round.
