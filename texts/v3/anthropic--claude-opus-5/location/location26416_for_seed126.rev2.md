# The Sift

## 1. The Ground, Which Is Not Ground

Walk out onto it and the first thing you understand is that you are not standing on a planet. You are standing on the *top of something*, the way you stand on the roof of a cathedral: a surface that has an underside, an intention, a load rating.

Underfoot is powder. Pale grey-gold, warm as a countertop in sun, fine enough to take the print of a fingertip's ridges and hold it for months. Lift a handful and it runs out cool and slow, more like flour than sand, and leaves your palm faintly dry-slick, as if you'd handled talc or graphite.

Look at that handful under any magnification and the illusion of geology collapses. There are no mineral grains. There are *parts*. Every particle was manufactured: hexagonal wafer fragments, snapped lengths of wire so fine they curl like hair, hollow spheres with ports in them, tiny toothed rings, shards of glass with lettering on them that runs to the edge of the shard and stops. The average grain of the Sift is eleven microns across and it used to do something.

Dig. Below the loose powder the material compacts, then cakes, then fuses. At two meters you hit a crust like coral limestone, which is exactly what it is — a rubble of small made objects cemented by their own corrosion into a rock that is not rock. At forty meters the crust becomes *legible*: you can see the outlines of collapsed chambers, the ghost-plan of corridors, conduits running in parallel like the fibres of a wood grain. At four hundred meters there are rooms still standing, crushed down to a third of their original height, doorways squeezed into lens shapes.

At four kilometers there is another crust, and beneath that another set of rooms, differently built.

No core sample has ever found natural substrate. There is a standing joke among the surveyors that the Sift is manufacture all the way down, and a standing counter-joke that the joke is not a joke, it is the survey result.

## 2. The Sky, Which Is a Ceiling

There is no sun. There is no day.

At an altitude that measures out at nine to eleven kilometers depending where you stand, the atmosphere ends in a luminous white shell. Not clouds — a surface. It glows across its entire area with a soft, even, enormous brightness, the light of an overcast noon that has been going on for a very long time and does not intend to stop.

The important consequence, the one that takes travelers weeks to stop flinching at: **there are almost no shadows.** Light arrives from every point of a hemisphere at once. A standing figure casts a faint grey pooling around its own feet and nothing else. A tower casts nothing. A hand held above the powder does not darken it; it merely makes it very slightly less bright, a smudge of ambiguity.

Without shadow, the plain loses its depth cues. A ridge two hundred meters away and a ridge nine kilometers away look identical: a pale line against a pale ground under a pale ceiling. Distance has to be *computed* here, by parallax and pacing and patience. The Sift is the flattest place there is, and it is also, for that reason, the easiest place to become lost in — not lost in the sense of not knowing where you are, but lost in the sense of not knowing how far anything is, which is worse.

The ceiling breathes. Over a cycle of roughly forty-one hours the luminance rises by perhaps eight percent and falls again, so slowly that no one notices it happening and everyone notices it having happened. The bright phase is called *high white*; the dim phase, *the grey hour*, though it lasts nine. In the grey hour shadows *almost* appear. Faint, doubled, uncertain — the edges of things suggest that they might be solid. This is when most travel is done. This is when most of the living things move.

## 3. Air, Dryness, Quiet

There is no water anywhere on the Sift and there never was, not in any form the strata record. No lakebeds, no evaporites, no salt, no hydrated minerals, no rust. The chemistry here is a dry chemistry. Bonds are made and broken by charge and heat, not by solution. Things do not dissolve; they *abrade*, they *migrate*, they *sinter*.

The atmosphere is thin — about a fifth of what a human lung wants — and mostly nitrogen and argon with a persistent haze of long-chain fluorocarbon vapor that gives the air a faint, clean, sweetish smell like new plastic warmed in the sun. It is warm: thirty-two degrees at the surface at high white, twenty-six in the grey hour, and utterly stable. There is no winter. There has not been a winter in any sense for a span of time that the strata suggest but do not number.

And it is quiet. Profoundly, structurally quiet.

Part of this is the thin air, which carries sound badly and carries it *strangely* — voices come out high and papery and don't travel; a shout dies within forty meters. Part of it is the powder, which swallows impact. But part of it is design. Whatever built this place built it with acoustic damping in its bones, and even the ruined remnant of that engineering still works. Strike two pieces of crust together and you get a click with no ring. Drop something heavy and you get a *thump* that arrives already ending.

The sounds that do exist:

- **Sift-hiss.** The grains moving. A whisper, like a stylus on a record with no music on it, constant and directionless. It rises with the wind and never entirely stops.
- **Ticking.** Thermal ticking from the crust as the grey hour comes on — irregular, small, everywhere, like a cooling engine the size of a continent.
- **Chimes.** Rare. When the static field reaches a certain threshold, buried things discharge, and a cluster of high thin tones comes up out of the ground, four or five notes, a second or two, gone. The surveyors call these *inventory pings*. No one has ever demonstrated that they mean anything. No one entirely believes they don't.
- **The wind itself**, which in this air is more a pressure on the skin than a sound.

## 4. Erosion Without Weather

Nothing here has ever been carved by water. Everything here has been worn down anyway, and almost all the way.

Four processes do the work:

**Thermal cycling.** Eight degrees of swing, forty-one hours, over a span long enough to be a geological agent. Every joint, every weld, every interface between two dissimilar materials flexes and flexes and eventually gives up. This is the slow patient hand that took the towers apart.

**Saltation by charge.** With no water and constant illumination, the powder holds an electrostatic charge. Grains repel each other, levitate a millimeter or two above the surface in the high white, and drift. The whole top layer of the Sift is very faintly airborne all the time, a floating skin of dust a knuckle deep. Walk through it and it parts around your shins without touching them.

**Self-consumption.** This is the one that matters, and the one that makes the Sift what it is. The original environment was maintained. Everything in it had repair routines — swarms, spiders, reconstitution beds, whatever the local term was. Those routines outlived their subjects. A maintenance system with nothing left to maintain begins, eventually, to interpret its own components as damage. The Sift has been disassembling itself according to correct procedure for an extremely long time. The fine grain of the dust — eleven microns, so uniform you could use it as a standard — is not the product of chance grinding. It is a *specification*. Something decided, long ago, that eleven microns was the size at which a thing was finished.

**Load.** Pure weight. Kilometers of accumulated remains pressing the earlier layers into flatness the way a book presses a flower.

The result is a landscape at the end of its life, and calm about it. Relief across a thousand kilometers might be forty meters. The highest features are worn nubs: the stumps of structures, rounded like sea-glass, standing a few meters above the plain and trailing long soft tails of accumulated drift. From a distance a field of them looks like a herd of animals lying down.

## 5. Density: The Crowded Flat

You would think a plain this flat would be empty. It is the opposite of empty. It is *saturated*.

Stand still and look at one square meter of ground. There is the drift-skin. Beneath it, ripple structures — not random ripples, but ripples with a periodicity that repeats at 3.1 centimeters and holds that periodicity for kilometers, because the grains still contain fragments of addressing hardware and still weakly sort themselves by type. Walk a hundred meters and the ripple-field shifts hue, from grey-gold to grey-green, because you have crossed from a district that was once one kind of machine to a district that was once another. The Sift is a map of what used to be here, printed at the resolution of dust, and nobody can read it but everyone can see that it is writing.

Below that: the crust, which is crowded. Ten thousand collapsed objects per cubic meter. Some of them still have power. Put an ear to the ground — put an instrument to the ground, your ear will hear nothing — and you pick up a haze of faint activity, ten million tiny processes idling at the bottom of their duty cycles, waiting on inputs that will never arrive. Not ghosts. Just unfinished business, spread thin, spread everywhere.

Above: the air is crowded too, with the perpetual drifting haze, so that the brightness has *texture*. You are never looking through nothing. You are looking through a very thin, very bright, very fine suspension of everything that used to be here.

Density here is not crowding. It is *layering*. Presence per volume, stacked, near-touching, quiet.

## 6. The Growing Things

Sparse. Kilometers between them, usually.

They are called **wicks**, and the question of whether they are alive is a question about your definitions. A wick stands one to four meters. It has a stem of drawn grey material, faintly fibrous, cool to the touch, and at the top a fan or rosette of black collector surfaces angled uselessly in all directions — useless because the light comes from everywhere, so the wick doesn't need to track anything, just to present area.

A wick grows by taking powder out of the air and out of the drift, sorting it, and using the useful fraction. It is slow: a centimeter a year, maybe. Its root is a fused taproot that goes down twenty or thirty meters to find an old power spine and drink the residual potential — millivolts, but steady. A wick that finds a good spine will stand for ten thousand years and put out a slow crown. A wick that misses will stall at twenty centimeters and stand there stalled, indefinitely, not dying because there is nothing here that dies properly.

Their sap is a dry ion current. Cut one and nothing runs out. There's a faint tang of ozone, a slight warmth at the cut, and then the surfaces at the cut go matte as they passivate.

Wicks reproduce by shedding a few hundred grains a year that are slightly different from ordinary dust — heavier, hollow, instruction-bearing. These ride the drift-skin until a static discharge pins them somewhere, and then, if the ground beneath is right, they begin.

There are also **mats**: low grey-green crusts on the flanks of the worn stumps, a few millimeters thick, spreading centimeters per century. And **bells**, extremely rare, which are hollow structures a meter across that hum in the wind at frequencies below hearing and appear to do nothing else. There are perhaps four thousand bells on a surface the size of a continent, and they are not distributed randomly; plotted, they fall along enormous smooth curves, thousands of kilometers long, which the surveyors have named and argued about for two generations and which correspond to nothing in the strata beneath.

## 7. The Rare Fauna

You could cross the Sift for a month and see nothing move except dust.

**Grazers.** Flat, pale, palm-sized to wheelbarrow-sized, they lie in the drift with only a brush-fringe exposed and comb the ripple-fields for the grain fractions they need. A large one moves maybe nine meters in a grey hour. They do not have eyes. They have a slow chemical patience. Split one open and you find a gut that is a sorting cascade — a series of graded chambers separating incoming dust by size, charge, and composition, keeping perhaps two percent, voiding the rest. Their waste is cleaner than the ground they eat, and you can track a grazer's whole life by the thread of slightly-purer powder it has laid down behind itself. Some of those threads are four kilometers long.

**Sails.** In the grey hour, when the static field builds, thin things rise off the ground: sheets a meter across and a few molecules thick, silvery, rippling. They ride charge and light-pressure at fifty or sixty meters up and go wherever the drift goes. They may be a dispersal stage of something else. Nobody has caught one intact; they collapse on contact into a smear of grey powder and a faint smell of hot metal.

**The Sorter.** One taxon of predator, if that's the word. It is a low, many-legged, mostly-buried machine-animal, three meters long, that hunts grazers by their sorting rhythm — it listens through the ground for the cascade and comes up under them. It does not eat them. It *disassembles* them, entirely, with great precision, and sets the components out on the surface in a neat graded field, largest to smallest, a fan of parts four or five meters across. Then it takes a very small number of those parts and leaves. The rest sits there and is gradually reclaimed by the drift.

The surveyors' first expedition assumed these fans were artifacts, graves, or messages. They are none of those. They are just what happens when a system that was built to service machines gets hungry.

Total biomass of the Sift, across an area larger than a continent, would fill a small hall.

## 8. Weather

There is weather, and it is silent, and it is beautiful, and there's a lot of it.

**Drift.** The perpetual low migration of the skin. Direction varies with the luminance cycle — outward from the bright zones in high white, inward in the grey hour — so that over a year the Sift *breathes* its top layer back and forth across hundreds of kilometers, and the ripple-patterns dissolve and re-form with their periodicities intact.

**Sweeps.** Once or twice a grey hour, a discharge front crosses the plain. You see it coming as a change in the ground's texture: the drift-skin goes flat and glassy for a hundred meters ahead, then the front arrives, a wall of suspended dust three meters high moving at walking pace, and it passes through you with a crawling sensation on the skin and a faint blue tint at the corners of vision, and behind it the ground is *swept* — combed into perfect parallel lines, dead flat, immaculate. Sweeps are the reason the Sift looks maintained. It is maintained. Nobody's doing it. It's just what the physics of this place amounts to now.

**Silent devils.** Thermal columns lift the dust into rotating pillars that stand four hundred meters tall and drift for hours. In any normal atmosphere these would roar. Here they make no sound at all. A silent dust devil the height of a skyscraper crossing your path in full brightness, moving at the speed of a slow bicycle, absolutely soundless, is the single most commonly reported cause of expedition members refusing to continue.

**Fall.** Rarely — a few times a year — the ceiling sheds. Material comes down from nine kilometers up, takes half a day to arrive, and lands as a fine white snow of something like collapsed aerogel, warm and weightless, which accumulates in a layer a few millimeters deep and then is gone within a cycle, assimilated. The ceiling, too, is wearing out. It is simply doing it more slowly than everything beneath it.

## 9. The Rules

Places develop rules. The Sift's are consistent enough to list.

**There is no bedrock.** Every excavation ends in more manufacture. This has been tested to eleven kilometers.

**Nothing decays into nothing.** Things decay into smaller made things. The end state of matter here is not mud or gas; it is an eleven-micron part. Mass is conserved and, apparently, inventoried.

**Nothing left on the ground stays whole.** A dropped tool will be intact for perhaps four days. Then it will begin to go soft at the edges. Within a season it will be a slightly-mounded patch of grain with an anomalous composition. This process is not aggressive, cannot be observed in the act, and stops immediately and permanently if the object is picked up and carried. Expeditions sleep with their equipment in their arms.

**Distance is not quite additive.** Pace out a straight line of a hundred kilometers on the Sift and the sum of the legs will exceed the endpoint separation by about one part in nine hundred, consistently, in all directions. The surveyors log it and correct for it and do not discuss it much. The plain is very slightly larger than the space it is in. Whatever is folded up in there is folded up very finely and very evenly and shows no sign of being a problem.

**The horizon is closer than it should be, and then it isn't.** On a surface this flat, at eye height, the horizon should sit at a certain distance. It sits nearer. But objects appear over it at the correct distance for the true curvature. So the edge of the world is a false edge — a haze-line in the suspended dust, not a geometric limit — and you can watch a wick's crown standing in the sky above the visible ground, disconnected, unsupported, for an hour before its stem rises into view.

**Nothing is loud.** Explosives have been tried. They make a dull flat cough and an enormous silent cloud. The energy goes somewhere. It does not come back as sound.

## 10. Standing There

So: imagine it.

Flat to every horizon, warm, bone-dry, and shadowless. A sky of uniform white light that is nine kilometers of ceiling, glowing at you from every angle so that you cannot squint away from it and cannot find a dark place in the visible world. The ground pale gold-grey and combed into parallel lines, the combing perfect, extending past the limit of resolution, with a faint mist of levitating dust standing a few centimeters above it that parts around your legs. Here and there a worn stump like a lying animal. Very far off — impossible to say how far — a single black wick standing with its collector-fan open like a hand.

Nothing moves. You can hear the sift-hiss and your own blood and that's all.

Then the grey hour begins, and the light goes down by eight percent over three hours, and the world *acquires edges* — faint, soft, doubled, but edges, the first shadows in nineteen hours — and the ground starts to tick as it cools, everywhere, irregularly, the sound of an enormous machine shutting down that has been shutting down since before anything you know began. Sails come up off the drift and rise silently past you. A discharge front lays a new comb across a hundred kilometers of plain. Somewhere under your feet, ten million small unfinished processes register the temperature change, log it, and go back to waiting.

There is no threat here. That is the thing people find hardest. It is warm, it is bright, it is clean, it is stable, it is quiet, and it is entirely finished. The Sift is not dying and is not dead. It is a made place that has completed the process of becoming what it will finally be, and has settled into that, and will hold it — combed, drifting, ticking, glowing — for a duration against which the question *what was it for* simply stops being a useful question.

You may stay. Keep your things in your hands.
