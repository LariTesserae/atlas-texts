## The Yield

The land here is younger than the people standing on it.

Come at Cant from inland, from the high dry backcountry, and you cross first through the Stales: eighty years of abandoned town, roofless, root-broken, the streets filled to the sills with dwarf birch and rusty sedge. This is where the ground has finished happening. It is cracked, patient, dead-still, and thoroughly green. Fern grows out of every window. The old jacking-screws that once held the houses level stand in the undergrowth like a crop of iron mushrooms, seized solid, nothing left to level. Foxes den in the counting-houses. The mist beads on the birch and runs down and the ground drinks it instantly, so the Stales are damp to the touch and bone-dry underneath, and the leaf litter never rots into mud, only crumbles.

Then the ground begins to tilt.

You feel it in your ankles before you see it. Everything from here to the sea is leaning, very slightly, uphill-behind-you, because the whole country is rising out of the water — a hand's width a month at the shore, less inland, so the land comes up not evenly but as a slow swell, an enormous shrug that has been going on longer than anyone's records and shows no sign of finishing. The sea gives ground. Every year there is more world. Every year the harbour is further away.

---

Cant is the town that follows the water down.

It is packed. There is no room in it. It occupies the eleven-kilometre band between the set line and the tide, and in that band it is stacked, spliced, and shouldered together so tightly that from the ridge it reads as a single organism with chimneys: eight thousand roofs at eight thousand slightly different angles, cranes standing in the gaps like herons, the whole thing threaded with belt-drives and rail and the pale runs of pipe. Where there is not building, there is garden — every wall is furred with moss-mat and quickthorn and pale cold cabbages in trays, every north face is a hanging bed of liverwort. Nothing is bare. Nothing is unoccupied. The town has learned that space, like ground, is temporary, and it uses all of it.

And it is quiet.

That is the thing that undoes visitors. Twelve hundred machines are running within earshot and you can hear water dripping off a gutter three streets away. The mills turn on felted belts and hide-lined bearings. The heave-engines — the great ones, the ones that draw their power from the rising ground itself, pistons bedded into bedrock and pushed by the swell of the country at a rate you could measure with a hair — make no sound at all, ever; they are the size of a chapel and they move like a tide, and the only indication that one is working is a slow tick in the gear-train, once every few minutes, like something turning over in its sleep. Speech in Cant is low and close and half-signed. Doors have leather jambs. Wheels have rag tyres. Children learn before they learn letters that you do not strike stone, you do not shout in a street, you do not ring anything, ever.

The reason is under their feet.

---

New rock here arrives soft. It comes up out of the sea-margin as *loaf*: a pale, warm, vesiculated stuff, the colour of wet ash, with the give of hard cheese and a smell like struck flint and rain. For roughly nine days it can be cut with a wire, pressed, extruded, thumbed, folded. Then it sets. Then it is stone — and the stone it becomes is a bell. Unweathered, unrelieved, with no crack or joint or grain in it anywhere, it holds a note the way a wine glass does, and it holds it across whole shelves and districts, and if you give it the wrong note it will not ring, it will split. Cant has lost two districts that way. One to a bandsaw with a bad bearing. One, in the old accounts, to a wedding.

So the loudest thing in Cant is the loaf-works, and the loaf-works are quiet.

They stand along the seaward third, straddling the fresh ground: long low sheds on skids, and inside them the presses and wire-frames and rolling tables, and men and women in oilcloth working fast and without conversation because the clock is nine days and the material is cooling under their hands. They mill the ground and press it back out as brick, pipe, sheet, roof-tile, ductwork, curbstone, bathtub, headstone. Cant is built out of Cant. Everything you see was under the sea within living memory. Everything you see will be inland derelict within another lifetime. Whole cargoes of pressed pipe go out on the inland rail to countries that have no ground of their own coming up, and the same trains bring back the things Cant cannot make: timber, glass, oranges, salt, brass.

The metal is the other harvest. New land comes up seeded — no one knows why, and the assay offices have given up asking — with ore in concentrations that would be a lie anywhere else: nodules of copper the size of loaves lying in the mud like something left out to be found, thin bright seams of nickel running in the fresh rock at a depth you can reach with a spade. Cant's smelters are electric and low-roaring and hooded with felt. The pouring floors are the one place the town permits noise, and they are sunk in pits, and lined with old dead Stales-stone that has already cracked and can't crack worse.

---

The water situation is a joke the locals have stopped laughing at. There is sea on one side, mist in the air, condensation on every surface, a permanent slick of damp on every wall — and almost nothing to drink, because the new ground is a sponge and swallows whatever falls on it. Rivers here are embryonic; they have not yet decided where to go. They come off the high back country in braided, absent-minded sheets, wander a kilometre across the new flats in twenty channels, and then simply stop — sink, mid-course, into the loaf, leaving a fan of wet gravel that goes nowhere. There is no erosion to speak of. Nothing has had time to be worn. Every edge in the landscape is the edge it was born with: the cliffs are sharp-lipped, the boulders are unrounded, the shore has no beach, only a scarp of raw stone and a skirt of grey mud.

So Cant harvests fog. The seaward flats are ranged with fog-fences — combs of thread on frames, thirty rows deep, five hundred metres long, standing out in the wind, and the mist walks into them and comes off as water, and the water goes into the pipes the town pressed for itself last spring. The sound the fences make is the sound most people mean when they say the sound of Cant: a wide, low, tissue-thin hiss, several kilometres broad, and under it the tick of drops in ten thousand gutters.

---

The turbulence is not in the noise. It's in the fact that nothing stays.

The land rises unevenly, and so the town is always tilting, and so half of Cant's tradespeople are *canters*: they go house to house with dial-gauges and spanners, and they turn the jacking-screws in the cellars a quarter-turn, a half-turn, and bring the floors back to level. Every building in Cant sits on iron. Every building has a plumb-line in its stairwell. The rails are relaid weekly; the sleepers are on wedges and the gangs go out at dawn with shims in their aprons. The main quay has been rebuilt eleven times in thirty years and each time further out, and the previous quays stand in a series behind it, stranded, converted — the first is a market, the second is a laundry, the third is still called the quay by old people, and is a school. Pipe runs are laid in accordion joints. The gasworks moves on rollers, and it takes six weeks, and it is done at night, and it is silent.

There is a phrase in the leases: *while the water is within four thousand paces.* That's how long you own something.

---

The living things love it. Colonisers always do. The fresh ground goes from grey to green in a single season: moss-mat first, then quickthorn, which grows a hand's breadth a week in soft loaf and dies the day the loaf sets, leaving little brown ghosts of itself embedded in the stone forever — the pavements of Cant are full of them, black filaments in pale rock, like flaws in ice. Sedge and cotton-grass. Cold ferns in the seep-vents where the ground still breathes warm. Willow scrub thick enough to lose a dog in.

Birds beyond counting, and none of them songbirds — waders, mostly, thousands of them, stilts and dunlin and grey plover working the new mud in enormous silent congregations that lift all at once with a noise like a page turning. Loaf-mice, which burrow the soft ground and get walled in when it sets and are found, later, as perfect little hollows. A grazing snail with a rasp for a tongue that eats fresh stone and lays down a pearl track behind it. Midges in cathedral columns over the fog-fences, soundless. Offshore, seals on the newest rock, and further out something large that surfaces twice a winter and that nobody in Cant will discuss with a stranger.

---

And then there is what the ground brings up.

Not often. Often enough. A shelf comes out of the water with hollows in it: a stair of eleven treads, worn, going down into stone that was seafloor last year and has never been walked on. A void in a fresh cliff face the precise shape and size of a seated person. In the year they extended the north main, a district rose containing a run of pipe-shaped cavities of exactly the bore Cant presses, at exactly the depth Cant lays them, following the line the surveyors had drawn the previous autumn and not yet built.

Cant does not have a theory. Cant has a custom. When the ground gives you a shape, you use it. You lay your pipe in the hole that was waiting. You put a bench at the top of the stairs that go nowhere and you let people sit there in the pearl-grey afternoon light, in the drip and the hiss, watching the fog-fences work and the sea keep its slow retreat, and you don't talk about it above a murmur, because the stone is listening for its note, and because in Cant it is simply understood that the world here is not finished, and that it is being made in the same direction you are going, slightly ahead of you, and that this is either a kindness or a habit, and that it is bad manners to ask which.
