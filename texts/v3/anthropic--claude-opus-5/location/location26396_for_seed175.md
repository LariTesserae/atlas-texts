## The Shelf

Come up through cloud for most of a day and the cloud ends the way a page ends: abruptly, no thinning. Above it there is a bench cut into the flank of something much larger — a mountain you never see, whose existence you infer from the way the light leans. The bench is small. A hundred and fifty paces across the long axis, perhaps ninety on the short, tilted a few degrees toward a notch in its outer rim. You can stand in the middle and be inside the whole of it. This is the entire world available here; everything else is white.

The floor is moss over clay over rotted rock. The moss is not one plant but a felt — three or four fingers deep, springy, the colour of old brass where the light hits and nearly black in the hollows. Walk on it and you sink to the ankle and it closes again behind you within the hour. It holds water the way bread holds water. Press with your palm and a clear lens rises around your fingers and does not drain; lift your hand and the lens is reabsorbed. Everything here is saturated to a degree just short of pooling. The air itself is at the edge — breathe out and you can watch your breath thicken, hang, and be taken back.

It is warm. That is the thing that arrives second, after the silence, and it goes on arriving. Warm at this altitude is wrong and the wrongness is comfortable: blood-warm in the low places, cool only along the outer lip where the mist runs. Sit down and the ground gives you back heat. The heat comes from below, from some slow reservoir in the mountain's body, and it comes up as diffusion rather than event — no vents, no boiling, no hiss. The rock sweats. That is the whole of the geothermal expression here. The rock sweats, and the sweat is warm, and it never falls.

---

That is a rule, and worth stating plainly, because it takes a while to notice and then it cannot be unnoticed: **on the Shelf, water does not fall.** It seeps, wicks, creeps, films, beads and travels along the beads, hangs in the underside of leaves in rows like held notes. It moves downslope at the speed of a shadow. There is no drip anywhere. There is no place where a drop gathers weight enough to let go, because the moss and the leaf-felt and the mineral crusts intercept it at every scale, and the surfaces are all so wettable that the water would rather crawl than leap. In a rainless place this would be unremarkable. Here, where the air is at ninety-something percent and the walls stream, it is uncanny. The Shelf is drenched and utterly quiet.

Which is the second rule: **sound does not carry.** The felt eats it. There is no echo — none, not from the seep-wall, not from the corestones. Speak at conversational volume and your voice arrives at your own ears with an intimacy that is unpleasant, as though it never left your skull. At six paces a spoken word is a shape without content. At twelve it is nothing. The only sounds the Shelf makes on its own are contact sounds: the faint tick of a mineral flake settling in a scree fan, a leaf's slow separation from a leaf, a soft internal gulp when a saturated moss cushion redistributes its load. You will spend the first hour thinking you have gone partly deaf.

---

The rock is coarse and pale, some old feldspar-rich flow long since chemically undone. It has weathered the way such rock weathers when it is given enough time and enough water and nothing interrupts: down to spheroids. The Shelf is studded with corestones, twenty or thirty of them, rounded boulders sitting in the crumbled rinds of themselves, each one a slightly different degree of gone. Some are still hard at the heart and ring dully under a knuckle. Some have become entirely soft and hold their boulder shape only because nothing has asked them not to. You can push a finger into one to the second joint and withdraw it wearing a grey glove of clay so fine it feels like nothing at all.

Nothing here is sharp. Every edge on the Shelf has long since been rounded off; every slope stands at the angle it discovered for itself and has held that angle for a very long time. This is a landscape that finished happening. Its remaining changes are so slow that they are better described as posture than as motion: the notch deepens by a hair in a human lifetime, the corestones subside, the moss thickens and compresses its own lower layers into a brown paper that will one day be something like peat and never becomes soil, because soil requires animals.

There are none.

---

No animals. No worm-castings in the clay, no bore-holes, no chewed leaf-margins, no droppings, no bones, no shells, no wing-cases washed into the drift lines, no midges in the warm damp air where midges ought to be in their billions. The absence is not ominous. It is *clean*, and the cleanness is one of the strongest sensations the place offers: an air with nothing in it but water, warm mineral, and a faint green-sweet smell like cut stems and beeswax. Nothing has ever died here in a way that smelled.

The plants are correspondingly few and correspondingly strange, since nothing carries their pollen and nothing eats them and nothing has ever pruned or trampled or fertilised them. They are scattered — that is important. Across the whole bench there are maybe eighteen plants large enough to have a shape you could describe, and between them there is open moss, wide floors of it, so that each one stands in its own considerable emptiness like a thing set down deliberately.

Chief among them are the **pale columns**: unbranched trunks, waist-thick, the colour of candle-tallow, rising to head height or a little above and ending in a low crown of leaves so heavy and so lax that they lie back down along the trunk. They grow perhaps the thickness of a fingernail in a year. The tallest of them was already a plant before any language you know existed. They reproduce by leaning: when a column finally lies down, the crown roots where it lands, and the new plant is the old plant, forty years later, one body-length downslope. You can read the history of the Shelf's traffic in the faint linear troughs their fallen trunks have pressed into the felt — long slow drag-marks, each one a lineage.

Then the **cordwort**, which is the first thing that will make you uneasy. It grows as a flat green ribbon along the ground, and as it dries at the tip it twists; and because the ribbon has two structurally different halves, the twist reverses on itself and locks. What lies across the moss, in single strands ten and fifteen paces long, is two-ply cord. Properly laid, evenly spun, of a quality you would be pleased to have made yourself with a spindle and an afternoon. Pick it up and it is strong enough to lift you. Nobody spun it. Nobody has ever spun anything here.

---

And then the seep-wall, at the inner edge, where the Shelf meets the invisible mountain.

The wall is nine or ten times your height, streaming, black with mineral crust, warm to the touch. Two thirds of the way up runs a bed of clay that the water has been silicifying for longer than the bench has existed. It has become chert — and chert, when it is jointed by heat and cooled by film-water, sheds. It sheds in conchoidal flakes. The scree below the bed is a fan of them, a slope of glass shingle, half-drowned in moss, glittering wetly where the light comes in flat.

Among the flakes are the finished things.

Bifaces. Scrapers with one steep worked edge and one blunt back. Awls. A long blade-form with a bevelled tang. They are heaped in the fan, they lie singly out on the open moss where the slope has walked them, and where the felt has polished them their edges have a soft wear-gloss like tools that have been used for years and set down warm.

There are explanations. The bed's jointing may be regular at conchoidal spacings. Freeze cycles at the top of the wall, in the two months of the year when the cloud floor sinks and the Shelf stands out under a hard clean sun, could plausibly detach flakes in series. Slow creep drags each piece downslope a hand's width a year, turning it, pressing it, abrading it against grit. All of this is probably true and none of it accounts for the grip.

Because each of the larger pieces has, on the butt end, a set of hollows. Four along one face, one opposed. Worn smooth. Sized for fingers slightly shorter and thicker than yours, and shaped, unmistakably, to be *held* — held in a particular way, with the thumb braced against a raised ridge that no fracture mechanics would put there.

At the bottom of the Shelf, where the tilt gathers everything before the notch, there is a shallow warm pool, clear, perhaps four paces wide, moss-lipped, silent. It is floored with tools. Thousands. Sorted by size across its width, the way water sorts things, the smallest carried furthest. They lie in bright overlapping ranks under a hand's depth of water and they are still arriving, one hand's width a year, for as long as the wall has been sweating.

Nothing has ever come here to pick them up. The water goes over the notch, becomes cloud before it can strike anything, and makes no sound at all.
