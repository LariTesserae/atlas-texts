## The Shoulder

At twenty-eight hundred metres, on the seaward side of a mountain that did not exist when the oldest of the swifts overhead was hatched, there is a terrace about the size of a village square. It is roughly triangular, floored in pale stone, and it steams.

Call it the Shoulder. It is small enough to cross in ninety seconds and complicated enough to occupy a life.

### The floor

The rock underfoot is not lava. That is the first thing to correct. There is no glass froth, no ropy pahoehoe, no cinder. The stone of the Shoulder arrives from below already stone: a fine-grained pale grey, dense, faintly translucent at broken edges like the rind of a very hard cheese. It comes up at about two centimetres a day here on the flank, faster in the throat above, and it comes up *warm* — not scalding, not molten, but the temperature of tea you have let sit ten minutes. You can put your palm flat on the newest ground and hold it there, and feel, if you are patient, the ground rise very slightly against your hand.

Because it is young, it is sharp. Every edge is an edge. The terrace is broken into plates and risers with corners that will cut a boot sole, and the joints between plates are so clean that rain drains through them without a sound. Nothing here has been rounded by anything. Nineteen years is not long enough for weather to get a grip, and in any case the weather has been given very little to work with: no soil to move, no scree, no dust. The Shoulder is a place of angles and vapour.

The strangeness comes later, and it comes as history.

Newest stone — the day's stone, at the upslope lip where the terrace is still widening — is blank. Featureless. It has no grain, no bedding, no crystal, nothing for the eye to hold. It looks less like rock than like the *idea* of rock, an unfilled field. Over the following two or three days it acquires texture. Laminations appear, a millimetre at a time, in the wrong order — top layers first. Then flecks of mica that were not there at dusk. Then, in some plates and not others, inclusions: a rusty streak like an iron-rich seam, a pocket of quartz, a hairline of what is unmistakably a dried mud-crack.

And then, sometimes, fossils.

Not many. A handful per season. Bivalve casts the size of a thumbnail. Once, on the plate nearest the fig, the pressed spiral of an ammonite eleven centimetres across, in stone that had been blank ground four days earlier, at an altitude where no sea has ever been. The fossils are correct. They have been examined. They are internally consistent, they show the right diagenetic mineral replacement, they are exactly what they claim to be, and they are younger than most household appliances.

The working phrase for this is *ornament*, and nobody likes it much.

### The water

Water is the second thing about the Shoulder, and arguably the first.

The whole terrace weeps. Fresh stone comes up saturated, and as it cools it gives its water back, so every riser has its slow film, every joint its dark tongue, and the low corners hold shallow warm pools that go milky with dissolved silica. The largest of these — three metres by five, thigh-deep, floored in a soft white gel that takes a footprint and keeps it — is the Sump. It is 44°C at dawn and 39° by evening, which is backwards, and it smells clean, faintly mineral, like a wet knife.

Every afternoon around two the cloud comes up the flank. It arrives from below, which never stops being remarkable: a white ceiling rising past you until you are inside it, and then the light goes flat and pearly and the visibility drops to twenty metres, and the terrace becomes the entire world with a soft wall around it. Inside the cloud everything runs. The fig drips. The stone drips. Your eyebrows drip. The heat does not lessen — it thickens, becomes a wet hand on the back of the neck — and for two hours the Shoulder is a hot glass room with water on every surface.

By five the cloud has gone over the summit and the terrace is left steaming visibly, ribbons of it standing off the plates in the low sun.

At night the dew is heavy enough to harvest, and there are leaves here shaped, plausibly, for exactly that.

### What grows

Vegetation is lush where it exists and absent everywhere else, and the boundary between the two is abrupt — a hand's width from green to bare.

There are eleven vent-gardens on the Shoulder, each a metre or two across, clustered where warm damp air seeps through a joint. They are dominated by a fern that grows fast enough to watch: a frond will unroll from crozier to full extension in nine days, hold for a month, brown, and collapse into the only soil the mountain has. The gardens are dense, glossy, and slightly obscene in their vigour. Around them: sixty, seventy metres of bare pale stone with steam over it.

And there is the fig.

One tree, three metres tall, with a grey trunk and a canopy wider than it is high, standing at the western point of the terrace where two plates have opened a joint the width of a wrist. Its roots go down into that joint and out across the bare stone in flattened straps, gripping like solder. It fruits twice a year, small hard figs that are pollinated by something nobody has yet seen. It is estimated to be sixty to eighty years old by every method available: trunk anatomy, root mass, the accumulated scarring.

The mountain is nineteen.

The tree does not appear to be moving upward with the rock. Over four years of survey it has stayed at the same altitude, which means the terrace is rising *past* it, which means the joint it is rooted in is being continuously supplied. It is either being maintained or it is maintaining itself. The distinction may not be meaningful.

### What was put here

Nine structures, of which six are the same.

The six are stoups: hip-high pillars of Shoulder stone, hexagonal, each with a shallow groove cut round the top like a gutter, spaced at intervals that are not quite regular but are not random either. They are always dry. In the middle of the afternoon cloud, with water running off everything else in sheets, the stoups are dry, and warm, and if you put your ear to one you get a low steady tone somewhere near the bottom of hearing, more felt than heard. They do not respond to being struck. They cannot be marked; a scratch closes overnight.

The seventh has fallen and split. Inside it is not stone. Inside it is a packed mass of pale filaments finer than hair, laid in bundles, with the sheen of fig-flesh — and where the filaments are exposed to air they have gone dull and slack and stopped, so the interior is a photograph of a machine rather than a machine. This is as close as anyone has got to the mechanism, and it is not close.

The eighth is a shelter: four courses of broken plates dry-stacked into a windbreak against the east riser, roofed with a green polymer tarp that has been *incorporated* along one edge — the tarp does not lie on the stone, it continues into it, fibres and mineral interleaved for a centimetre and a half. It has held that way for eleven years.

The ninth is a boot.

A left boot, size 44, sole upward, standing in relief on a plate near the Sump, rendered entirely in pale grey stone with the tread pattern intact and legible and the stitching preserved. It is not a cast or an impression. It is the boot, converted, and it stands proud of the surface by four centimetres because the plate around it has worn very slightly faster.

This is the reason for the only rule that matters: *do not sleep on the ground.* Hammocks are slung from the fig and from two pegs that the mountain has, so far, tolerated.

### Sound, and its absence

The Shoulder is quiet in a specific and unusual way. New stone is dense and dead; it does not ring, it does not echo. Shout and the shout goes out from you and does not come back from anywhere. There is no wind here to speak of — the terrace sits in a lee, and the afternoon cloud arrives in near silence — so the acoustic budget of the whole place is: drip, drip, the hiss of a vent, the tick of a cooling plate contracting, the wet slap of a fern frond letting go of its accumulated dew, and the stoup-tone under all of it, at the edge of what a body registers as sound at all.

Twice, at night, a frog. Never twice from the same direction.

### Fauna

Rare, and mostly aerial. Swifts pass over daily and roost here in the dry season, on the underside of the fig, which is the only surface on the mountain that will hold a nest. A pale crab, wrist-wide and eyeless, lives in the Sump and eats the silica gel; there are never more than three, and there have never been fewer than two. Ants, black, carrying chips of new stone downhill for reasons that do not resolve into anything. Some flying pollinator, inferred from the figs, never observed.

You can spend a full day on the Shoulder and see nothing move but water and vegetation.

### What the place is doing

It is not finished, and it is not slowing.

The terrace widens seaward by roughly seven metres a year and rises by seven, so its position in space is a diagonal. Last April a new vent opened three metres from the Sump; by June it had a fern garden. In 2031 the upslope lip will reach the second stoup, and either the stoup will go up with the rock or the rock will go up past the stoup, and there is a small unofficial betting pool on which.

The mountain is being made. That much is not in question. Something below is laying down stone at a rate no geology allows, with grain and inclusions and a plausible sedimentary past appended afterward like a signature, and it is arranging water, and it is keeping one fig tree alive at a fixed altitude while the ground streams upward past its roots, and it has taken one boot into the record and kept it perfectly.

What it is not doing is hurrying, or explaining, or making a sound.

Stand on the eastern point in the last hour of light, with the cloud gone over the summit and the whole terrace exhaling, and the stone under your feet at blood heat, and the ammonite four paces away in rock that will be five years old in November. Below, forest. Above, the throat, blank and pale and rising.

It is hot. It is wet. It is very quiet. It is entirely new.
