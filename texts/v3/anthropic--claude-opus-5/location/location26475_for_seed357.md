## The Massif of Standing Green

### Approach

You come up through cloud for a long time, and the cloud gets warmer as you rise, which is wrong, and you keep rising anyway.

At about three thousand four hundred metres the cloud thins into steam, and then thins again, and you step out onto a floor.

It is a floor. That is the first thing and it never stops being the first thing. It is level to within a hand's width over distances you can walk for an hour, it is warm — blood-warm, sometimes hotter — it is faintly springy, and it is alive. Not covered in life. *Made* of it. A single continuous sheet of low fused growth, leaves welded edge to edge into a leather pavement, veined, exhaling. Push a finger into a seam and it closes behind you slowly, like flesh. There is no soil. There is no stone. There is nothing under the floor but more floor, older, darker, pressed to the density of horn, and under that darker still, and under that a black warm compacted mass that has never been sampled to its base and may not have one.

The massif built itself. Everything you will see here was made. Nothing that made it had hands.

### Heat

The air at this altitude should be thin and cold. It is thin and it is thirty-four degrees at dawn.

The green makes its own heat. Every square metre of the floor, every column, every hanging curtain of the towers is shot through with thermogenic tissue — the same trick a skunk cabbage uses to melt itself out of snow, run at continental scale and never switched off. The massif burns its own sugar to keep its own body at a working temperature, and it works at a temperature that would kill most things. Lay your palm on a load-bearing trunk and take it off again quickly.

The consequence is weather. The massif transpires so hard, so hot, that it manufactures its own atmosphere: a standing dome of saturated air that condenses against the cooler true sky and comes back down. It rains here nearly every afternoon and the rain never came from anywhere else. It is the same water, cycling, sometimes three times a day, water that has been inside the plants so many times it has a taste — green, faintly metallic, sweetish, like the water in a cut-flower vase.

Every vertical surface weeps. There is no dry object in the region.

### The Standing Things

Now look out across the floor, and here is the strangeness that takes the longest to accept: the emptiness.

Total vegetation, and almost nothing in sight.

From the top of any tower you can see perhaps twenty kilometres before the steam closes, and in that twenty kilometres you might count six other structures. Six. Between them: floor. Flat, green, steaming, immense, empty. The massif is not a jungle. A jungle is a scrum. This is a plaza the size of a county with a scattering of cathedrals on it, and each cathedral is four or five kilometres from its nearest neighbour, and the space between them is meant. The floor is one organism and it does not permit competitors; the towers are the places where it has decided, for reasons of drainage or heat or light, to stand up.

And they *stand up* as architecture. This is the part nobody gets used to.

The towers have colonnades. They have arches — proper arches, with something functionally identical to a keystone where two growth fronts meet under load and fuse. They have stairs: helical risers spiralling the outer face, each tread a fused leaf-plate, each rise about the height of a human step, because that is what the material and the gravity together happen to produce. They have doorways. They have rooms — hollow chambers off the central pumping column, walled, lintelled, floored, with a niche in the wall at sitting height and sometimes a slot in the outer wall shaped like a window and positioned as a window would be positioned.

Nothing sits in the niche. Nothing has ever sat in the niche. There are no animals in this world at all — not a beetle, not a bird, not a worm in the not-soil, nothing with a nerve. The forms are convergence, not intention: the cheapest way to hold up an enormous wet mass in hot competition with your own shade is arcade, buttress, vault, void. Architecture is just what structure looks like when it is honest. But you will walk up a staircase that fits your legs, through a doorway that fits your shoulders, into a room with a window looking out over the plain, and you will find the room warm and damp and slightly pulsing, and the sensation is not one of discovery. It is one of intrusion.

The towers run to two hundred metres. They are hollow. They are pumps: enormous vertical vessels lifting water out of the mass, spraying it into the dome, keeping the whole system in circulation. The tallest ones have aqueducts — arched living pipes that leap kilometre-gaps from tower to tower on a row of piers, carrying water horizontally for reasons that only make sense if you accept that the whole massif is one distributed body solving a plumbing problem. The aqueducts leak. Beneath each arch, a permanent hot drizzle falls onto the floor, and the floor beneath is paler and softer there, and steams harder.

### Sound

It is loud. It is astonishingly loud, and the loudness is constant, so that after a day you stop hearing it and start hearing only the failures of it.

The base layer is the transpiration roar — millions of stomata venting hot vapour, which at scale is a sound like a distant crowd or a weir, coming from everywhere including under your feet. Over that, the hollow towers: wind across their open tops and through their vents makes them organ-pipes, and a tower will hold a note for an hour, and two towers a kilometre apart will beat against each other and produce a slow pulsing throb that you feel in the sternum.

Over *that* is the growth. This is the part that startles. New internodes come in fast enough to fail audibly — sharp cracks like rifle fire, dozens per minute across the visible plain, each one a segment of green cane snapping into place under its own turgor pressure. When a bud-front opens on a tower flank, it sounds like canvas tearing, and goes on for twenty minutes.

And then, three or four times a day, a Fall.

### Turbulence

A tower can rise forty metres in eight days. It is still, at that height, mostly water and pressure. It will get top-heavy, or its shade will kill the floor that feeds it, or an aqueduct will overload it, and it will go.

You see it first — an entire green cathedral leaning, then unzipping down one flank, then coming apart into a long slow avalanche of pale interior — and it is silent for the length of time it takes sound to cross five kilometres, half a minute, forty seconds, in which the world remains loud in its ordinary way while a mountain is dying in absolute quiet. Then the boom arrives, and it is a physical event, and the floor under you jumps, and the aqueducts sing.

Where the tower lands, the floor opens. Within an hour, root-mouths — blunt white organs, the size of a fist, thousands of them — have come up through the pavement and begun to close on the wreckage. There are no fungi here, no bacteria of decay worth the name; the massif eats its own dead directly, absorbing the fallen tower into the floor over about nine days, leaving a paler scar which is itself the ideal nursery bed. Two weeks later a new column is up to head height on the same spot.

Nothing is old here. The oldest visible feature in the entire region is eleven years old. Estimate any structure's age in months.

### The Edge

The massif has an edge, and the edge is fresh.

There is no talus at the rim, no scree, no apron of debris, no rounded shoulder. There is a floor, level and green and steaming, and then there is a torn vertical face six hundred metres deep, and then there is cloud.

The massif grows outward faster than it can support itself, so periodically a plate of pavement the size of a small town shears off along a clean fracture and drops into the weather below. What is left is a cross-section: eleven years of laminated green, banded like a cut of muscle, weeping down its whole height. The water coming out of that face falls as a curtain the entire length of the rim and never reaches bottom as water — it disperses into the cloud that the massif's own heat has made, and is drawn back up, and rains again on the plaza.

Nothing has ever weathered here. The word does not apply. Things are new, and then they are gone, and the interval between is measured in seasons.

### Rules

1. There is no substrate. Dig as long as you like.
2. Nothing that lives here has a nerve, a muscle, or a choice, and yet the region is full of decisions.
3. Water does not leave, except as cloud, and the cloud comes back.
4. Heat is manufactured and never rests. If the massif ever stopped burning sugar it would freeze solid overnight at this altitude, and it knows this in the only way a thing without a mind can know something: by having done it and not being here.
5. All the doors are real doors and none of them are for you.
6. If you stay a month you will not see the same skyline twice, and you will find, going back to a room you slept in, that the room has moved, or grown a second window, or closed.

Stand in the middle of the plaza at noon, four kilometres from anything, in the wash of steam, in the roar, with the floor pulsing warm under your boots and six green cathedrals standing far off in the mist and one of them audibly under construction.

It is the most built place there is, and no one built it, and no one is coming.
