**The Basin of Slow Masonry**

You come down into it over a lip of ground so new it still has burrs — a rim of stone flaked outward like the edge of a torn page, sharp enough to cut a bootsole. Nothing has worn it. There is no rain here to round anything off, no frost-splitting, no roots prying. What breaks in this country breaks once, cleanly, and then keeps its corner forever.

Below the rim the land is low and flat and pale, a shallow pan perhaps two thousand paces across, and it is full of a town.

Not ruins. Not a town in any sense that involves people. But streets — real streets, with a crown to them so that nothing would pool if there were anything to pool; and thresholds, and lintels, and courtyards with a step down into them; and blind arcades running along a wall for eighty paces before deciding to stop. Chimneys, though there is no fire in this basin and never has been. Stairs, half a hundred flights of them, going up to landings that face a blank face of wall. The whole of it is made of one material and grown in one piece, as a frost-flower on a window is grown in one piece, and it is a little under three storeys at its highest, and it is very quiet, and it is entirely empty.

---

The material is what the people who once mapped this place — never lived here; nobody lives here — called flourstone. Underfoot it feels like packed flour that has set: fine-grained, cool, faintly warm-tinted in the way of bone, and dry in a manner that is hard to describe to anyone raised near water. Not thirsty-dry. Simply complete without moisture, the way glass is. It takes a sharp arris and holds it. Struck with a knuckle, a wall gives no ring, only a small dull *tuk* that goes nowhere.

There is no water in this basin, none in the ground beneath it, none in the sky over it. Instead there is the gas: a heavy, colourless, slow-moving air that lies in the pan like milk in a saucer and comes up to about the height of a person's chest. Cool — everything here is cool, the temperature of a cellar in early spring — and cool is the whole engine of the place. Below a certain threshold the gas dissolves mineral out of the basin floor and carries it in suspension, invisibly, a solution of stone in something that is not liquid. Above that threshold it cannot hold its load and lets it go.

So the town is built every dawn.

The light comes up behind a permanent lid of suspended fines, a ceiling of dust glowing a weak pearl, and the sun is never a disc, only a brightening quarter of sky. As that brightening reaches the floor of the pan, the gas warms by a degree or two and sheds what it was carrying. It sheds it, not as dust settling, but as growth: onto surfaces, along edges, following the geometry already present, thickening what is thick, extending what points. Over about four hours a wall gains the thickness of a fingernail. A stair gains a stair. An arcade decides on one more arch.

By midday the new flourstone has set hard as unglazed porcelain and the shedding stops. Through the long dim afternoon nothing happens at all. Then, as the basin cools toward evening, the gas takes up its appetite again and begins to dissolve — not randomly, but along the same lines by which it built, so that the town comes apart in the grammar of its own construction. A courtyard un-corners itself. A chimney reduces politely from the top. Nothing here erodes; things are *unbuilt*, and unbuilt tidily.

The books called this the ledger, because over a year the basin neither gains nor loses. Over a night it can lose a whole quarter and gain a different quarter somewhere else. The town is the same age as this morning and the same age as the basin, which is young, which is barely finished emerging.

---

Its motifs are the strangeness. Nothing here needs a door and yet there are doors — proper ones, with jambs and a rebate for a leaf that has never existed. Nothing needs a stair and there are stairs beyond counting, and they are correctly proportioned for a human tread, a hand's-width rise, which is the fact that empties the stomach of most visitors on the first afternoon. There are window openings at sitting height with a sill worn — no, not worn, *made* — with a shallow dip in the centre as though something had leaned there for four hundred years.

And it never repeats a plan. Walk the basin twice and the second walk is a different town with the same vocabulary. It has an enormous number of things to say and only about nine words.

---

The vegetation grows only on flourstone less than a day old, which is to say it grows in the morning and is dissolved out from underneath by evening, and survives by being fast. It comes up in the freshly shed layer as quill-grass: stiff, glassy, translucent blades a hand high, hollow, taking their substance directly from the loaded gas and giving off a fine powder that drifts and lies in windrows against the base of walls. In the plazas there is nothing. Along the shadowed north side of a long wall you might find a stand of it thirty blades strong and no other living thing within a hundred paces. That is the density of the place: intervals. Vast unpopulated intervals between one small presence and the next, and rooms — hundreds of rooms — with absolutely nothing in them, and nothing having ever been in them, and no dust on the floor because the floor is one day old.

Twice in the basin there are older growths, in two courtyards where for some reason the ledger balances in favour of accretion. Here the quill has had years and has thickened into a low glass thicket, blue-grey, the blades fused where they crossed, hollow throughout, and utterly still, because there is not enough wind at floor level in the heavy gas to move anything. You can put your hand into such a thicket and it will not stir. It will break, though, with a sound like a small bird's bone.

---

Animals are rare enough that you will not be sure, the first day, that there are any.

There are the grazers: pale slow-bodied things the size of a hare, with no eyes worth the name, that work along a fresh corner in the morning licking mineral off it with a broad rasping tongue. They eat the architecture. Because they favour edges and arrises — the richest, softest new stone is on an edge — they leave the only rounded features in the whole basin: a doorjamb with one soft flank, a step scooped shallow. Where you see roundness, something has been feeding. You will see one grazer a day if you are lucky, and it will not care about you, and it will make no sound at all except, occasionally, the rasp, which does not carry.

And there is whatever lives in the sumps.

---

Which brings up the sound.

The heavy gas does not carry noise. It absorbs it, and worse, it *drains* it: sound goes downhill in this basin. Speak in the middle of a street and your voice does not travel to the walls — it falls, spreads along the pavement, runs to the nearest low place, and is gone. There is no echo anywhere in the town. Clap in a stone courtyard the size of a chapel and you get a single flat *pat* and then a silence so complete that you become aware of the working of your own throat.

The low places are the sumps: a dozen sunken courts, four or five steps down, floored flat. Sound arrives there and does not leave. Stand at the top step of one and you can hear it — not your own noise, but an accumulated murmur just at the edge of the audible, an old soft granular hum with nothing in it that can be resolved into a word or a footfall. Days of quill-grass breaking. Grazers rasping. Weather. It sits in the sump and slowly cools out of existence.

Something in the sumps eats it, is the local theory, and there is no local, so it is nobody's theory. Down in the flat floor of the deepest of them there are shallow soft-lipped hollows, wet-looking though nothing here is wet, arranged the way a spider's funnels are arranged, and if you go down among them your footsteps make no noise at all — not muffled: *absent* — and the murmur stops for as long as you are standing there, and resumes above and behind you when you climb out.

---

The rules of the basin, as anyone who has spent four nights here would give them:

Nothing pools. There is no low water, no mud, no rot, no smell. Cut yourself and the blood beads and sets like sealing-wax. Nothing decays; it is dissolved, on schedule, along its own joints.

Do not sleep indoors. The dawn shed follows existing geometry, and a doorway that is a doorway at midnight can be a shallow niche by nine in the morning. It will be soft for those hours — you can push through new flourstone with a shoulder while it is still flour — but not after noon. After noon it is porcelain and you are inside a room that has decided, in its own grammar, to become a wall.

Do not trust the light for distance. Under the pearl lid there are no shadows to speak of, and every surface is one colour, and every edge is razor-new, so a wall at eight paces and a wall at eighty look the same. The town is small. It will feel enormous, and then, when you have walked its whole diameter in fifty minutes, it will feel very small indeed, and both feelings are correct.

Do not put your ear to a sump floor. No reason is given.

---

At evening the shedding reverses and the town begins, imperceptibly, to come apart. There is no sound to it. The gas thickens and settles and the pearl overhead goes down to a dull cinder-grey, and quill-grass loses its footing and tips over and is taken back into solution before it can fall, so that the stands simply grow shorter. A staircase that had eleven treads at noon has nine. The windrows of powder lift off the wall-bases and hang, and in the last of the light there is a slow lateral drift across the basin, a whole town's worth of stone in transit, silent, going to whatever quarter tomorrow will be.

If you stand on the rim then, above the level of the gas, in the ordinary thin cold air where sound behaves, you can hear something at last: your own breathing, and the tick of the rim-stone contracting, and the very faint hiss of two thousand paces of dust going somewhere with a purpose it has never explained.
