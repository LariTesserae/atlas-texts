## The Increment

Above the cloud floor, at the height where breath turns visible and stays visible longer than it should, there is a mountain that nobody carved and nothing grew. It was assembled. It is still being assembled, from underneath, at a rate of about four centimetres a year — which means that the summit terraces, the oldest, are perhaps three hundred years above their casting, and everything else is younger than that, and nothing anywhere has had time to weather. The Increment has no soil. It has no bedrock. Push a probe anywhere into its flanks and you strike plate, girder, packed swarf, cable bundle, the ceramic shoulder of a kiln, another plate. All the way down, presumably, to the foundry that keeps pushing.

The light comes from below. The cloud deck lies about six hundred metres under the lowest inhabited terrace, an unbroken white floor that stretches to every horizon and catches whatever the pale high sun sends down, then throws it back up with interest. So the Increment is lit from underneath. Balcony soffits glow. The undersides of walkways are the brightest surfaces in the world, blinding, and people wear brimless caps with a stiff lower flange to shade the eyes from the ground. Shadows pool on top of things: on roofs, on shoulders, in the upward-facing cups of machinery. The city's rooftops are its darkest places, and children are told not to play there, and go anyway.

Everything is sharp. That is the first thing a body notices, before the strangeness — the sheer unworn *edge* of the place. Plate corners still bear their shear-burrs. Aluminium has not clouded. Rolled steel keeps the faint diagonal grain of the mill. Rain has fallen on these surfaces only in the intermittent, mean-spirited way of high air, and mostly what falls here is not rain but fog dragging itself upslope through the terraces at midmorning, condensing along every cold flange, beading, running in threads down the inside faces of things. You learn the drip-lines the way lowlanders learn footpaths. You learn which stair rails will soak your sleeve. The whole massif ticks and trickles. That is most of its sound.

Because the Increment is quiet. This is the hardest thing to believe of it, and it is where the world's break begins to show.

The terraces are packed with industry — near-touching, layered, stacked four and five deep so that a single vertical slice from cloud-floor to summit might pass through a rolling mill, a condensate hall, a dormitory, a press shop, a gallery of lathes, and a chapel, each pressed against the next with only a service gap between. Ten thousand machines are turning at any moment. And what you hear, standing among them, is one lathe. One press. One belt slapping its splice. Clear, unhurried, close, as though you had wandered into a small workshop on a Sunday.

Here is the rule of the world: on the Increment, identical things are not merely alike. They are *the same thing*, occupying many places. The engineers call this concordance. Nine hundred presses stamped from one pattern in one shop in one month are, thereafter, one press with nine hundred addresses. They rise and fall together — not synchronised, *singular* — and they produce, between them, one press's worth of noise, one press's worth of heat, one press's wear on one set of dies. Concordance is why the Increment can be enormous and cheap. It is also why a single dropped spanner can, in a bad month, put nine hundred addresses out of service at once, and why the maintenance guilds are the most feared and best-fed people on the mountain.

You feel concordance most in the stairwells. There are thousands of stairwells, cast from the same mould, and they are one stairwell. Climb long enough without paying attention and you arrive somewhere you cannot have arrived at, four districts across, sixty metres down, still climbing. The remedy is local defacement: every stair in the Increment has been scratched, painted, chipped, hung with a particular rag, given a particular smell of grease — anything to break the sameness and pin the address. The mountain is covered in these small violent acts of distinction. Doorframes wear scars like tally marks. Handrails are wrapped in mismatched cord. Children are taught to notch, before they are taught to read.

There is an etiquette that follows: you do not count things aloud in company. Counting is a kind of pressure. Say *one, two, three* over three concordant cups and something strains; the cups grow reluctant; occasionally one of them splits off, becomes only itself, and then it can be broken alone, and it can hold a different tea, and it is worth more than the district it stands in. This is not a trick to be done casually. Splitting is a craft, done by the Nominators, who work in a windowless hall at the ninth terrace with their sleeves pinned back, and who go blind young.

And this is what the Increment is *for*. It is an industrial complex of extraordinary scale, terraces of mills and kilns and electro-baths and slow patient hydraulic engines, all of it dedicated to the manufacture of singular objects. Uncopied things. Things that are only themselves. A generation of labour, ten thousand concordant machines running for eleven years on schedules calculated to the hour, and out of the end of it comes: one hinge. One glass bowl of a green that exists nowhere else. One spoon. The output of the entire mountain is perhaps forty objects a century, and they are carried up to the summit vault by hand, in the arms of a single unaccompanied carrier, because two carriers might be concordant and the object would not survive the ambiguity.

The vault is a low room with a stone floor — real stone, the only natural substrate on the Increment, brought from the world below at a cost nobody has ever fully accounted, so that the singulars can rest on something that is not part of the general sameness. There are two hundred and six objects on its shelves. Among them, at the end, is the bell. It is small, hand-sized, and it is the only bell here that has no sharers, and when it is rung — three times a century, at accessions and at certain deaths — every person on the mountain hears it, all the way down to the cloud terraces, and afterwards there is a full day in which nobody works, because the ears will not settle.

The living things came for the water and stayed for the multiplication. Vegetation is thin, deliberate, and mostly confined to the condensate channels: hair-mosses in the gutter seams, a black wiry sedge that colonises any joint holding damp for more than four days, and the fern trays, which are cultivated in racks along the sunward flanges because ferns are the only crop that tolerates being concordant without going strange. Nothing lichenous, though. Lichen needs old rock and old time, and the Increment has neither yet. Its surfaces are too new to be forgiven.

But the fauna are abundant, and they are eerie. Sparrows arrive in their thousands and are not thousands: there is a sparrow on the Increment, possibly two, with a great many addresses, and when a flock turns above the fifth terrace, it turns without lag, without ripple, one body wearing eleven thousand pairs of wings. It makes almost no noise. Eleven thousand sparrows, one sparrow's chirp, threading thinly through the mist. Goats — the mountain calls them aunes, hard-hoofed, grey, browsing the sedge out of the drainage seams — are more individuated, because they scar themselves on the sharp edges of things and thereby split, slowly, over a lifetime, into persons. An old aune is entirely itself, and is left alone, and is sometimes named. Beetles the length of a thumb live in the oil scum of the settling tanks and are farmed by nobody but eaten by everybody. There are cats, in reasonable numbers, and one dog, which is on every terrace, which is the same dog, which is very old and permanently mildly wet, and which the whole mountain feeds and nobody owns.

The dynamics are slow. Nothing here happens quickly except accidents. The mountain rises four centimetres a year, and each rising cracks something, and the cracks are patched, and the patch-lines make a stratigraphy that the surveyors read like tree rings. The summit sheds — this is the only erosion the place has — a fine scree of manufactured debris that slides off the topmost plates and gathers in glittering fans down the northern flank: glass swarf, ceramic chips, bright curls of turned brass, all of it still sharp, none of it rounded, a talus that cuts boots to ribbons in a season. Fog comes upslope in the morning and drains away by afternoon. Twice a year the cloud floor thins and the drop becomes visible and everyone stands at the rails and looks down at the machinery of their own foundation, glowing far below, and says very little, and goes back to work.

The temperature holds around eight degrees. People cluster at the condenser walls for warmth in the way lowlanders cluster at fires. The air smells of cold metal, hot oil, fern, and wet stone that isn't there.

And it is bright. Ferociously, evenly bright, all day, upward-flooding white light off six hundred kilometres of cloud, so that the Increment glares like something newly unwrapped — a whole mountain of unpatinated surfaces, edged and lit from beneath and nearly silent, ticking with condensate, turning ten thousand identical wheels that are one wheel, patiently making the few things in the world that will only ever be one of.
