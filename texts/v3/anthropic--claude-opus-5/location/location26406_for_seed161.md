## The Table

There was a mountain here once, and it was enormous. What remains is its palm.

Time has taken everything above this line — the summits, the ridges, the whole cathedral of it — and carried the pieces off into a gulf so deep that the bottom is only a rumour of blue. What is left is a floor of pale rock roughly a thousand paces across, faintly domed, sitting at an altitude where the sky above is not blue but a dark burnt indigo, almost black at the zenith even at noon. The sun crossing that sky is a small white nail. There is not enough air up here to spread its light around. Sunbeams arrive hard and narrow and shadows have edges like cut paper, and everywhere the beams do not directly touch, the world is dim — a warm bronze dimness, the light of a late afternoon that lasts all day.

The rock is granite so old it has gone glassy at the surface, case-hardened, wind-licked into long grooves that all run the same way, northeast, the direction of the wind that has been blowing here since before there were eyes to feel it. Harder veins stand up out of the floor a hand's height, black ribs the erosion could not chew through, and they divide the plateau into rough plates like the shell of something. Nothing here is sharp. Nothing has been sharp for a very long time. This is a landscape that has finished happening, and is now simply enduring the consequences.

And out of that finished floor comes a forest.

## The Grove

They are columns. Grey-green, waxed, fluted, thick as grain silos at the base and rising twenty, thirty, forty times a person's height — succulents, though the word sits badly on things so large. Their trunks are stacked segments, like barrels set on barrels, and each segment is a decade or two of growth, so that reading a trunk from the ground up is reading five hundred years. High overhead they split into blunt candelabra arms, and only at the very tips do they carry leaves: thick paddles the colour of old brass, held edge-on to the sun so as to catch as little of it as possible. The rest of the plant's surface is spines — grey, dull, dense as fur, an insulating pelt.

They grow near-touching. This is the important fact. The columns stand at arm's length from each other across the whole thousand paces of the plateau, and where their arms reach out at height they interlace into a low ceiling of spines and paddles and dead lattice. Beneath that ceiling the wind cannot get in. The heat that the black rock soaks up all day cannot get out. The grove is a warm room — genuinely warm, sweating warm at midday, twenty degrees kinder than the killing air fifty paces beyond its edge. Everything alive up here is alive because of this room.

Between the columns the floor is cushioned. Hard grey mounds of a plant that grows the thickness of a fingernail per human lifetime; sit on one and you are sitting on something older than any story anyone knows. There are strap-leaved rosettes wedged in the rock joints, and a black creeper with leaves like beads that runs up the fluting of the columns and mats the lower trunks so densely you cannot see the host beneath. Layer upon layer. Saturation. You cannot walk ten steps in a straight line.

And there is soil, of a kind: a fine black flour, ankle-deep in the hollows, dry as ash, that has no mineral component worth naming. It is made entirely of bodies. Pollen, moth wings, beetle shells, droppings, spines, skin, the powdered dead of a hundred thousand years, held in place by the wind-shadow of the grove. Take a handful and it is weightless and warm and smells like a hot attic.

## The Rule About Water

There is no water on the Table. None. Not a spring, not a seep, not a stain of damp on the north face of a stone, not ice in any crevice, not dew — the air is far too thin and dry for dew. It does not rain here. It cannot; the plateau stands above the level where clouds form.

You can, however, watch it rain. From the edge, looking down into the gulf, there are days when a whole weather system assembles itself two thousand feet below your sandals and lets go — a grey braid of storm dragging over country nobody up here has ever walked, thunder arriving thin and late. Children are brought to the edge to see it. It is a spectacle, the way a foreign coronation is a spectacle. It has nothing to do with them.

The water in this world is in the rock, chemically bound. The granite is shot through with pale seams of a hydrous mineral — gypsum-like, satiny, easy to scratch — and it holds water inside its crystal structure, one molecule folded into the lattice of the next. It is not wet. You can hold it, grind it, sleep on it, and remain thirsty.

The columns can get it out. Their roots do not spread; they go down, one taproot per plant, splitting the seams, dissolving the lattice with acids over centuries and drawing off the water of crystallisation drop by drop. A mature column is a standing reservoir of some thousands of gallons, sealed under wax, defended by spines, accumulated at a rate of perhaps a cupful a day.

So: everything that drinks here drinks the plants, or drinks something that drank the plants. This is the entire economy, ecology and morality of the place.

## Fauna

The grove is loud. This is the first thing that surprises, given how thin the air is — sound ought to be feeble here, and each individual sound is feeble, and yet there is a *wall* of noise, because there are so many makers of it and because the rock beneath is riddled with old gas-cavities that boom like a body of a guitar.

The dominant noise is a sawing drone from the canopy, produced by a shield-backed insect the length of a thumb that spends its adult life clamped to the upper trunks with its mouthparts in the plant. There are millions. The drone rises with the warmth and falls at night but never stops, and it is loud enough that people up here shout by default, and their language has developed a whole register of hard consonants and hand-signs to survive it. Visitors — there are almost no visitors — describe it as maddening for four days and then inaudible forever.

Under the drone: the tick and grind of boring beetles, whose larvae tunnel the columns and whose galleries are the source of most of the water anyone drinks; the rattle of long-legged lizards through dry leaf-litter; the shriek, twice a day, of the colonies. The hollow rock holds bats — leathery, small, dense-boned, poor fliers in this air, more clamberers than fliers — and they come out in a screaming spiral at dusk and return in a screaming spiral at dawn, and hunt moths under the canopy all night. There are birds that have mostly given up on flight, heavy little grey things that run and hop through the cushion plants and stab beetles.

And there are the shearers.

They are the large herbivores: low, broad, slow, shelled across the back with fused plates, roughly the size of a settee, with a horned beak that can take a bite out of a column's wax armour the way a shovel takes a bite out of clay. They browse. They are the only animals capable of opening a mature column, and they store what they take in a sac beneath the throat — thirty or forty litres in a well-fed adult. A shearer moves about two hundred paces a day. It lives, as near as anyone can tell, four or five hundred years.

## The Town

Nobody up here herds shearers, exactly. It is closer to say the town and the shearers have an old arrangement, and that the town's part of it is being useful enough to be tolerated.

There are perhaps four hundred people on the Table, and their settlement is not beside the grove or in a clearing in the grove — there are no clearings, there is no room. The town *is* the grove, built into it, around it, up it. Platforms of stacked granite slabs bridge from column to column, mortared with hardened sap. Walls are wattle of dead spine-lattice, plastered with the same sap and the black body-flour, set as hard as fired clay — which is useful, because you cannot make pottery without water and nobody here has ever made a pot. Roofs are woven paddle-fibre, waxed, low, doubling as terraces for the level above. Four, five, six levels in places, climbed by notched poles and rope ladders, so the town has a vertical grain: the old and the honoured live low where it is warmest and the walking is flat, the young live high in the racket of the canopy where the bats come and go through the rafters and there is a view, twice a day, of the sun.

The streets are gaps between trunks. They are narrow, warm, dim, and full of noise and people and hanging bundles. Everything is within a nine-minute walk of everything else. The whole world is within a nine-minute walk of everything else.

Their tools are stone, bone, spine, fibre, and wax. There is a little metal — soft yellow beads and one ceremonial blade, from below, from before, nobody can say — and it is not used, it is inherited. What they are extraordinary at is cutting. A tapping knife is a blade of black volcanic glass hafted in resin, and a tapper's job is to open a column's wax with a wound the width of a finger, angled so it will scar closed within two seasons, and take not more than the plant can replace. The wounds are catalogued. Every column in the grove has a name and a history and a family that holds the right to tap it, and the scars run up the trunk in a vertical column of pale ovals like the rungs of a ladder — your great-grandmother's cut here, your mother's there, yours next, a little higher. Tap too deep and the column dies in about ninety years, which is to say your grandchildren will find out what you did.

Alongside the tappers are the shearer-keepers, who walk with the animals, clear their paths, drive off the bats that pester them, and in return draw off two or three litres a day per animal by the throat sac. Shearer water is thick, greenish and faintly sweet, and it is what children and the sick drink. Column sap, taken directly, is stronger stuff and mildly narcotic, and is drunk at feasts.

Nothing here is ever washed. Cleaning is done with dry ash, dry sand, wax, and the tongues of small trained lizards kept for the purpose. Cooking, when it happens, is dry-roasting on hot rock in a beam of sun. Spitting is a crime of shocking magnitude, and weeping is a formal gift — to weep in front of someone is to give them something irreplaceable of yourself, and it is done rarely and deliberately, at reconciliations and at deaths.

The dead are not burned; fuel is water in another form. They are carried to the base of a chosen column, into the dry black flour, and left in the roots, and their name is added to that column's history. So the trunks are genealogies as well as reservoirs, and the water everyone drinks has been through the rock and the root and, at a slower remove, through everyone who came before. Nobody up here finds this metaphorical. It is simply plumbing.

## Time

Almost nothing changes quickly on the Table. The erosion is finished. The plants add a fingerwidth a decade. The shearers outlive dynasties. Political disputes over tapping rights are resolved on a scale of generations, argued by grandchildren of the original disputants, and the town's council does not so much make decisions as slowly notice them. A house is a hundred and fifty years of accumulated additions. A path is a groove worn shin-deep into glassy granite.

There is one event. Once every thirty-eight or forty years — nobody can quite pin it, and the arguments about prediction are the closest thing here to science — the columns flower together. All of them, in one week, in the warm season: white waxy trumpets the size of a child's head, opening at dusk, smelling of hot resin and something like almonds. They dump nectar. It runs down the fluting and pools in the cushion plants and this is the only time in a human life that liquid can be found lying loose on the ground of this world.

The moths come up out of the gulf for it in a column visible for miles, and the bats go mad, and the drone of the shield-insects is drowned for the first time in four decades by the noise of wings. The town does not sleep. People drink from the rock. Children are conceived in numbers that produce a visible bulge in the population every forty years, so that the town's demography breathes in slow deep pulses, and everyone belongs to a flowering, and you can ask a stranger's age simply by asking which one.

Then the flowers brown and drop and are ground into the black flour underfoot, and the drone comes back, and the grove settles again into its long warm dim humming patience, up here on the last flat handspan of a mountain that has otherwise entirely gone away.
