## The Increment

Come up the last ladder and you are standing on the newest ground in the world.

It is a terrace, roughly oval, about the size of a village green — two hundred paces the long way, a hundred and thirty across — and every particle of it was made. There is no soil here. There is no bedrock here, not in any sense you could dig down to and touch. Somewhere far below the cloud floor there may be an original mountain, a true one, but nobody living has seen it and the old ledgers do not go back that far. What you stand on is Course Ninety-One: a raft of cast greenstone blocks, still pale at their edges, still shedding the fine parting-sand they were struck from, laid over Course Ninety, which lies over Eighty-Nine, and so on down into the white.

The cloud floor is about twelve meters below the parapet. It is not a static thing. It rises — a hand's width a year, the ledgers agree, sometimes two in a bad decade — and this is the reason for everything you will see here. The Increment climbs to stay dry. It builds upward at precisely the pace of drowning.

---

**Underfoot.** Because nothing here is old, nothing here is smooth. The stone has arrises so crisp they will slice a thumb: no weather has yet had time to soften them. You can read the mold-marks on every block, the little ridged scars where the slurry was poured, the maker's punch — a dot-and-comma, a hand-sign, a number. Walk with attention. The whole terrace is a fresh geology with no habits yet. Rain arrives and does not know where to go. It sheets sideways across the pavers, ponds in corners the masons did not anticipate, gathers in a hollow that will be a lake for eleven hours and then be gone, dry as a plate. There are gutters cut, of course, beautiful ones, but the water has not yet agreed to use them. Watching it choose — watching a stain of damp explore a joint, hesitate, and then commit — is what the tenders do instead of watching a river.

The greenstone itself is grown, not quarried. Quarrying is impossible and also unthinkable; there is nothing to quarry. In the six casting bays at the north end, ash and burnt shell and lime and gray flour of old crushed course are mixed with water and with *quickener* — a slurry of mineralizing moss cultured in the warm drains — and poured into oiled wooden forms. Over eighteen days the mix stiffens, warms, and knits itself, and the moss dies inside it having left behind its structure: a fine reticulation you can see in a broken face, like the ghost of a sponge. The stone is faintly green because of this. It smells, when wet, of pondweed and struck flint.

**Heat and water.** You expected cold at this altitude and there is none. The Increment is warm, mildly, evenly, about the temperature of a greenhouse at nine in the morning, and it is warm because it is a machine that has been running a long time and has never fully been allowed to cool. Under the pavement runs the flue network from the calcining kilns — six of them, squat, brick, breathing at the terrace's east lip — and their exhaust is not vented to the sky but threaded back and forth beneath the stone, up through hollow columns, out along the parapet copings. Put your palm flat anywhere and you will feel it: not hot, just alive.

That warmth meets the rising cloud and the whole place condenses. This is the water economy. Every vertical surface here is designed to sweat. The parapets are ribbed on their outer faces with hundreds of little vanes so that fog, drifting up and touching warm ceramic, gives up its load; the water runs down into channels, into settling basins, into cisterns cast into the body of Course Eighty-Nine. Certain walls are hung with combs of black slate and wire fleece that drip continuously into lead troughs — you can hear them everywhere, the *tick, tick* of the fleece-combs, the softest industry imaginable. Nothing here is ever quite dry. Rope is heavy. Salt does not exist as a loose thing. There is moss in every joint, and moss is not weeding but crop.

**Green.** With no soil, the vegetation has had to be invented, and it is exuberant. It grows in trays, in slung baskets, in split pipes filled with charcoal and crushed shell, in the hollow tops of balustrades, in the seams between blocks where quickener-moss has been deliberately seeded, in the flues' warm draft where nothing should live and yet ferns unroll in a green fringe like eyelashes. The density is such that you move through the Increment as through a room hung with cloth. Bean vines run up rebar frames set into the pavement, four meters, five, their leaves the size of a hand and dripping. Tree ferns stand in cast urns as tall as a man, their crowns making a low false canopy over the water-stairs. Whole walls are ladders of epiphytes — bromeliads in coir pockets, each one a small cup of its own private water with a single mosquito larva in it. There are figs in tubs, kept dwarf and furious, and one fig that got out.

That fig is the most watched organism on the terrace. It rooted eleven years ago in a coping joint on the south parapet and has since put a finger of white root nine centimeters into Course Ninety, and the crack it made is the first crack here not made by a person. They have left it. There is a small numbered tag on the branch and a mark on the stone where the fissure ended last winter. The tenders call this *the beginning of the outside*, and they are not sure whether to feel dread. Everything here is pre-erosive, unweathered, embryonic; the fig is the future arriving early.

**Sound.** It is quieter than you would believe for a place with six kilns and a pumphouse. The pumps are slow — a two-meter flywheel turning at eleven revolutions a minute, driving the lift that raises cistern-water up to the mixing floor — and their sound is a *whum . . . whum* you stop hearing within an hour and then feel in your sternum for the rest of your life. Above that, the drip. Below that, the wind combing through the fleece-walls and the guy-cables, a note that rises and falls a quarter-tone. Voices carry oddly and don't go far; the vegetation eats them. Once each shift a bell is struck at the casting bays — one note, unhurried — and once every eighteen days the forms come off a new pour, and that is loud, a long clattering wooden avalanche, the only real noise the Increment makes, and afterward the quiet seems to have been rinsed.

**Living things that came on their own.** Very few. Two pale swifts nest in the dead flue at the west corner and hunt the fog for whatever tiny things ride it; they are the only large animals born here and the tenders regard them as a kind of weather. There are cave-white salamanders in the warm drains, six or seven, fed on the mosquito larvae they cull from the bromeliads. There are copper hives, but bees do poorly at this pressure, so most pollination is done by hand, at dusk, with sable brushes — a row of people moving slowly through the bean frames, touching flower after flower, saying nothing. If you arrive during the brushing hour you will think you have walked into a rite. You have.

**The rules, as they are understood here.**

Nothing may be taken from below the cloud. Everything must be made from what is already up, or from what the air gives.

The stone must be grown, and it must be grown faster than the cloud rises, and the margin — the difference between the two rates — is the entire wealth of this place. It is currently about four centimeters a year.

When a course is complete, the whole works must move up onto it: kilns rebuilt, urns hoisted, cisterns re-plumbed, the fleece-walls unbolted and carried, the fig tub lifted with three people and a sling. It takes a season. This has happened ninety-one times.

Nothing is repaired below the current course. What sinks into the cloud is finished with. Some evenings the fog thins and drops and you can see, twenty meters down, the coping of Course Eighty-Seven with its old moss still growing in the dark, and the tops of urns nobody bothered to raise, and a bronze bracket gone the color of a leaf. It is the only ruin available for looking at, and it is not weathered either. Down there, undisturbed, everything stays as sharp-edged and unfinished as it was on the day it was left, drowning very slowly in white.
