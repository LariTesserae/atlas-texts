## The Valley of Made Ground

Come up the switchback road in the late afternoon — which here is most of the day — and the first thing you understand is that the mountain has been unmade and remade until nothing of the original slope remains.

There is no bedrock at the surface anywhere in this valley. There is fill: crushed stone, sifted ash, spent brick, riverbed gravel hauled uphill in cable buckets and tamped into place by generations of feet and rollers. Under the fill there is masonry — retaining wall behind retaining wall, some of them buried by the terrace they were built to hold, so that when a wall fails and the earth slumps you see an older wall inside it, and inside that older wall, sometimes, an older one still. The valley is a wall wrapped around a wall wrapped around whatever the mountain used to be. Nobody alive has seen it.

The terraces climb both flanks in shallow steps, three to five metres of rise each, and the steps are not level: they are given a fall of a hand's width in every pace so water moves. Everything here is arranged so water moves. Water is the whole argument of the place.

---

### Water

It rains, or it is about to have rained, or the cloud is simply resting in the valley and depositing itself on every leaf and lintel without falling at all. Two hundred and something days of measurable precipitation; the rest are fog. At this altitude the air holds water the way wool holds it — not in drops but in weight. Your coat does not get wet from above. It gets heavy from within.

Consequently the built surfaces weep. Wall faces are dark to a certain height and pale above, and the dark part is not shadow, it is saturation. Mortar joints extrude little white beards of lime. Every stair has a green stripe down its centre where the runoff has trained a moss to live in the traffic lane. The masonry runs, drips, seeps, and the whole city is plumbed to catch it: flagstone gutters, half-pipes of glazed clay, iron grates, sumps, and beneath everything the great intercepting drains that take the hillside's water down to the turbine houses.

The river at the bottom of the valley is not a river anymore. It is four rivers in parallel — a stone-lined channel for the flood, a flume for the mills, a covered aqueduct for the drinking supply, and a fourth, the tailrace, warm and brown, carrying away what the turbines have finished with. They braid and separate under bridges you do not notice you are crossing, because the bridge is the street, and the street has houses on it, and the houses have gardens on their roofs.

---

### The green

It is lush past the point of politeness. There is no bare made surface in this valley that has not been colonised or planted, and often both.

The terraces are planted in a dense mixed regime: barley and bitter potato on the wide upper benches, then a band of alder and shrub-willow grown as coppice for the charcoal kilns, then, lower and warmer, the glasshouse belt — long low sheds of small panes, thousands of them, stepping down the slope like scales. Inside: tomato, tea, forced strawberry, and the enormous fern-leaved lilies grown for the alkaloid that the pharmacy works pay for. The glass sweats. From above, in fog, the glasshouse belt is a soft grey shine with dim yellow lamps inside it, and the lamps make each pane a little separate window into a warm somewhere else.

But the planting is only half of it. The other half is the volunteer growth, which nobody planted and nobody entirely resents. Moss, first — twenty kinds, thick as a mattress on north-facing walls, so deep on the parapets that children scoop it out in slabs. Then liverworts in the gutters. Ferns in every wall crack, whole hanging gardens of them from the stone courses of the aqueduct arches, their fronds combed downhill by the drip. Rowan and birch seed into any roof gutter left alone for two years, and there are roofs — abandoned works, roofs of buildings whose owners are in litigation — carrying small forests that shed leaves onto the street each autumn as though they were entitled to.

Ivy, of course, holds a great deal of the older masonry together and is slowly taking a great deal of it apart. This is discussed in the same tone people elsewhere use for the weather.

Everything is a shade darker than it should be, because the light is thin and plants here grow big soft leaves to catch it. The green is not a bright green. It is the green of a cellar where something is thriving.

---

### Light

Dim, and consistently dim, which matters more than its dimness. The cloud base sits below the upper terraces for most of the year, so the top of the valley is inside the sky and the bottom of the valley is under a lid. Noon has the quality of four in the afternoon. Four in the afternoon has the quality of a room where somebody has forgotten to light the lamp and everyone is too polite to mention it.

So the valley is electrified, and has been for a long time — the water gave them that early. But the lighting is a working lighting, not a display. Carbon arcs in the mill yards, tuned down to a smoulder. Filament strings along the stair-streets, one lamp per landing, each with a tin shade that concentrates the light on the treads and leaves the walls to the moss. Glasshouse lamps burning all night in the growing sheds so the tea flushes early. From a high terrace, looking down, you see the whole valley as a slow constellation in vapour — every light with a halo on it, nothing sharp, the far side of the valley present only as a paler patch of fog with orange in it.

Sunset is not an event. The lamps do not go on; they were already on. What happens instead is that the fog stops being luminous and starts being merely dark, and the difference takes about an hour, and there is no line in it.

---

### Sound

Here is the surprise. A valley of turbines, looms, kilns, cable-hoists and pumps, and it is quiet.

Partly this is the fog, which eats the top of every sound and leaves you only the body of it. Partly it is water power: the works are driven by falling water through pipe, and the noise stays in the penstocks and the wheelpits, underground, felt through the soles more than heard. And partly it is a matter of long habit. The valley is a place of belts and shafts and gearing, of things turning steadily rather than striking. There are no whistles. Shift change is signalled by lamps.

So what you hear, standing on a stair-street at midday, is: water. Water in a hundred registers — the gutter beside your foot, the roar under the grate at the corner, the aqueduct's flat hush overhead, the drip from the fern above the door. Then, under it, a low broad hum from everywhere at once, like a held note on a very large organ, which is the turbines. Then, occasionally, a specific human sound at a distance and utterly clear: a shovel on stone. A dog. Two people talking three terraces below, every word available to you, and neither of them raising their voice.

At night, when the loom sheds shut, the water gets louder without changing.

---

### Fauna

Almost none, and this is the valley's one real melancholy.

There is nothing wild left up here to speak of. No large animal has anywhere to be — every square metre is terrace, works, glasshouse, or wall. The forest that would have held them is nine hundred years of charcoal. What remains: crows, which do very well and know every kiln that gives off warmth; a small brown thrush that nests in the aqueduct arches and sings so audibly in that quiet that people identify individual birds by their errors; rats, kept nominally in check; cats, kept for the rats; the mules and the terrier-sized draft dogs of the cable stations; a few thousand cage-birds, which is a sentimental industry here.

And crucially: not enough insects. The glasshouses are too clean and the cold too general, and the flowering crops must therefore be pollinated by hand. There is a trade for it — women and children with rabbit-tail brushes working the lily sheds and the strawberry rows, twelve hundred plants a day, in warm damp glass-lit air, in silence. It is considered good work, indoors and dry.

The absence has a sound to it, or rather a lack of one. There is no insect noise. Summer dusk here has water in it and machinery in it and no cicadas, no crickets, no whine at the ear.

---

### The rule of the place

Everything is made; nothing stays made. That is the whole of it.

The valley's form is old and settled — the terrace lines have been in roughly these positions for centuries, the streets follow contours established before the turbines, the shape of it is finished, weathered, in balance. But the balance is not a rest. It is a rate. The slope wants to go downhill and the rain is very willing to help, and against this there is only labour, applied continuously, forever.

So the valley is calm in outline and frantic in detail. On any given day some section of it is failing. A wall bellies out and is buttressed. A terrace slumps and forty people re-lay it before the next rain. A drain silts, backs up, saturates the fill behind a wall, and the wall goes at three in the morning with a sound like a wardrobe falling over in another house — and by evening the debris is graded, the stone is stacked, and the reconstruction has a foreman and a number. Scaffolding is not temporary here; it is a category of architecture. There are streets that have been scaffolded for longer than anyone's memory, the poles replaced piecemeal, ferns growing on the lashings.

Nothing in the valley is more than thirty years from having been rebuilt, and nothing in it looks new. That is the trick of the place, and it is not a trick, it is only what happens when a made thing is loved and wet.

Stand still for an hour on a high terrace in the fog. You will hear water, and the hum, and — from four directions, faint, unhurried, absolutely constant — the sound of someone somewhere putting a stone back.
