## The Underplace, at the Growing Front

There is no water here and there never was. Understand that first, because everything else follows from it: the shape of the leaves, the way the dead are used, the reason the noise cannot stop.

You come into it from above — but not far above. Forty, fifty body-lengths of raw stone, and then the world opens. It is not a cave in the way that word usually means. Caves are carved by water, and water leaves signatures: smooth flutes, dripstone, the patient rounding of everything. Nothing here is rounded. Every rock face is a fresh fracture, glass-sharp, faceted like something broken this morning. The stone is warm to the touch and smells of struck flint and hot pepper. It is *new*. The whole region is new. It is being made, right now, by roots.

---

**The gas.** The atmosphere is thick, dry, and warm — around blood heat, hotter as you descend — and it is not air as you know it but something denser, faintly sweet, faintly resinous, a gas that carries dust the way water carries silt. It has weight. It pushes back when you walk. Sound moves through it slowly and enormously, so that a crack from far off arrives as a physical shove against the sternum. Nothing evaporates in this gas because there is nothing to evaporate. Nothing rots. Nothing rusts. A thing that dies here stays exactly as it died, forever, until something eats it or builds with it.

**The plants.** They are the landscape. There is no landscape apart from them.

Because there is no water, they cannot be soft. Turgor pressure — the trick of the green world elsewhere, cells swollen tight with fluid — is unavailable, so they have solved standing-up-ness by other means: silica, wax, spun fibre, and hollow architecture. The great trunks are tubes of laminated glass and horn, some of them thirty paces around, rising from the floor to lose themselves in the ceiling-canopy above, where they flatten and fuse and *become* the ceiling. There is no bedrock roof over most of the region. The roof is plant. Layer on layer of interlocked frond-plate, three hundred feet thick in places, holding up the stone above it like a bracing of ribs. Where the plate has failed you can see up into a raw dry chimney, and light comes down.

**The light.** This is the strangest mercy of the place. Certain trunks are grown as light-pipes: solid rods of transparent plant-glass, milk-clear, that run up through the ceiling and up through the bedrock to some surface nobody living has ever visited. They carry daylight down. It arrives cool and undiminished at the pipe's mouth and then splashes out into the gas, scattering off suspended dust into broad soft columns, so that the whole understory is lit at the level of a bright overcast afternoon. There are thousands of these pipes across the region. When surface-cloud passes over — up there, wherever *there* is — the light in the Underplace dims and brightens in slow wandering patches, and everything that lives here responds to that, and the response is deafening.

**Circulation without water.** A plant that cannot move sap must move powder. So they do. Inside every trunk are helical galleries in which the plant pushes granulated sugar and mineral flour upward by peristalsis, slow muscular ripples, a swallowing motion — an entire forest of throats, gulping. You can lay a palm on a trunk and feel it going down and up, down and up. Some species have given up on doing it themselves and have outsourced the work: their vessels are open at intervals to the outside, and crumb-mites — flat, pale, thumbnail-sized, uncountable — climb the interior stairwells carrying granules on their backs in an endless ascending file, and descend the outside carrying spores and waste, and are paid in wax. A single mature trunk may house nine million of them. You can hear the file. It sounds like sand poured slowly onto paper, and it never stops, and it is one of the quietest sounds in this world.

---

**The noise.** Here is the central fact.

The plants of the Underplace set their fibre by vibration. Their structural silk is extruded as a syrup of glassy sugar and only crystallizes correctly — only achieves alignment, and therefore strength — when it is shaken at the right frequencies while it hardens. In silence, new growth cures wrong: cloudy, brittle, powdery. It fails within days. So the ecology has arranged, over whatever unthinkable span, to be *loud*, and to be loud constantly, and to be loud in the specific bands the fibre needs.

The rasp-crickets do the low work. They are the size of forearms, armour-plated, and they strand themselves along the trunks in ranks of hundreds, drawing serrated femurs across ridged flanks, producing a sound like a saw going through slate. Bellows-moths handle the mid-range: they don't fly so much as pump, wings clapping in a cavity of their own thorax, a tearing whir. The canopy-plates themselves are tuned, and in the intermittent gas-currents they boom like enormous slack drums. There are trumpet-pods that mature and then detonate, hurling seed and a report that rolls for miles. There are stridulating vines that shiver as a whole hillside at once. And there are the flutes — hollow reed-stems the height of towers, holed at intervals, and the warm gas rising off the hot floor plays them, so that beneath everything else there is a continuous chord, immense, unresolving, changing key when the temperature changes.

The result is a sound pressure that a soft-bodied thing cannot survive unmodified. It is not a loud place; it is a place made of sound the way a reef is made of coral. Everything living here has been remade to accommodate it: the fauna are deaf in the ordinary sense and hear through their feet, through bone plates, through resonating gas-sacs. If the noise ever stopped, the forest would begin dying within a day. Nothing is more frightening in local reckoning than a lull.

---

**The fauna.** Overwhelming is the correct word. There is no volume of this world without animals in it. Where you would expect empty gas, there is a hanging haze of drift-mites, buoyed on the dense atmosphere, feeding on pollen; where you would expect bare rock, there are sheet-colonies of something like an armoured woodlouse grazing the mineral crust off the fresh stone and excreting it as usable nutrient. The trunks are furred with life. The canopy-plates have an entire second ecology on their upper surface, pressed against bedrock, which nobody has seen.

None of them drink. They get what they need from powdered wax and from the plants' fat-stores, and they conserve so ruthlessly that their waste comes out as dry pellets of near-pure salt, and there are animals that eat only that. There are pollen storms — real weather — when a district blooms at once and the gas goes opaque gold and the drift-feeders arrive in a wall you can hear coming for twenty minutes before it hits, and after it passes the floor is knee-deep in yellow flour, and the flour-eaters come, and then the things that eat the flour-eaters, and for a week that district is a riot and then it is quiet-ish again and cratered with feeding pits.

And there are the borers — tongue-worms, blind, the thickness of a man's leg, that chew the raw stone at the growing front and pass it as grit. They are the reason the world is expanding. They and the roots together.

---

**The front.** Downhill — down the heat gradient, since down here *warm* means *deeper* — the region ends in a wall. That wall is where the world is being made.

The roots reach it first: white, blunt, thick as barrels, and they don't grow through rock so much as split it, wedging into fractures and then swelling with crystallizing sugar until the stone bursts. The sound of the front is a slow irregular artillery, hours between shots sometimes, and sometimes fifty in a minute. Behind each burst, borers pour into the new fissure and widen it. Behind them, secondary roots. Behind those, light-pipe seedlings groping upward for a chimney. Within a season a new gallery has been opened, floored in fresh grit, roofed in green-glass plate, lit and inhabited and screaming.

The whole region — and it is a region, a valley's worth, a day's hard walk end to end, with named districts and distinct climates — is only a few hundred years old at its oldest end. It is embryonic. It is still deciding its own shape. And it is unstable: plates calve without warning and come down in acre sheets; a district can be buried in a night; a trunk that has stood a century can be undermined by its own hungry roots and fall, taking a hundred others in a chain, opening a raw new chamber and killing everything in it and making everything in it into building material. Turbulence is the normal condition. There is no old growth. There is no equilibrium. There is only the front, moving, and the wreckage behind it being reincorporated.

---

**The people.** There are people. Not many.

Call them Ringers, because that is what they do. They are lean, dark, waxy-skinned, with heavy bone plates over the ears and a resonating pouch at the throat, and they do not sweat and do not weep and would not know what to do with water if you offered it. They live in the middle canopy, in hollowed seed-shells hung on braided fibre — pods the size of small rooms, sealed with wax, packed with dry stores. A settlement is nine or ten pods and a workfloor lashed between two trunks. There are perhaps two hundred pods in the whole region, scattered, out of sight of one another.

Their technology is glass and cord and bone. They knap blades from the silica plates — the fracture is beautiful, and a fresh edge will take a limb off — and they have no metal except a few beads of native copper picked from the raw rock at the front, which they treat as a kind of currency for information. Everything else is plant. Rope, cloth, cups, ladders, splints, needles, hooks, sacks. Their diet is pith-flour and mite-wax and roasted grubs, and they take their liquid — such as it is — from the fat of the bellows-moths.

Their work is tending, and their tending is percussion. Every settlement keeps a drum-frame: a rack of tuned plates, struck in rotation, day and night, by whoever is not sleeping. This is not ritual, or not only. New growth near a settlement cures true because the Ringers keep it shaken. In return the growth is generous and predictable and doesn't fall on them. They know the frequencies the way other peoples know soil. A good Ringer can hear a district and say *that one is curing cloudy, there's a dead patch upwind, somebody's drum has stopped*. And then somebody walks four hours through the noise to find out why, and usually finds a pod hanging empty and a rack of plates split, and takes down the copper beads, and begins drumming.

They bury nothing, because nothing rots. The dead are lacquered and set into the structure of the settlement — ribs into ladder-rungs, long bones into frame-struts, skulls faced outward along the doorway of the pod in which they lived. A very old pod is more ancestor than pod. This is not sentiment either; bone is good material and there is no other way to keep a thing. They will tell you, if you can make yourself heard, that everything here becomes furniture eventually, including the world, including the noise, including you.

---

Three rules, then, and they are the whole grammar of the place:

**Nothing dissolves.** Whatever is made, stays made. There is no rain to soften it, no rot to reclaim it. So the Underplace is not shaped by removal but only by addition, breakage, and reuse. It accumulates. It never smooths.

**Silence kills.** Sound is not something that happens in this landscape; it is a nutrient and a load-bearing element. To be quiet is to weaken the roof over your head.

**Down is forward.** The heat comes from below and the world grows toward it, splitting rock as it goes, and every generation lives a little deeper than the last in a valley that did not exist when their grandparents were born, under a ceiling that is still curing, listening to a chord that has never once resolved.
