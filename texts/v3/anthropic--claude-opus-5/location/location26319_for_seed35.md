## The Bell

**Getting in.** Twelve meters down, under a dry lake of magnesium salts that crunches like sugar-crust underfoot, there is a crack in the anhydrite about as wide as a person turned sideways. Above it the surface is flat to the horizon in every direction, white, blinding, and utterly silent. You put your ear to the crack and the silence stops being true. Something down there is running.

The descent is a slot, then a chimney, then the last four meters are open air because the ladder — a steel one, bolted, obviously human, obviously old — ends short. You hang and drop. The floor takes you with a slap of dry heat, about forty degrees, the heat of an attic in August, and the noise closes over your head like water closing.

**The shape of it.** The chamber is a lens perhaps thirty meters on its long axis and eight at its tallest, floor slightly dished, ceiling a smooth compound curve as if a bubble had been blown into the rock and had then hardened while still perfectly a bubble. Which is roughly what happened. This is a young cavity — a gas intrusion punched up through an evaporite bed sometime in the last century or two, geologically an infant, and it shows. Every crystal face on the wall still carries its growth terminations, sharp enough to open a knuckle. There are no rounded edges anywhere. No talus cones, no fillet of dust where wall meets floor. The floor is swept. Nobody sweeps it.

The rock is banded: bone-white anhydrite, lenses of pale-green polyhalite, and threading through everything the native sulfur, which is not yellow here but amber, and translucent, so that where the wick-plants glow behind a curtain of it the whole wall lights up the color of weak tea.

**No water, none, not a trace.** The chemistry runs on something else. The walls sweat *tallow* — a heavy, waxy, long-chain sulfur-selenium oil that seeps from the joint planes in slow beads and never drips, because it is too viscous, and never accumulates, because it is eaten. It smells like hot electrical insulation and faintly of almond. It is the water-substitute and the food-substitute and the lubricant of the entire place. Your own body is the wettest object for kilometers, and the Bell notices. Sweat leaves the skin the instant it arrives; by the second hour your eyelids are gritty with your own salt and your lips split in fine parallel cracks. Bring in a flask and unstopper it: the water goes off it like smoke, faster than it has any right to, and the air remains absolutely, arithmetically dry.

**The Works.** In the center-left of the floor, on a low plinth of its own crystal, stands the machine.

Nobody built it. It grew, the way a geode grows, except that what crystallized was a mechanism: a copper shaft, native and unalloyed, running in two bearings of clear sulfur; four blades of anhydrite mounted at a pitch; a wound band of fibrous selenium doing the work of a belt; an eccentric arm terminating in a shallow scoop. It stands about chest high. It turns at sixty-three revolutions per minute and it has, as far as anyone can establish, always turned at sixty-three revolutions per minute.

The blades sit in the throat of a vent, and there is a pressure differential across that vent that drives them, and the exasperating thing — the thing that has ended careers — is that both sides of the vent are at equilibrium. There is no gradient. There is no reservoir being drained. The differential exists as a stable property of the chamber, like its temperature, and the machine harvests it, and the harvest does not diminish it.

And the Works does nothing. Or rather: once per revolution the eccentric arm dips its scoop into a trough of tallow at the plinth's edge, lifts a measure of the stuff, holds it at the top of the arc, and pours it back into the trough from a height of perhaps thirty centimeters. That is the machine's entire output. A splash, sixty-three times a minute, forever.

That splash is the basis of the local economy.

**Sound.** Above the Works the vent throat is fluted — a dozen ribs cut into the rock, sharp-edged like everything else here, cut at formation and never deepened since. Gas moving through them produces three notes, low, in a fixed chord, at a volume that would be measured in the neighborhood of 105 decibels and never varies by so much as a hair. Under that sits the machine's one-per-second thud and the papery whir of the blades. You cannot speak. You cannot hear yourself think in the ordinary way; thought becomes something you do at the pace of the thud, slower and squarer than usual. The old survey teams used a hand cant, and there is a partial vocabulary of it chalked on a wall panel, mostly faded, mostly obscene.

The chord does not echo. Or: the echo has been folded into the chord so completely that there is no separating them. It is a standing wave and the chamber is its instrument, and if you stuff a coat into one of the flutes the volume does not drop — the loudness simply reappears elsewhere, redistributed, the total conserved to the limits of measurement. Take the coat out and it goes back.

**What lives here, which is a great deal.** The air is not empty anywhere. Density is the first thing the body registers after the heat and the noise: things are near-touching, layered, occupying every cubic meter.

*Wickwood* grows from the tallow seeps in cream-colored waxy stalks, knee-high, branching in threes, no leaves. Chemosynthetic, coated in a luminous bacterial film that puts out a dim greenish light — the chamber's only illumination apart from one artifact. Density of stalks: sparse, actually, maybe one clump per two meters. They never get taller. Grazing exactly matches growth.

*Scale-mites*, silver, pinhead-sized, in sheets. They graze the tallow film off the walls and off the wickwood and off you, if you sit still, which is not painful but is deeply unpleasant.

*Clatterers*, palm-sized, many-legged, carapaced in something micaceous that has grown to them rather than been secreted. They cling to the Works and to the flute ribs and vibrate in sympathy with the chord. They are the reason the chord has a shimmer in it. They eat mites and each other.

*The haze* — a swarm of gnat-scale fliers riding the warm updraft over the Works, thick enough to smudge the far wall. The swarm holds a shape: a slightly tilted torus about two meters across. Not a shape it returns to. A shape it holds. Photograph it in 1961 and photograph it today and lay the images together.

*The Long One.* One animal, a meter of blind jointed chitin, circling the chamber at the wall-floor junction on a fixed circuit. Eleven minutes and some seconds per lap. It does not accelerate, does not stop, does not deviate for obstacles but goes over them. It is the only one of its kind here and there has only ever been one.

**Rules.**

Nothing accumulates. No dust, no droppings, no wear on the sulfur bearings, no rounding of crystal edges. The Bell is young and stays young.

The census is fixed. Not approximately — exactly, at every taxon. Kill a clatterer and take the corpse up the ladder with you; before you reach the surface there is another clatterer, indistinguishable, in a plausible place, of plausible age. Nobody has caught the substitution happening. Everyone has tried.

Things brought in and left stay, and stop. There is a leather glove on the floor near the plinth, palm-down, still holding the curve of a hand. It is not dusty. It is not stiff. It has been there since the second survey. There is a mine-lamp on a bracket with a filament still lit, dim orange, whose cable runs six meters and ends in cut copper. There is a bolted plate stamped **BELL 4 — DO NOT VENT**, a coil of cable, a doorframe with no door and no wall around it, and a chalk tally of eleven marks in a hand that stopped mid-twelfth.

And nothing here needs time. That is the thing you take back up the ladder with you, more than the heat or the noise. Every other landscape is a sentence being spoken. This one is a chord being held. The machine lifts its measure of tallow and pours it back; the plants grow exactly as much as the mites can eat; the swarm holds its tilted ring; the Long One comes around the wall again at eleven minutes; the three notes go on and on with the clatterers shimmering in them — and the whole arrangement has evidently arrived, at some point in its short brutal youth, at an answer it found sufficient, and has not since seen any reason to go on being asked.

You climb out and the white plain is silent and blinding and the crack at your feet is humming, and it will still be humming when everything you can see from here is gone.
