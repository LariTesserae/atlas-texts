## The Nap

The ridge came up recently — recently enough that its edges are still edges. It runs maybe nine hundred paces from shoulder to shoulder, a saddle of raw basalt hung between two cloud-swallowed masses that no one has ever walked to. The rock on the arête is glassy and unrounded; step wrong and it will take a shoe apart, then a foot. There is no talus fan yet, only the beginning of one, a spill of fresh black chips at the north drop that clinks faintly when the fissures breathe.

Nothing here is old except the fog.

At this height the air is thin, cool, and permanently on the verge of becoming water. It does not often rain. Instead, the mountain exhales: warm damp comes up out of the seams in the new rock all night, meets the cold, and by first grey the whole ridge is inside a cloud that sits on it like a hand on a mouth. The fog gives all the water there is. Everything living here is a device for combing water out of air.

---

## The felt

Cover the ridge, over rock and root and roof, is a moss the people call *the listening*, or just the felt. It is grey-green, layered, faintly warm to the palm, with the loose fibrous nap of an animal's underfur. Pull a sheet of it back and the underside is pale, veined, slightly moist, and busy — it comes away with a soft tearing that is the loudest ordinary sound on the mountain.

The felt eats light, a little; the light here is dim, a green underwater dimness for most of the day, and no plant could be this lush on it. What the felt mostly eats is vibration. Sound falls into that nap and does not come back out. It is converted — nobody here has a word for the mechanism, only for the result — into pale swollen growth. The felt grows *toward* noise. A dropped basket makes a hand-sized bloom by morning. A quarrel makes a mattress.

So the ridge is quiet, and the quiet is not peace. It is thrift, and it is fear, and it is manners, all grown together into one habit.

Everything that lives on the Nap has been shaped by this. The trees — hollow canes, finger-thick to thigh-thick, jointed, rising in sparse stands with wide bright gaps of nothing between them — would whistle in the updraught, and so the felt plugs them: every hollow stem is stuffed with a soft grey column of itself, a throat packed with wool. The canes lean, dripping, combing fog into their joints, and their leaves are fringed at every margin so that wind passes through them without a sound. The whole forest moves in a strong gust like a crowd of people shrugging, and makes nothing.

## The fauna

There are more animals here than there is room for them, and you will hear none.

Lift any handful of felt and it comes alive in your palm: mites, blind pale shrimp-things that live in the moss's water film, jointed hunters no bigger than an eyelash, worms of a translucent lilac that pack the fibre so thickly that a fistful weighs like meat. That is the floor of it. Above that, everywhere, spaced out and unhurried, the larger silent things.

Pad-toed climbers with fingers like wet gloves go up the canes in a rippling motion, never dislodging a chip. Slugs the size of cats, mustard-coloured, graze the felt in wide spirals and leave shaved paths that are legible for days. There are birds — many, and of many kinds — whose feathers are fringed like the leaves; they fly with an absolute noiselessness that is somehow more disturbing than any cry, and they do not sing. They signal instead: a bird lands on a moss face and drums with its feet, and the drumming makes no sound but leaves a pattern of raised bumps that other birds come and read with their beaks, and then rub out. The felt is the language. Everything here writes on the walls.

The predator is a long low thing the people call the shrug. It hunts by pressing its whole flank to the ground and feeling where the felt is growing fastest, because the fastest growth is where something has just made a mistake.

Only one order of creature makes noise: certain insects, thumb-sized, that stridulate at a pitch so high it seems to come from inside the ear. The felt loves them. A single one of them, unchecked in a doorway, will grow a wall of pale swollen moss across it overnight — a soft blindness, a room's mouth filled in. The town's whole animal husbandry consists of small mute birds kept for eating them.

## The town

Forty-one houses, and you can stand in the middle of it and not be sure you are in a town at all, because they are far apart. Density is a danger here. People build with long gaps between them, one dwelling per shelf of rock, because concentrated life means concentrated sound and concentrated sound means the felt closes over you.

The houses are split cane, lashed with hair-cord, roofed in beaten sheets of the felt's tough dark light-fed growth, which cures into something like leather and sheds fogwater beautifully. No metal. There is no ore in rock this young. What the mountain gives instead is glass — black, fresh, wickedly sharp — and every blade, scraper, drill and needle on the ridge is knapped from it, and every household has a scar or two to show what that costs. Tools are wrapped in moss when carried, to keep them from clicking.

The infrastructure is water and hush. Dew-looms: frames of strung hair and glass filament, twice a person's height, set out on the windward lip, dripping all night into cisterns cut in the basalt. Aqueducts of halved cane on forked sticks, each joint packed to keep it from gurgling. Ladders over fissures, wrapped. Bridges of laid cane, wrapped. A single stair cut in the arête going down into whiteness, unwrapped, unused; nobody living has been down it, and the children are told it goes to where the sound is kept.

Underfoot, everywhere, the padded paths — beaten strips where the felt is kept shaved down by daily traffic and by the constant careful work of the path-scrapers. This is a third of all labour. The paths want to close. Left three weeks, the whole town is a green solid.

People speak with their hands, mostly, and with their faces, and with a touch on the forearm that means *yes, I heard you*, which is a joke that stopped being a joke generations ago.

## The hum-pits

South of the houses the ridge widens into a shelf, and the shelf is terraced with pits: round, waist-deep, lined with hard dark cured moss, each with a stone seat at the centre. This is the farm.

A person climbs down into a pit at first grey and sits and talks. Any talking. Genealogies mostly, because they are long and no one has to invent them — the names of the dead going back to the first who climbed up — but also lists, recipes, insults about people in the next pit over, weather, the same seventeen songs. Hours of it. The voice goes into the pit's walls and the walls swell. By evening the rim carries a fat pale crest of sound-fed growth, sweet, faintly warm, with the texture of a lung, and this is the food of the town: cut in slabs, pressed, dried, eaten. It tastes different from every pit. Some say it tastes of who was sitting.

That is not a metaphor here. Sound-fed felt takes the grain of the voice that fed it. Whorled for one man, close and combed for another. In the old houses the interior walls are read by touch in the dark by widows and children, and what they are reading is a person: this ridge of nap is grandmother arguing, this smooth patch is the year everyone was too tired to speak. The dead do not leave. They are upholstery.

Which is why the only loud thing that happens on the Nap is a funeral. They lay the body on bare rock and then everyone — everyone, all forty-one houses — stands around it and shouts. Sings, keens, hammers cane on cane, screams, the one night of noise, and the felt comes for it. It comes visibly. It comes like dough rising, like a tide coming up a beach, pale and fast and swelling from every direction, and it takes the body and the noise together, and in the morning there is a green mound and the mound has a texture, and the texture is everybody who loved him, and it is quiet again.

## The day

Before light: the fissures exhale, the cloud settles, water begins to run in every cane joint and rope and loom. The town is invisible from twenty paces.

Grey: path-scrapers out, bent, working. Dew-looms drained. The birds begin their soundless drumming and the walls of the world fill with writing.

The thin gold hour, once, near midday, when the cloud lifts off the saddle for as long as it takes to eat: and then you see it, the whole small sharp new ridge, black glass and grey fleece and the wide bright spaces between the leaning canes, and the two unreachable shoulders, and below and all around, white, going down forever. Everything steams. The animals go still and hold their faces to the light. Nobody says anything, obviously.

Afternoon: the cloud collapses back. Voices in the pits, muffled, low, patient, like a hive under a blanket.

Night: the shrugs come up out of the fissures and lay their flanks against the world and feel for whatever was careless. The felt grows a hand's width in the dark. Water drips into stone. A chip of new rock lets go somewhere on the north face and goes down clinking, and everyone who hears it has the same thought, which is that the mountain is still coming up, is not finished, and one day will not be a ridge but a peak, and no one knows whether the felt will follow it that high.

They sleep in wool that remembers them.
