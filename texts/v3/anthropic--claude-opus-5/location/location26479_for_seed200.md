## The Basin of First Rock

### 1. The Fact You Notice First

There is no dust.

You understand this only after some hours, when you have been rubbing your fingers together for a while without knowing why. The air is thick enough to drink — ninety-some percent humidity, thirty-eight degrees, air that arrives in the lungs already the temperature of blood — and it carries pollen, spores, insect scale, the smell of crushed leaf and warm frog. But nothing mineral. No grit in the eye. No film on the leaf. No silt at the bottom of any pool.

The water proves it. Every pool in the basin, from the palm-sized cups in the rock to the wide shallow lawns of standing water out toward the middle, is optically clear. You look down through half a metre of water and see the stone beneath as if through nothing at all, every crystal face on it distinct, and the fish suspended over it appear to be lying on air. There is no sediment because nothing here has ever been ground down. The rock is new and the rock stays new.

### 2. The Rock

It is basalt, or something so near basalt as makes no difference: dark, dense, glass-rinded, still holding the shapes it took while cooling. Pillow-lobes the size of oxen, piled and welded, with the fine radial cracks of quenching still crisp on their skins. Ropy sheets frozen mid-fold. Fissure-fields where the crust split into polygonal columns, and the columns stand with their tops as flat and sharp-edged as the day they parted.

Nothing has softened them. Run your hand along a column edge and it will open your palm. Rain has been falling on this stone for however long the basin has existed, and the rain has not rounded a single corner; streams slide over it and the stone beneath the streams is as keen as the stone beside them. The water does not seem to be *working*. It goes where it goes, and lets the rock alone.

Consequently there are no valleys. There is no drainage pattern in the sense you might expect — no dendritic tree of gullies, no floodplain, no delta, no beach. The basin's shape is the shape it was poured into. Water collects in the original hollows of the cooling: in the troughs between pillow-lobes, in the shrinkage polygons, in the long axial groove where two flows met and failed to fuse. Where it moves, it moves by spilling from cell to cell, sheet to sheet, a slow lateral seepage rather than a river. In the flat middle of the basin the water table stands a finger's width below the surface everywhere, and in ten thousand small places a finger's width above it.

### 3. The Skin

Since there is no soil — soil being rock made small, and no rock here has been made small — the living world sits directly on the stone, and has made its own ground to sit on.

It is a mat. A felt. Two hands' depth of it in the shaded places: dead leaf, root-hair, moss rhizoid, fungal cord, beetle husk, frog bone, all matted into a springy brown-black quilt that is entirely organic and holds water like a sponge. It has no mineral fraction at all. Where it has been torn open — and it does get torn open, constantly, by digging animals — you see the clean black glassy rock immediately beneath, wet, gleaming, unweathered, a floor rather than a subsoil.

Walking on the mat is like walking on a mattress laid over knives. It gives, then it stops giving, and the stopping has an edge in it.

Nothing here roots downward. Roots run *sideways*, in the mat, in dense braided sheets, and where they need anchorage they send out flat grey holdfasts that clamp the rock like limpets. Big trees stand on a cage of their own buttresses, splay-footed, gripping. In the fissure zones, whole groves have poured their roots down into the cracks like candle wax, and where a fissure is wide the roots hang free in it for two or three metres, dripping, hung with their own gardens of moss and small ferns and small sleeping animals, before finding the wet floor below.

### 4. The Light

Grey-green and even. There is a cloud ceiling over the basin, low, no more than a few hundred metres up, formed by the basin's own exhalation and never entirely absent, and beneath it a canopy in three or four layers of leaf. What arrives at the mat is perhaps one part in fifty of what falls on the cloud-top: enough to read by, not enough to cast a shadow.

The absence of shadow is a bigger absence than you would think. Without shadow there is no direction to the light, and without direction there is no time of day. Morning and evening are shifts in temperature of a degree or two, and a change in the register of the chorus. There is a night — the grey goes to a deep charcoal, and the water surfaces become the brightest things in the world — but it is a shallow night, and the heat does not go out of it.

Because so little light reaches the floor, everything green climbs. The vertical is dense to the point of absurdity: lianas, root-ladders, tank-bromeliad analogues stacked up trunks like barnacles, epiphytic ferns whose leaf-bases form cups, moss cushions on the moss cushions. A single trunk of the big grey-barked tree may carry two hundred distinct species, and in each of the leaf-cups is a private pool of water, and in each pool is a life.

### 5. Fauna

Here is the honest difficulty of describing this place: there is no volume of it that is not occupied.

Put your open hand anywhere and it touches something living. The undersides of large leaves are shingled edge-to-edge with moths at rest, overlapping like roof tiles, so that the leaf appears to be made of pale grey scale. Every fissure is packed with land-crabs and isopods, stacked in the dark, clicking. The mat crawls. The air has a standing population of small flying things at what one can only call a *concentration* — you breathe through your teeth, you learn to blink often, you learn that a swallowed midge is not a disaster.

The dominant sound is amphibian. Frogs here have taken every niche that light and water allow: pool frogs, cup frogs the size of a thumbnail that live their whole lives in a single bromeliad, sheet frogs that ride the flowing water films on rock faces, and the big-throated barrel frogs down in the fissures whose call is a physical pressure on the sternum. Above them, insects. Above the insects, the singers of the canopy: small, feathered, hot-blooded, invisible.

The chorus is loud — loud enough that two people would have to raise their voices — and it does not stop and does not vary in volume. Ever. Individual voices come and go. The *level* does not move. In the dead of night the composition has changed completely, and the meter on the sound (if you had a meter, which you do not, there being no devices here) would not have twitched.

There are two behaviours in the basin that come close to making things, and neither quite crosses the line. The sheath-crabs cut discs from the broad low ground-fern and carry them over their backs, holding them by the stem in a specialized claw; the disc keeps the crab's gill-chamber wet while it crosses open rock, and is discarded and re-cut at the far side. And the gallery weavers — pale, near-blind, communal — spin covered roadways of silk along the buttresses and up into the branches, roofed tunnels tens of metres long with side-chambers and refuse pits, so that a colony's territory is a visible white net over the trunk. Both are astonishing. Neither is architecture. There is nothing in the basin that was made by a mind that intended a thing to last.

### 6. The Rule

The reason there is no dust is the same reason there are no seasons, and the same reason nothing is ever new here except everything.

The basin does not change.

Not slowly — *not at all*, past a certain grain. The rock does not wear. The water does not cut. The cloud ceiling does not lift or lower more than a few metres. The mean temperature holds. The chorus holds its level. Count the frogs in a pool this year and count them in a hundred years and you will get the same number, and not one of the frogs will be the same frog.

You see the mechanism if you watch a big tree. Beside each mature trunk stands a second stem, half the height, unbranched, waiting — an heir, held in suspension by the parent's shade, growing perhaps a centimetre a decade. When the parent goes, the heir has the gap filled within a season, and its own heir starts at its foot. There are no clearings in this forest, because there is no interval between falling and filling. There is no succession. There is only the one climax state, running forever, with the individuals swapped out beneath it like water through a standing wave.

And so the geology stays embryonic. The basin is a landscape at the instant of its formation, a raw poured thing, every edge original — and it has been at that instant for an unguessable time, and will be at that instant tomorrow. It is not young. It is *unaged*, which is a different condition entirely, and the difference is what makes the place feel wrong in the back of the skull even while every individual thing in it — the frog, the leaf, the crab under its parasol — is behaving perfectly reasonably.

### 7. Standing in It

You are in the middle flats, ankle-deep in warm clear water over black glass rock, and the water is exactly blood temperature so that below the ankle you cannot feel where you end. The mat-islands rise around you, each a low dome of brown felt bristling with fern and stemmed with grey trunks that go up out of sight into the leaf. Steam does not rise, because the air is already saturated; instead the moisture simply stands, and the far side of the flats, four hundred metres away, is a soft grey suggestion.

The sound is a wall. Under the wall, the tick and slop of ten thousand small water-cells overflowing into one another. A land-crab crosses your foot without concern, holding its green disc up like a small formal umbrella.

There is no horizon; there is no shadow; there is nothing that will be different when you come back.

And under all of it, one finger's depth down, the floor of the world: black, wet, glassy, sharp as the hour it cooled.
