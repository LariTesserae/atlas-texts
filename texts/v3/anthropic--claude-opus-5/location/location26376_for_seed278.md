## The Plateau Under the Lid

Begin with the ground, because the ground here has already finished arguing.

It is welded ash — a grey-fawn tuff laid down so long ago that the volcanoes responsible are not even hills anymore, only broad soft swellings you cross without noticing you have climbed. Everything is rounded. There are no cliffs to speak of, no fresh scars, no gorges with white water at the bottom. Instead there is *grain*: the whole plateau, for hundreds of kilometres, is combed into long low whalebacks running roughly northeast, the fossil signature of a wind that stopped blowing in some other era. Walk across the grain and you rise and fall every forty paces, gently, as though crossing the wake of a ship that passed a million years ago. Walk along it and you can go for a week without turning.

The surface is pavement: gravel sorted by frost into a tight mosaic, each stone seated, each stone varnished on top with a thin dark manganese sheen and pale on its buried underside. Kick one over and you have made the largest change this square metre has seen in a century. It will take about that long for the varnish to grow back.

Height: high enough that the air is thin and takes the sound out of things, high enough that the cold is not seasonal but structural. Not lethal cold. The cold of a stone cellar, all day, all year, with a bite at night that stiffens the lichen and grows crystal whiskers on every north-facing edge.

## The light

There is a lid on the sky. A high overcast, unbroken most days, thin enough to be luminous and thick enough that the sun is only a slight brightening on one side of it, moving. The light is the light of the half-hour after sunset, held indefinitely. Shadows exist but they have no edges. Distance goes silver, then pale, then simply stops — the horizon does not so much end as run out of contrast.

At night, if the lid thins, there is a strange second illumination from below: the drifts.

## Water

No rivers, most of the time. The channels are all there — broad, shallow, floored with sorted gravel and utterly dry, an entire drainage network in a state of retirement. Water arrives in three ways.

First, the fog tide. Twice in the plateau's long year — and its year is long, measured against the slow wobble of the lowland monsoon far below and to the east — a mass of cloud climbs the escarpment and moves across the plateau at walking pace, taking eight or nine days to pass. It does not rain. It *deposits*. Every stone, every lichen, every hair and plate and mineral scale of every animal beads and runs. Water gathers in the pavement joints, sinks a hand's depth, and travels there, in films, in the dark, for years.

Second, rime. Clear cold nights pull moisture out of even the dry air and grow it into feathers on the windward faces. By mid-morning it is gone, and what it wet has already drunk.

Third, and rarest, the playas fill. There are perhaps a dozen closed basins on the plateau, floored with pale clay, and once in a human lifetime one of them holds water for a decade — a shallow grey mirror twenty kilometres across and never deeper than your knee, with nothing in it but brine shrimp and their patient uncountable eggs, which have waited in the clay since the last time and will wait again.

## The vegetation, briefly, because it is brief

Crustose lichens, mostly, in maps and continents on the varnished stones: black, mustard, a chalk green. They grow at a rate best expressed in millimetres per century. Cushion plants in the pavement joints, tight grey hemispheres the size of a fist, hollow inside with their own dead, each one a hoarded microclimate. In the lee of the whalebacks, where the drifts collect, there is something more like a moss — a low bronze felt that is not entirely a plant, since it feeds on metal dust and hosts about eleven kinds of animal in every thumbprint.

That is all. Between these there is bare ground, and there is a great deal of between.

## The industry

Here is the thing about this plateau: it is an industrial landscape with nothing built on it.

Come over a whaleback on a still afternoon and you will see, three kilometres off, a shape you take at first for an outcrop. It is the length of a barge and the height of two rooms, backed like an overturned hull, standing on six columnar legs whose feet are broad as millstones. Its hide is not hide but plate — overlapping mineral scales, dull, faintly iridescent at the edges where they are newest. Along its spine runs a double row of thin vertical vanes, arranged like the pages of a book left open in the rain. These are fog-combs. During the tide they run with condensate, channelling it down grooves in the plates and inward.

It is grazing. Not on plants — on gravel, on the pavement itself, taking in a mouthful of sorted stone and fog-water every few minutes, moving forward the length of its own foot in an hour, a hundred and some metres in a day.

Inside, in a series of fermentation chambers and something that is, functionally, a very large and very slow electrochemical cell, bacterial cultures strip trace metals out of the rock and the water and lay them down on a membrane. The animal maintains a standing voltage across its own body to do this. It is a walking electroplating bath. Its waste product — its exhalation, its shed antler, its fingernail clipping — is **foil**.

Sheets of it, thin as onion skin, the size of a hand or of a door, in copper-rose, in iron-grey, in a nickel white that stays bright for decades. They peel loose along the flanks and the trailing edge of the vanes and go where the wind takes them, which is: along the grain, into the lee hollows, into the dry channels, into the joints of the pavement.

There are perhaps four hundred of these animals on the whole plateau. Perhaps six hundred. Nobody could count them; they do not gather. Each one walks a circuit measured in decades, and their circuits interlock like the gears of something too large to see the whole of, and the product of the entire mechanism, the sole output of this continental factory, is soil.

Because the foil oxidises. Slowly — this is a cold, dry, ungenerous chemistry — but over fifty or eighty years a drift of bright metal goes dull, goes green, goes rust-red, goes to a fine mineral flour rich in exactly the trace elements the lichen and the bronze felt cannot otherwise obtain. The plateau's stone is nearly barren. Everything that lives on it lives, at one remove or nine, on metal that was mined, refined, rolled, and abandoned by animals.

## The drifts

They are the second landscape, laid over the first.

In every lee hollow, banked against every whaleback, filling the old dry channels: metal leaf-litter, ankle deep, sometimes waist deep, layered by age — bright on top, then dull, then the green-red crumble, then a compacted floor of it, then true dark earth, the only earth here, a metre down.

Wind stacks the sheets on edge in places, in long rippled combs and curved shells and, occasionally, in something that looks so much like a wall or a doorway that you will stop walking. A shed spinal vane, standing upright where its owner left it, three metres of fluted grey plate, catches the eye like a monument. There is nothing built on this plateau. There are only things that fell in suggestive arrangements and were not disturbed, because nothing here has hands and nothing here is in a hurry.

The drifts are also where the fauna is.

## The overwhelming

Density and abundance are different quantities, and this place holds both extremes at once.

Stand on open pavement and you are alone. A kilometre of stone in every direction, one lichen map, one cushion plant, nothing moving. This is most of the plateau. Emptiness is the default condition and it is enormous.

Now take a double handful of foil from a drift and hold it up in the dim light. It weighs more with animals than with metal. Foil-lice, flat as the sheets they live between, in nine species graded by depth. Mites in the millions per square metre, orange, translucent, some of them mining pits in the oxide layer, some of them eating the miners. A blind pale springtail-thing that lives in the film of condensate on the underside of sheets and travels the whole drift without ever seeing the sky. Below them, in the compacted floor, colonies of burrowers — soft, segmented, the length of your forearm — that follow the water-films through the pavement joints for kilometres and constitute, by mass, the largest animal population on the plateau, entirely invisible.

And in the air, on still evenings, the plankton comes up. Wingless invertebrates the size of a grain of rice, each carrying a filament of charged silk, riding the potential gradient between the foil-covered ground and the underside of the lid. They rise in the millions. The air acquires a texture, a faint moving grain, like looking through slowly falling dust. Feeders come after them — kites, membranes a metre across with a body no bigger than a bird's, which do not flap, ever, but tilt and hold and slide along the gradient with their mouths open. They can stay up for eleven days.

And where a smelter dies — every three centuries or so, one of them stops, and settles, and does not rise — the whole distribution of life on the plateau reorganises around the corpse. It is a mine. It is fifty tonnes of concentrated metal and organic matter, the single richest object in a hundred thousand square kilometres, and it will take two hundred years to be dismantled. Things come from very far away. A dead smelter is the closest this world gets to a city, and it is silent, and it is temporary, and afterwards the outline of it stays legible in the lichen for ten thousand years.

## The silence

Nothing here has a voice.

The air is too thin to carry sound well. The ground is porous tuff and the drifts are metal leaf, which is one of the finest sound-absorbers in nature. Between the two, an acoustic dead room the size of a continent. Evolution did not bother with calling. It went instead to charge — the plankton's silk, the smelters' standing voltage, faint fields read by faint organs — and to scent, and to tremor conducted through stone.

So the sounds available are these: your own breathing, loud and embarrassing. Your heartbeat, if you stop moving. The crunch of pavement gravel under your foot, which sounds enormous and dies within four metres. And in the drifts, on nights when the temperature drops fast, a small dry *tick*, then another, then a scatter of them — foil sheets contracting, shifting, settling one leaf against the next. That is the loudest thing the plateau produces on its own. It sounds like a fire that gives no heat and no light, and it goes on all night, and it is thousands of years old.

During the fog tide there is not even that. Fog kills the tick. The drifts go quiet and beaded, the smelters stand with their combs streaming, the kites come down, and for nine days the largest factory in the world runs at full capacity in total silence, in a light like the inside of a pearl, with nobody watching.

## Rules

Metal is food, not treasure. There is no scarcity of it and no meaning in it.

Nothing is constructed. Things are secreted, shed, sorted, occupied. A shape that looks intentional is a coincidence that has been left alone long enough to look like a decision.

Everything moves at the speed the water allows, and the water arrives twice a long year, so the tempo of the world is set by a cloud climbing an escarpment. Fast things exist only at the millimetre scale.

The plateau is not becoming anything. Erosion here has reached its settlement — the grain is cut, the channels are graded, the varnish is grown, the balance between the smelters' output and the oxide's decay is old enough to be called a climate. It will look like this for a very long time and it has looked like this for longer.

You will feel, crossing it, that you have arrived after the interesting part. This is a misreading. There was no interesting part. There is only the long middle, and it is working, quietly, at the speed of metal turning green.
