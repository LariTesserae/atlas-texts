## The Standing

Come at it from the plain first, in the hour that passes for morning here — a grey seep of light along the southern rim, enough to see silhouettes by and nothing else.

What you see is a range. That is the first mistake everyone makes and it is a reasonable one. There is a wall across the world, black, running east and west past the limits of sight, notched and pinnacled, ten and twelve thousand feet where it is lowest. It has the profile of young mountains: no rounded shoulders, no aprons of scree, no valleys cut by water. Sharp as a break in glass. The plain runs flat right up to its foot and stops.

Then the wind changes and the whole range moves — not much, a few degrees of lean, a slow shrug propagating from west to east over the course of a minute — and you understand that you are looking at stalks.

---

### The stalks

They grow from the plain in a scatter, not a thicket. Between one stalk and the next is anywhere from four to nine miles of empty air. From the ground the range appears solid only because of perspective, the way a sparse orchard closes into a wall when you stand at the end of a row. Walk in among them and the world opens into something almost unbearable: a floor of dark plain, a ceiling of low cloud, and columns a half-mile thick holding the two apart, so far apart from each other that you can spend a day walking from the base of one to the base of the next and see nothing in between but ground-frost and your own breath.

This is the arithmetic of the place. It is nearly all plant and nearly all void. The living mass is total — there is no rock here, no mineral soil, nothing that is not either the growth or something that lives on the growth — and yet the density of the world is close to nothing. A thousand cubic miles of air with a few million tons of black stem standing in it. Everything is either substrate or emptiness. There is no middle condition.

The stem material is not wood. It is closer to a very dense reed: fibrous in long parallel bundles, faintly warm to the palm, and so dark that it eats the little light there is. Rub the surface with your thumb and it takes an oily sheen and smells of iron and crushed stems. Cut it and it weeps a clear sap that stiffens into a gum in about an hour. Nothing here has been cut with metal — there is no metal, no ore, no stone even to knap. Tools are made of stalk-fibre bundled and gum-set, of the shed rib-plates of climbers, of bone.

Each stalk is hollow. That fact is the entire physics of the world.

---

### Inside

Find a wound — a seam split by wind-torsion, or a shed leaf-scar the size of a barn door — and go in, and you are inside a chimney four hundred yards across and two miles tall.

It is dark. Not cave-dark: there is a dull, sourceless red-green where the wall thins, the colour of a hand held over a candle, coming from the light-tissue that the plant keeps in its inner rind, and it is just enough to see the wall by and never enough to see the far side. The floor of the chamber is warm and soft, a matting of shed fibre and fallen bodies, and it steams.

And the air is going up. Always, everywhere, up. The plant burns something down in its root mass, slowly and hugely, and the heat sets the whole column drawing like a flue. Stand in the base of a stalk and there is a steady twenty-knot updraught that will take your hat and your hair and never give them back. The pull is not gusty. It is a fact, like gravity, at right angles to it.

The upper column is cold. Somewhere around eight thousand feet the rising air gives up its water on the inner wall, and it rains inside the stalk — a fine, permanent, warm-then-cooling drizzle that beads on the fibre and runs down in vertical threads by the thousand, so that the inner wall is a two-mile-tall sheet of trickling condensate. Above that it rimes instead. The topmost quarter of every stalk is furred white on the inside with feather-ice, which sheds in slabs when the wind leans the range, and those slabs fall the whole way down and detonate on the warm floor with a sound like a felled tree. This happens perhaps twice an hour in any given stalk. It is the reason nobody sleeps directly beneath the shaft.

Outside the stalks it rains rarely, in short cold squalls that the range makes for itself out of its own exhalation. Most of the water in this world is inside, on a wall, going down while the air goes up.

---

### Sound

You will not be able to hear yourself think, and after a season you will stop trying.

Every hollow stalk is an organ pipe two miles long, open at both ends, with a permanent draught through it. Each one hums a fundamental so low it is felt in the sternum and the teeth more than the ear, and above that a stack of overtones, and above that the shriek of wind through the leaf-scars and split seams and the mouths of the climbers' burrows, which are countless and which the plant does not repair. When the range leans, the pitches slide. When a cloud shadow crosses (there are cloud shadows; the range is large enough to have its own weather and does), a whole district of stalks detunes at once.

The result is an enormous, slow, unresolving chord that changes over hours and never stops, and inside it the sharp events: the ice-slabs, the barking of the climbers, the clatter of shed rib-plates falling, the boom when a stalk somewhere out of sight splits under torsion — a sound like a continent clearing its throat, which arrives twice, once through the air and once, earlier and deeper, through the ground.

People here speak by touch and by drum-tap on the stem wall, which carries beautifully. Voices are for grief and for very close conversation.

---

### The animals

Abundant, and yet you can go three days without seeing one.

They live on the surfaces. There is no living in the empty middle; the middle is only a place to fall through. So everything that moves here clings, and the walls — inner and outer, over hundreds of square miles of them — are populated the way a shoreline is populated: densely, in a film.

**Rung-climbers**, the common animal: six-limbed, hand-sized to dog-sized, with hooked pads and a low flat body, grazing the light-tissue in slow-moving companies of hundreds. Their shed rib-plates are the region's timber.

**Sift**: pale, sail-winged, the size of a coat, which do not fly so much as ride. They hatch at the base of a stalk, are lifted, feed on spore-fog on the way up, mate at the freezing line, and come out of the top dead or nearly. Their bodies fall for four or five minutes. There is a whole guild of things that live entirely on sift falling out of the sky, including the people, sometimes.

**Braid**: predators, long as a rope and thin as a wrist, which live in the seams and take climbers, and which hunt by sound, so they cannot hunt during the loud hours.

**Sinkers**: heavy, slow, quadruped, blind, living on the warm floor mats in the base of the stalks, eating the fallen. They are the only large animals that stand on anything like ground.

Almost nothing crosses the open plain between stalks. That distance is a genuine ocean.

---

### The people, and their problem

There are marks of habitation and not much more: a hanging platform of lashed rib-plate, a ladder of gum-set fibre pegs going up a hundred feet of inner wall and stopping, a smoke-blackened hollow, a cairn of sift-skulls, a single tall carved post standing alone on the plain between two stalks for reasons nobody now can explain. A day's walk apart, mostly. There are perhaps four thousand people in an area the size of a small country, and they are not lonely, exactly; they are simply calibrated to this.

Their problem is that the stalks grow.

Not slowly. The range is younger than its oldest inhabitant — there are people alive who remember the plain when it was only plain, and the first season of black shoots coming up out of it like a field of spears. The stalks put on about the height of a man every day, and they do it at the top, and everything below the growing tip is carried upward with it.

So: a house built at a comfortable warm elevation is, in three years, in the freezing zone. A ladder is a good ladder for a while and then it is a ladder into ice. Every trail must be re-cut downward at the same rate the world climbs. The whole material culture is arranged around descent: dwellings that can be untied and lowered, hearths that are just a gum-sealed basket of embers on a line, burial by simply leaving the body high and letting the stalk carry it up out of reach, which it will, forever.

They speak of the dead as *above*. This is not a metaphor. If you climb high enough into an old stalk, past the raining zone, into the rime, you come into a long white gallery of frozen people sitting in the postures they were left in, tens of thousands of feet up, still going, gaining a man's height a day, and the wind screaming up past them out of the mouth at the top and into the dark.

### Rules

- Nothing is level. Everything is either wall or fall.
- Up is warm becoming cold. Down is cold becoming warm. Sideways is empty.
- What you build, you will have to move.
- Silence means a braid is hunting.
- The range is still going up. Nobody has seen a stalk finish, or flower, or die. Everything anyone here knows about this world is the knowledge of its first thirty years.
