## The Shelf

Begin with the noise, because there is no way in that does not go through it.

Two thousand meters below the lip of the plateau, a river that has no name in any language spoken at this altitude falls off the edge in seven separate ribbons and lands on rock that has been rock for perhaps four hundred thousand years — young, by the standards of stone. The sound this makes is not a roar. A roar has structure, has a mouth behind it. This is a flat wall of white pressure that arrives through the soles of the feet as much as the ears, and it never stops, and it never varies, and within an hour of arriving you have stopped hearing it in the way you stop seeing your own nose. What you notice instead is your body's response: the slight permanent clench in the jaw, the way you find yourself shouting at nobody, the strange loneliness of it. Two people standing an arm's length apart on the Shelf are, acoustically, in separate countries. They speak with hands. They write on slate with a wet fingertip and the words dry off in four minutes.

## Light

The second thing is that the light is intolerable and everyone here has beautiful eyes, ruined.

At this elevation the air has given up most of its business of filtering. The sun comes through it undiluted and hits a landscape that is almost entirely composed of water in one form or another — standing water, seeping water, water suspended as fog in the sponge of the ground, water in the air as a permanent aerosol thrown up from the falls. Every one of those surfaces is a mirror. The glare comes from above and also from below and also, maddeningly, from the sides, and at midday the plateau is a white forge in which shadows appear only directly beneath overhangs, small and blue and precious as coins.

The spray from the falls stands in the air along the eastern rim in a slow rolling curtain a hundred meters deep, and inside that curtain there are rainbows that do not move. Not fleeting arcs — fixed structures. They have positions. You could survey them. In the older survey books of the Shelf they are marked as landmarks, with bearings, the way a lighthouse would be: *Bow Three, visible 1030h–1500h, use for orientation from the shed line.* They have been in the same place, doing the same thing, for as long as there have been people up here to write things down.

## The ground, which is alive

There is no soil on the Shelf. The rock is too new. The uplift here runs something like eleven millimeters a year, which is preposterous, geologically indecent, and the water has not had time to do its patient work of cutting and grinding and making dirt. What lies underneath is raw: angular, unsculpted, fracture-fresh gneiss and pale intrusive granite standing in blunt unweathered blocks and slabs, without valleys, without the smooth shoulders that a mountain earns. Nothing has been carved. The water doesn't run in beds — there hasn't been time for beds — it sheets. It spreads across the tilted slabs in films a centimeter deep and finds the edges and goes over them.

So the plants made their own ground.

It is a cushion mat: a single, essentially continuous organism-community of peat-forming plants, hard little rosettes packed shoulder to shoulder like cobbles, threaded through with liverworts and hair-mosses and a sedge that flowers greenish-white and smells faintly of iodine. In places it is thirty centimeters thick. In hollows it is four meters thick and floating, and beneath it the water is black and still and colder than anything. The mat covers everything the water hasn't scoured. It goes up the vertical faces. It caps the boulders like fitted upholstery. It grows over the pipes and it grows over the roofs and it has grown, in the last sixty years, entirely over the tramway.

To walk on it is to walk on a body. It gives, and then it gives back, and where your boot pressed a hollow the hollow fills with brown water and does not drain, and it will hold the shape of your foot for two seasons. There is no such thing as a dry step. Every footfall makes the mat weep. And there are places — the *false grounds* — where the mat has bridged a gap in the rock and looks exactly like all the other mat and is not, and every generation up here loses somebody to that. It is the plateau's only real appetite.

## Sparse

And yet — and this is the thing that no one expects — it reads as empty.

Because the mat is a skin, not a forest. Nothing on the Shelf is taller than your ankle. There are no trees, no shrubs, no thickets, nothing to stand between you and the horizon, and the horizon is fifteen kilometers off in every direction and then it stops, because the plateau ends and the cloud begins. So you have total vegetation and total openness at once: a green-black-and-ochre floor stretching out under a violent sky, absolutely covered in life, absolutely bare of objects. What few objects there are, you can count on your fingers.

Nine sheds. One dam, which is not really a dam. A cableway. Two cairns and a grave.

You can see all of it at once from the middle of the Shelf, and the distances between them are large enough that walking from one to the next takes most of a morning, and for that whole morning you are the tallest thing in the world.

## The works

The industry here is water, of course, and it is very old and very simple and very loud.

The intake sits where the sheeting films gather into the one thing on the Shelf that resembles a river — a shallow braided seep three hundred meters wide and knee-deep — and there they built a low weir of dressed granite blocks, mossed over now to the point of looking geological, to pool a few million cubic meters and steer them into the penstocks. Four iron pipes, riveted, each wider than a man is tall, running down the eastern face into the cloud. They are the only straight lines in the landscape and they have a queer authority for it. Where the pipes leave the lip there is a bolted collar and a valve wheel the size of a millstone, and the vibration coming out of that steel is such that you cannot put your palm flat on it for more than a moment: it isn't hot, it's *singing*, a whole-body hum somewhere below the pressure of the falls, and if you stand with your hand on the collar you can feel four hundred tons of water a minute going down to do work you will never see. The powerhouse is below the cloud. Nobody who works up here has ever been in it.

The sheds are moss-roofed timber and corrugate, half-sunk, guyed against a wind that comes maybe twice a year and comes seriously. Inside: a bunk, a stove that burns compressed peat cut from the mat's deep layers (legally, by permit, four cubic meters a year, and the cut heals in a century), a grease-pencil log going back to 1911, brass earplugs on a lanyard, and a small library, always, of soft-paged books. Reading is what there is. Conversation costs too much.

Six people live on the Shelf. In summer, nine. They are inspectors and greasers and the mat is their real employer, because the mat is what keeps the water slow — a bare plateau this steep and this wet would flush its rainfall down the pipes in a brown scouring hour and wreck the turbines by August. The mat holds it. Releases it over weeks. The entire economics of the valley below, its lit streets and its two mills and its electric tram, rests on the water-retention properties of a plant nobody in that valley could name. The inspectors know this. They walk the mat and mark rot, and lay slat walkways where the boots have opened a wound, and pull the one invasive grass by hand, and they are — although the word is not used in the ledgers — gardeners of a thirty-square-kilometer organ.

## Fauna

Almost nothing lives here. The mat is nitrogen-poor and acid and the air is thin and the noise, presumably, is unbearable to anything with an ear.

But: there is a small brown bird, a kind of dipper adapted absurdly upward, that works the sheeting films for larvae and cannot be heard even when it is singing directly at you with its throat visibly working. The inspectors are fond of it out of proportion. And in the mat itself, if you split a cushion, a slow world: white segmented things, mites, a translucent worm that survives freezing. Twice a decade a fox comes up from the treeline for reasons nobody has established, crosses the whole Shelf in a straight line, and goes back down.

## Time

Nothing here is finished. That is the essential fact.

The mountain is still coming up. The falls have not yet cut a gorge — they will, in a hundred thousand years, and then this plateau will be two plateaus and the Shelf will be a memory in the rock. The mat is still making the soil that does not yet exist. The rainbows hold their bearings. The iron sings its one note. Somebody in Shed Four writes *clear, wind nil, intake 0.6m, all normal* in grease pencil for the eleven thousandth time, and does not hear the pen, and does not hear the water, and steps outside into that white murdering light with a hand up to shade eyes that stopped being any good years ago, and looks out across a green skin stretched over new stone, and finds, as everyone does eventually, that the noise has become a kind of silence, and that it is beautiful, and that there is no one within four kilometers to say so to.
