## The Lower Volumes

There is no ground. Start with that, because everything else follows from it.

Whatever you set your foot on here was poured, cast, rolled, welded, bolted, or extruded by machine. There is no bedrock under the concrete; the concrete *is* the bedrock, six metres of it, and under that another slab, and under that the drainage lattice, and under that the grout curtain, and under that more of the same for as far as anyone has ever cored. The engineers stopped calling it a floor a long time ago. They call it the invert. The invert of Cell 40 is nine hundred kilometres across and it has never seen a stone that wasn't made in a kiln.

You come down into it. Always down. Every route in is a shaft or an inclined adit, and the pressure change makes your ears crackle for the first hour, and at the bottom of the descent there is a bulkhead door, and through the door, light.

That is the thing nobody prepares you for. It is *bright* down here. The soffit — the roof, the underside of the world — carries lamp strings in a rectilinear grid, and the strings are hung low, and the water is so newly filled that it hasn't earned any turbidity yet. The result is a lit sea. Not the blue-black cathedral gloom of a natural ocean but a pale, glassy, faintly greenish daylight that goes down two hundred metres and keeps going, and from a gallery you can look out over a body of water the size of a province and see the far-off lamp grid receding in perfect perspective, string after string after string, each reflected twice, once on the surface and once in the wet soffit above it, until the two grids converge and the eye gives up.

The water is cool. Eight degrees, nine in the upper metre near the fixtures. It smells of nothing much — wet stone, ozone from the switchgear, the faint chlorine-and-chalk breath of new cement curing under a sea. It tastes sweet and slippery if you get it in your mouth, and it will strip your lips raw within the hour, because the concrete is still leaching lime and every cell in the Lower Volumes runs alkaline. That is the great labour of this decade: the dosing barges, hundreds of them, ugly flat-bottomed things trailing plumes of acid and buffered brine, crawling back and forth across the cells in mowing patterns that take four months per pass. Everything alive here is alive because a barge went past.

---

**Weather.** A cell has weather. It cannot help it.

The air gap between the water and the soffit is anywhere from sixty to four hundred metres, and it holds a genuine atmosphere: cold, saturated, moving. Warm water rises off the outfall aprons where the return flow comes in, and the vapour climbs, and it hits the soffit, which is always colder than the water because the mass above it is colder still, and it condenses. It condenses in sheets. Drop growth on a concrete ceiling has a rhythm to it, and when a hectare of soffit reaches its threshold simultaneously — which happens, because the soffit is flat and cast in one pour and thermally uniform — you get *soffit rain*: a curtain of fat cold drops falling all at once from a great height, hammering the surface white, so loud that the crews wear ear defenders as a matter of course. The rain walks. It has fronts. It has a season, three weeks out of every eleven, keyed to the maintenance schedule of the lift houses. Fog forms in the mornings — there are mornings, the strings dim and brighten on a twenty-six hour cycle — and lies on the water in long combed bands aligned with the current, and out of the fog you can hear the knocking.

**Sound.** This is a loud world. Loud in air, catastrophically loud in water.

The lift houses stand at the cell margins like blank cliffs, and inside each one, columns of water three metres in diameter are being raised eleven hundred metres by machinery on an unhelpful scale. You feel it before you hear it, in the fillings of your teeth. Then the airborne sound arrives: a huge patient bass throb, sixty or seventy per minute, the pace of a large animal at rest, overlaid with the shriek of ventilation and the arrhythmic bang of valve gear. Under the surface it becomes something else entirely. Water carries sound so well and the cells are so hard-walled and so empty that a single valve slam in Volume Four can be heard, faintly, five hundred kilometres away as a click, and the cells *ring*. Each one has a note. Cell 40 is somewhere near a very low A. Divers describe working in it as being inside a struck bell that never quite stops.

And there is water hammer. The knocks. When a gate closes wrong, a pressure wave runs the length of a conduit and hits the far end with the force of a landslide, and every cell it passes through booms, and the crews look up from what they're doing, and count. Three knocks in a shift is normal. Six means somebody is going to have a difficult conversation.

---

**Emptiness.** Here is the paradox of the place: it is entirely built, and it is almost entirely nothing.

The made-ness is in the boundaries. In between the boundaries there is water and light and no content whatsoever. You can swim for a day in the mid-volume of a large cell and pass one thing: a mooring buoy, a sounding chain, a lamp string dropping down out of the glare like a plumb line. A survey launch crossing Cell 40 at eighteen knots takes two and a half days and the pilot has nothing to steer by except the strings, which is why the strings are colour-coded, red running one axis and amber the other, so that a person alone in the middle of a lit sea can say *I am at red nineteen, amber four* and be located within a kilometre. Beyond that, void. Bright, cold, resonant void, with a fog bank in it somewhere.

**Life.** And yet it is thick with animals, which is the second paradox, and it resolves the same way: everything living clings to the edges.

The walls are where it happens. Every vertical surface in the Lower Volumes is a coast. The seeded stocks went in as soon as the pH would allow — mussels first, then the tube-worm mats, then grazers, then the silver: a schooling fish the length of your hand, put in by the trillion as a trophic floor, and now present in shoals that hang against the wall faces in slabs half a kilometre long, turning together, flashing like a change in the weather. The shoals attract everything. Squid come up the wall at lamp-dim to hunt in the shadow zone and go down again at lamp-bright. There are eels in every construction joint, one to a joint, territorial, and the joints run in perfectly straight lines for hundreds of kilometres, so an eel's whole world is a horizontal crack and its two neighbours. There are the lampfish — not their real name, nobody uses their real name — which have learned that a lamp string is a permanent island of food, and which orbit the strings in vertical columns, and which have already, in one generation, begun to school more tightly under amber than under red.

Above the surface there are birds. Nobody quite invited them; they came down the intake shafts with the ventilation. Now there are gull colonies on the gantry ledges of every lift house, screaming, whitening the concrete, robbing the crew mess, raising chicks that will live and die and never once see the sky. They fish the outfall plumes. They ride the thermals off the warm-water aprons, up towards the soffit, and turn just short of it, because there is nothing above.

Plants, though, are thin. This is a cool, deep, alkaline, four-year-old world and it has not learned photosynthesis at scale. What there is: green fur on the illuminated wall bands, thick as moss in the sweet spot four metres down and absent above and below it, so that every cell is ringed with a single green stripe at a perfectly consistent depth, like a tidemark on a bath. Kelp trials in the shelter of the training walls, patchy, half of them failing. And the cress rafts — hectares of them, tethered in lines off the buffer barges, hairy pale mats that pull metal out of the water and are harvested by machine and get eaten by nobody, because everyone knows where they've been.

---

**Youth.** Nothing here is old. That is the last thing to understand, and the strangest.

The arrises are still sharp. Every edge of every structure is a crisp ninety degrees, unsoftened, and it *hurts*, and the crews wear their gloves through in a fortnight. You can still read the formwork on the walls: the grain of the shuttering, the neat lines of the tie-holes, the pour lifts stacked like sedimentary strata that were laid down in an afternoon each. There is no scour except a faint bright polishing at the outlet throats, a metre or two of it, the very first stirring of erosion in a landscape that will eventually be worn round like anywhere else but not for ten thousand years.

And there are the weeps. Every steel fitting has begun to rust, and every rust stain has begun to run down the concrete beneath it, and the run-rate is known: about a hand's breadth a year on a wet wall. So the crews date things by eye. A gallery with tears to the skirting has been standing eight years, and was therefore part of the first construction, and its drawings are unreliable. A fitting with no tear at all was installed since the last flood-up, and is not yet on any drawing at all.

Which is the rule of the Lower Volumes, the one that gets stencilled at every bulkhead: **this place is younger than your assumptions about it.** The cells fill and drawdown on a schedule that changes; a chamber that was dry last season is under ninety metres now, with silver shoaling through its doorways; a shaft that carried air last year carries water at four metres per second and will kill you for entering it. Before any descent you read the board. You listen for the whistle codes carrying down the adits, three long for a drawdown, two and a repeat for a knock in the system. You keep a hand on the string.

And when you surface in the middle of a cell, in the middle of the enormous cold lit nothing, with the pumps thudding away below the edge of hearing and the soffit rain walking towards you across a hundred kilometres of flat water, you understand that this was all built, every millimetre of it, on purpose, by people, and that it is a sea now regardless, and that it has begun to do whatever seas do.
