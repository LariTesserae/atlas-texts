## The Wick

At the top of the cloud-sea there is a black spine of glass, and it is still coming up out of the world.

You would find it by sound before sight. There is no sight to speak of. The spire stands in the long shadow of something larger to the east — a mass so high and so far that it is never itself visible, only inferred from the fact that dawn never arrives here. Light comes only as a grey rumour along the northern rim of the cloud floor for an hour each day, enough to grey the fog and no more. The rest is dark: a wet, breathing, crowded, screaming dark.

---

### The stone

The Wick is a spine of extruded glass, pushed up from below like a splinter working out of a thumb. It is not a mountain in the ordinary sense — not built of layers, not carved by rivers. It is a single object. Perhaps two hundred paces across at the cloud line, tapering to a blade-crest you could straddle, and it rises, measurably, about the width of a finger joint each year.

Because it is new, nothing has had time to soften it. The flanks are fluted where the glass tore against its own sheath on the way up, and every flute has an edge, and every edge is fresh. Not sharp like a knife — sharp like a broken bottle, sharp in the specific way that only cooled melt is sharp. A hand laid flat on the rock will bleed from four places. Everything that lives here has adapted to this: horn pads, scale, callus, an inherited caution. The Wick is a landscape with a grain to it, and everything alive moves along the grain.

It is also warm. Not hot — you would not call it hot — but at this altitude, where the air is thin and near freezing and the wind comes off the cloud-sea with a wet knife in it, the stone holds a faint deep heat from wherever it is being made. Press your palm to it (carefully, on a smooth face) and you feel something like the warmth of a large sleeping animal. This is the entire reason for everything else that happens here. The Wick is the only warm rock above the cloud for a hundred leagues in any direction. It is the hearth in the dark, and so every living thing that can reach it has come.

The glass ticks. As it rises, it cools, and as it cools it cracks — small ticks and pings, thousands a night, a constant faint percussion under the noise, like a stove going cold, except this stove never finishes going cold. Occasionally a flute lets go and a sheet of black glass the size of a door slides off into the cloud, and does not make a sound anyone up here ever hears.

---

### The water

There is no rain worth the name. There is fog — the cloud-sea sloshes up the flanks twice a day in a slow tidal way, and the cold glass strips water out of it. Every flute becomes a thread of running condensate. The whole spire, all night, is quietly weeping.

The water collects in the crotches between flutes, in blowholes, in the pocked scars where gas escaped the melt. These pockets are the only standing water above the clouds, and they are guarded, and they are foul. Each holds perhaps a bucket. Each is thick with a soup of guano, drowned insects, and a pale rubbery algal skin that must be broken before you can drink.

And in a few places — this is one of the strange things — the grooves that carry water are too regular. They run at a shallow diagonal across otherwise vertical flutes and they are polished, and they are old, and they lead water to particular basins and not to others. Nobody built them. They were licked. Generation upon generation of tongues, following the wet, wearing a path in glass. It is the closest thing to architecture on the Wick, and it took ten thousand years, and it looks like plumbing.

---

### The living dark

The sound is the fact of the place. From the cloud line to the crest, the Wick is packed.

The lowest band, where the fog is thickest, belongs to the **wailers** — sooty, hook-winged, gull-sized, nesting shoulder to shoulder in every flute, three and four deep, layered on ledges you would not call ledges. They do not see. Their eyes are vestigial studs. They navigate by voice: a continuous descending shriek, each bird's slightly distinct, the whole colony a wall of overlapping cries that never stops, never even lulls, day or night, all year. It is loud enough that you feel it in the sternum. It is loud enough that a person standing here for an hour would begin to lose the thread of their own thoughts. The wailers fly out over the cloud-sea in the black, screaming, and read the echoes off the drifting swarms of aeroplankton — the drifting, faintly luminous chaff that rides the high air like snow that will not fall.

That plankton is the base of everything. It is the only light: a green stipple, so faint you must not look at it directly, drifting past the spire in slow banks. When a big drift comes through, the whole colony changes pitch at once, and the spire briefly outlines itself in a haze of moving specks, and you can see — for a few minutes — how absolutely covered in bodies the mountain is.

Above the wailers: **the crawlers.** Flightless, fist-sized, many-legged, armored in overlapping plates that let them move across glass edges the way a caterpillar crosses a saw. They farm the guano. Ten thousand of them per flute. They keep the fungal mats grazed, which keeps the mats from choking the water grooves, which is why the water grooves still work.

Above them: the mats themselves, and the **combs** — a pale, cold-loving fungus that grows in fans a hand's breadth wide, feeding on the endless rain of droppings, and which is warm to the touch, warmer than the stone. And in the combs live the mites, and in the mites live things smaller still, and by density of separate living presences per volume of air, the upper Wick is one of the most crowded places in the world, and almost all of it is smaller than your thumbnail.

Vascular plants: eleven species. All cushion-forms, all in the dew basins, all older than any animal here. One of them flowers once every nine years, at random, in the dark, and is pollinated by something nobody has ever identified.

---

### The knappers

And then there are the ones with hands.

They are small, waist-high, dark-furred against the cold, wide-footed. They live in the upper third, above the worst of the noise, in the wind-scoured flutes where the wailers won't nest. They have not built anything. There are no shelters, no walls, no hearths — you cannot make fire in this wet, and there is nothing here to burn.

What they have is the mountain's edge.

The Wick sheds fresh glass continually, and the knappers gather it, and they work it: percussion flakes, blades struck off prepared cores, scrapers, and long thin lancets they carry in bundles wrapped in fungal fiber. They use them to open crawlers, to cut nest-fiber, to shave the algal skin off the drinking pockets, to trim their own foot-pads. They are astonishingly good, and they have been astonishingly good for a very long time, and they have not improved and will not. The technique is fixed. It is the same six gestures in the same order, taught by imitation in the dark, by feel, in the middle of a noise so loud that no instruction could ever be spoken.

Here is the strange part, and the reason people who reach the Wick tend to sit down and stop talking.

The glass of the spire, in places, contains tools. Not embedded surface artifacts — inclusions. Blades and cores and half-finished scrapers, suspended inside the black glass, visible when a fresh face splits, sometimes a hand's depth in. They are the same six gestures. They are the same tradition. And the glass around them has never been anywhere but underground, coming up, brand new, for the first time.

The knappers, when they find one, chip it out and use it. They do not appear to find this remarkable.

---

### The rules

**Everything falls eventually.** Nothing on the Wick is safe from the flake-slide. Nests, water grooves, colonies, cushion plants, bodies — the flute gives way and the whole assemblage goes into the cloud without a sound.

**Everything rises.** A ledge that is good nesting now will be, in a lifetime, part of the wind-blasted upper crest, and in ten lifetimes, gone off the top as spall. Every creature here lives on a slow escalator. Nothing has a permanent address. The colony's memory, if it has one, is a memory of altitudes.

**The mountain gives the edge and takes the ledge.** These are understood to be the same transaction.

**Nothing here is fast.** The plankton drifts. The fog breathes twice a day. The stone ticks. The spine comes up a finger a year. The knapping tradition has not changed in an unmeasurable span. Even the screaming, which sounds like frenzy, is a flat unvarying tone across decades — not an event, a condition. The only fast things on the Wick are the falls, and they are over before they register.

---

If you climbed here, out of the cloud, in the cold and the dark, into that wall of sound, with the warm black glass under your bleeding hands and the green chaff drifting past and something small and clever watching you from a flute above with a blade in its fist —

you would understand very quickly that you had not discovered a place. You had arrived, extremely late, at an extremely crowded one.
