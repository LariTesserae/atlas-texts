## The Pan

The basin is small enough to cross in nine minutes at a walk, if you walk without hurry, which is the only way anyone walks here. It sits at sixty metres above the sea in a flat grey country of wet gravel and birch scrub — no view from the rim, only more of the same going out, low and unremarkable, the sort of land that maps mark with a single word and leave alone.

The rim is a horseshoe of glacial grit, waist-high to chest-high, laid down by ice and softened by ten thousand years of nothing much happening. Inside it the ground is pale marl over black peat, and the marl gives underfoot with a faint chalky squeak. The floor is drier than you'd expect. Water comes in as a thread off the gravel — the locals call it the Thread, and it is not always there — and sinks almost immediately into the marl, where it collects in a lens no deeper than a cellar. Everything in the Pan lives off that lens and off the fog.

## The Stems

Standing in the basin, spaced four to seven metres apart, are between two and three hundred columns.

They are plant. One plant, in the technical sense: a single clone, joined below by a rhizome mat as thick as a mattress. Above ground each column is a hollow segmented stem, nine to fourteen metres tall, up to a metre and ten across at the base, tapering, ringed at every node like a bone. The walls are eight centimetres of laminated fibre stiffened with silica, grey-green at the growing end and going the colour of old pewter as they age. They do not bear leaves. Instead, at each node, a whorl of thin fluted blades stands out horizontally, arm's length, like the vanes of a pipe organ laid flat. The skin of the stem does the photosynthesis. The flutes do the water.

Because the spacing is wide, the Pan is not a thicket. It is a room. Daylight comes down between the columns in flat grey shafts, plenty to read by, tinted very slightly the green of pond glass, and the floor between the stems is open marl with patches of a low grey liverwort and nothing else. You can see from one side of the Pan to the other. You can see, at the far end, the town lamps burning at four in the afternoon in November, small and yellow, high up in the nodes.

## Water, and How It Is Not Wasted

The Pan is damp, not wet. That distinction organises everything.

Roots draw from the shallow lens all night and push water up under positive pressure — three, four atmospheres by the small hours. By day the flutes exhale and the column pulls instead. So each stem is a pressure system that swings twice a day, hard, on a schedule you could set a clock to and in fact they do.

The flutes are condensers. Cool air off the gravel rim meets the warm humid breath coming out of the stem, and water beads on the underside of every vane and runs back down grooves into the node, where it is recovered. The plant loses very little. What it loses it mostly gets back.

The consequence, for anyone standing beneath, is drip. Morning fog most days of the year; a slow patter after it burns off; a heavier session an hour after rain, long after the sky has finished. It falls into hollow chambers of varying volume and the whole basin becomes a distributed instrument, low and unmusical and constant, a few hundred tuned wooden buckets being tapped at random by a very patient hand. This is the loudest thing in the Pan on most days.

## The Works

There is a town here and it is genuinely a town: about a hundred and thirty people, four levels, a charter, a debt ledger, a fibre shed, a lamp works, night shifts.

None of it was built in the ordinary sense. The rule, unbroken for as long as records go, is *nothing cut that can be bent*. So the town has been grafted. Stems were leaned as saplings and tied until they took the lean; nodes were girdled to swell into rooms; laterals were trained across gaps and allowed to fuse, and now serve as beams, walkways, stairs with treads that are the plant's own healed callus. Floors are pressed fibre from shed sheaths, retted in the Thread, dried in the dead end of the basin. Doors are lacquered sheath on hinges of the same. The lacquer is the plant's own wound resin, tapped and cooked: amber, hard, smelling of iodine and cut grass.

The industry is pressure.

Every dwelling node has a tap. Every tap goes to a rack, and every rack goes to the Turn — a grown machine at the centre of the Pan where a swollen lateral organ, three metres across, inflates and deflates on a forty-minute cycle and rocks a beam like a slow animal breathing. Geared up eleven hundred to one, that beam runs a fibre carder, a hoist, and a lathe. It is the most powerful engine in the basin and it moves, at its working end, about the speed of a minute hand.

Electricity comes from the other end of the Pan, from the dry ruins, where they stack sap cells: paired discs of pith and inner bark separated by brine felt, sixty to a rack, four hundred racks in the dead nodes. Each cell is worth almost nothing. Together they run filament lamps at a low sullen orange, a signal needle, and the coil that keeps the lacquer pot at temperature. It is not much current. It is entirely reliable, which the Company values more.

## Why It Is Quiet

The stems clamp.

A sharp sound above roughly the volume of a raised human voice triggers the node valves to shut — a defence reflex, presumably against something that no longer exists. A slammed door costs the Works forty minutes of pressure. A dropped iron bar costs the afternoon. A shout costs the shift.

So: felt over the shoes, felt on the stairs, felt on the tool racks. Hand signs for everything routine, of which there are two hundred and eleven, and a written code for everything else. No bells; instead a green shuttered lamp on the Turn's mast, and each node has its own shutter facing it. Children are taught the signs before speech and speak late and softly for life. Visitors are given a card at the rim listing the four things they must not do, and are watched.

The quiet is not reverent. It is economic. But it has been economic for so long that the two are indistinguishable, and the Company's oldest surviving document is a fine, in fibre, levied against a man for laughing.

Under the quiet: drip. The creak of stems flexing at the dawn pressure surge, a long low groan that runs around the Pan like a rope being pulled through a ring, and which is the one loud sound the plant makes and forgives. The tick of the Turn. Fog on gravel, which has a sound, faintly. Feet.

## Fauna

Rare, and specialised, because the plant is defended with tannin and silica and almost nothing can eat it.

The valve-beetle, thumb-sized, pale as candle wax, lodges in valve throats and keeps them from fusing shut. The Company breeds them in trays and considers them staff. Second: a small grey bird, the fluter, which nests only in the dead nodes at the trailing edge and sings four notes at dusk, which is permitted. Third: a moth, seen once in nine years. Fourth: mites in the liverwort, and a bracket fungus on the dying end that the fibre crew harvest for sizing.

No fish — the Thread runs black with tannin. No grazers. Occasionally a deer comes over the rim, stands among the columns for a while, and leaves. People stop work to watch.

## The Nine-Year Flowering

Once in nine summers, the whole clone flowers. Every node throws a spike; every valve opens to the spikes; pressure goes where the plant wants it and not where the taps are.

The Works shuts. The Turn stops mid-stroke and hangs. The lamps go out over eleven days as the cells run down, and the town lives by candle and by the enormous rotten-honey smell of the spikes, and the moths come, thousands of them, the only week the basin has anything you could call abundance. There are songs for it, sung under the breath. There are also account books which show that the Company has never once failed to survive it, and never once done so comfortably.

## The Walk

Here is the fact that arranges the town's whole sense of itself.

The clone advances. New stems come up at the northwest edge, where the marl is undepleted; old stems die at the southeast, where four hundred years of the same plant have taken what there was. The rate is about one metre twenty a year.

So the Works walks. Slowly, downstream, across its own basin, at roughly the speed a fingernail grows.

The leading edge is a fringe of pale green columns, soft-walled, being tied and leaned by grafters who will not live to move into them. Behind that, the working town. Behind that, the sap-cell racks in stems the town lived in when the current elders were children — dry, hollow, silvered, still weatherproof. And behind that, the ruins: grey shells full of standing fog, forty generations of abandoned architecture, roofless, ticking with drip, going soft at the bottom and collapsing into peat at the very back of the Pan.

The plant engulfs whatever is left inside it. So the ruins are a stratigraphy of the town itself, read backward as you walk southeast: a bicycle frame swallowed to the hubs in healed callus; a lamp bracket; a pair of spectacles in a node wall, in profile, perfectly clear through the amber; hand-sign charts in an older dialect; tools whose purpose no one now knows, held in the wood at chest height as though offered.

The Company owns no land. Its charter specifies a *position relative to the leading edge*, and every ninth year the surveyor drives a new pin and the deeds are re-copied against it. Everyone here knows exactly where the town will be in a hundred years, and that it will run out of basin in roughly four hundred, when the leading edge reaches the gravel rim.

There is no plan for that. There are, in the ledger, three separate proposals, all rejected, all preserved.

## Rules

1. Nothing cut that can be bent.
2. Every wound lacquered before dark.
3. Silence through the dawn surge.
4. The beetles are staff.
5. What the plant takes into itself is left there.
6. Nobody owns ground. Everybody owns a distance.
