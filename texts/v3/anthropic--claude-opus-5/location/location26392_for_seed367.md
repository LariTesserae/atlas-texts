## The Well

### Above

For four days' walk in any direction the plateau is finished. Not ruined — finished, the way a bowl is finished when the potter stops. This was a mountain range once, in some earlier draft of the continent, and the wind and the temperature and three billion years have taken the summits off and rounded the roots and polished the roots down to a floor. What remains is a pavement of quartzite and case-hardened dolomite the color of old teeth, lying at nearly four thousand meters, with a skin of desert varnish so smooth that the sun comes off it in sheets. There is no soil. There is no sand, even — the sand blew away long ago, to some lower country. The rock has reached its final form and has no further business with erosion.

Nothing lives up here except the things that pass through. Bare rock, hard light, and a cold that arrives at dusk like a door closing.

And then, somewhere in the middle of all that pavement, a hole.

### The Rim

It is not large. A hundred and ten paces across at the lip, oval, its long axis running northeast. From ten meters away you cannot see it; the pavement lies flat right up to the edge with no warning ridge, no talus, no debris apron. The hole is old enough to have finished making a mess.

What you notice first, from that ten meters, is the smell — warm, green, resinous, faintly sweet-rotten, entirely wrong for the altitude — and then the noise. A dense, layered, unbroken roar rising out of the ground. Cicada-thrum in three registers, a mineral chittering, the whir of something enormous made of small wings. It comes up out of the pavement like sound out of a grate.

Lean over the lip and you are looking down into a green throat.

### The Chemistry, Which Is the Rule Here

Understand the arrangement, because everything else follows from it.

Under the plateau, four hundred meters down, lies a bed of gypsum laid down when this was a sea, in an age that has no other survivors. Below that, deeper still, the crust is thin and warm. The heat has been working on the gypsum for a very long time — patiently, at the rate of a few degrees, converting it to bassanite and then to anhydrite and driving off the water that was locked into its crystal structure. Not liquid water. Water as vapor, as the last exhalation of a mineral being cooked over a fire so slow it will take another ten million years to finish.

That breath rises through fractures. It comes up warm and humid into the Well, which is a collapse pit — the roof of an old solution cavity that let go, one time, without a witness — and the Well holds it, because the Well is a sump for air, and warm wet air is heavy with everything that matters.

So: no rain. There has been no rain on this plateau in the memory of the species that live in the Well. No streams, no seeps, no puddles, no dew. Nothing here ever pools, ever drips, ever runs. Water exists only as vapor and as the water already inside living things. It moves from mineral to leaf to animal to corpse to leaf again, and never once passes through a free surface. Drop something wet in the Well and you have made a small scandal; every ant within twelve meters will come.

### The Descent

You go down the northeast wall, where the collapse left a ramp of megablocks the size of granaries, all of them rounded, all of them furred. The stairway cut into them is so worn that the treads are convex, dished in the middle by feet — not by weather; nothing weathers here anymore. Rope of twisted bast, greased black, runs down through eyes drilled in the rock.

Light drops fast. At the lip it is that white plateau glare; twenty meters down it is aquarium-green; forty meters down, on the floor, it is the light of a room with one shuttered window. The canopy is responsible. The Well's mouth is bridged by an ancient strangler — a fig-thing with no fruit anyone can eat, older than the town, its trunks welded down the walls like candle wax and its crown lying across the opening in a horizontal raft. Through it comes a single mobile coin of full sun, which crosses the floor of the Well from about the fourth hour to the ninth, moving a hand's width every few minutes. It is the clock. Every plant with any ambition is arranged along its track, and every child knows exactly where it will be at any hour, the way children elsewhere know the smell of rain.

Temperature at the floor: warm, steady, twenty-eight or twenty-nine, day and night, summer and winter, forever. Four thousand meters up, in a place where the exposed rock swings sixty degrees in a day, the Well does not vary. It is a thermos with a garden in it.

### The Green

Lush, and not one leaf of it is soft.

Everything in the Well is a water-miser, because there is no water to be a spendthrift with — only vapor, and vapor must be caught. So the vegetation is thick-skinned, waxy, corrugated, ribbed, blue-grey, silver-hairy, glaucous, and packed shoulder to shoulder in layers from floor to rim. Rosette plants the size of cartwheels clamp themselves to the wall in tiers, each rosette a shallow trap for warm air, each leaf grooved to funnel condensate into its own axil where it is absorbed before it can become a drop. Barrel-forms in the sunny track, ribbed like accordions, swelling and shrinking with the season. Curtains of a jointed, leafless, chlorophyll-green stem-plant that hang from the megablocks in ropes forty feet long and rattle when disturbed. On every surface not otherwise claimed: resurrection ferns, crisped to grey fists in the dry months, unclenching to green in the humid ones, and a felt of moss with hairs so fine they harvest vapor directly out of the air the way a fleece harvests fog.

Nothing here has thin leaves. Nothing wilts. The whole community is leathery and slow and immensely, immensely old — the wall rosettes put out one leaf a year, and some of the big ones have three hundred leaves.

Under all of it, the true engine: mycelium and root-mat, a spongy floor a meter deep in places, warm to the touch from below.

### The Town

Around thirteen hundred people live in the Well, and they live in it the way barnacles live on a hull.

Because there is no water, there is no clay-working, no puddling, no mortar, no fired brick, no pot. Because there is no wood worth the name — the fig is sacred and everything else is succulent — there is no timber frame. So the town is dry-stacked stone and glue.

The stone is easy: the collapse left them a quarry of pre-broken blocks, and the walls of the Well are a soft tufa-like calcrete you can shape with a chert adze. Houses are cut into the wall and then extended outward on corbelled stone balconies, so that the whole northeast and east curve of the Well is a vertical town six and seven levels deep, rooms behind rooms, stairs threading between, each floor's roof being the next floor's terrace, doorways so close that a woman on one balcony can hand a bundle to her neighbor without standing up. Density is not a choice; the Well is the only habitable volume in a thousand square kilometers and every cubic meter of it has been argued over for two hundred generations.

The glue is the town's genius. There is a carton-building termite in the Well whose nests are made of a chewed, resin-bound board, light and hard and waterproof — a pointless virtue here, but there it is. The people farm the termites in log-galleries and harvest the board in sheets, and they trim it and lap it and pin it with cactus spine. Roofs, shutters, boxes, drums, boat-shaped cradles, the funnels of the vapor-catchers. To this add: the resin of the fig, cooked in stone bowls over ember-pits to make a black cement; the silk of a colonial caterpillar, spun into cable; and the outer rind of the big barrel-plants, which dries into something between cork and horn.

Tools are chert, obsidian traded up from somewhere with volcanoes, bone, and tooth. There are perhaps forty pieces of metal in the Well, all of them copper, all of them inherited, none of them made here — you cannot smelt without water any more than you can throw a pot, and nobody has been able to spare the sixty liters it would take. Metal is jewelry and memory.

Cooking is dry: roasting, ash-burial, ember-grilling, sun-track drying. Nobody in the Well has ever eaten soup. Washing is with sand, with the crushed saponin-rich pulp of a wall rosette, with fine ash, with the leaves of a scouring plant. To bathe is a nonsense word; the closest term means *to be sanded.*

Drinking is done by cutting. Every household maintains its own stand of storage-succulents on its terrace, planted by grandparents, opened by grandchildren. A tap is a slot cut in a stem, plugged with a carved bung, and it yields a cool, bitter, faintly slimy sap that is ninety-six percent water and is never, ever, allowed to fall on the ground.

### The Fauna, Which Is the Weather

There are more animals in the Well than there is room for animals.

Start with the noise. Three species of cicada hold the canopy, and they run in shifts, so that the roar is continuous from the third hour until deep in the night — you learn to shout, and the Well's language has a low, carrying, chest-driven register for exactly this, all long vowels and no sibilants, because sibilants die in the cicada band. Under the cicadas: the tick and rasp of beetles in the litter, so dense that the floor sounds like fat frying. Above them: swiftlets, thousands, nesting on the north wall in a chattering white-noise colony that goes quiet twice a day for reasons nobody has explained. And in the deep flutes of the wall — the old solution pipes, going back into the rock — bats. Two hundred thousand of them, minimum, in a colony that has been counted only by weight of guano.

The guano is the town's fortune. It is the only fertility this place imports. The bats fly out over the plateau each night and come back with the bodies of moths from four hundred kilometers away, and they turn that into the black crumbly soil in which the terrace gardens grow, and the harvesting of the flute-floors is the Well's most prestigious, most dangerous, and best-paid work. Guano-men go in with resin lamps and leather hoods and come out grey with mites.

Then the ants, who are the real government.

Twice a month, on no schedule anyone has cracked, an army-ant bivouac somewhere in the wall dissolves and a raid front comes through the town — a moving carpet two meters wide, unstoppable, flowing up walls and through rooms and over the terraces, eating every cockroach, scorpion, cricket, spider, larva, and unattended morsel in its path. The town does not fight this. The town *evacuates,* cheerfully, with a practiced clatter of shutters and the lifting of infants into hanging cradles, and stands aside for two hours while its houses are cleaned better than any human could clean them. Ant-day is a half-holiday. The birds know it too: a hundred antbirds and gleaners come down out of the fig and work the front, snatching what flees, and the raid moves in its own weather system of wingbeats and shrieking, and behind it the floor is bright and bare and smells faintly of formic acid.

And besides these: geckos on every wall, so numerous they are furniture. Whip-scorpions in the roof-thatch, tolerated, useful. Fig-wasps in clouds. A blue-black roach the length of a thumb that is farmed in bins for protein and is the town's staple meat. A single lineage of small silent grey monkeys, high in the fig raft, that steal and are not hunted, because of an agreement. Moth clouds at lamp-hour so thick that a lit doorway becomes a solid grey column.

### Dynamics

Nothing in the Well is still, and none of it is fast.

The vapor pulses — a diurnal cycle, heavier at night when the plateau above cools and the Well's air stops rising. Dawn is when the plants drink; every stoma in the Well opens between the twelfth hour and the second, and the whole pit inhales together and swells fractionally, and by morning the barrel-plants are visibly rounder.

The sun-coin crosses. The cicada shifts change over. The bats leave at dusk in a spiral column that takes forty minutes to clear the mouth, and the town goes out onto the terraces to watch it, every night, forever, and still watches it. The swiftlets come home into the bat column going the other way, and the two streams thread through each other without a single collision, which is the sort of thing that gets talked about even after two hundred generations.

Once a year the wall rosettes on the sunny side flower — a single spike four meters tall, once, and then that rosette dies, having spent everything, and its dry socket becomes a nesting hole. Once in a decade the fig does something none of the botanists here can predict, and there is a mast, and the monkeys get fat and the town gets drunk on the resin-beer they brew from the pressings, dry-fermented in carton casks.

And underneath all of it, on the timescale that nobody experiences: the gypsum breathing out, the plateau lying flat and finished, the Well slowly widening by one block per century as the walls give up their last unstable corners. Eventually the fig raft will fail, or the vapor will stop, or the walls will spall out into a shallow bowl and the whole green throat will open to the sky and the sky will kill it in a season.

Nobody in the Well pretends otherwise. There is a word for the future here that is also the word for *the last leaf on a rosette* — the one after which the flower comes.

### At Night

The lamps are resin in stone cups, and there are perhaps six hundred of them, and from the rim at midnight the Well is a bowl of dim orange points at every level, half-obscured by the moth-column and wholly drowned by sound. Above the rim: the plateau, black, hard, at minus fifteen, absolutely silent, absolutely empty, stretching four days in every direction under a sky so clear the stars do not twinkle.

You could walk within eight meters of the edge and never know. The whole world is down there, roaring, warm, and dry as bone.
