## The Pan

There is a place where the land ran out of ideas.

It is a shallow bowl about six hundred paces across, floored in clay the color of weak tea and skimmed with two fingers of water. Around it runs a lip of higher ground, nowhere more than the height of a standing person, and that lip is all that remains of a mountain range. Not a foothill of one. The whole range, folded and thrust and cooked and lifted, has been reduced by patience to this soft berm you could step over without breaking stride. Beyond it the flats continue in every direction until the heat blurs them, and there is nothing out there either — no scarp, no butte, no stubborn core. Everything that could be taken has been taken. What remains is the final settled form of a continent: flat, warm, wet, and finished.

The clay is extraordinarily fine. It has been through so many cycles of suspension and settling that it has sorted itself down to a uniform particle size, and the result feels less like mud than like the inside of a lip. Press a hand into it and the print holds for a moment, sharp-edged, and then relaxes. Come back in an hour and the ground is smooth. This is partly the water, partly the tiny burrowers, and partly something else, which we will come to.

## Light

The Pan is bright to the point of discomfort, and the brightness comes from two directions.

Above: a white sky, humid, with the sun somewhere inside it as a general glare rather than a disc. Below: the sheet water, two centimeters of it lying perfectly still over a level floor, functioning as a mirror the size of a village. Light comes down, bounces, comes up again. Nothing here has an underside. A wading bird's throat is lit as brightly as its back; the belly of a leaf is as bright as its face. At midday there are effectively no shadows, only faint doubled halos where a body blocks light from both directions at once.

The ecology has responded. Almost everything in the Pan is countershaded *upside down* — dark below, pale above — because the dangerous light comes from underneath. Fish here have dark bellies and silver spines. The grove's leaves are waxy and pale on the underside, matte and green on top, the reverse of any sensible tree.

## Water

The water breathes. Twice a day, without any sea in reach, the sheet thickens to knee depth and then withdraws to a film. It is groundwater, not tide — the water table here sits at or just above the surface and rises and falls on a slow twelve-hour swing, driven by something below that has been keeping time for a very long while. The rise is nearly invisible while it happens. You notice it only when your boots are wet to a new line.

Everything weeps. The three outcrops of harder material in the Pan — knuckles of dark glassy stuff, waist-high, worn smooth as soap — are permanently beaded with condensate that trails down them in slow threads. The air is at saturation more often than not. In the afternoon it drops below dewpoint and the entire landscape puts on a coat of moisture: every leaf, every shell, every pillar bright with fine droplets, the whole bowl flashing at once as if it had been dressed. It does not rain. It simply arrives on things.

## The Quiet

This is the first thing anyone notices, and it takes a while to understand what is being noticed.

The Pan is nearly silent. Not muffled — *cancelled*. A clap made at the center of the bowl travels perhaps four meters and then stops. Not fading: stopping, with an edge to it, like a sentence cut off. Speak, and your own voice reaches your ears and no further. Two people standing ten paces apart cannot hear each other at all, no matter how they shout, and shouting here feels obscene very quickly, the way shouting into a pillow does.

The cause is in the clay. The particles are not entirely mineral. Somewhere in the sediment budget — perhaps one grain in forty — sits a manufactured object, a little polyhedral thing far too small to see, which takes mechanical vibration and turns it into a very small amount of heat. There are enough of them, spread thinly through the top meters of mud and suspended in the water column and drifting as haze in the humid air, to eat sound. They are also, slowly, making more of themselves out of silicate and iron. This is why the clay is so uniform, why footprints heal, and why the Pan is a degree or two warmer than the flats around it.

They were built to make a quiet basin. Somebody, at some point in the enormous past, needed a place on this planet with no acoustic noise at all, because they were listening for something extremely faint — through the rock, or through the water, or through spacetime itself. The listening apparatus is still here.

## The Comb

At the north edge of the bowl stand eleven pillars in an arc, each about three meters tall, each the diameter of a thigh, made of the same dark glassy material as the outcrops — or rather, the outcrops are pieces of the same thing, fallen and worn. The pillars are not wet. Water beads and runs off them instantly; condensation refuses to form. In the doubled light they are the only things in the landscape that look properly three-dimensional, because they are the only things that cast a real shadow — a shadow that stays put, that does not weaken at noon, and that is a little too dark.

Nine of the eleven are dead. Two still work. If you stand near a working pillar and hold your hand a palm's width from the surface, you feel a fine cool pressure, like the exhalation of a sleeping animal, and the fluid in your inner ear goes briefly, harmlessly strange, so that the horizon seems to tilt and correct itself.

Nothing labels them. There are, elsewhere in the Pan, a few other remains: a low dome sunk to its shoulders in clay, its aperture silted shut; a footbridge over a channel that no longer exists, nine paces long, still perfectly level, still dry; a post of the same material driven into the berm with a shallow relief on one face so worn that only the rhythm of the marks remains, a pattern of groups — three, then five, then three. Somebody was counting something. That is all that survives of the meaning.

The structures are scattered widely and there are not many of them. This is not a ruin field. It is a landscape with a few objects left in it, the way a table is left with a few objects after the meal is cleared.

## The Grove

Vegetation in the Pan is sparse in number and immoderate in size. There are, in total, eleven woody plants.

Nine of them stand together on the only rise inside the bowl — a clay dome a meter and a half high, an island at high water. They are fig-like: thick pale trunks, buttressed, with aerial roots that drop in curtains and thicken where they touch, so that the nine trees have become one organism with nine crowns and perhaps forty legs. Beneath them is the only shade in the world, and it is crowded.

The other two grow apart, one at each end of the bowl, spindly and half-drowned and covered in epiphytes to the point of shapelessness.

Between and around all this: hair-weed. Mats of fine green filament lying on the clay under the sheet water, breathing out oxygen bubbles that collect beneath the mats and lift them into slow domes until they tip and release. This is the closest thing the Pan has to a sound — a soft irregular *tick* as bubbles break, all across the bowl, thousands per minute, each one dying within a meter of its birth so that the collective effect is not noise but a kind of textured stillness.

## The Fauna

Here is the paradox of the place. It is nearly silent, and it is nearly solid with animals.

The clay is not clay so much as a matrix of burrows. A handspan of mud contains hundreds of thumbnail-sized crustaceans, pale, blind, working. The sheet water above them is thick with fry and copepods to the point of turbidity in the shallows — you can scoop a double handful and see it move. Over the surface skate hundreds of thousands of long-legged striders, spread so evenly they look like a texture printed on the water. In the air, at any moment above the Pan, there are perhaps a million small fliers: soft-bodied, transparent-winged, and, like everything else, mute. In the grove, the branches are loaded — small climbing things with grasping tails, and roosting birds packed shoulder to shoulder along every limb, and under the canopy a permanent hanging cloud of the fliers, and among the aerial roots, several hundred pale eel-like animals that live in the wet leaf litter and can climb.

There are herons here, or something in that role: tall, slate-backed, silver-bellied, walking the flats in ones and twos. They open their beaks and nothing comes out. Their syrinx is vestigial. They are the descendants of birds that lived a hundred million years in a place where calling did nothing.

Because sound is useless, everything talks in other channels.

**Ripple.** This is the main language of the Pan. In two centimeters of perfectly still water over a perfectly level floor, a wave travels beautifully and visibly and far. The striders make patterns with their legs — rings, doublets, chevrons, trains of eight — and the patterns cross the bowl and are read at the far side. Larger animals drum a single foot. The herons tap. There is a constant low traffic of interference patterns crossing the sheet, and if you learn to see it you will realize the entire surface of the Pan is a conversation, and that when the sheet goes suddenly and completely flat for a few seconds, something has passed overhead and everyone has stopped talking at once.

**Light.** After dark, and the dark is total, the grove and the flats come alive with signaling — cold green, cold blue, in the fliers and the eels and the mats of hair-weed, which pulse in slow sweeps when disturbed. A predator moving through the shallows at night draws a wake of light behind it and is thereby betrayed, which is presumably the point.

**Scent.** Heavy in the warm, wet, unmoving air. Layered. It does not disperse; it stacks. Walking across the Pan you pass through discrete rooms of smell — a corridor of something like crushed pepper and iron, then twenty paces of nothing, then a wall of sweetness so thick it is almost a taste. These are messages, and you are walking through the middle of them.

Predation is constant and almost entirely soundless, which makes it look like choreography. A heron strikes; the water takes the shock and eats it; there is a splash you can see and not hear. A cloud of fliers folds itself sideways around a swooping thing in absolute quiet. The overall impression is of a landscape running at full biological intensity behind glass.

## Rules

A few things hold here reliably enough to be called laws:

1. **Sound does not travel more than about four meters.** Learn to signal with your hands, or with your feet in the water.
2. **The water finds its level.** Dam it, dig it, wall it — within a minute it is flat again, by mechanisms that are not entirely hydrostatic.
3. **The ground closes.** Any wound in the clay smaller than a body is healed by morning.
4. **Nothing casts a shadow except the old material.** If you see a shadow in the Pan, you are looking at something built.
5. **Do not sleep touching a working pillar.** People who have report the same dream: an enormous slow rhythm, three then five then three, and the certainty of having almost heard a word.

---

## What It Is Doing

The Pan is not dying and it is not waiting. It is finished, which is a different condition — the stable end state of a landscape, reached and held. Nothing here will get any lower. The clay is as fine as clay gets. The mountains are gone and are not coming back. The animals have filled the place to capacity and hold it there, generation over generation, with almost no variation.

Only two pillars still run, and their exhalation is fractionally weaker every century. When the last one stops, the little machines in the mud will keep multiplying for a while out of habit, and the Pan will stay quiet for another few million years — a warm, bright, crowded, soundless bowl full of animals talking in rings on the water, holding a silence for a listener that finished listening a very long time ago and did not say what it heard.
