## The Pan

You come down into it rather than onto it. The land tilts so gently that for an hour you think you are still on the plain, and then you notice the horizon has closed a little at your back, and the air has thickened, and the sound has arrived — not begun, arrived, the way weather arrives, from ahead and below and everywhere.

The basin is perhaps thirty kilometres across at its widest and nowhere more than sixty metres deep. It holds no lake. It holds heat, and vapour, and noise.

## Ground

The floor is a pale crust — sinter, mostly: silica and carbonate laid down out of solution over a span of time it is not useful to imagine. It has the colour of old teeth, of unglazed porcelain, of wet paper drying. Under sun it goes almost white and hurts to look at. In the shade of the low terraces it turns the grey-blue of skimmed milk, and where iron has come up out of the underlying basalt it bleeds long ochre tongues downhill, fifty metres, a hundred, fanning out and thinning like something spilled from a tipped drum.

Everything is rounded. This is the first thing your hands learn. There are no fresh edges anywhere in the Pan — no fracture faces, no knapped surfaces, no rubble that has not already been worn smooth by the constant fine spitting of vapour and grit. Ridges have the softness of soap left too long in the dish. Terrace lips curl over and hang. The whole basin has the look of something finished — not ruined, finished, the way a riverstone is finished. There is nothing here that is still becoming.

Underfoot the crust rings. Thinly, in places, over cavities — you learn to walk where it thuds rather than where it chimes. In the thin places you can put your ear down (you will not, because of the heat, but you could) and hear the water working beneath, in its channels, doing the same thing it has done all day and all year.

## The Throats

Scattered across the floor, spaced out generously with a great deal of white nothing between them, are the vents. There are hundreds. They are why you have to shout.

They are not chimneys. Millennia of dissolution and deposition have shaped them into forms that would be shameless if anyone had made them: swollen bulbs with narrow apertures, paired horns, long recumbent tubes that run half a kilometre under the crust and open sideways into a bowl. Some are barely a hand's width and scream. Some are three metres across and produce a sound so low that you feel it in the soft parts of your face and not in your ears at all. There is one, on the northeast side, that opens under an overhang into a chamber the size of a barn, and it makes a noise exactly like a very large flywheel with a bad bearing, and it has made that noise, without pause or variation, for longer than there have been ears in the basin to receive it.

Together they produce an industrial soundscape with no industry in it. A layered, sustained, mechanical roar: the hiss of pressure escaping through slots, the thudding of water slugs travelling up flooded tubes and slamming into constrictions, a rhythmic double-beat from the paired horns that is unnervingly like a compressor cycling, a high continuous shriek from a group of small vents in the south that could be a bandsaw held to metal forever. Beneath it all a bass drone you stop hearing and start wearing.

And nothing is built. That must be said plainly, because the mind will keep trying to find the plant, the works, the pipe run, the shift change. There is no metal in the Pan. There is no wall, no track, no post, no cairn, no bone tool, no scratch that anyone put there. The basin is untouched in the strictest sense and it sounds like the inside of a foundry.

## Why it does not change

Here is the rule that governs the place, and it is a rule about erosion rather than about sound.

The vents began as ragged holes and shrieked in ragged ways. But a resonating aperture erodes fastest where the flow is turbulent, and turbulence is what a badly shaped opening produces. So each throat wore itself, over uncounted centuries, toward the geometry that flowed most smoothly — and the smoothest flow is the steadiest tone. The Pan tuned itself the way a stream straightens itself. It reached its shapes and then, because those shapes no longer wear, it stopped.

That is the equilibrium. The system arrived. Deposition and dissolution balance in every throat; the water budget balances; the heat from below arrives at a rate the crust has learned to pass. A survey done today and a survey done four thousand years ago would differ by less than the measurement error. The Pan is not stable in the sense of being quiet. It is stable in the sense of being loud in exactly the same way, forever.

## Water

Damp, never wet. There is no standing water you could swim in and no river you could follow out.

What there is: steam, everywhere, in low ragged banners that lean whichever way the basin's own convection sends them and never quite reach the rim; condensation, thick, on the undersides of every overhang, gathering into slow drips that have bored small perfect wells in the crust below; seeps, warm as bathwater, running twenty or thirty metres in shallow braided channels before the ground drinks them; and, three or four times a year, actual rain, which arrives on the floor as a smell and a mist and is inaudible.

The water is a closed circuit. It goes down through the crust, gets warm, comes up through the throats, falls out of the air, goes down again. Nothing leaves. There is no outlet from the Pan. That is why the salts have built to such thickness on the western flats — pale efflorescent crusts, crisp as frost, that crunch and then powder, warm to the touch, faintly bitter on a wet finger.

## Nulls

Standing waves. Where the sound from several throats meets at the right spacing, there are pockets of destructive interference — dead zones. The Pan has perhaps forty of them, and they are fixed, because nothing here moves.

You find one by walking. The roar thins; then something drops out of it, then something else, and you take three more steps and you are standing in a bubble two or three metres wide where you can hear your own breath and the click of your knee. Step out and the wall of noise closes over you again. The nulls have been in the same places long enough that the crust inside them is different — no vapour-spit polishing, so the surface is faintly rougher, faintly darker, and dust collects. From above they would look like a scattering of pale grey coins on white.

## What lives here

No plants. Not a stem, not a lichen, not a moss on the coolest north-facing lip. The chemistry is wrong and the water never sits long enough.

There are films. In the warm seeps, colourless-to-faintly-pink mats, thin as varnish, that you would take for mineral staining until you scrape one and it comes away in a sheet. They live on the sulphur and iron the water brings up. They are the whole primary production of the basin and they are almost invisible.

Above them, sparsely: midges. Grey, small, hatching from the seep films, and mute — they have no need for sound and no ability to make any that would carry. They swarm in the dead calm above the channels and are eaten.

Eaten by the only animal you are likely to see: a pale, long-toed, flat-bodied thing about the length of a hand, close enough to a lizard that you will call it one. It lives in the nulls. It has to — its hearing is the whole of its predator-detection and its courtship, and outside a null it is functionally deaf and stupid. So each null holds one, or a pair, or a small quarrelling family, and they make the only intentional sound in the Pan: a series of dry clicks, precisely at the frequencies the null preserves, audible for the two metres they need and no further. They shuttle out into the noise to hunt midges and come back looking exhausted.

Rare, high, twice a season, birds cross the basin without descending.

## Light and night

Ordinary sun, slightly hazed by the steam, so that shadows have soft edges and the horizon dissolves at both ends of the day into a white nothing. Midday glare off the sinter is severe. Late light turns the iron tongues to rust-orange and the salt flats to a pale rose, and the steam banners go golden and look, for about twenty minutes, like smoke from something enormous burning just out of sight.

Then the light goes and the sound does not. This is the part that visitors report. You lie down in a null with a roar four metres away in every direction, under an ordinary sky with ordinary stars, in air at blood heat and full of water, and you understand that when you leave tomorrow it will continue, and that it continued last night, and that the particular grinding rhythm of the north-east throat which has now got inside your pulse was already exactly that rhythm when the first thing with ears wandered down over the rim and stood there, and will be exactly that rhythm afterward.

The Pan is not waiting for anything. It has already arrived. It is simply running.
