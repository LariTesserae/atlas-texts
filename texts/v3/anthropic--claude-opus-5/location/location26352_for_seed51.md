## The Fen That Finished Thinking

**I. The valley**

It is a floor more than a valley — a shallow, immense trough perhaps thirty kilometers across at the waist, walled by hills so worn they read as suggestion rather than boundary. Nothing here has an edge. Every slope has been rained on so long that it has arrived at its final angle and stopped arguing. The terraces of some enormous vanished river step down in five broad treads, each tread now a separate world of standing water, and the risers between them are green shoulders of moss over gravel, seeping continuously, so that the whole staircase weeps quietly into itself from top to bottom.

You come down into it and the air thickens. Not fog exactly — fog is a weather event, and this is a condition. Moisture at the threshold of visibility, beading on the hairs of your arm within a minute, so that you are wearing the valley before you have walked a hundred paces.

**II. Ground and water**

The water table is roughly two centimeters below your notion of the ground. Step and the print fills. Stand still and you sink to the ankle with a sound like a slow swallow. There is no dry land in the strict sense; there are degrees of buoyancy. Sedge tussocks ride the peat like moored boats. Sphagnum lawns, forty centimeters thick and the color of old brass and raw liver, hold water the way a lung holds air — press with your palm and the water rises around your wrist, lift and it withdraws.

Between the lawns run sloughs of black, absolutely still water, and in the stillness the sky is reproduced with such fidelity that the sloughs look like tears in the ground with clouds behind them. Ancient oxbows have gone stagnant and become gardens. Drowned trunks — a forest that died standing, so long ago that the wood has turned to gray felt — rise in ranks across the middle terraces, still upright, still spaced as they grew, holding up nothing.

The whole basin breathes. Twice in a period roughly equal to a day, without moon and without any sea within a thousand kilometers, the water rises about eleven centimeters and falls again. On the inhale you hear the peat creak. On the exhale the sloughs run backward for an hour, a slow reversal of every current in the valley, and the mats of duckweed reassemble in the patterns they had that morning — not similar patterns. The same ones.

**III. The cold stones**

Scattered across the terraces, perhaps one every two hundred meters, are boulders that do not belong to any rock in the surrounding hills. They are smooth, unfractured, gray-green, roughly the size of a resting cow, and they are cold. Not cool — *cold*, painfully so, several degrees below the air, permanently, in all seasons. Air touching them gives up its water instantly, so each stone wears a skin of dew that runs off in threads and keeps a small fern-crowded pool at its base. They are the reason the valley is cool. There are enough of them, distributed with a regularity too even for glaciers and too irregular for masonry, that they hold the entire basin about six degrees below what the latitude and the season would otherwise permit.

They are not built. This is important, and it is the first thing the place teaches you: nothing here has a seam, a joint, a fastener, a tool mark, a right angle, or a face that was ever cut. There is no ruin, no fallen wall, no glass, no wire, no dust of a burned town. The valley is undeveloped in the way a mushroom is undeveloped.

And yet something is running.

Break a cold stone — you can't, but suppose — and you would find no mechanism, only stone, all the way down, until you reached scales where "stone" and "mechanism" are the same claim. The engineering here is in the grain of the mud. It is in the ordering of clay platelets across ten thousand hectares. Whatever civilization or organism or intention once occupied this place did not build; it *specified*, downward, into the material, and then it finished, and now the specification is simply what the ground is. Cold stones draw heat and disorder out of the valley and pay for a computation that has no housing, no console, and no visible output. The valley is a substrate that has been thinking so long that its thinking has weathered into a habit, and the habit is gentle.

**IV. Sound**

It is loud. This surprises everyone. A place this soft, this drowned, this unpeopled, should be a hush; instead it is a continuous roar with structure inside it.

The base is water: ten thousand seeps, a hundred small falls off the terrace risers, sheet-drainage through sedge that hisses like a held breath, and rain, which arrives four or five times between one inhale and the next in short heavy pulses that hammer the sloughs to static.

Above that is the ground-tone. A low chord, somewhere near the bottom of hearing, that you feel in the sternum and the back teeth. It rises out of the peat everywhere at once and it has never stopped. Where the cold stones cluster, the chord acquires overtones, and standing between three of them is like standing inside a struck bell that is still ringing from a strike a very long time ago.

And then, at intervals of no fixed period, the valley performs the **Reprise**. Everything that happened in the last minute happens again, in sound only, at greater volume: your own footfalls, the rain-pulse that already passed, a heron's call, the swallow of the mud around your ankle, your cough — all of it played back over the top of the present, twelve or fifteen decibels up, and slightly wrong. Wrong in the way a memory is wrong. Rhythms cleaned up. One or two sounds included that you did not make and did not hear. The Reprise does not repeat itself; each one has been worn smooth by however many million iterations, so it arrives less as an event than as weather. The plants are adapted to it. Certain mosses release spores only on the second Reprise after rain.

**V. Light**

Dim, always. Overcast is the standing condition, a low white-gray lid, but that alone doesn't account for it. Light seems to be *spent* here. It comes down through the vapor and does not come back up; the sloughs are mirrors from above and voids from beside; the undersides of leaves are darker than they should be. Shadows lag. Move your hand quickly in front of a pale stone and the shadow catches up about a third of a second later, then settles, embarrassed.

What light there is, the valley makes itself, at the bottom. Root mats under the sphagnum glow a very faint blue-green, enough to see the shape of a hummock from below if you press your face into the moss. In the deepest sloughs there is a downward-going glow, dim points at what should be an impossible depth for a fen: the water goes down farther than the ground does.

**VI. Plants**

Lush, layered, near-touching, and cool-adapted. Alder and willow carr on the drier tussock rises, tangled to the point of impassability, every branch furred with liverwort and hung with pale beard-lichen that drips. Beneath: horsetails at chest height in colonies of thousands, dark green, jointed, faintly whistling in wind. Beneath that: ferns in six or seven kinds, and then the moss floor, and then the interior of the moss floor, which is a world of its own — a saturated brass forest at the millimeter scale, dense past counting.

Some of the vegetation participates in the strangeness. There are stands of sedge that grow in perfect concentric rings around nothing at all, rings that migrate a few meters a year, always maintaining their centers on points where the ground-tone is quietest. There are willows whose leaves, on a still morning, all turn to face the same direction, which is not the direction of the light. And there is the widespread and unremarkable-looking marsh plant with the divided leaf, of which you will find, if you look carefully in a stand of a hundred, that eleven or twelve are not merely similar but identical — the same plant, the same insect damage on the same leaflet, the same asymmetry, standing four meters apart. Nobody made copies. The valley simply has a limited number of ways to be a plant in that spot, and it does not mind reusing one.

**VII. Fauna**

Rare. This is the cost.

The valley is a poor place to be an animal because animals require an unbroken thread of self, and here the thread is repeatedly picked up in the wrong place. Most vertebrates that wander down from the hills leave within a day, or don't, and are not seen again in a way that can be described.

The ones that persist are the ones whose lives already loop. The eel in the third-terrace sloughs is a single eel, encountered perhaps twice a week, always the same size, always the same notch in the dorsal fin, in sloughs kilometers apart within the same hour. The gray herons come in twos: the same bird at two ages, hunting the same shallow, ignoring each other with elaborate care. There are frogs, whose entire lives are a repeated call, and who thrive. There is an insect ecology of great richness and small variety — midges in columns, water beetles, one kind of dragonfly the color of wet slate, which lays eggs into its own reflection and does not appear to suffer for it. There are no large mammals. There are old, softened trails through the alder carr, exactly the width of something large, kept open by continuous use, and no user.

**VIII. Rules**

They are simple, worn, and stable. They are not hostile. They have been in balance for so long that they operate more like erosion than like magic.

*Distance is approximately conserved but not exactly.* Cross the second terrace west to east, then east to west, and the return is shorter by five or ten percent. Do it enough times and the valley will simply have moved you a little east of where you insist you are. Nothing tears. It's a slow drift, like silt.

*Nothing is made here.* Whatever you set down — a knife, a coin, a folded note — will be found again, later, elsewhere, unmade: the metal returned to grains in the clay, the paper to fiber in the moss, with the *shape* of the object retained as an arrangement of those grains, faithful to the micron, and utterly useless. The valley does not destroy artifacts. It translates them into the only medium it uses.

*Sound is the only thing here that goes forward in time reliably.* Everything else — light, shadow, current, your own recent history — is subject to the Reprise, the tide, the drift. This is why the place is loud. The loudness is the one clock that works.

**IX. Toward evening**

There is no sunset, only a dimming, and with the dimming the vapor comes down out of the air onto everything as a hard, heavy dew, so that for an hour the whole valley is audibly *ticking* — every leaf, every dead trunk, every cold stone shedding drops into water. The root-glow strengthens. The ground-tone drops a fourth and stays there. The heron and the heron stop hunting and stand in the same posture facing opposite directions.

And then the last Reprise of the day comes, and it plays back the whole valley's evening at once — the ticking, the chord, the frogs, the rain that fell at the far end where you never went — and inside it, if you have been here long enough to have made enough noise to be worth including, you will hear your own breathing, cleaned up, corrected, held a little steadier than you have ever managed to hold it, and repeated on afterward for a few seconds after you have stopped.

It is not a warning and it is not an invitation. The valley finished thinking a long time ago. What's left is the enormous, well-worn, waterlogged, gently glowing habit of having thought — running down its terraces, condensing on its cold stones, being loud in a soft place, and taking whatever arrives and slowly, patiently, without malice, filing it into the mud.
