## The Precipitate City

### I. Arrival is not possible, but it happens

You come up out of cloud and the cloud does not end so much as thin into something you can stand in. The air is hot. That is the first wrongness, and it is not the largest one. At this height the air should be thin and cold enough to crack your lips; instead it is dense, saturated, blood-warm, and it presses on you like a hand on the back of the neck. You are somewhere above five thousand metres — the light says so, hard and white and falling almost without angle, the sky above the vapour a blue so deep it verges on the ultraviolet edge of what an eye is built for — and yet the stone under your palms is as warm as a living animal's flank, and steam comes off it in slow ropes.

The second thing you notice is that you cannot hear yourself notice it.

### II. The roar

There is a sound here and it is the only sound. It is not a noise you listen to; it is a condition of the medium, the way pressure is a condition of water. Somewhere over the rim, hundreds of falls go off the plateau at once — thick ones, ropy ones, gauzy ones that never reach a bottom — and the plateau itself is hollow, riddled, a stone sponge kilometres deep, so that every collapsing column of water rings the whole massif like a struck bell that is never allowed to stop. Add the boil of the source basins, the hiss of vents, the endless drip-drip-drip of ten million weeping ceilings, all of it multiplied and phase-shifted through galleries and flues and fused colonnades until it arrives at you as one flat white unvarying immensity of sound.

It does not rise or fall. It has no events in it. You will search it for a rhythm and find none, and after a while you will stop searching, and after that you will stop being able to hear it, and this is worse: it becomes the shape of your silence. You will speak and produce nothing. You will clap your hands together and feel the impact travel up your arms with no report at the end of it. Your own heartbeat is gone. There is no way, here, to confirm by ear that you are alive.

This is why there are no animals. Not one. No bird, no insect, no worm turning in the wet moss. Anything that navigated by sound, courted by sound, fled by sound, was simply never able to begin here — and anything that tried to evolve deafness found that it also had to evolve indifference to a world that was screaming, and indifference of that order is not compatible with hunger. So the plateau is a place where the vegetable kingdom got everything, and kept it, and stopped.

### III. What made the streets

The city was not built. This takes some time to accept, because it is a city — there is no honest other word. There are streets that run straight for a kilometre and then turn a considered corner. There are stepped plazas, tier on tier, thirty deep, each one holding a still basin of water the colour of a peacock's throat. There are colonnades where the columns are spaced with the regularity of counting. There are aqueducts on arcades three storeys high, carrying hot water across a gorge and delivering it, precisely, to a fountain-head that overflows a rimmed lip into the next district down. There are walls with courses in them. There are what can only be called doorways, and what can only be called roofs, and in one quarter a spiral ramp descends nine turns into a lit shaft and comes out into a hall the size of a cathedral whose ceiling is held up by fused pillars of stone drapery, and there is not a chisel-mark in the entire world.

It is travertine. All of it. The plateau is fed from beneath by hot mineral water under pressure, and hot mineral water carrying calcium in solution does two things when it meets air: it lets go of its gas, and it lets go of its stone. It builds. Given a lip, it builds a rim; given a rim, it builds a dam; given a dam, it builds a pool, and the pool overflows and builds the next one down, and the overflow finds the fastest line and cuts a channel and armours the channel's sides, and the armoured channel is an aqueduct, and where the aqueduct's spray hangs in a gallery it deposits pillars, and where the pillars crowd they fuse into walls.

Water, working at sufficient scale for sufficient time, produces order that is indistinguishable from intention. That is the whole rule. Everything here that looks designed is the residue of a preference — water's preference for the shortest steep line, for the existing lip, for the already-wet surface. There is no architect. There has never been anyone to be one. The infrastructure is dense because the water is dense; the districts are laid out on a logic of gradient and dissolved load; the city is not a thing standing on the landscape but a thing the landscape has been doing, continuously, for longer than you have a number for.

The stone is warm, porous, cream and rust and rose, and it rings hollow underfoot in the way of a hive.

### IV. The equilibrium

And it is finished.

This is the part that will not sit down in the mind. A travertine terrace should grow — a millimetre a year, a centimetre, sometimes shockingly faster. These are not growing. They are also not decaying. Deposition and dissolution here are exactly matched, everywhere, always, to a precision that no natural system should be able to hold: every gram the water lays down on the rim of a basin, the water takes back somewhere on that basin's flank. Nothing accretes. Nothing slumps. The rounded shoulders of the stone say weathered — a hundred thousand years weathered, ancient, settled into its own state, all the sharpness long since gone — and yet if you could return in a hundred thousand more you would find every rim at the same height and every column the same girth and the same nine turns in the same spiral ramp.

The city is being made and unmade at identical speed. It is simultaneously brand new and beyond old. There is no direction to time in it, and therefore, functionally, no time. Nothing here is *before* anything else.

You notice that the pools do not ripple, though water pours into them. You notice that the steam leaves the vents in the same slow ropes, in the same coils, at the same three places on the same coil — that the vapour is not flowing but standing, a fixed sculpture of a flow. There are rainbows in the spray of the great fall at the eastern rim, and they do not move when you move. They are in the world, not in your eye. They have been in that exact place, at that exact chord and radius, since before the concept of an eye.

### V. What grows

Lush, and mineral, and mad.

Moss in cushions a metre deep, hot to the touch, so dense you sink to the knee, and the lower half of every cushion is already stone — the water has been through it and left the moss's own architecture behind in calcite, so the plateau is upholstered in living green over a substrate of its own petrified past. Fern fronds three metres long, hanging from the aqueduct arcades, black-green, glossy with permanent condensation, each frond tipped with a bead of water that never falls because the moment it swells enough to fall it deposits a ring of stone around its own neck and holds itself on.

Trees, if you allow the word: thick trunks that are wood on one side and flowstone on the other, half-encased where the spray reaches them, branching only in the dry lee. They lean out over the terraces and their leaves are enormous, thin, translucent, and utterly still. Algal mats paint the shallow basins in bands — sulphur yellow at the scalding source, orange, then that impossible peacock blue in the merely hot water, then a green so dark it reads as ink where the pools are only warm.

Nothing pollinates. There is nothing to pollinate with. So nothing flowers, and nothing seeds, and everything reproduces by breaking: a frond snaps, floats down the channel, lodges on a rim, roots, and becomes an identical frond. Every plant on the plateau is a fragment of a plant on the plateau. There are perhaps forty living things here, in the sense of forty distinct lineages, repeated ten billion times. The lushness is a copy of a copy of a copy, layered near-touching, one clone shading another clone, saturated to the point of near-solidity — and there is nothing here that any two of them could ever become.

### VI. Where it stops holding together

Walk the streets and they compose. Three blocks north, three blocks south, and you are back at the fountain-head with the peacock basin. Fine. Now do it in the other order: three south, three north. You will arrive at a fountain-head with a peacock basin, and it will be the same one, and you will be at a different altitude, and there is no ramp or step in the intervening distance that could account for it. The city is consistent locally and refuses to be consistent globally. It is a street repeated at many scales and joined to itself along the seams, and the seams are not visible, and you will walk them without noticing except that the light changes register and the pools are suddenly a size that means something different about your body.

Go to the rim. There is cloud below, a floor of it, and the falls go into it and never make an impact sound — nothing in the roar corresponds to a bottom. And in the gaps of that cloud, far down, you will see a rim, a terrace, a stepped plaza thirty deep, and it is right side up, and it is at your altitude. You can sight across to it dead level. It is this place. Height here is not a quantity; it is a *state the place is in*, and the place is in it everywhere, so the plateau has no summit and no base, only edge, indefinitely, in the direction called down.

Some of the falls go up. Not many — four, that can be seen from the eastern quarter — and they behave exactly as the others do, with the same braided ropes and the same fixed rainbows, and they deposit terraces on the undersides of overhangs where terraces cannot be, and those terraces are also in equilibrium, and also ancient, and also new.

The mist holds shapes. Standing shapes, in the colonnade at the head of the spiral ramp: seven of them, vapour, hip-height at the shoulder, in a rough ring, not moving. They are not ghosts of anything, because nothing with a shape has ever lived here to be ghosted. They are the same seven shapes in the same ring as the rainbows are the same rainbows. They are a feature of the water. They have the standing-shape of things that would listen, if there were anything to listen to, if listening were possible.

And here is the last rule, the one you will find out for yourself: everything that falls here is enshrined. Drop a leaf in a basin and within the hour it wears a white glove of calcite; the plateau records, instantly and permanently, every event that occurs on it. But you leave no print in the deep hot moss. You displace no water. You cast a shadow, and the shadow does not deposit its cool edge on the stone the way the fixed shadows of the columns have deposited theirs. The city is not recording you. In a place where nothing changes and everything is written down, you are the only thing happening, and the only thing that will not be kept.
