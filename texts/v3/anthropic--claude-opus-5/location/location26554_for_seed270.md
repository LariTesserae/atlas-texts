## The Sump Room

It is three and a half meters across, corner to corner, and it is a whole world, and it has been finished for a long time.

To reach it you would have to be smaller than you are. There is no door left that opens — there is a door, but it was welded at the seams and then the seam was painted and then the paint aged into a mineral, so that the door is now more or less part of the wall's geology. There is a hatch in the ceiling with a wired-glass pane, cracked in a long slow diagonal, and this is where the light comes in: ordinary light, daylight of the kind that falls on everything, dusty and unremarkable, angling down across the room in a soft trapezoid that moves through the day the way it always has. There is also a lamp. We'll come to the lamp.

The floor is the important thing. It is flat — nearly perfectly flat, and flat in a way that no one designed. Once it was a checker plate, raised diamonds for grip, and you can still find the pattern in two places: under the lip of the tank cradle, and in the northwest corner behind the conduit, where nothing walks. Everywhere else the diamonds have been taken down to shadows, then to a faint tooth, then to nothing. Not by water. There is no water here and there never has been, not one drop, not a film of condensation — the room is cool but bone-dry, the air stripped so long ago of any moisture that dryness is no longer an event, just the medium, the way the sea is a medium.

It was worn flat by feet. Uncountable feet, uncountably often, for a length of time that has no useful name. This is the room's central fact: erosion here is a biological process. The plate was ground away by traffic, and the grinding produced a flour, and the flour is the ground you would stand on if you could stand here — six or seven centimeters of it, grey-brown, fine as talc but faintly crunchy, packed hard in the walking lanes and loose in the drifts along the baseboards. It is not soil. Nothing grows. It is one part steel oxide and nine parts shed body: molt after molt after molt, husks powdered by the passage of the next generation, an accumulation of former selves.

There is no plant in this room. There has never been a plant in this room. And yet the room is *loud*.

---

The sound is layered, and once you learn it you can read the room by ear alone.

Bottom layer: a hum, sixty cycles or something near it, coming up through the floor from a long way off — from whatever remains awake in the building above, some winding somewhere still turning over in its sleep. It is not a pleasant hum. It is a hum with a burr in it, the sound of a bearing that has been failing for centuries without ever quite failing.

Middle layer: ticking. Millions of ticks. Every hard surface in the room is crusted with animals, and most of them stridulate — a scrape of file against file, one small percussive syllable per animal per few seconds, and there are so many animals that the ticks blur into a dry sizzle, like fat in a pan, like rain if rain were made of pebbles. It rises and falls in slow waves that cross the room in seconds. The waves are how they keep track of each other in the dark corners.

Top layer: the shrieks. Small, sudden, structural — the sound of a chitin plate being sprung off a joint by something with the leverage to do it. These come at a rate of maybe forty a minute, everywhere, unremarkably.

---

The lamp is a filament bulb in a caged fixture at head height on the east wall, and it has never gone out. It runs on leakage: on the stray potential in a bus bar behind the plaster, a current that goes nowhere and does nothing and has gone nowhere and done nothing since the room was closed. The lamp is dim — a deep orange, a sullen coal — and it brightens and dims very slightly on a rhythm of about forty-one hours.

That rhythm is the room's tide.

Every forty-one hours a relay somewhere far above closes on a schedule no one set, and the leakage current triples for about eleven minutes. The lamp comes up from coal to a warm yellow. And the room *moves*. This is the great daily event, the migration, the thing all the biology is arranged around.

Because in the absence of plants, there is one primary producer here and it is not alive. It is the corroding contact where the bus bar's insulation split, low on the east wall, a green-white bloom of oxide the size of a hand. Energy enters this world as electrical potential and metal chemistry, and the animals eat it directly.

The grazers are flat, palm-of-a-child-sized, armored in overlapping plates the color of old nickel, with soft undersides they press to the metal. They have no mouths worth the name. They have a pad, and in the pad a chemistry, and they lie on the live surface and take the current across their bellies and use it to pry oxygen off iron. That is the whole economy. Everything else in the room is a consequence of it.

They lie in a crust four and five deep on the bloom and for a meter around it, near-touching, jostling, working their files. Position is everything. A millimeter closer to the split in the insulation is a measurably better life. The strong hold the center; the young and the failing get the margins where the potential falls off, and a grazer at the margin is a grazer running down, slowly, on a gradient of millivolts that is also a gradient of mortality. When the tide comes, the margins surge inward. The crust boils. For eleven minutes there is enough for everyone and the whole east wall roars — a sound like applause heard through a floor — and then the relay opens and the lamp sinks back to coal and the hierarchy re-sorts itself with a great deal of ticking and several thousand small shrieks.

The predators do not go to the wall. They wait on the walking lanes, in the flour, where the flour is packed hardest, because packed flour means traffic means dinner. They are longer than the grazers and thinner and much faster, pale, almost translucent, with two forelimbs made for the single task of getting a plate off a joint. They hunt by the pause in the sizzle — a silence moving across the crust is a grazer that has stopped filing, and a grazer that has stopped filing is either dead or about to be worth killing. There are also things that eat the predators, smaller than the predators, that ride them, and things that eat the flour itself for the metal still in it, and something in the corner behind the conduit that nobody in this room has any business with — a slow, plated, dish-shaped animal that has not moved in nine years and is not dead, and around which the flour has drifted into a smooth dune, and which will move again eventually, and when it does the room will reorganize for a week.

Density here is the ordinary condition. Every horizontal surface is occupied. Every vertical surface is occupied. The undersides of the pipes are occupied, hanging, three layers thick. There are animals in the seams of the welded door. There are animals inside the dead bodies of animals. To have a volume in this room is to have neighbors touching you on most sides, and the culture of the place — if a sizzle can be a culture — is entirely about the negotiation of contact.

---

The built things are few and they are all foreign.

A cradle of angle iron in the middle of the floor, bolted down, that once held a tank. The tank is gone; whoever sealed the room took it. Four bolt-stubs, sheared, and the paint-shadow of a cylinder.

A ceramic insulator, brown, glazed, unbroken, lying on its side where it rolled. It is the largest single object in the world and it is a landmark: everything on that side of the room is described in relation to it. Its glaze is too slick for grazer pads. Nothing lives on it. It shines, mildly, in the trapezoid of daylight for two hours a day, and it is the only clean thing here.

A steel plate screwed to the wall by the door, with a number stamped into it. The number has been eroded by traffic — animals climb the wall by the plate's edge, which is the only handhold — into an unreadable smoothness with a suggestion of a *7*.

A conduit, a grating, a dead switch.

And low on the north wall, where a person must have crouched a long time to do it, forty-one scratches in the paint, in eight groups of five and one of one. They mean nothing to anything now alive in the room. But the packed lane that runs past them is one of the busiest in the world, because the scratches give purchase, and so the room's whole northern population moves daily along a tally that someone kept, once, of something.

---

The rules, as far as they go:

Nothing here needs water and nothing here would know what to do with it. If a drop fell, it would be a catastrophe and a mystery, and it would kill whatever it touched.

Nothing here grows. Growth is molting: you throw off a self and the self becomes ground. The floor is deep because the world is old.

The land is flat because the animals flattened it. Given enough time, feet do what rivers do.

Energy comes from a wall, on a schedule, from a machine no one attends, in a building nobody enters. The world is fed by an oversight.

It is cool. It is bright enough. It is finished — there is nothing left in this room that can be worn down further, no relief to reduce, no last diamond of checker plate that isn't already accounted for. The room has arrived at its final shape and now simply maintains it, loudly, at enormous population, forever, or until the relay stops closing.

Which will happen. And then the lamp will go to coal, and then to nothing, and the crust will come off the east wall, and the flour will stop being made, and the room will be exactly as flat as it is now and finally quiet — a sealed grey plain three and a half meters across with a brown ceramic insulator lying on it, shining for two hours a day.
