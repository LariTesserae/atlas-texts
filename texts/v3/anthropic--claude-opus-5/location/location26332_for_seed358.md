## The Bell of Kettra, and the works beneath it

You come to it downward, which is the only way. The land above is a young limestone plateau, warm and half-drowned, gullied by rains that have not yet decided where they want to go — no valleys yet, just fields of shallow bruises in the rock where the water gathers and thinks. Then one of those bruises opens. A shaft. A throat forty metres across, its lip fringed with a single ragged crown of ferns, and beyond the ferns nothing but wet black and a sound coming up at you like heat off a stove.

The sound is the first fact of the place and the last. Everything else is arranged around it.

---

A river falls into the Bell. Not a waterfall in the picturesque sense — no lace, no veil. The plateau's rainfall collects in a shallow sink and simply drops, a solid grey column with a twist in it, one hundred and sixty metres, and lands on rock. That is the crucial thing: on rock. There is no plunge pool. The cave is too new. The water has not yet had time to dig itself a soft bed to land in, and so it strikes bare unmodified stone at terminal velocity and shatters, and the shattering fills a chamber the size of a market town.

This is the first rule here, and the workers state it as a proverb: **young water is loud water.** Erosion is a long apprenticeship in quiet. Given ten thousand years the column will hollow a basin, the basin will fill, and the river will land on its own accumulated softness and speak in a lower voice. Given a hundred thousand it will have worn the fall into a staircase and will chuckle down it almost pleasantly. None of that has happened. The Bell is an infant and screams like one, ceaselessly, at a pitch that lives in the sternum. Conversation is conducted by hand within four hundred metres of the fall. Beyond that, in the outer galleries, you can shout and be heard, and the shouting sounds to you like whispering, and for two days after you leave the plateau the silence up there will feel like an illness.

---

The chamber itself is not yet a room. It is a room being roughed out. Everything in it is angular. The breakdown blocks on the floor — some of them the size of chapels — have edges you could cut a hand on, and they lie where they fell, unsorted, unrounded, no rubble packed between them, so that crossing the floor is a matter of straddling and bridging and dropping into slots. The walls are scalloped in the fresh, sharp way of rock that has been dissolved rather than scraped, and the scallops are large, which means the water that made them was fast. Overhead, the ceiling is a low dome of pale grey stone still weeping the last of itself away, and it is studded — not hung — with stalactites no longer than a thumb. Nubs. Buds. Ten thousand of them, each with its bead of water at the tip, each bead falling and being replaced. The great columns and organ-drapes that make older caves feel like cathedrals have not been invented here. There is nothing to admire. There is only the raw armature of a place, standing exposed like the wet frame of a ship.

And yet nothing is empty. This is the strange thing about the Bell: it is unfinished and completely full. There is no spare space anywhere in it. Fill the gaps between the breakdown blocks with warm standing water, and thicken the air until it is water too — the humidity is total, the temperature holds at a mild sweaty twenty-six all year, and the atmosphere condenses on you as soon as you enter, so that within a minute you are as wet as the walls and cannot tell your own sweat from the cave's. Every surface is beaded. Every crack seeps. The floor water is never still; it shudders in sympathy with the fall. Lay a plank across two blocks and it will hum.

---

Now put the animals in.

Above, in the dome, live the swiftlets — perhaps four million of them, nobody has counted properly since the survey of the old regime — and their nests are cemented to the ceiling at a density of one every hand's breadth, an unbroken mosaic of black cups over ten hectares of stone. They navigate the dark by clicking. Four million birds clicking is a texture, not a sound: a fine dry crackle laid over the roar like salt on wet ground. They pour out at dusk in a rope that takes fifty minutes to leave the shaft and fifty to come back, and while it passes the shaft is not a hole in the ground but a solid moving thing.

Below them, on the roof of the second gallery where the air is warmer and staler, the bats. Below the bats, on the guano slopes, everything that eats guano and everything that eats the eaters: beetle carpets you can hear over the water if you kneel; crickets with legs like hair; harvestmen in colonies so dense they look like a single trembling fur; cave crabs, pale and thumb-sized, in the seeps; blind catfish standing in the shallow warm water with their whiskers spread like the fingers of a man reading. The guano hills are alive in the literal sense — put a lamp near one and the whole surface flows.

Vegetation gets almost nothing. Light gets almost nowhere. At the base of the shaft there is a green ring perhaps thirty metres wide where the falling column carries daylight down with it in a diffuse smear: mosses, a few dozen ferns, a liverwort skin over the wet stone, one improbable strangler seedling trying and failing every year. Past that, dimness, and then the dark. The only other green in the whole system grows in rough haloes around the works lamps, and it is green because the lamps are there — soft algal fur, moss no thicker than felt, arranged in perfect circles like the light's own shadow. The workers call them the lamp gardens and are sentimental about them, and when a lamp is moved the garden dies, and a new one begins in the new place at the rate of a few centimetres a year, which is the fastest botany the Bell has ever seen.

---

The works are small and stubborn and they are everywhere in the way that scattered things are everywhere.

Because the Bell's water is warm and clean and constant, someone in the last century decided to sell it. There is a pump house on the second bench above the flood line — brick, tin-roofed, absurd, a building put inside a cave — with two vertical engines in it whose noise is a total gift to the place, since nothing they do can be heard over the fall. Their exhaust goes up a flue pipe stitched to the wall for six hundred metres. From the pump house, iron pipes run out along the galleries on trestles, sweating, lagged in rotting felt, and along the trestles run catwalks of bolted grating, and along the catwalks run cables strung on porcelain insulators, and every ninety metres a bulb in a wire cage, burning at maybe a quarter of the brightness it should, because the cable is long and the dynamo is old and the damp steals current wherever it can. That is the light of the interior: a chain of small orange suns, each one making a sphere of visibility about eight metres wide, and between them, dark. You walk from world to world.

Elsewhere: a narrow-gauge track for the guano trams, laid on sleepers that have never dried; a winch drum, seized; a tin shrine at the foot of the ladder with a candle stub in it that no one ever lights, because the air will not hold a flame steady; a bathhouse with no roof, since roofs here are ornamental; a bunkhouse on stilts; a chalkboard by the pump house with the water level marked in a column of dates going back eighty-one years, in eleven different hands.

That chalkboard is the second rule of the place. **Read the board and you will see almost nothing happening.** The level drops about a centimetre a decade. That is the whole plot: the water table is retreating, slowly, and as it retreats it hands new chambers up out of the flood into the air, one every few generations. The galleries the workers use today were sumps when their great-grandparents came. There are men here who have spent forty years in the Bell and have seen the floor of one small side-passage break the surface — a metre of new world — and they regard it as the great event of their working lives, and describe it to visitors, and are boring about it.

Nothing here is level, nothing here is finished, nothing here is smooth. The rock is a week old geologically speaking and it behaves like it. In the long run the fall will quiet, the basin will deepen, the drapes will grow, the shaft will widen and collapse and become an ordinary sunlit valley with a stream in it and a road, and the four million birds will have to be somewhere else. In the meantime you stand on wet grating in a warm orange bubble of light, with the whole ceiling ticking above you, four hundred metres from a noise that will not stop, and you understand that you are inside a landscape at the exact moment before it becomes scenery.
