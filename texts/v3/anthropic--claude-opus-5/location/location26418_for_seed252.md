## The Vug

Two kilometers under the floor of an ocean, in rock that is three weeks old.

It is a room, and only a room. Nothing else. Call it four paces long, two wide, its ceiling low enough that a person standing in it — an impossible person, a person made of some other physics — would have to stoop. It has no door. It has instead a throat at one end, a fracture two fingers wide that narrows upward and westward for four hundred meters before it becomes too tight for anything but the fluid, and at the other end a hairline seam that leaks. That is the whole of its geography. Everything that happens here happens within the volume of a small pantry.

The room exists because a dike came through. A blade of magma, seven meters thick, forced itself up a crack in the crust and stopped a hundred and eighty meters short of anywhere interesting, and cooled, and in cooling contracted, and in contracting tore a lens-shaped void out of the country rock at its shoulder. That was twenty-six days ago. Nothing here has had time to become smooth.

---

**The light.** The dike's flank is the room's floor and part of one wall, and it is still incandescent — not lava, not liquid anything, but solid basalt at a little over eight hundred degrees, and solid basalt at eight hundred degrees glows. Deep orange, the color of a stove element, but far larger and closer than any stove element: a glowing floor. The light comes up out of it and hits the ceiling, which is older, cooler, darker rock — gabbro, coarse-grained, four hundred degrees, radiating mostly in wavelengths no eye can use — and the ceiling throws some of the orange back down, and the walls throw it sideways, and the room fills.

It is bright. Brighter than a lit room at night, brighter than anything within a thousand meters in any direction. And it is bright in a particular way that only enclosed hot places are bright: **there are no shadows.** Every surface is both illuminated and a source. Put an object in here and it does not cast a shadow; within a minute it comes to the same temperature as its surroundings, and once it does, it becomes invisible — glowing exactly as brightly and in exactly the same color as everything behind it. The room is a furnace's interior, and the interior of a furnace is the one place where perfect light and perfect blindness are the same condition. The only things visible here are things that are cooling, or things that are heating. Contrast is a synonym for change.

Right now there is a great deal of contrast, because the room is young and the floor is losing four degrees a day.

---

**The water.** The room is full of water, and you would not recognize it.

At this depth the pressure is five hundred atmospheres, and at five hundred atmospheres and eight hundred degrees, water has no interest in being either liquid or steam. It is supercritical: a single phase, thin as a gas, dense enough to dissolve rock, with no surface tension and therefore no droplets, no bubbles, no meniscus, no wetness in the sense the word usually carries. It does not run down the walls. It does not pool. It cannot form a drop, because a drop requires a skin and there is no skin to be had. It simply occupies, the way air occupies, and it flexes and streams and shears, and where a cooler thread of it meets a hotter one you can see the boundary as a shimmer, a schlieren, a moving wrinkle in the orange.

It is also the most aggressive solvent in the region. Supercritical water strips silica out of basalt the way hot tea strips color out of a leaf. It carries dissolved quartz, iron, sulfur, sodium chloride, carbon dioxide from far below. Its internal structure — the way the hydrogen bonds flicker in and out at these conditions, forming and dissolving networks on scales of picoseconds and nanometers — is not something anyone has satisfactorily described. It is not mysterious. It is only complicated in a way that outruns the available mathematics: an ordinary substance doing ordinary things at a rate and a scale where the equations go soft.

---

**Sparse.** The room is mostly empty. This matters. It is not a jeweled grotto; it is a bare void with a few occupants, widely spaced, and the space between them is the dominant fact.

On the cooler wall, seven quartz crystals. Just seven, the largest the size of a thumb joint, each grown in the last nineteen days from silica the fluid dropped when it touched cold stone. They are perfectly sharp. Not a facet on them is chipped or rounded, because there is nothing here to chip or round them — no sand, no wind, no rolling water, no grit. Erosion has not been invented yet in this room. Their tips are the sharpest objects for a kilometer in any direction.

Near the leaking seam, a bloom of anhydrite: white blades in a rosette the size of a coin, precipitated out of the seawater sulfate because anhydrite is the rare mineral that becomes *less* soluble as things get hotter. It grew in a single afternoon. It will dissolve again, entirely, when the room drops below one hundred and fifty degrees, and it will leave nothing behind, not a scar.

In the floor's glowing surface, six vesicles — old gas bubbles frozen in the basalt, now open to the room, each a small hemispherical cave a few millimeters across, each one a tiny furnace within the furnace.

Along the base of the far wall, a dark green-black seam where olivine crystals in the gabbro have met the water and begun to change. This is the busiest place in the room, and it looks like almost nothing: a discoloration.

That is the entire inventory. Seven crystals, one rosette, six pits, a stain. Four paces by two.

---

**The machinery.** Here is what the stain is doing.

Olivine plus water becomes serpentine, magnetite, and hydrogen gas. The reaction releases heat, which the room does not need but takes. It also expands: the new minerals occupy more volume than the old ones, up to forty percent more, and since the rock cannot expand, the rock cracks. The cracks admit more water. The water reaches more olivine. The reaction propagates itself along the fractures it manufactures. It is a process that builds its own plumbing, fuels its own continuation, and generates, as a byproduct, free hydrogen — the most reducing, most chemically willing substance available.

And on the fresh surfaces the cracking exposes: iron and nickel sulfides, precipitating out as films a few tens of nanometers thick, riddled with pores of roughly the size and shape of a cell. These films are semiconducting. They hold a voltage across themselves, because the fluid on one side is alkaline and the fluid on the other is not. They catalyze, weakly and stupidly and continuously, the reaction of that hydrogen with dissolved carbon dioxide, producing formate, acetate, small carbon chains — the first two rungs of a ladder that has, elsewhere, in other rooms like this one, been climbed all the way.

There is no builder. There is no design. There is a mineral membrane maintaining a proton gradient and doing carbon chemistry across it at nanometer scale in a rock, and it is happening because the thermodynamics of a wet hot planet make it nearly unavoidable. The most sophisticated engineering in the room has no engineer, and the room contains no artifact, no structure, no trace of intention, nothing made. It is untouched in every sense the word supports. It is also, at the scale of tens of nanometers, dense with function.

Nothing here is alive. Not quite. Not yet. The distinction may not be as sharp as the quartz.

---

**Rhythms.** The room is not still. There are, at minimum, five clocks running.

Twice a day the tide of the ocean overhead loads and unloads five hundred meters of water onto the crust, and the crust flexes by a fraction of a hair, and the room's throat opens and closes by that fraction, and the fluid pulses — in, out, in, out — a respiration with a period of six hours and twelve minutes. On the inhale, cooler fluid arrives from up the fracture and the room's temperature drops a degree or two, and silica comes out of solution onto the seven crystals, and each crystal gains a layer four molecules thick. On the exhale the room reheats and some of that layer redissolves. The crystals grow in bands, a striped record, two stripes a day. They are keeping time and nobody will ever read them.

Slower: the floor cools. Four degrees a day, decelerating. In about eighteen months it will fall below red heat and the room will go dark — not dim, *dark*, absolutely, permanently — and everything visible in it now will become nothing but hot rock in the black. The bright period is a brief accident of the room's youth.

Slower still: the serpentine seam expands and, sometime in the next few years, will close the throat, or crack a new one, or both. Then the room will seal, and its fluid will go static, and its chemistry will run down to equilibrium and stop. The room is temporary. It is a bubble in the middle of a two-hundred-million-year process, and it has the lifespan of a dog.

And erratically, unpredictably: the seam at the far end lets go. A thermal crack propagates thirty centimeters, and the pressure differential drives a jet of fluid out into colder rock, and the room drains and refills in about ninety seconds. Afterward there is a new crack, a new white bloom of anhydrite, a slightly different circulation. This has happened four times since the room existed.

---

**Sound.** Almost none. The great sound of hot water is boiling, and supercritical fluid does not boil — no bubbles form, no bubbles collapse. The fluid moves silently. What sound there is comes from the rock: the *tick* of a cooling crystal splitting along a plane, four or five ticks an hour, sharp and small, arriving through stone rather than through the fluid, so that you would feel them in your feet before you heard them. Once every few days, a longer groan from the dike as it contracts. Once in a while, from very far off and very deep, the low pressure of the earth moving somewhere else entirely, arriving as a pressure rather than a noise, the way a distant door slams in a large house.

Between these, silence of a quality found almost nowhere on the planet's surface: no wind, no water noise, no voice, no leaf.

---

**Fauna.** None here. Nothing survives eight hundred degrees; nothing survives four hundred.

But three hundred and ten meters up the throat, where the fluid has cooled and mixed with seawater and settled to a hundred and twelve degrees, the hydrogen and the acetate from this room arrive as food, and are eaten. There is a film there, a few cells thick, black-brown, methanogens and sulfate reducers, the whole population of it perhaps the mass of a grain of rice, spread over four square meters of crack wall. And in that film — occasionally, not always, perhaps for a few decades every few centuries when the plumbing happens to connect — a nematode. Half a millimeter long, blind, transparent, grazing. An animal, a real one, with muscles and a gut and a nervous system, in a crack in the ocean crust, at the absolute edge of where animals can be.

It has no idea about the bright room below it. It only tastes the hydrogen on the current, arriving twice a day with the tide.

---

**Rules.** In this place: light and blindness are the same. Water cannot form a drop. Nothing is old, nothing is worn, and every edge is original. There are no shadows and therefore no directions. Nothing was made, and yet something is working. The brightest era of the room is also its briefest. And the most important thing in it is a stain on a wall the size of a hand, quietly cracking its own rock open so it can keep going.
