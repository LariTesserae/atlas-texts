## The Held Room

There is a mountain in the world that is eleven weeks old.

It came up through a plate the way a knuckle comes up through a fist — fast, and with a certain rudeness — and it is still coming, still stacking itself, still adding a hand's width of altitude every night. Its summit stands somewhere above eight thousand metres and has never been surveyed, because by the time a figure is confirmed it is wrong. Its flanks are glass-veined and black, sharp as a struck flake, unweathered except at the very top where the wind has begun to polish a few edges into dullness. Nothing lives on it. Nothing has had time.

Inside it, roughly two-thirds of the way up, there is a room.

---

The room is a bubble — an original one, blown in the rock while the rock was still deciding to be rock, then found by a bore-crew and taken. It is not large. Four hundred paces across the long axis, maybe ninety high at the crown. You could walk its perimeter in the time it takes to lose an argument. And yet the first thing anyone says on entering is that it is *empty*, because the town at its centre occupies so little of it: a cluster of pale terraced structures in the middle of a black floor, with two hundred paces of nothing on every side. From the town's edge the walls are a suggestion. The crown of the ceiling is a rumour of smoothness overhead, sensed as a change in the way sound doesn't come back.

The ceiling is smooth because it is being held. This is the central fact of the place.

Six thousand metres of new mountain wants to close this cavity, and would, in about forty minutes, if not for the stint-frames. There are eleven of them, cast into the rock at intervals around the chamber, and they are not struts. They occupy the place where a strut would be with something else — a standing refusal, a re-routing of the rock's own compressive urge back into itself, so that the mountain squeezes against its own squeezing and the bubble persists. Where a frame does its work the air has a faint scorched quality, a not-quite-light like the after-image of a filament. Look directly at one and there is a grey armature and a plinth of sintered aggregate. Look slightly away and there is a column of dark brilliance going up through the ceiling and, presumably, out.

The frames grind the ceiling flat as a side-effect. Fine stone flour drifts down constantly, so slowly it seems to hang. It settles on everything. It is swept.

---

The rock is hot. Not glowing at this height, but hot enough that a bare palm on the chamber wall is a decision you make once. Down through the floor, deeper, the heat is worse; that is where the mountain is being made, and it is being made upward, so the floor of the room is rising, slowly, and cooking as it comes.

The town's answer to this is to climb. Every eight days or so the settlement adds a level and abandons its lowest one. The result is architecture like a core sample: seven storeys of occupied town standing on nineteen storeys of sealed, emptied, gently roasting town beneath. If you lift a floor-plate on the lowest inhabited street you can look down a stairwell at a street from two months ago — bollards still in place, doorways, a painted number on a wall, everything intact and everything the dull deep orange of an iron in the fire. The stairwell breathes upward, dry and enormous. It has a smell like a kiln and a struck match. People do not linger there, but they do look. It is one of the only views the room offers.

Materials, upward from the dead levels: cast stone the colour of old bone, poured in place; sheets of a grey ceramic that stays cool no matter what it is touching; struts of drawn glass, spun out of the mountain itself in the first weeks and now amber with strain. Everything is built to be jacked. Everything can be unbolted and lifted. There are no foundations anywhere in this town, and everyone who lives here has the particular calm of people who have stopped expecting the ground to be a promise.

---

Water arrives as breath.

Twice a day, on a rhythm nobody set, the porous rock of the eastern wall exhales. You can hear it coming: a low grinding somewhere far below, then a long soft push of vapour into the chamber, and the whole room goes visibly humid, the not-quite-light of the frames suddenly beam-like in the mist. The vapour crosses the empty floor and finds the sink-plates — banks of that cool grey ceramic standing in ranks on the western side, angled, ribbed, the only structures out there in the void. The plates are the town's heat exhaust: everything the settlement cannot bear to keep is pumped into them and thrown up and out of the mountain, which is why the summit steams and why, from a hundred kilometres away, this peak looks like it is smoking.

The plates' faces run cold. The mountain's breath hits them and condenses and runs down the ribs into grooves, and the grooves into channels, and the channels into three cisterns under the town. This is the entire hydrology of the world. Two exhalations a day, four hundred litres, give or take.

Between exhalations the room is dry enough to crack lips, and the sound of the place is water: individual drops on ceramic, on stone, in cistern-water, spaced out so widely you can attend to each one. That is the loudest thing here for hours at a stretch. That, and the tick. The rock ticks as it grows — small, dry, arrhythmic reports from the walls and floor, the sound of a new world settling its shoulders. Residents stop hearing it within a month and cannot sleep anywhere else afterwards.

---

Light: the frames' scorch, which is grey and gives no colour to anything. The orange coming up the sealed stairwells. Lamps, kept low, because the sink-plates can only carry away so much and light is heat. And once a day, for about eleven minutes, the Vent.

The Vent is a bore left over from the original crew — a shaft going up and out at a slant. When the sun crosses it, a coin of white daylight drops through the crown of the ceiling and lands on the chamber floor, and travels, and thins, and goes. It is savagely bright. Nothing else in this room is white. People walk out from the town, sometimes, to stand in the moving coin. There's no ritual to it. It's just the only place in the world where you can see the actual colour of your own hand.

---

There is nothing alive here. But there is something on the eastern wall, in the fan of rock where the breath comes through, and it is the closest thing.

Call it sinter fur. Pale grey-green, densely branched, growing in mats and rosettes wherever vapour passes regularly, thickening a millimetre a week. It is a crystal habit — a mineral doing what minerals do in supersaturated conditions — and it is not alive by any test anyone has run. But it grows toward the flow. Block a vent-pore with a plate and within nine days the fur around it has curved to reach past the plate. Nobody has explained this and nobody working here is a biologist, so it stands in the logbook under *behaviours*, which is a word doing a lot of work.

And there are the flicks. Something small and fast at the corner of vision, near the cisterns, always to the side, never in the coin of daylight. The standing theory is that they are artefacts — the frames doing something to the way light arrives at a moving eye. The other theory is not written down. Nine people live in the room. All nine have seen flicks. None has ever caught one, and none has ever seen two at once.

---

The rules of the place, as posted at the town's edge in cast letters:

*Do not raise your voice in the chamber.* The frames answer sound; a shout returns as a pressure that can be felt in the teeth, and a sustained one has, once, put a hairline through the ceiling. Everyone here speaks at a level that visitors mistake for grief.

*Water is measured at the plate, not at the tap.*

*Do not open a sealed level.*

*If the ticking stops, walk to the Vent.* No further instruction is given, because there isn't one.

---

The room is active. It is not a still place, only a quiet one. The mountain rises, the floor cooks, the town climbs, the ceiling is ground down and the flour falls, the wall breathes twice, the plates weep, the cisterns fill, the summit smokes, the coin of daylight crosses and goes, the fur reaches, the flicks flick, and every eight days a section of street is unbolted and lifted a storey and welded down, and the street below it is sealed and left to glow.

Nine people. Four hundred paces of dark floor. Eleven refusals holding open a bubble in something that is still arriving.

And above all of it, unseen from inside, a black glass peak going up into thin air at a hand's width a night, steaming, brand new, with no name yet and no plant on it and no animal on it and no rain that stays — carrying, somewhere in the middle of its chest, one lit room where the water is the mountain's own exhalation and the light is measured out in minutes.
