## Cell Nine of the Freshworks — "the Weeping Cell"

The first fact of the place is the noise, and the second fact is that the noise never changes.

It arrives through the soles of your boots before it reaches your ears: a flat, enormous, four-note roar with no top and no bottom to it, no rise, no lull, no breath. Standing in it is like standing in a solid. Within a quarter hour your ears surrender and reclassify it as silence, and from then on you hear only your own pulse and the small dry clicks of your jaw, and the room seems to have become terribly quiet — which is when new tenders panic, and have to be held.

The cell is small. Eleven paces across at the floor grating, roughly hexagonal, though the corners have been softened by growth into a shape more like the inside of a molar. It rises. How far, nobody says with confidence: the lamps give out at about the height of four men, and above that there is only a dim throat of vapour and the wheeling of the swifts. The whole world here is this one room and the ladder-lock beneath it. There is no outside inside the cell. There is a mountain out there, presumably — the air is thin enough that you climb the ladder slowly and your lips split — but the mountain is hearsay.

### The walls

There is no natural surface in the cell. There is no natural surface anywhere in the Freshworks. Everything was made, including the stone.

Forty iron nozzles, brass-collared, are set into the wall-ribs at staggered heights, and out of each of them comes a fine cold calcareous fog called the milk. It is pumped up from the sumps by the Constant below and it hisses out at a pressure that has not varied in living memory, because the governor has been pinned. The milk settles on the wall and begins to become travertine. It does not finish.

That is the thing about this room. Put your hand flat against the wall — the tenders will let you, once — and it gives very slightly, like the skin on scalded milk, like the moment in a cooling crucible before the surface hardens. The stone here is permanently one second old. It has the pearl-bone colour of new sinter, faintly blue in the lamp-shadow, translucent to about a finger's depth, and if you lean close you can see the laminae beginning — hair-fine, hopeful, aborted. Nothing in the cell is weathered. Nothing has an edge that has been rounded by anything. There are no cracks, no stains, no scars, no old parts. Raw geology, held at its instant of birth, forever, in a room the size of a chapel.

It is held there by mouths.

### The grazers

The walls are furred. From knee-height to the limit of the light, and above it, on every surface the milk touches, the rasping millers are packed shoulder to shoulder at something like nine thousand to the square metre — pale flightless moth-things the size of a thumbnail, wing-cases fused into a single blunt shield, legs short, mouthparts a rotary file of chitin. They have no eyes worth the name. Their blood is a syrup that will not freeze. They graze.

Each miller takes off, per hour, a layer of new-formed mineral almost exactly as thick as the layer the milk lays down. Deposition and grazing cancel. This is not a coincidence; this is the design, or possibly the discovery — the doctrine on that point is unclear and nobody argues, because arguing takes shouting and shouting is impossible. The result is the cell's central miracle: a landscape under construction that will never be constructed, an emerging stone that emerges and emerges and never arrives.

Where they rasp, they strike sparks. Not fire — a cold triboluminescent glitter, blue-green, a thousand pinpricks a second across the whole standing crowd, so that the walls seem to be very faintly raining upward. In the dark stretches between lamps this is the only light, and it is enough to walk by, if you already know the floor.

Over the miller-carpet walk the combers: long, blind, six-legged, knee-high, with legs like calipers, treading on the living fur as though it were pavement, which for their purposes it is. They comb. They eat millers. They eat exactly enough millers. When a comber dies — this happens; things do die here — it does not fall, because the carpet holds it, and it is dismantled and taken into the wall-mouths within the hour, and there is already another comber. There is always the same number of combers. Eleven.

### The hang

The noise does something to the water.

The cell weeps constantly. Condensate runs from every rib, every nozzle-collar, every bolt-head, and the air is at ninety-something percent and just above freezing, so your breath goes out as a fat white ghost and your eyebrows bead. But the mist in the middle of the room does not drift. The four-note roar sets up a standing lattice in the shaft, and the droplets collect at its nodes and stay there — a three-dimensional grid of hanging beads, about a hand's width apart, filling the centre of the cell from the grating to the invisible ceiling.

You can put your arm through it. It feels like walking through a beaded curtain that reassembles behind you.

Each bead is a pond. Inside them live the hangers: transparent, comma-shaped, less than a millimetre, filter-feeding on whatever the milk carries, luminous when disturbed, so that your arm passing through the lattice leaves a fading green wake in the air like a scratch on glass. They are born, breed and die without ever touching a surface. There are perhaps forty million of them. Their whole cosmology is one droplet wide.

And through the lattice fly the swifts — the vent-swifts, sooty, sickle-winged, a column of two or three hundred of them turning endlessly in the updraught at the room's centre, mouths open, harvesting beads. They do not land. There is nowhere for them to land that is not already occupied by millers, and the millers would strip them. They sleep on the wing in shifts, one hemisphere at a time. They arrived, the tenders say, and could not get out, and this was long enough ago that "long ago" has stopped meaning anything.

Nothing leaves the cell. That is the first rule and all the other rules are commentary on it.

### The vegetable exception

Almost nothing grows here that is not an animal. But there are three green things and they are noted in the book.

A palm-sized patch of moss beneath Lamp Four, cropped by millers to a perfectly even nap, the way a lawn is cropped by sheep; it has been that exact size and that exact nap for as long as there has been a book. A pale ribbon-weed in the warm outflow where the sump water returns, four fingers long, waving in the flow, never five fingers long. And a grey lichen that grows only on old grease, on the Constant's crank-cheek, and is therefore wiped away every shift by the tender's rag and is therefore always exactly as thick as one shift's growth.

That is the vegetation of Cell Nine. It is enough that visitors stare at it.

### The machinery, and the tender

The Constant is a beam engine, or was one before the accretions. Cast iron, riveted, greased, geared down to the pumps, running at a speed that is not a speed so much as a fact. Its bearings are checked by thumb, since no gauge can be read over the vibration. Its governor is pinned. There is a story that a man once unpinned it, and the noise changed by a semitone for four seconds, and in those four seconds the droplet lattice collapsed, forty million hangers hit the floor, the swifts struck the walls, the millers stopped, and the walls began — for four seconds — to set. They say you can find the place. You cannot. There are no old surfaces here.

There is one tender per shift and the shift is not measured in hours. She wears felt and lead-cloth, and a wire cage over her head against the swifts, and she is deaf, which is not a disqualification but a stage of the career. She speaks by knuckle: two taps on your collarbone, a slow drag on the sternum, a grip on the wrist. She keeps the tally book in pencil, because ink weeps. The book records eleven combers, forty nozzles, the moss at seventy-one millimetres, the weed at four fingers, the swift column "full", the millers "even". Page after page after page, in a dozen different hands, all of them saying the same thing.

That is the point of the book. Not to record change. To prove, page by page, that there has been none.

### Leaving

You go out through the double lock, and the second door shuts, and the roar drops to a hum, and you find that the hum is inside your own head and will be there for three days. Your coat is wet through and stiffening in the cold. Your ears ring in the four notes. Behind you, the cell continues at precisely the rate at which it has always continued: the milk hissing out, the millers taking it back, the beads hanging in their grid, the swifts turning, the eleven combers walking on a floor of mouths, the pencil moving, the wall one second old.

Somewhere in the Freshworks there are other cells. The numbering implies at least sixty. No tender of Nine has seen one, and Nine is not curious, and this is not incuriosity but doctrine: that a thing which never changes cannot be understood better by looking at it twice.
