## The Valley That Runs on Cold

At three thousand seven hundred metres the air has no body to it, and light arrives unsoftened, and yet nothing here is bare. From the pass you look down into a trough thirty kilometres long, four wide at its waist, glacier-cut and then abandoned by its glacier a very long time ago — and the trough is full to the brim with green. Not forest. There are no trees, nothing that could be called a trunk. What fills the valley is a single continuous body of plant matter, perhaps eleven metres deep at the centre, thinning to a crust at the walls, and it has the shape of the valley the way water has the shape of a bowl.

The colours are wrong for green, mostly. Slate-green, oxide-green, the grey-blue of old kettles. Cushion growth on the surface, tight as a scalp, mosses in domes and pavements, lichen crusts in concentric maps. Where the sun holds an aspect for a few hours, the felt goes brassy. In hollows it goes almost black, and those hollows are warm to the hand — three, four degrees above the air, which is generous, since the air spends most of the year just below freezing.

Rock appears in four places only: the two cliff bands at the head of the valley, one landslide scar on the north wall, and the boulders in the streambed at the outlet, worn smooth and wearing caps of moss like hats. Everywhere else the stone is under the plant, and has been for so long that the plant has stopped acknowledging it.

---

**The layers.** Cut down through it (you can, with a knife; it parts like upholstery) and you find, in order:

The *felt* — thirty to sixty centimetres of living surface, dense, water-holding, and utterly silent underfoot. It absorbs sound the way a recording booth does. You can clap and hear the clap arrive at your ears and then nothing, no return, no room. This is the first thing visitors notice and the last thing they get used to.

Below that, the *works*. Three to eight metres of pale fibrous tissue arranged in ways that are hard not to read as machinery. Bundles of hollow stem, some as thick as a wrist, twisted into cables and running in bundled parallel for hundreds of metres. Banks of bladder-cells stacked in rows, each bladder the size of a fist, each connected to its neighbour by a valve of two overlapping flaps of cuticle. Sheets of tissue folded into bellows, hundreds of pleats deep. Vertical columns of capillary matting, sopping, that lift water without any pump you could point to. It is all plant. There is no metal in it, no mineral except a scatter of silica beads in the valve seats where wear is greatest, laid down like ball bearings. Nothing was made. Everything was grown, and grown for a purpose the valley has been refining for something on the order of forty thousand years.

Below the works, the *archive*: compressed dead tissue, brown to chocolate to near-black, going down and down. At about nine metres it is frozen and stays frozen. It does not rot — too cold, too acid, too airless — so the valley cannot dispose of its own history and has instead learned to stand on it. The floor of the trough is a stratigraphy of every previous version of the machine.

And through the archive, not touching stone at any point, runs the river: meltwater from the head cirque, contained in a tube of living tissue perhaps two metres across, insulated by a metre of peat on all sides. You can hear it if you lie down and press your ear to the felt in the right places. It is the only sustained sound in the valley, and it is very far away.

---

**What the machine does, and what runs it.** It runs on cold. Specifically, it runs on the difference between day and night, which up here is enormous and reliable: eighteen degrees of swing, three hundred and forty days a year.

At night the valley freezes from the top down. Water in the outer bladder-banks becomes ice, expands, and pushes. The push travels through the valve rows, each valve opening one way only, and by dawn the valley has moved several thousand tonnes of water uphill and outward — laterally toward the drier walls, and vertically up the capillary columns to the crust six hundred metres above the floor, which has no other way of drinking.

At dawn the freeze reverses. The felt glazes, and then the glaze catches the sun, and the surface fogs. For about forty minutes after first light the entire valley exhales: a low white haze standing two metres off the ground along its whole thirty-kilometre length, drifting, thinning. This is the valley shedding the heat of the thaw. It smells like cold tea and turpentine. It is completely soundless.

By mid-morning the works are running the other way, water descending, bellows filling. This is when the surface begins to move.

---

**Turbulence.** The valley's outline is ancient and stable. It has been the same shape since before there was anyone to see it. Its contents are in continual violent revision.

Growth here is fast — not plausibly fast, *visibly* fast. A bladder-bank builds itself in a day and a half. Cable bundles reroute in a week. On any given afternoon, standing on the pass with a good eye, you will see between three and nine *slumps* in progress. A slump is what happens when a region of the works — a hectare, five hectares — releases its turgor all at once. The surface drops. Not a collapse into a hole: a settling, forty centimetres to a metre and a half, over about six seconds, with a soft dimpling of the felt like a bedsheet being pulled taut from underneath. A puff of spore-dust lifts off the edges and hangs. Then it is over, and the valley floor has a new shallow basin in it, and in eleven days the basin will have filled back level with fresh works of a slightly different design.

They happen constantly. The whole floor is pocked with the healing evidence — faint rings, faint bowls, greener patches, the way a lake looks under light rain if the rain were very slow.

Nobody watching has established a pattern. The best guess is that the machine is doing what a river does: keeping its form by continually destroying its arrangement. Every configuration is temporary and every configuration is adequate. It is testing itself to pieces at all hours and staying exactly the same size.

And it does this in near-total silence. Six seconds of a hectare falling produces a sound like someone turning over in bed in the next room. When a large bellows-bank cycles you feel it in the soles of your feet as a pressure, not a noise — a hum at the very bottom edge of hearing, and possibly below it. On a still afternoon the loudest thing in the valley is the inside of your own head.

---

**The scarcity of animals.** There is nothing here to eat that is not fibre, and fibre at this altitude is a poor bargain. So: mites, in their millions, blind and white, living in the felt, silent. A springtail. A pale slug the length of a finger that eats cable insulation and is eaten by nothing. Two species of moth that never fly in daylight because there is no wind window narrow enough.

And the walker — a grey bird the size of a small hen, long-toed, that goes about on the felt with its head down and flies perhaps twice a year, badly. It eats slugs and mite-mats and whatever the slumps turn up. It does not call. Its throat structure suggests that it used to. In a place where every sound is swallowed within four metres, a call is a wasted investment, and the valley is very old, and has had time to withdraw the investment.

---

**Rules, for anyone who goes down.**

Nothing here decays; it accumulates. Whatever you drop is not lost, it is filed. There are objects in the archive at four metres down that were dropped a hundred and fifty years ago and are in better condition than when they arrived, dyed brown, held.

Fire is not available. Everything is wet through by a millimetre below the surface. In the deep winter, when the felt freeze-dries, the risk exists; the lightning scars from those centuries are still legible from the pass as strips of paler moss, dead straight, healing at a rate of about a metre a decade.

Do not stand still. This is the only real hazard. Growth rates in the works being what they are, a boot left in place for six hours will need cutting out, and a person who lies down to sleep on the felt without a groundsheet will wake pinned at the ankles, wrists and hair by fine white rootlets that have found something novel and are exploring it. It is not predation. There is no intent in it at all. It is simply that the valley cannot leave a new thing alone, and a person is, briefly, a new thing.

There is no shelter. Nothing here has ever been built, by anyone, for any reason. The one flat place with a view down the whole trough, where you would expect to find a cairn or a wall or the black ring of an old fire — has moss on it, unmarked, sixty centimetres thick, and something moving quietly underneath.
