# The Kettle

## I. The country that surrounds it

To find it you first have to cross a mountain that has stopped being a mountain.

The massif is old past the point of drama. Whatever it was — and the rock says it was enormous, says it in the way a bent iron bar says it was straight — has been sanded down into a high tableland of red clay and rotted granite, four thousand meters up and almost flat. The peaks that remain are stubs: molars, knuckles, the last hard cores of vanished summits, standing in a wide scatter with kilometers of nothing between them. Rain has taken the rest away to some delta so far downstream that its name belongs to a different language.

Up here the sun is thin and white and hard, and the wind moves without obstruction, and the ground is warm-dry, cracked into hexagons, dusted with lichen the color of old bone. Grass in bunches, every few paces. A shrub every hundred. It is a landscape of *intervals* — you can see a hawk's shadow travel unbroken for a full minute.

And then, in a fold you would walk past nine times out of ten, the ground opens into a bowl about forty paces across, and it is full of steam and noise.

## II. The rim

The rim is a ring of upright stones, seven of them, each the height of a standing man, tilted at all angles like a jaw that has lost most of its teeth. They are not placed. They are the roots of something — the hard remnant of an intrusion the surrounding rock gave up on and left behind. Where they meet the ground there is a lip of fused red material, glassy, smooth as a bathtub, that cannot be geology and does not admit to being anything else.

Cross that lip and the temperature rises nine degrees.

The change is not gradual. There is a surface in the air, a hand's width thick, and passing through it feels like stepping into a stable in winter: sudden animal warmth, sudden wet. Your cold cheeks flush. Your breath, which was smoking, stops smoking. Behind you the plateau is still white and dry and open. In front of you the air is green-lit and heavy and going nowhere.

## III. Below the soil

Dig anywhere in the bowl — the soil is loose black loam, unnaturally rich, only about a forearm deep — and at the bottom you meet the lattice.

It is a mesh. The gaps in it are about the width of a fingertip and hexagonal, and the struts between the gaps are so fine that you cannot see them from the side, only from directly above, where they resolve into a pattern and then dissolve again if you shift your head. It is not warm to the touch, exactly. It is *at your temperature*, whatever your temperature is, immediately, and it stays there — press your palm flat and after a moment you cannot feel where your hand ends.

The mesh continues under the whole bowl and up the inner faces of the standing stones, where it has been overgrown by moss and cannot be found. Nobody has located an edge, a seam, a power source, or a purpose statement. What it *does* is not mysterious at all, and can be listed:

- It holds the air inside the bowl at thirty-one degrees, day and night, winter and summer.
- It pulls water out of the thin dry mountain sky, in quantity, continuously, and beads it on every surface that will hold a bead.
- It takes away anything that dies.

The third one is what makes people uneasy. A dead moth on the loam is gone in an hour, without ants, without decay, without a smell. A dead bird takes most of a day; the feathers go last, sinking. There is no hole, no mouth. The ground simply becomes less occupied. This is presumably where the loam comes from, and presumably why the loam is so black, and presumably why the bowl can support what it supports on rainfall that would not keep a garden alive.

The mesh is indifferent to living things. You can lie on it all night. It will not take your boot, or your knife, or your body while you are using your body.

## IV. Water

It does not rain in the Kettle. It condenses.

Everything in the bowl is beaded. Every leaf carries a rim of droplets along its underside, every stone sweats, the moss is heavy as a soaked sponge, and there is a permanent fine mist at knee height that thickens after dark into a standing white lake you can wade through. The water gathers, runs, finds the low southern corner, and drops into a plunge no wider than a barrel — a shaft in the glassy red material, ringed with black slime, down which the whole day's harvest goes with a hollow *glub, glub* and does not come out anywhere on the mountain that anyone has found.

Carry a full cup up over the rim and by the time you have gone thirty paces down the outer slope, the cup is half empty. The plateau is very dry, and takes what it is owed.

## V. What grows

Four trees. That is all, and it is enough.

They are fig-kin of a type that occurs nowhere else on the massif, with pale trunks like poured wax, buttressed, and crowns that reach just to the height of the standing stones and no higher — the warm air ends there, and so do they, sheared flat as a hedge along an invisible ceiling. Their roots run along the top of the lattice and never through it. Three are ancient and hollow and full of bats. The fourth is young, maybe eighty years, and grows in the exact center of the bowl.

Otherwise: moss, thick and every green there is, in continents with bare warm loam between them. Six or seven tree-ferns spaced like chess pieces. A single vine, enormous, one individual plant, that has spent centuries climbing all seven standing stones and now hangs between them in loops, flowering white and stinking of overripe fruit, three flowers open at any given time, always three.

The spacing is the strange part. It is lush without being crowded. There is *room*. You can see across the whole bowl. It looks less like a jungle than like a room that has been arranged.

## VI. What lives

And here is the thing the Kettle actually is: it is the only warm wet place within a hundred kilometers, and every living creature on the plateau knows it.

The noise arrives before you reach the rim. From outside it sounds like machinery — a flat, sawing roar with a ringing edge. Inside, it is unbearable in the first minutes and then becomes weather.

There is a cicada here, black with a red seam, that has locked its song to the hum of the lattice; ten thousand of them in four trees, all on the same carrier tone, and the bowl is a resonator with a glass floor. The sound stands in the air in bands. Move your head and it changes pitch. Put your hand over your ear and you hear it through the bones of your arm.

Under and through: frogs. A dozen species, thumb-sized to fist-sized, jewel-colored, most of them found nowhere else and each with a call like a different tool being used badly — a rasp, a plucked wire, a wet cork. They breed in the beaded water on the leaves. They do not need pools. They have never seen a pool.

Above: swifts, in a column that spirals out of the bowl mouth at dawn like smoke and comes back at dusk in a rushing funnel that pulls the mist down with it. Two thousand birds pouring into a forty-pace hole, and the sound of it is a long tearing sheet.

At the top of it all, one predator, and only ever one at a time: a long weasel-thing, honey-and-soot, immensely old-looking, that lives in the hollow of the eastern fig and takes bats and swifts and frogs and is never hungry and never leaves. When it dies the ground takes it, and within a season another arrives from somewhere out on the dead plateau, and the arrangement resumes.

And insects past counting. Columns of them in the shafts of light. Moths the size of a hand asleep on the wax bark, mantids like folded leaves, a species of ant that farms the moss and marches its harvest along a road it has worn into the glassy lip, and everywhere the low continuous *sizzle* of small legs on wet leaves.

Nothing here is rare. Everything here is dense to the point of absurdity, packed into a garden the size of a threshing floor, and around it, for as far as anyone can walk in three days, almost nothing at all.

## VII. The scattered marks of people

They came later. They came for the warmth, obviously, and they did not stay.

There is a cistern cut into the base of the northern stone, waist-deep, catching the drip off the vine; the tool marks in it are blunt and honest. There is a bench — three slabs, two uprights — set facing the young central tree, and the seat is worn into a shallow curve by generations of sitting. Because the lattice runs beneath it, the bench is always exactly blood-warm, and to sit down on it in the roaring green dark is a physical relief so profound that visitors have wept.

There is the knee-high footprint of a round hut against the western stone, its stones tumbled inward, moss-swallowed, with the fig's roots growing through where the door was.

There are eleven small clay figures set in a line along the glassy lip: badly made, no two alike, each one a four-legged animal. They are not old. The newest is a few years at most. Nobody has ever met the person who leaves them.

And carved into the eastern stone at chest height, in letters worn nearly to nothing, four words in a language the plateau herders can still half-read. The usual translation is: *it was already working.*

## VIII. The day

Before dawn the bowl is a white bath of standing mist and the frogs own it entirely; the sound is a rattling, plucking, wet chaos with no rhythm anywhere in it.

At first light the swifts go up, all at once, and for two minutes there is nothing but wingbeat and the mist tearing.

Mid-morning the sun clears the eastern stone and comes in at a low angle and the whole bowl steams — every leaf smoking, the moss releasing, the light standing in visible columns full of insects — and the cicadas start, and by the time the sun is high the sound has flattened into that single roaring plate you cannot talk over.

Midday is the quiet hour, relatively. Heat presses. The frogs go silent. The weasel-thing sleeps in the open on a root, flank rising, absolutely unafraid.

Late afternoon, the sky over the plateau does what it does most days at this altitude — hauls up a squall out of nothing, and it rains for eleven minutes on the dry tableland, and in the Kettle the rain arrives as a brief thickening of what is already there.

At dusk the swifts come home. The vine's three flowers open harder and the smell of fruit becomes almost solid. Moths lift off the bark in their hundreds. The bats come out of the hollow trees, and the whole air becomes a knitted traffic of small bodies, and the noise rises again to its full pitch and holds there all night, and above the flat-sheared ceiling of the four trees the sky is enormous and black and pierced and freezing cold, and only forty paces of warm loud green stands between it and the lattice, patiently sorting water out of nothing, forever, for no reason anyone has established.
