## The Shelf

The mountain is new and has not yet learned to slump. It came up as a single tilted block — you can see the tilt from anywhere on it, a two-degree lean toward the northwest that makes every floor in the city subtly wrong — and its edges are still edges. Where the rim breaks off into the fog below, the break is clean, a bevel of pale grey-green stone with a glassy conchoidal fracture, sharp enough to open a palm. There is no talus apron softening the base of anything. There is no soil in the cracks. The scree slopes on the eastern flank are made of shards that lie exactly as they fell, still bladed, still ringing when you walk them, and they have lain like that longer than anyone can account for.

Water runs on the Shelf but does not carve it. This is the first thing a visitor notices and the last thing they accept. The streams occupy channels they did not make — joints in the rock, opened when the block rose, straight as ruled lines, crossing at stubborn geometric angles — and they run clear and cold along them without deepening them by a hair's width. At the rim, the largest of these, the one the town calls the Spill, goes over in a white rope of about nine feet's thickness and falls two hundred feet into the fog, and there is no plunge pool at the bottom. There is no bottom that anyone has found. The stone beneath the fall is smooth but not hollowed. Whatever dust the water lifts, the night puts back: on cold mornings the whole plateau sweats a fine mineral film, a chalky condensation that settles into the exact places from which the day's grinding took it. You can watch it happen if you are patient and cold-tolerant. Most people are not.

The air at this height is thin and cool and smells of wet stone and crushed green. Breath shows in shadow and not in sun. It never truly freezes and it never warms past the temperature of a cellar. Wool is worn year-round, undyed, in layers, and the collective smell of the town is lanolin and woodsmoke and the resinous sap of the tall plants.

Those plants are worth walking out to see. The Shelf is lush and nearly empty at once, which sounds like a contradiction and is simply what it looks like. Vegetation here does not spread; it concentrates. Every hundred feet of bare rock pavement gives way to a single, extravagant occupant: a moss cushion four feet high and eight across, so dense and so saturated that pressing it releases a cup of water and the shape of your hand stays for a minute; a tree-fern with a trunk like braided rope and a crown you could shelter six people under; a colony of bell-stalks — pale, hollow, waist-high, faintly warm to the touch — standing in a ring of about forty with nothing else inside the ring. Between these, stone. Clean stone, lichen-freckled, tilted two degrees northwest. The overall impression is of a vast rock garden tended by someone with unlimited patience and a horror of clutter.

The city sits on the plateau's wide middle, and it is a city, not a village: eleven thousand paces from the east gate to the terrace wall, four levels of construction stepping down the tilt, and enough roofline that from the ridge above it reads as a single grey organism. It is built entirely of the mountain — the same grey-green block stone, dressed by hand, laid dry. No mortar. No arch. Doorways are corbelled: courses stepped inward from either side until they meet, giving every entrance the shape of a narrow pointed tent. Roofs are slabs, and above the slabs a second layer of thin stone combs stands on edge, rank after rank of them, like the gills of a fish. These are the dew-fins. They catch the morning fog that comes up over the rim in slow tongues, and the water runs off them into cut gutters, and the gutters into cisterns, and the cisterns feed the joint-channels that were already there. Half the city's engineering is the humble business of joining what it made to what it found.

There is no iron. There is copper, in pins and chisels and the flat lamps that burn plant oil, and the copper is old and unpitted. Everything else is stone, wood, horn, wool, fired clay, and cord. The stonework is superb — not decorative, but fitted with the sort of accuracy that comes from having nothing else to do with a century — and it is finished. That is the strange part. Nobody is building. There are no scaffolds, no quarry sledges, no fresh chippings. The masons' guild has twelve members and their work is measurement: they go around with knotted cords and check that the walls are where the walls were, and they always are, and they write nothing down because there is nothing to record.

And it is quiet. Not silent — a city cannot be silent — but hushed in a way that makes visitors speak lower than they meant to. There is no traffic and no construction, which removes nine-tenths of what a settlement usually is. What remains: water in the gutters, everywhere, constant, a soft plural trickling that becomes the baseline of your hearing within an hour and is unbearable to remember afterward. The knock of a wooden shutter. Sheep-bells, far off on the terraces, thinned by distance and altitude. Feet on stone stairs. Someone winnowing. Birds. Long gaps.

The animals are the loudest thing and the least afraid. Fauna is heavy here — the emptiness between plants and buildings is not empty at all once you sit down. Stone hares, grey and ridiculous, doze in the doorways of unoccupied houses. Quill-mice go along the gutters in files, drinking. Sill-hawks nest in the corbelled niches and stoop on the mice with a noise like a slapped sheet, and neither the mice nor the town seem to consider this an emergency. On the eastern scree there are big shaggy browsers with splayed feet, four hundred of them, that eat the moss cushions down to a certain height and no further. Grey deer keep the terrace hedges at exactly hedge height. Pale moths live in the rock joints and come out at dusk in numbers that make the air granular. Nothing here has any reason to fear a human, and nothing does, and so you walk through a city where a hawk will not move off the wall to let you pass.

Here is the rule of the place, and everything above follows from it.

Nothing on the Shelf accumulates. Individual things change — a knife dulls, a woman ages, a moss cushion is browsed and regrows, a slab cracks in a cold snap — but the totals hold. Exactly. The number of sharp knives in the city is constant. The number of cracked slabs is constant; when one splits, another, somewhere, is found to have closed. Rain falls on unpredictable days in perfectly predictable amounts, so that any ten-day span receives the same measure to within a thimble. The snowline on the ridge above sits at one altitude and has never moved. And the population of the town is four thousand one hundred.

They call it the Even. It is not a belief; it is arithmetic, and they do the arithmetic constantly, which is the town's real occupation. There is a Count — a body of nineteen, and there have always been nineteen — and the Count keeps tallies of everything countable: people, sheep, hares, hawks, cisterns, courses of wall, bell-stalks in each ring. The tallies are kept on knotted cord because ink implies revision. In four generations of records, no tally has changed. When a death is announced in the lower quarter, the Count does not grieve first; it waits, and within the day a birth is announced, or a traveler comes up the switchbacks from the fog, and then the town grieves properly, because the ledger is closed.

The consequences of this are practical and strange. You may not leave the Shelf unless someone arrives to replace you; the gate-keeper's scale is a literal thing, a stone bench where the departing sit until the arriving sit. Some people wait years. There is no shortage of housing and no surplus either — a whole northern quarter stands empty and swept, forty-one houses with their dew-fins maintained and their hearths cold, kept ready, because the count of houses is also fixed and a house that fell out of use would be an imbalance. Children play in them. Nobody builds a forty-second house; nobody would know what to do with it.

And there is no history. Nothing wears, so nothing is old; nothing grows, so nothing is new. There are no ruins on the Shelf, and no ruins is the eeriest absence of all — a made place of this size with no dead layers under it, no earlier walls in the foundations, no midden. The mountain came up sharp and young, and something caught it mid-gesture, and the city that stands on it is either extremely ancient or was finished last week, and the distinction has stopped meaning anything.

Stand at the rim at the end of the day. The sun goes down clean because the air is thin and the light is unmediated and hard, and the tilted pavement throws long precise shadows off every bell-stalk ring. Below the bevel of the rim, the fog lies in the valley like standing water, level as water, and the far ridge stands up out of it grey and unrounded. The Spill goes over and does not land. The moths come up out of the joints. Somewhere behind you a sheep-bell, and the gutters, always the gutters. Four thousand one hundred people, and it will be four thousand one hundred tomorrow, and the hawk on the wall three feet from your elbow does not turn its head.
