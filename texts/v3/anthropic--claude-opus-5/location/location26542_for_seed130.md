## The Bedplate

Begin with the floor, because the floor is the ground and there is no other.

It was poured, once, in a single campaign of work whose duration nobody kept: one continuous slab of grey concrete aggregated with slag and crushed brick, reinforced with rod, laid across the whole basin from ridge to ridge. It was flat then. It is flatter now, but not level — long ages of water and vibration have worked it into a topography of the gentlest possible kind. Swales a hand deep and forty paces wide. Broad low domes where something beneath still runs warm. Shoals of exposed aggregate polished glassy, and between them the pale grey mud that is what concrete becomes when it has been wept on for long enough: a soft flour, slick underfoot, ankle-deep in the hollows, holding footprints for a week.

Anchor bolts stand out of it in rows and grids, thousands of them, worn down to smooth brown stumps like the teeth of an old animal. They are the surest evidence that machines once sat here that no longer do. You can read the shapes of vanished engines in bolt-patterns half a mile long.

Nothing here is stone. Cut anywhere and you find made things: rail, cable, tile, firebrick, bottle glass, cast iron, more concrete beneath the concrete. The basin is constructed all the way down as far as anyone has ever dug, and the digging always stops for the same reason — water.

## Water

The water table sits a finger's width under the slab and is always trying to come up. It arrives as a sweat on the cold iron; it arrives through hairline cracks in bead-lines that grow into rivulets by afternoon; it arrives from above, which is the strangest of its arrivals.

Overhead, at seventy or eighty feet, there is a truss. Not a roof — the plating went long ago, and what remains is a lattice of riveted girders spanning the basin in bays, black against a sky that is itself barely a sky. But the lattice catches the basin's own breath. All the exhalation of all the working machinery below rises, cools on that ironwork, and comes back down as rain. It rains here every day, from the roof, in fixed lines. The drip-lines are permanent enough to have carved their own vertical geology: dimpled bowls in the slab, each with a rim of white scale, each half a thumb deep after however long a thumb's depth takes.

The rain is warm and tastes of iron and of the inside of a kettle. Underfoot, everything is wet. The basin drains northeast, sluggishly, through channels that were once precisely engineered and are now sagged and silted into a marsh — the Sump Fen — where grey water lies in sheets among reed-beds and slowly disappears into pump intakes that have never, so far as anyone can determine, stopped.

## The Pumps

You hear them before you understand anything else, because you hear everything before you understand anything else.

The pumphouses stand along the northeast ridge, nine of them, brick shells with their windows gone and their roofs gone and their engines running. They are the reason the basin is a landscape and not a lake. Great vertical beams rise and fall through the empty gables in slow alternation, and each downstroke pushes a column of water up a rising main and out over the ridge, and each stroke arrives in your chest as a physical event, a thud you feel in the sternum a half-second before you feel it in your ears.

They are not maintained. They lubricate themselves from a grease reservoir that has become a landform — a tallow-colored bluff below the pumphouses, stratified in bands of amber and black, weeping in the warm hours, cold and waxy in the cool. Wire-grass grows on its terraces. Whatever supplies the reservoir has never been found.

The pumps drift in and out of phase with one another over a period of about eleven days. When they align, the whole basin's water level drops a visible amount, the mud stiffens, and the noise becomes something the body cannot politely absorb. When they scatter, the fen creeps out across the flats and the sound becomes a chaos rather than a hammer.

## Sound as Terrain

This is the essential thing about the basin: sound is the dominant medium, the way that air is elsewhere.

Layer one is the Drone, a bass so low and so constant that within an hour you stop hearing it and begin only feeling it — a pressure in the joints, a fine tremor that keeps the standing water perpetually crazed with ripple so that it never once reflects anything. Its source is the Gearfields, and we will come to those.

Layer two is the Stamp. Somewhere west, one enormous drop-hammer strikes every four and a third seconds. It has been striking the same spot long enough to have worn a bowl three hundred feet across and to have compacted the slab beneath it into something denser than rock. The bowl holds water; the strike throws a ring of spray; the spray drifts downwind as a permanent fog bank streaked in the dark. You navigate by the Stamp the way you would navigate by a bell buoy.

Layer three is the Shriek — a belt-drive somewhere southeast running metal on metal, a high pure ascending tone that goes on for hours and then stops for hours, and when it stops the absence is louder than the tone.

Layer four is everything else. Ten thousand bearings. Ventilation howl. The rain on iron. Steam blowing from a relief that has been lifted for centuries.

You cannot speak here. Two people walking the flats communicate by grip and shove and by writing in the mud with a stick. Shouting into an ear at a range of one inch achieves nothing. This is not an inconvenience; it is a rule of the world, and it has consequences. Everything in this basin that has ever been decided was decided in silence, by pointing.

## The Silence Pits

And there are silences. This is the basin's central strangeness and its whole ecology.

Because the sources are ancient, fixed, and rigidly periodic, and because the truss overhead reflects with the consistency of a cathedral, the sound field is not random. It is a standing pattern, stable for lifetimes. There are nodes — places where the Drone cancels itself, where the Stamp's wave arrives out of phase with its own reflection, where the pressure simply drops away.

Step across the boundary of a Silence Pit and the world's weight leaves you. The boundary is sharp: a stride wide, sometimes less. Inside, you can hear the rain hitting your own shoulders. You can hear yourself swallow. People weep in them and cannot say why.

They are also where everything grows. Outside a node, the vibration keeps the mud in suspension and shakes seed and spore off any surface before it can hold; the flats are bare grey, scattered with wire-grass only where grease has stiffened the ground. Inside a node, silt settles. Sediment accumulates. There is soil, of a kind — grey flour plus rain-scale plus the fibrous dust of a hundred generations of disintegrating insulation — and in it grow pale sedges knee-high, and cushions of a slow grey-green moss that is the only soft thing for miles, and mats of a fungus that fruits in flat white shelves smelling of wet ash.

From above, if there were an above to look from, the basin would read as a dark plain stippled with perhaps three hundred green discs, some the size of a room, a few the size of a village square, arranged in a pattern that a mathematician would recognize instantly and an ecologist not at all. They do not move. They have not moved in the entire recorded wear of the plain. Their positions are a map of a machine that no longer exists in any other form.

Two of them, near the fen, hold cable-willows: bundles of steel wire rope, broken off and unravelling, the frayed ends drooping and furred with algae, swaying in the drafts exactly as willows do. They are not alive. They are the only things here shaped like something alive, and the effect at close range is not comforting.

## The Gearfields

South and central, four square miles of them.

Set into the slab, in ranks, are wheels — spur gears, six to thirty feet across, cast iron, mounted vertically on bearings that go down into the wet dark. Once they meshed. Once this was a transmission of some prodigious kind, carrying power from somewhere to somewhere. But the teeth have worn. Millennia of contact under load have rounded every tooth to nothing, and now each gear is a smooth disc, and the discs turn side by side with a finger's clearance, touching nowhere, transmitting nothing at all.

They still turn. Belts run to them from below, from a level nobody reaches, and they spin at a stately eleven or twelve revolutions a minute, all in the same direction, rims polished to a dull mirror, throwing off sheets of collected rainwater in flat arcs. The Drone is their bearings — four square miles of bearing hum, coupled into the slab, coupled into your bones.

Walking the Gearfields at what passes for night, you see them mostly as absences: black discs interrupting the faint smear of light on the horizon, appearing and vanishing as the sheets of thrown water catch and lose the glow.

## Light

There is no sun in any account of this place. There is a permanent dim, enough after an hour's adjustment to walk by, and it has three sources.

The first is the Arc-Front, on the western horizon: a low continuous violet-white flicker where something is welding, or arcing, or failing to weld, and has been for as long as the wear-record runs. It casts shadows. It is the closest thing the basin has to a sun, and like a sun it moves — northward at roughly a hundred feet a year, according to the scale-lines it leaves on the slab. It has crossed the basin at least once before. There are old fused glass pavements in the east where it went.

The second is filament: exposed resistance wire in the guts of stripped heaters, glowing dull orange, thousands of them scattered across the flats like coals in ash. You can warm your hands. You cannot see by them at any distance, but they mark the ground.

The third is corona — pale blue fire crawling on the wet insulators of the transmission gantries that stride the basin from ridge to ridge. On heavy-condensing nights the gantries glow along their whole length, a blue dotted line running to the horizon, hissing under the general noise, dropping the occasional bright spit of a flashover.

Everything is cool, though: forty-five degrees, forty-eight, the temperature of a cellar. Only the warm domes and the outflows break it.

## Fauna

None. This is worth stating plainly because visitors keep looking.

No birds, though the truss overhead is the finest imaginable roost — the vibration would shake eggs to slurry. No insects; nothing hatches in water that is never still. No rats, no worms, nothing in the moss of the Silence Pits, which is the thing that unsettles people most, because the pits look exactly like habitat and are exactly empty.

The one exception, if it is one: in the warm outflow below Pumphouse Four, where the water runs at blood heat over a spillway of broken tile, there are pale threadlike creatures, eyeless, perhaps a quarter-inch long, that swim against the current. Nobody has determined whether they are animal. They are the only things in the basin that move because they choose to.

And there are absences shaped like animals. Grease-strata sometimes weather out to reveal a hollow — wings, a keeled breastbone, a beak — the void left by a bird that fell in and dissolved. The bluff below the pumphouses is full of them. Whatever era those birds came from, it had a sky.

## Rules

Wear is the only clock. Nothing here is dated; everything is measured. A bolt-stump's height, a drip-bowl's depth, the width of the polished band on a gear rim. The basin's whole history is a set of erosion rates, and the numbers all agree, and the number they agree on is very large.

Nothing here is being maintained and nothing here is failing. The machinery has reached the condition every machine tends toward and never reaches: a state so worn that it can no longer wear. Toothless gears cannot strip. Rounded cams cannot break their followers. A hammer that has compacted its own anvil-bed strikes forever without deforming anything further. This is the basin's final form, and it will hold it indefinitely, running, drained, loud, cool, wet, lit by its own arc, growing three hundred discs of moss in the quiet places where the sound cancels out.

Anything left standing still is annexed. Scale builds at a rate you can watch — a dropped tool is crusted in a season, cemented in three, indistinguishable in ten. Which means the basin has an inventory of everyone who has ever stopped moving in it, and the inventory is part of the floor now, and you are walking on it.

The last rule is the one people carry out with them, if they go out.

Stand at the eastern ridge at the end of a walk and look back across it: the black plain streaked with drift-fog, the blue dotted gantries, the ember-scatter of filament, the four square miles of mirror discs turning in unison, the beams of the nine pumps rising and falling out of phase, the violet flicker crawling one hundred feet a year toward the north, and the whole of it screaming at a volume the body treats as an injury —

and understand that it is not a ruin. Ruins have stopped. This is a landscape that finished becoming itself a very long time ago and has simply continued, and would continue identically whether or not anything ever came to look at it.

That is the part that's hard to put down.
