## The Sill

You come up out of the dark valley for a day and a half, following the warm stream that nobody sensible follows, because it smells of eggs and iron and because it steams in the wrong season. Then the gorge opens and the light hits you like a slap of white paint, and you are standing on the lip of the Sill.

It is not large. That is the first surprise. You could walk its length in the time it takes a pot to boil, if anything boiled here in a pot. It is a shelf notched into the high shoulder of the range, perhaps six hundred paces long and half that across, bounded above by a cirque wall of black slate hung with frost, and below by the Cold Lip, where the water goes over and falls out of the world into cloud. Everything happens between those two boundaries. Everything is stacked there, layer on layer, in a volume too small for it.

The second surprise is that it is warm, in a place that has no business being warm. At this altitude the air is thin as pulled glass and the shade temperature — where there is any shade — will crack your knuckles open. But the rock beneath the Sill is thin over something ancient and heated, and water comes up through it at forty vents, at a temperature just short of pain.

### The stone that grows

The water arrives loaded. It has spent centuries in the dark dissolving limestone, and when it reaches the air it lets go of what it carried. It lays that burden down as a rim, and the rim becomes a lip, and the lip catches the next flow, and holds a pool, and the pool overflows and starts another rim below it. So the Sill is a staircase of basins, hundreds of them, white and cream and butter-yellow, some the size of a hat and some the size of a threshing floor, terraced down from the vents to the Cold Lip in a fan of shallow curves that look, from above, like the inside of an ear.

Understand what this means: **the stone here is not a material. It is a process.** A wall that water runs over gains the thickness of a fingernail in a month. A step becomes a ledge becomes a shelf becomes a room's worth of solid mass. Nothing here is eroded away — the whole logic of the place is deposition, addition, encrustation. And yet the forms are old, settled, worn into their own long habits; the fan has been fanning for so long that its curves are as inevitable as a shell's. The Sill is not being built. It is being kept, by a hand that never lifts.

### The town

There are perhaps two hundred people. They live in the terraces, and the terraces are eating their houses.

A house begins as a basketwork dome — willow withies from the single stunted grove at the Cold Lip, lashed with sinew, plastered with algae mat and dung. It is placed in the wet, deliberately, in the splash-line of a warm channel. Within a season the water has glazed it. Within three years it is a stone shell, translucent at the crown where the deposit is thin, a hard bright pod humming faintly with the flow around it. It is a good house. It is warm from beneath. It will last a thousand years.

It will also close.

The doorway narrows a fingerbreadth a season. The smoke-hole shrinks. The floor rises as the water beneath lays down its film, until the ceiling comes to meet a sitting head. A family spends its life scraping — that is the constant small labour of the Sill, the scraping of thresholds with tools of horn and ground quartz — and eventually gives up, unpacks, and builds above. So the town is layered like a wasps' nest of white porcelain: five, six, nine generations of sealed rooms below, each one holding its furniture inside the stone. Grandmothers' looms are in there. Cradles. A dog that would not be moved. When a new channel cuts down through an old level, it opens a window into the encased past, and people come and look, quietly, at a lamp still standing on a shelf under two hands' thickness of clear stone, and then they go back to work.

There is no metal to speak of. There is no fuel: no wood but the willow grove, which is holy and cut only for withies and for the little charcoal used to blacken eyelids. Nothing gets hot enough here to smelt anything, and the water would rot iron in a month anyway. So the tools are horn, bone, obsidian traded up from below, and the local stone itself, which can be sawn while it is young and soft as cheese and which hardens in the hand. Cooking is done in graded pools: the near-scalding one for eggs, the middling for the algae bread, the cool ones for keeping. The whole town's kitchen is a plumbing problem.

### The light

Bright is the wrong word. There is no adequate word.

The air is thin and the sun is unfiltered. Above, the cirque holds an amphitheatre of snow. Below and all around, the terraces are white mineral, wet, and every basin is a mirror. Light comes at you from above, from the side, and *from underneath*. There are no shadows. A person standing on the Sill at midday casts nothing; the light arrives from too many directions at once and cancels itself. Children are taught this as a first fact, the way other children are taught not to touch fire: *you have no shadow here, so watch your feet.*

Everyone wears slit-visors of ibex horn, boiled and pressed flat, and blackens the skin around the eyes. Shade is the scarce resource, hoarded, negotiated, inherited. The few overhangs — the lee of the slate wall, the hollows under old sealed roofs — are owned in the way that water rights are owned elsewhere. To be given the use of someone's shadow for an afternoon is a serious kindness. Old people, whose eyes have gone milky, are called *the shaded*, and are led about by dogs, and are consulted, because they have stopped seeing and started listening, and listening is the skill that matters most.

### The quiet

Here is the strangeness at the centre of the Sill: it is a frantic place, and it is nearly silent.

The water does not roar, because it never falls far. Forty vents, four hundred rims, and the whole flow broken into sheets a finger deep — the sound is a hush, a fizzing whisper, ten thousand tiny tricklings that sum into something closer to breath than to noise. Over that, the shuffle of birds' feet. Wind, sometimes, off the snow, but the basin is sheltered and the wind is mostly overhead.

And the people are quiet on purpose. Not reverent — practical. The springs announce their intentions in a very small voice. A vent about to shift changes its pitch three or four days beforehand; a rim about to fail begins to click, a dry tick like a beetle in a wall, as the water finds a fault behind it. If you can hear that click, you can move your family, your grain baskets, your grandmother. If someone is singing, or arguing, or hammering, you cannot.

So: felt on the tool handles. Felt-soled boots. A hand-language of about six hundred signs, used at conversational speed, elegant and dismissive and joking. Speech reserved for the indoors and the night, murmured, close to the ear. Bad news travels by a runner touching people's wrists. Good news too. The loudest sanctioned sound in the Sill is the two-note whistle that means *your terrace is going*, and it is heard perhaps four times a year, and nobody who hears it ever forgets it.

### The fauna

The Sill is a warm wet island in a cold sterile ocean of rock and ice, and every living thing for forty miles has crowded onto it.

The pools are thick with a pale blind shrimp, a finger-joint long, in numbers that make the water look like it is being stirred. Fly larvae carpet the algae mats in mattresses you can lift off in sheets. Above them: birds, absurd numbers of birds. Long-legged waders with recurved bills that sieve the shrimp and stand in the warm shallows all night with their heads on their backs, and dun-coloured divers, and a small black-and-white gull-thing that nests on the sealed roofs and has learned to steal from cook-pools. They are not loud. That is the astonishing thing. They mutter. A colony of ten thousand birds on the Sill produces less noise than one crow in a valley, because they have all, over some incomprehensible span, taken up the manners of the place — or because the ones who screamed were the ones who stood on the failing rims. Nobody knows which. Both stories are told.

And the combers: long-bodied swimming animals, chest-high if they stood, whiskered, with paddle hands. They dig. They keep the channels open, undercut the young rims, drag debris to the Cold Lip, and in doing so they decide, more than the people do, where the water will go next year. The Sill's people do not tame them and do not hunt them. They *bribe* them — leaving heaped shrimp-cake at the mouths of channels they want kept clear, and stone-blocking the ones they want abandoned, and arguing among themselves in silent hands about whether the combers are being managed or are managing. The town's shape is a negotiated document. It has two authors and one of them cannot be spoken to.

Where a bird dies standing in the shallows, the stone takes it. The walls are studded with them, mid-stride, thin bright encrustations in the shape of a wader — some so old they are only a suggestion, a swelling in the plaster with a beak.

### The turbulence

Nothing here holds still for a year.

Vents wander. A spring that fed the upper town for four generations will cool over a fortnight and open again eighty paces east, and eighty paces east is somebody's roof. Rims fail and dump a terrace's worth of water down six levels in a minute — never enough to kill by force, usually, but enough to scald, and enough to strand a whole quarter dry, which is worse, because dry means cold, and cold at this altitude means you have three days to move or start burning the willow.

Every night the mist off the pools meets the frost coming down off the cirque, and lays a lace of ice over the entire town, an inch thick, feathered, glittering, unbearable to look at when the sun clears the wall. The first hour of every day is spent breaking it, quietly, with horn paddles, by everyone. This is the closest thing the Sill has to a liturgy.

And so the town moves. Constantly, in every direction, at the pace of a slow bazaar: baskets going up, thresholds being scraped, withy-domes seeded in fresh splash, channels dammed with wet stone that sets by evening, children carrying shrimp-cake to the combers, the shaded sitting still with their heads tilted, listening for the click. Anthill energy under a hush. From the Cold Lip at dusk, when the sun goes down through the cloud-sea and the whole white staircase turns the colour of a peach and every basin holds a separate small burning sky, you can watch two hundred people working at full speed and hear almost nothing at all.

### Rules of the world

They are not written. There is nothing to write on that would not thicken and seal.

Do not step into the sky. (In total light a still pool is a perfect mirror; the taboo is against walking on the reflected cirque, and the practical reason is that you cannot judge the depth or heat of a pool you are seeing the sky in. The impractical reason is older and nobody explains it.)

Do not hammer. Do not shout except to warn. Do not sing outdoors before the frost is broken.

Do not block a comber's road without leaving it another.

Do not open a sealed room to take what is in it. You may open one to look.

Give shade when asked, and remember who gave it to you.

Build low, build wet, build knowing it will close. A house is not a thing you own. It is a thing you are inside of for a while, on its way to becoming the floor of the next one.
