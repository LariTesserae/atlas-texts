## The Warm Mouth

At four thousand three hundred metres the limestone gives up on being a mountain and becomes something more like a ruin of itself. The ridge here is all fluted towers and blades, a karst that has been rained on for so long that the rain has carved its own signature into every surface: runnels the width of a finger, running always downhill, endlessly forked, so that the rock reads as though water had written on it in a script of parallel strokes. Nothing about this landscape is young. The edges have been rounded and then re-sharpened and then rounded again. It has arrived at its own form and settled there, and the settling took longer than the memory of anything alive.

Above, the air is thin and bitter and the sky at midday has that bruised violet weight it gets when there is not enough atmosphere left above you to make a proper blue. There is little to see growing. Cushion plants in the shelter of the blades, hard as knuckles. A saxifrage in the crack, one rosette, maybe two, keeping a low grey profile against the wind. Lichen in the pale mineral colours — bone, rust, a green that is barely a green. That is the whole botanical inventory of the upper world, and it takes about a minute to catalogue.

Then the ground opens.

---

The shaft is not large. You could throw a stone across it, and the stone would strike the far lip, and everything inside would react. It is perhaps forty metres across at the mouth, oval, its long axis running with the ridge, the rim scalloped by collapse — old collapse, healed collapse, the fallen blocks below now furred with moss and long since gone quiet. The rim rock is polished on the windward side and undercut on the lee. There is nothing built here. Nothing has been made here except by weather and appetite. That is worth saying plainly, because from a distance the shaft has the proportions of an architecture: an apse, a chapter house, a round room lidded with sky.

The first thing that comes up out of it is warmth.

You feel it on your face before you understand it, and at this altitude it is an incorrect sensation, almost a nausea — a humid animal warmth like the breath of a large sleeping thing, rising steadily against gravity and the cold. It carries a smell: ammonia, first and hardest, and under the ammonia something sweeter and more complicated, a smell of dampened dust, of feather oil, of chitin, of fermentation. The air where it meets the outside air makes a haze. On cold mornings the shaft smokes, a slow column that leans off downwind and dissolves, and from the neighbouring ridges you can find the place by that smoke alone.

The second thing that comes up out of it is sound.

---

Descend. There is no path but there are ways — a spiral of ledges on the northeast wall where a bedding plane has weathered proud, wide enough in places for two feet side by side, narrow enough elsewhere that you go down facing the rock with your palms flat against it. The limestone here is not dry. It sweats. Condensation beads on it in the warm updraught and runs in the fluting and collects and drips, and the drips are the metronome under everything else, a million of them, unsynchronised, so that what you hear is not dripping but a soft continuous hiss like distant applause.

Fifteen metres down, the daylight has already become a memory of daylight. The oval of sky above narrows and brightens and stops meaning anything. By twenty-five metres you are in the true dark, and the dark is full.

Full is the word. Not busy, not populated — full, the way a granary is full. Above you, on the overhanging vault where the shaft belled outward as it deepened, the swiftlets are nesting, and there are so many of them that the rock has no colour of its own: it is a texture of small dark bodies and their cupped brackets of hardened saliva and moss, pressed nest to nest, tier on tier, and above and among them the bats — three species at least, sorted by height and by heat, the smallest in the warmest pocket of the dome, the largest hanging in the draught. Everything is layered. Everything is nearly touching. When one bird shifts, forty shift.

The noise at this depth is not describable as noise. It has structure. The swiftlets echolocate with a sharp double click, audible, unlovely, and forty thousand of them clicking at once produces a dry crackling roar like a fire made of gravel. Through it run the bats' calls at the top edge of hearing and above it, so that you feel them in your teeth as much as hear them. Under all of it: the drip-hiss, the low organ note of wind combing the rim, and the wet muttering of the floor.

You must not go barefoot on the floor.

---

The floor of the Warm Mouth is not rock. It is a slope of guano seven or eight metres deep at the centre, sloping up to the fallen blocks at the edges, and it is not inert. It is the single densest thing in this landscape and the reason for the warmth: fermentation, a slow interior fire, forty degrees a handspan down. It is upholstered with life. Dermestid beetles in the millions, so that a torch beam laid on the surface shows it visibly moving, a nap of small backs stroking one way and then another. Blind crickets with antennae longer than my forearm, waving them like a man feeling for a door in the dark. Amblypygids — whip spiders — the size of a spread hand, flattened against the lower walls, holding utterly still and then not. Centipedes as long as a shin, banded the colour of old bruises. A snake, pale as a peeled root, that hunts the swiftlets by hanging from the vault and striking at wingbeats.

Nothing here has eyes worth the name and nothing here needs them. That is the first rule of the place: this is a world where light is not information. Direction is smell and echo and touch and the gradient of warmth. Space is known the way a room is known to a hand.

The second rule: the floor rises. It has risen for as long as the colony has existed, a millimetre or two a year, sixty thousand years of droppings and drowned nestlings and beetle husks compacting into a fossil archive of the mountain's weather. Every drought is a pale band in it. Every wet century is a dark one. The mountain does not go anywhere and the sky does not change much and so the only clock running in the Warm Mouth is this one, patient, upward.

The third rule is a tide. Twice a day the colony turns itself inside out. In the last blue of evening the swiftlets come home and the bats go out, and for forty minutes the shaft is a solid braided column of bodies moving in two directions at once, and the sound is a physical pressure, a wind of wings, and the whole ridge for a kilometre around fills with the smell of them. At dawn it reverses. Peregrines and bat hawks hold station at the rim on those two hours and take what they take, and the taking is statistically nothing, and the colony does not notice.

---

There is one more thing in the Warm Mouth that did not grow there.

On the northeast wall, wedged in a crack at the eighteen-metre level, a length of bamboo — greyed, split at one end, the split bound with a whipping of plaited fibre. Above it, another. They go up the wall at intervals of a body's reach, thirty of them, some rotten to a stub, the oldest ones petrified into the flowstone that has grown over them like a lip. There is no ladder here, no platform, no shelter, no hearth, no sign at all that anyone has ever stayed. Only the pegs, and a knot of vine rope on a spur at the rim, cut and left.

Once a year, in the dry weeks, people come up from the valley three days below. They bring bamboo, cane rope, a bone hook, a resin torch that they mostly do not light because light is useless and smoke makes the colony boil. They climb in the dark by feel, following the pegs their grandmothers set, and they take nests — the old vacated ones, high up, never the occupied tier, never in the season of eggs. Then they go down and take everything with them except the pegs, and the mountain closes over their absence within a week.

Whether this is restraint or just a rule so old that nobody remembers it as a rule, I could not tell you. But it is why the place is what it is: a single warm room at the top of a cold world, forty thousand years of dark accumulating gently upward, roaring, dripping, breathing out its column of smoke into the violet air — and untouched, essentially. Visited. Not owned.
