## The Sheet

There is no horizon here in the ordinary sense — only the place where the shine ends. For most of the day the land is a mirror laid over stone, an inch of warm water spread across a plain so level that the water does not know which way to go. You can walk for an hour and rise the height of your own shin. Standing in it, you stand in the sky.

The rock underneath is very old. It is the kind of old that has stopped being a story. Whatever it was — mountains, most likely, roots of mountains, the welded cores of ranges that stood when nothing on land had a spine — has been reduced by an unimaginable patience to a smooth grey-pink floor, veined with quartz that stands a finger's width proud of the surface because quartz is the last thing to give up. Run your hand along one of those veins and you are touching the leftovers of the leftovers: the mineral so stubborn that a hundred million wet seasons could not talk it into leaving. Everything else went. Went to clay, went to iron, went downstream to a sea that has since moved.

In the low places — and low here means the depth of a wagon rut — the water has stained itself the colour of strong tea, and the floor beneath is not stone but a red-brown crust, pocked and glassy, like slag. This is the iron the rock sweated out. In the dry weeks it hardens to a pavement that rings faintly underfoot. In the wet it goes soft at the top only, greasy, and holds a footprint for a season.

## Weather as a slow animal

The heat is thick and even, a bathwater heat, the sort that makes the skin and the air agree with each other so completely that you lose track of where you end. Nothing here is cold, ever. The nights lose maybe a hand's breadth of warmth and give it back before the light does.

Because the plain is warm and covered in water, it breathes. Mornings begin white: fog lying flat on the sheet, waist-high, so that the scattered features of the landscape — a low whaleback of bare rock, a fan palm, a termite tower — float without bases, like objects on a shelf of milk. By mid-morning the fog goes up. By afternoon it has become a single stack of cloud far off, blue-bellied, taller than any mountain that ever stood here, and it rains over there, and the sound arrives twenty minutes after the flash.

Then the rain comes across, and it is not a front but a wall, a visible edge dragging its own darkness, and it crosses the plain at walking speed, and when it hits you it is warm as spit. It lasts a quarter hour. Then the sun again, at a low slant, and every drop on every leaf and every square mile of the water sheet lights up at once, and the glare is so total that you learn to look at your own feet and navigate by the ripples.

This is the whole calendar. There is no season that arrives suddenly. Wetter months and less-wet months, gradients so weak that the animals here do not schedule themselves by the year but by cues too small for us to notice — a particular smell, a change in the water's taste.

## Where the water goes

There is a rule here, and it is the strangest true thing about the place: **water does not flow away. It flows around, and eventually it flows up.**

With a gradient near zero, drainage becomes a matter of hesitation. Water pushed by wind moves east in the morning and back west in the evening. It travels in long shallow tongues that fill a depression, hold, seep sideways into the crust, and reappear a mile off through the ground as a slow upwelling that dimples the sheet from below. Kneel in the right spot and you can feel a cool column against your ankle, water that entered the earth before you were born a mile away and is only now coming back to the light.

The sheet loses far more to the sky than to any sea. Most of this water's ending is evaporation, which means that every year the plain concentrates what it carries. In the flats furthest from the upwellings, white rings form — salt, soda, a faint sourness — and the edges of these rings are crusted like the rim of an old cup. Nothing grows in the rings. The birds walk there anyway, because the invertebrates in that brine are so dense that the mud moves.

Elsewhere the rock weeps. Where the whalebacks rise — never more than four or five metres, worn into shapes like the backs of half-buried animals — water seeps from the joints all day long, having gone in on top and taken a week to cross a metre of stone. The seep lines are black with cyanobacteria and green with a low velvet of moss, and they are always cool, and everything comes to them.

## What grows

Not much, and that is not a failure. The plain will not hold soil. It has been perfectly flat for so long that nothing new arrives from anywhere higher up, and the little that weathers in place either dissolves or is carried off as dust. So the vegetation is scattered, individual, each plant a decision.

Sedges in tussocks, dark and hard, standing in the water in loose constellations, sometimes a hundred paces between clumps. Around the tussock bases the roots have trapped enough silt to make islets the size of a hat, and these islets are the only "ground" for miles.

Fan palms, singly or in threes, marking where the water table sits closest. From a distance they look like people standing. They are the tallest things in the world here and they are perhaps six metres.

On the whalebacks: lichen in plates — orange, black, chalk-grey — growing at the pace of a fingernail per century, and in the rock hollows, small annual plants that complete an entire life in the eleven days a pool holds water. Around them, resurrection ferns that go grey and dead-looking and then unfurl within an hour of rain, so that a stone which was bare at noon is furred green by three o'clock.

And the mats. Vast low mats of algae and bacteria across the shallowest sheet, brown-purple, wrinkled like skin, releasing bubbles all day. Where they have grown for a very long time undisturbed, they stack — millimetre on millimetre — into cushions and columns a hand high. These are the oldest ongoing projects in the landscape, and they are also the noisiest producers of oxygen; walk through them and the water fizzes at your calves.

## The noise

It is loud here. Astonishingly loud, and almost none of it is fast.

The base layer is insect: a continuous shrilling from the sedge clumps, pitched high, so uniform that after ten minutes your ear declares it silence and you have to make an effort to hear it again. It rises with the heat and drops at dusk, and at dusk the frogs take over.

The frogs are the real event. Thousands of species-voices is an exaggeration; a dozen is not. There is a knocking one, like two stones tapped underwater. There is one that sounds like a thumb dragged across a comb. There is a small pale frog, no longer than a thumbnail, whose call is a single pure tone, and when ten thousand of them call from a flooded flat, the tone is so coherent it becomes a pressure in the sinuses, a sound you feel behind the eyes. It can be heard from over a mile off and it does not stop for six hours.

Over that: birds. The plain is a place of enormous shallow productivity, and it has more waders than it has plants. Long-legged, long-billed, mostly white or dust-grey, standing in dispersed thousands with a bird every ten metres for as far as vision holds, so that the loudness comes not from a flock but from a *field* of birds, each contributing one dry rattle a minute. When something disturbs one end of the field, the alarm crosses the plain as a wave of sound with visible arrival, faster than the rain wall, and then subsides in the same direction.

And under everything, the water. Rain on standing water at continental scale is not a pattering but a hiss — enormous, smooth, and strangely comforting.

## Who lives here

Everything that lives here has solved the same two problems: the water is always shallow, and the surface is always flat.

**Lungfish**, in the deeper pans, thick as a forearm, breathing air with an audible gulp. In the driest weeks they go down into the crust and wait, and the mud over them cracks, and they are still there.

**Crabs**, blue-clawed and stubborn, a hundred miles from any sea, living in burrows they excavate to the water table and plug behind themselves. Their burrow chimneys are the second most common architecture on the plain.

**The first most common** are the termite towers. They stand alone, a hundred metres apart, two metres tall, red as the iron crust, oriented all the same way — thin edge to the noon sun, broad face to the morning. They are the only built things in this world. No hand made them; no tool exists here, nor ever has. But they are unmistakably constructed: buttressed, ventilated, patched, inherited, older than the trees. Some are dead — hollow, softened, half-melted by rain into shapes like burnt candles — and these are taken over by lizards, by a small owl, by bees. Nothing here is wasted, and nothing here is fast, and a mound that took forty years to build takes two hundred to disappear.

**Buffalo-sized grazers**, few and far between, moving in loose lines across the sheet, throwing up spray, silhouetted with their own reflections so that they seem to walk on a second animal. They are the largest sound-makers and the least noisy.

**Dragonflies** in numbers that darken the low sun, hunting the midge hatch. **Turtles** that graze the mats. **Snakes** that swim with their heads out of the water like small periscopes.

## The rule underneath

This landscape has finished. That is the thing to understand about it. It is not being made; it is not being destroyed; it has arrived. There is no mountain upstream to feed it, no sea nearby to cut into it, no gradient steep enough for anything to move except the water and the animals. Erosion here is no longer a sculptor — it is a caretaker, going over an already-final surface with an infinitely fine cloth, taking away a grain, taking away a grain.

So the change that happens is biological and cyclical: fill and dry, hatch and die, mound built and mound melted. The place holds those cycles the way a bowl holds soup. Ten thousand years from now the frogs will be shouting from the same flats. The quartz veins will stand a hair less proud.

And the space between things — the long empty shining stretches with one palm in them, or one bird, or nothing — is not absence. It is the plain's actual substance. The scattering is the design. There is only so much water table to go around, and everything alive has agreed, over an unfathomable stretch of time, to stand very far apart and be extremely loud.
