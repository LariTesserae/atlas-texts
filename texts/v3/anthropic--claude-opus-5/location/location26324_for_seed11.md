## The Flats Above the Throats

### 1. The table

Begin four kilometers up, and then stop climbing, because there is nowhere left to climb. The land here has already finished. What stands at this altitude is not a peak but a *lid* — a tableland the size of a small country, laid flat by a patience so long that the mountains that made it have been reduced to knuckles: rounded stubs of grey-green metabasalt thirty or forty meters high, spaced kilometers apart, worn smooth as bone handles. You can put your palm on one and feel that it has no edges anywhere. Nothing on this plateau has an edge. Every angle was used up long ago.

Between the knuckles: pavement. Interlocking plates of ancient rock, polished to a low sheen, veined with milk-white quartz that catches the overcast and throws it back as a faint glow, so that on a bright cloud-day the ground seems to be lit slightly from within. Walk it and you cross a floor. It does not undulate; it drapes, in kilometer-wide swells so gentle you only detect them by watching your own horizon rise and fall over an hour's walking.

This is the oldest stable surface anyone knows. It has stopped becoming.

### 2. The throats

Then, without warning, a hole.

They are perforations in the table, ranging from a well's width to a stadium's, sheer-sided, dropping four thousand meters into the warm cloud-filled canyon-world beneath. They are the throats of dead volcanic pipes, or dissolution shafts, or both — long since scoured clean and glassy. There are perhaps two thousand of them across the whole plateau, and they are the reason for everything else here.

Because warm wet air from the lower world is always rising through them.

Always. A hundred kilometers of hot humid canyon breathing upward through two thousand stone flues. The air comes out of a large throat at the speed of a river in flood, warm as breath, saturated, and it comes out **loud**.

### 3. Sound

You must understand that here, sound is not an event. It is the medium. It is the weather.

Near a great throat the roar is a physical object; it presses on the chest, it makes the eyeball tremble so that the world seems to shiver at its edges, it dissolves your own name in your mouth. Stand at the lip of one of the big ones — the locals mark them with a single upright stone — and you will feel your teeth talking to each other. Sixty meters back and it is merely a jet engine. Two kilometers back and it is a permanent surf. There is nowhere on the plateau, not one square meter in a region the size of a province, where it is quiet. Even in the emptiest pavement-desert between throats, the sum of two thousand distant roars arrives as a floor of white thunder, and above it, threaded, the higher voices: the whistles of the small vents, the moaning of the flute-groves, and the immense slow *woom* — a note below hearing that you feel in the long bones — when a big throat's column stalls and re-establishes itself.

The consequence is total. Nobody here speaks.

### 4. The gardens of the throats

Now the strangest inversion: this is a lush world that is almost entirely empty.

The rising air is a fountain of warmth and water, and everything alive on the plateau clusters at its mouth. Each throat wears a garden. A ring, sometimes only twenty meters wide, sometimes a kilometer of dripping jungle, sharply bounded, and then — nothing. Bare shine-pavement to the horizon.

In the ring, in order outward from the lip:

**Flutewood.** Hollow-stemmed, segmented, growing in dense stands to five or six meters, black-green and rubbery. Each segment has a mouth-slit. The stand plays the updraft. This is where the melodic voices come from — chords that shift as pressure shifts, whole groves modulating together, so that a big garden sounds like ten thousand organs being tuned by a lunatic in a wind tunnel. Flutewood does not seek light. It seeks *sound*: cut a stand down and the stumps regrow leaning toward the loudest neighboring throat.

**Beard.** Grey-green filament hanging in curtains from the flutewood, doing nothing but combing water from the updraft. Squeeze a handful and you get half a cup, warm, faintly mineral, drinkable.

**Sponge-mat.** The floor. A living carpet, forty centimeters deep, chartreuse on top and black underneath, holding water like a mattress. Step and you sink to the ankle and it sighs. Under the mat: no soil to speak of. A few centimeters of matted root and dust on top of four billion years of stone.

**Cushion.** The outermost band, and the only plant that ventures onto the open pavement. Tight hemispherical mounds, ash-blue, hard as leather, each one an independent hydrological empire — condensing fog on its own hairs, funneling it inward, storing it. They range far out into the bare flats where the roar is only ambient. They may be extraordinarily old. Some are three meters across, and if the growth rate is what it appears to be, that is nine thousand years of dome.

Beyond the cushions: the great emptiness. Kilometers of glowing pavement, occasional dew-fins — thin upright natural plates of quartz-veined rock, waist-high, that sweat in the night and drip a hand's-width of dark stain onto the stone. That stain is the only water in a hundred square kilometers. Rain falls maybe twenty times a year, when a cloud-column overshoots and slumps sideways across the table. Then, for an hour, the whole pavement is a mirror, and the roar acquires a hiss on top of it, and every cushion swells visibly.

### 5. Turbulence

Nothing here holds still except the ground.

Above each throat a cloud is being born. Continuously. The column punches up, condenses at some invisible line, and unfolds into a boiling white tower that rises another two thousand meters before shredding. Every throat has one. Standing on a knuckle-top you look out across a plain of polished stone from which two hundred visible cloud-pillars grow, all leaning downwind, all collapsing and rebuilding themselves on a cycle of minutes. Shadow-blotches the size of towns race across the pavement. The light changes four times a minute: pearl, glare, slate, pearl. Dust devils spin up from nothing on the dry flats and travel for an hour without touching a single living thing.

And the columns are unstable. They stall. A throat will choke — some pressure knot in the canyon below — and its cloud pillar will detach and drift away like a released balloon while the throat itself falls to a murmur. Then the neighbor throats surge to compensate, doubling their roar, and their gardens shudder, and the flutewood shrieks into a register that splits your head.

### 6. The Silence

Rarely — five, six times a year — the whole system stalls at once.

It takes about ninety seconds. The roar draws down through registers, layer by layer, like something descending a stair. And then there is a quiet on the plateau that no other place on the world can produce: four kilometers up, no wind, no water moving, two thousand throats holding their breath, a floor of stone that stopped changing an age ago.

In the Silence, things happen that happen at no other time. The cushions open — every dome on the plateau splits along hidden seams and releases a slow smoke of spores that hangs, because there is nothing to move it. The beard-curtains drop their accumulated water all at once, a garden-wide patter. The **loaf** — the plateau's one large animal, a deaf slow armored grazer the size of a bathtub, which crosses the mats at a hundred meters a day and is seen perhaps twice a year — stands still and raises its head, which it otherwise never does. And the **throat-swifts**, which live their entire lives inside the updraft columns and touch stone only to lay, come down. Thousands of them, in absolute quiet, settling on the pavement in the strangest gathering in the world, and then rising again when the first low note returns.

The Silence lasts between four minutes and an hour. Nobody knows why. There is no known pattern. There is nothing to do but experience it.

### 7. The people, such as they are

Perhaps forty of them on the whole plateau. Maybe sixty. They do not gather to count.

They have no metal. Chert blades, bone awls, cordage twisted from beard-filament, water carried in flutewood segments plugged with wax, sandals of layered sponge-mat that squeak on the shine-pavement. Fire is difficult — everything is damp near a garden, and there is nothing to burn out on the flats — so they cook rarely and eat mostly raw mat-shoots, throat-swift eggs, and a bitter grub that lives in flutewood joints.

They wear ear-plugs of wax and packed fiber from the age of about one month. Some are properly deaf and this is not considered a misfortune.

They do not speak. Not because they cannot — the anatomy is there — but because it is useless, and useless things fall out of a culture in a few generations. They have hands instead: a full language, fast, made large so it can be read across a garden clearing in changing light. And they have **carving**, which is the plateau's great art, because on stone this old and this hard, a mark lasts. Every knuckle has glyphs on its lee side. Some are recent. Some are so worn that only the shadow at a low sun reveals them, and no one alive can read them, and they are treated with extreme courtesy.

Their built things are few and small: dry-stone spiral windbreaks with a sleeping hollow at the center; dew-combs, which are frames of flutewood set out on the bare flats to catch fog into a chert-lined pit; a roofless tower on the plateau's western swell, six meters, older than anyone's grandmother's grandmother, purpose unrecorded, maintained anyway; and single upright stones at the lip of every large throat.

Their rules, as far as an outsider can gather:

- Any stone you move, you set upright. The plateau has no edges left; uprightness is a human contribution.
- Do not sleep during the Silence. Whatever the Silence is for, it is not for missing.
- Names are carved, never signed. A person's name exists in exactly one place, on stone, and they will take you to it if they want you to know them.
- Do not descend. There is a whole warm world below breathing up through the floor, and it is not theirs, and the throats are sheer and glassy and four thousand meters deep, and the roar coming out of them is answer enough.

### 8. Standing there

Late in the day the overcast breaks somewhere far off and light comes across the table almost horizontally. The pavement lights up like wet slate. Every cloud-pillar in the visible hundred kilometers goes gold on one side and lead on the other. The gardens — those improbable rings of dripping black-green jungle, each a hundred meters of Amazon marooned in a stone desert — throw shadows five times their height. The roar continues, as it has continued for however long the throats have been open, indifferent, enormous, warm on your face.

And somewhere out on the flats, kilometers from any garden, a single ash-blue cushion the age of a civilization sits condensing its half-liter of night water, in a place where nothing else lives at all.
