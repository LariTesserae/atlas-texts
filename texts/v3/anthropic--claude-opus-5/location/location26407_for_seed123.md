**The Underfloor Delta**

You reach it by a hatch nobody labels, in the pump room's corner, behind the drum of chain oil. Lift the plate and warm air comes up at you — blood-warm, mineral, faintly sweet like hot wiring and wet cement. Then you go down four rungs and you are no longer in a building. You are in a country, and the country is nineteen meters across.

The ceiling is the underside of the factory floor: a grey sky of slab, ribbed with the shadows of beams, hung with a weather system of pipes. Chilled-water lines sweat. Condensation gathers on their bellies, walks along them to the low points, and falls. That is the rain. It falls in fixed places, so the ground below each low point is drilled with small clean pits, and around the pits, splash-fans of sorted grit. There is no wind here to speak of and yet everything is arranged as if by wind — because everything is arranged by drip, by seep, by capillary crawl.

The land itself is young. Not geologically young: young like bread. The oldest formation in the room is eleven years old and the newest arrived last night.

---

**Terrain.** Understand that the concrete slab above is not sealed. It has hairline cracks and one good fracture near the west footing, and the plant's wash-down water finds them. It comes through with whatever the plant was making that week dissolved in it, and it lays that down here in fans and terraces. So the ground is *striped*. Cut a bank with your thumbnail and you get the archive: a rust-orange band from the iron-oxide months, then a chalky band, then a band of astonishing magenta three millimeters thick from the year they ran the dye contract, then grey, grey, grey, then a thin glittering seam of glass microspheres that nobody has ever been able to account for. The animals dig into last year's magenta and come out pink-shouldered and go about their business.

The high ground — call them mountains, because relative to what lives here they are mountains — are clinkers. Fused lumps of furnace slag that were shoveled through a floor gap in the plant's dirtier decades. They are black, vitreous, sharp as new obsidian, unweathered because there is no frost and no wind and no rain except in fixed columns. Where a drip *does* strike a clinker, it has bored a hole through in a hundred years' worth of nights, and the hole whistles very faintly when the exhaust fans change speed. That is the loudest thing here.

Between the mountains: the delta. A single seep at the west footing feeds a river the width of two fingers, which fans into eleven distributaries across a plain of fine sorted silt, and reforms itself completely every eleven days when the plant does its Sunday wash-down and the flow triples. Everything on the plain is therefore provisional. Whole landscapes of levee and bar and cut-bank are built, colonized, and erased inside a fortnight. The residents know this. They have made it a way of life; they build nothing that cannot be carried.

The lowest point is a pool the size of a dinner plate, warm, faintly alkaline, ringed with a white crust of efflorescence. It never dries. It never overflows. Nobody knows where it drains and the level does not change.

---

**Light.** This is the surprise: it is not dark. There is a floor grate at the east end, open to a loading bay, and through it comes real daylight in a hard bright bar that moves across the plain over the course of a day like a slow searchlight. There is a second, weaker light from a vent stack. And there is one caged incandescent bulb, sixty watts, left burning by a man who died in 2009, which nobody has switched off because nobody knows which breaker it is on. Between the three of them the room is lit like a shed with the door open. You can read down here. Things cast three shadows, at three temperatures, in three directions, and the shadows cross the plain at three different speeds.

The bar of daylight is the primary ecological event of the day. It heats the silt it touches to thirty-eight degrees. A convection cell forms above it. Springtails ride the cell.

---

**Sound.** Mostly, quiet. The factory above is nearly automated now — two shifts of machinery, no shouting — and what comes down through the slab is not noise but *transmission*: a 60-hertz hum you feel in your teeth and the sternum before you hear it, plus the periodic thud of a press, softened by 300 millimeters of concrete into something almost tidal. Against that ground the small sounds are enormous. A drip landing in a pit. The tick of a clinker cooling. The dry rustle of a cave cricket's leg on paper. The pool makes no sound at all and its silence has a texture.

---

**Ecology.** Packed. There is no bare ground here. I want to be precise about this: there is no square centimeter of this room that is not occupied, layered, contested, or being actively renovated by something.

The silt plain is fogged with springtails — a living haze half a centimeter deep, hundreds of thousands, grazing the bacterial film. Push a finger into it and it parts like smoke and closes again. Over them, isopods: the big grey ones, moving in slow herds across the flats, plating and scraping the salt crust, leaving grazing trails that the river erases on Sundays. They are the cattle of the place. Where the herds concentrate, the ground is compacted hard and shines.

The clinker mountains hold the predators. Harvestmen stand in the crevices like derricks, legs spread across gaps they could not otherwise cross. Two species of spider, one of which builds sheet webs in the whistling holes and takes whatever the fan-shifts blow through. Centipedes in the warm seams. A rove beetle population that follows the isopod herds the way wolves follow caribou, and does exactly what that implies.

Under the grate, in the daylight bar: the vegetation. Sparse, and all of it wrong-colored. A tuft of etiolated grass, pale as endive, growing from a seed that came in on a boot. Three cushions of moss, deep and healthy, the greenest things in the room. A film of algae on the pool's warm rim. And a single tomato seedling, white-stemmed, thirty centimeters tall and utterly leafless except at the very top where it has found the light — it has been alive for four years, which is not something tomatoes do, and it flowers in November.

The mineral gardens count too, if you're generous. Efflorescence grows out of the slab in white needles and feathers, and it grows *fast enough to watch* — a crystal fan will put out a millimeter in a night — and it is grazed by something. Nobody has seen what.

Apex: a toad. One. Female, palm-sized, fourteen years old at least, living in a hollow under the west footing with her back to the seep. She is functionally the weather. When she moves, the springtail fog reorganizes across a third of the plain; the isopod herds shift their grazing rotation; the rove beetles follow the isopods. She eats perhaps forty animals a night and the room does not notice. She has never seen open sky, another toad, or a body of water larger than a dinner plate, and by every rule of her species she should be dead, and she is not.

---

**Built.** Scattered, and this is where it becomes strange.

There is a shrine. It is on a clinker outcrop above the pool, and it consists of: eleven hex nuts arranged in a ring; a doll's chair; a matchbox with the striker worn white; a brass key; a bottle cap, upended, holding four millimeters of clear water that is not the same water as the pool. Around it, five small lean-to structures made of copper wire and cardboard, each about the size of a walnut, each with a floor of sorted glass microspheres.

The plausible explanation is a packrat, and there is no packrat. The other plausible explanation is the man who left the bulb on, and he has been dead sixteen years, and the bottle cap's water is clean.

Elsewhere: a wooden pallet slat lying across two distributaries, functioning as a bridge, which is used. Its underside is polished. A length of conveyor belt half-buried in the magenta stratum, curling up out of the ground like a fossil rib. A pressure gauge, face intact, needle at zero, reading a system that no longer exists. Six bricks in a rough hearth arrangement with no scorch marks on them.

---

**The rules here.**

Rain falls at 06:12, 14:12, and 22:12, when the chillers cycle — not from the sky but from eleven fixed points, and only from those. If you stand between them you stay dry forever.

Nothing in this room is older than the plant. The land, the animals, the salt, the archive of colored strata — all of it postdates the pouring of the slab. This is a country with no prehistory, and its residents live at the top of a stack of their own recent minutes.

Land is disposable and life is not. Every eleven days the geography is deleted and rebuilt. Nothing has evolved to hold ground; everything has evolved to relocate cheerfully, and the whole ecology has the light-footed, provisional quality of a fairground.

Time is shift time. The isopods have a two-cycle day. The springtails follow the daylight bar. The toad wakes when the press starts. There is no dawn here, only a schedule, and everything alive in the room keeps it.

And: the room does not get more crowded and it does not get emptier. Whatever the accounting is — and there is an accounting, you can feel it, the way you can feel the 60 hertz — the total is conserved. Bring something in and something goes out. Nobody has established what.

Climb back up. Set the plate down gently; the seal of dust around the hatch is the room's only border and it re-forms in a day. Above you the plant hums along, running two shifts, nearly empty of people, making its product, and beneath its floor the little warm country works away at full capacity, packed edge to edge with life, quiet as a held breath, thirty-eight degrees in the light and no one's business at all.
