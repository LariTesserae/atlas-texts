## The Works at Vess

Begin at the lid, because everything here begins at the lid.

A meter and a half of black ice roofs the basin, freshwater ice, old and layered like slate, with the year-lines visible in it if you cut a core: bands of clear, bands of milk, bands full of trapped grit from some summer when the wind carried dust across. From beneath, the underside is not flat. It is fretted and scalloped into a low ceiling of domes and gutters, shaped by the warm water that rises and slides along it, so that the whole roof of the world reads like the inside of a cathedral vault carved by something patient and inattentive.

In nine places, the ice is open. These are the moon-holes, and they are the town's own doing. Under each one stands a chimney.

---

The basin is not large. Four hundred paces across at the waterline, less at the bottom, a quarry bowl with three cut terraces still legible in the rock, everything low — you could stand at the deepest point and the ice would be only eleven meters over your head. It is a hamlet's worth of space, holding a small city's worth of construction.

That is the first strangeness: the density. Vess was built for a workforce that had to be housed within a shout of the mill, and so it was built the way a wasp builds — cell against cell, wall shared with wall, a stair squeezed into every gap where a stair would fit and a pipe run through every gap where a stair would not. Six-story tenements of dark brick lean against the terrace faces. Between them run alleys the width of a wheelbarrow. Above the alleys, at three heights, the connecting bridges: iron catwalks, brick arches, and one long timber gallery that has gone to a soft grey pulp and holds together mostly out of habit. Cables cross overhead in bundles as thick as a man's waist. Pipes cross the cables. Chains hang from the pipes, and from the chains hang the counterweights, still level, still exactly balancing whatever they were meant to balance a very long time ago.

Nothing has fallen. That is the second strangeness, and it is not miraculous, only slow. Underwater, at this temperature, in this stillness, nothing has any reason to fall quickly. A slate loosens from a roof and takes four days to reach the street, tilting and gliding, and it lands without a sound, edge-on, and stands there in the silt like a small stone marker.

---

The iron is finished corroding. This is worth saying plainly: the rust has arrived somewhere and stopped. Every railing, every rivet head, every window frame and bollard and boiler flank wears a crust two fingers thick, the color of dried blood going brown, hard as any rock in the quarry wall and no longer eating what it covers. The crust has taken on the smoothed, dune-like forms that water gives things it has been touching for centuries — no sharp edges left anywhere in Vess, not one. Doorframes are rounded like the mouths of old cave passages. Bolt heads have become knuckles. The whole town has the shape of something worn by a river, though the water here barely moves.

Where the crust is thickest it has begun to grow outward in small, blunt, coral-ish knobs, and there are stalactite forms hanging under the balconies, ferric and glossy, that will take another thousand years to reach the ground.

---

Light.

At noon in high summer, for perhaps two hours, the ice glows a dim blue-grey and the top of the town becomes visible as silhouette: chimney tops, the mill roof, the crane. Below the third floor, that light does not go.

What goes below is lamps. Fifty-one of them still burn in Vess. They are filament lamps in cast housings with thick, pitted glass, and they burn a low amber, and each one lights a sphere about four meters across and nothing beyond it. From the mill floor, looking up through the alley-lattice, you see them scattered in the black at all heights and angles like a poorly-made constellation, each with its own halo of suspended silt, each halo drifting slightly.

Somebody changes the lamps. Not often. Perhaps three a decade. There is a rack of spares in the pump-house, and the rack is not full, and it was fuller once.

---

The engines.

Under the mill, the water is warm — eight degrees, against the basin's one — and it comes up out of the outfall grates in a shivering column and climbs the alleys and pours out of the chimneys and spreads along the ice, melting the moon-holes, cooling, sinking down the far walls of the basin, and returning along the floor to the intakes. This is the circulation. It takes a little under five hours to complete. It is the town's breath, and everything in Vess is arranged with respect to it.

The machines run on it. The great wheel in the mill, four meters across, turns once every forty seconds, driven by the rising warmth that the machines themselves produce. Which is, of course, not possible. The loop should have wound down long before anyone thought to be surprised by it. But the loop has run so long that arguing with it feels like arguing with the strata in the quarry wall — it is simply a feature of the place now, a thing that obtains here the way weight obtains elsewhere. The engineer's instinct is to look for the hidden input. There is a warm seam somewhere in the rock, probably. Probably.

What the machines make is not clear. There is a hopper, and there is a chute, and at the bottom of the chute is a bin the size of a bathtub, and in the bin is a fine dark powder, and the bin has been full for a very long while, and the surplus spills over the lip and drifts down and joins the silt on the mill floor, where it forms a slope with a beautifully consistent angle of repose. The powder is heavy and slightly warm to the touch. It does not react with anything.

---

Sound.

Water carries sound four times better than air, and Vess should be a bell. It is not.

Everything here is felted. Every hard surface wears silt or crust or the pale grey-green algal skin, and every cavity is packed with a soft sediment that has been settling for as long as the lamps have been burning, and the frequencies the machines produce are all below hearing — twelve hertz, seven, three. You do not hear the town. You feel it in the sternum and the back of the teeth, a pressure that arrives and departs on the forty-second cycle, so regular that within an hour your own pulse tries to join it.

Against that, the audible events are few and precise: the tick of a chain link resettling. The very small crackle of a rising warm plume meeting the ice. Once or twice a day, a note from higher up — a groan out of the lid as the ice adjusts to itself, coming down through the water as a long descending tone, the only thing in Vess that could be called loud, and even that arrives softened, like something remembered rather than heard.

---

Green things, barely.

Cold water and little light make for a thin flora. There is the algal skin, grey-green and slick, on every surface within reach of a lamp — it grows in rings, brightest at the sphere's center, fading to nothing at the edge, so that each lamp sits inside a green target painted on the brickwork. There are four kinds of it and they occupy the rings in strict order.

In the warm outflow, and only there, there is a stonewort: brittle, pale, calcified, growing in stands the height of a knee, gently combed in one direction by the current. Perhaps forty such stands in the whole basin. They are the only things in Vess that move continuously.

On the west terrace, in what was a yard, there is a single mat of something older-looking — a low, dark, dense cushion two meters across, unlike the rest, with a texture like wet felt. It has been measured. It is not getting bigger.

---

Fauna, rarer still.

You can pass an hour and see nothing alive. Then: a fish, twenty centimeters, colorless, with the reduced eyes of a species that has decided the lamps are not worth the investment. They are here in some number but they hold in the alley shadows and only cross the lit spheres when they must. They cross fast.

There are isopods in the silt, pale and slow, and a great many of them, and they do not come up.

And there is one grazer. It is a mystery how many there are — the working assumption is fewer than five in the whole basin. It is an amphipod grown to the size of a large dog, translucent, softly luminous along the gut, and it moves along the walls eating the algal rings with a delicacy that makes no sense at that scale. It works a lamp for perhaps twenty minutes, taking the outer ring only, never the center, and then swims off into the dark at a walking pace. The rings regrow. This has evidently been the arrangement for a while.

---

Rules of the place, as they present themselves:

The cold sets the visit's length. Forty minutes, at best, before the hands stop being useful. Everything you will ever learn about Vess must be learned in forty-minute pieces.

Nothing here is in a hurry, and the town does not respond to hurry. Kick off too hard and you raise a silt cloud that will not clear for six hours, and for six hours you have destroyed the only thing the place has to offer, which is the ability to see it.

Do not break the crust. Not for any reason of taboo — simply because underneath it the iron is still bright, and once you have opened it, the corrosion starts again from zero, and it will take four hundred years to finish, and the balance you came to see will be, in that spot, undone by you.

And the last rule, which is less a rule than an observation that everyone who spends time here eventually makes: the town is not waiting for anything. It is not a ruin, because ruin implies a direction. Vess arrived at its condition, found it satisfactory, and settled. The wheel turns, the powder spills, the plume rises, the ice thickens and thins, the amphipod takes the outer ring and leaves the inner, and someone, at intervals of several years, comes to change a lamp.
