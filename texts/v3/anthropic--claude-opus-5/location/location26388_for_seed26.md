## The Lower Cast

There is no rock here that was not poured.

That is the first thing to understand, and it takes a while, because the place has the manner of a valley — a wide floor, walls that rise and lean and fold into side-galleries, a ceiling far enough off to have weather under it. But run your hand along any surface and you find the seam of a formwork panel, the ridge where two casts met, the shallow chamfer that somebody's drawing specified. The walls have grain, and the grain is plywood. The boulders on the spoil ramps are broken sections of an older wall, numbered in white paint on faces that were never meant to be seen.

The excavation runs perhaps four kilometres end to end, on a shallow tilt, and calling it a valley is not far wrong once your eyes adjust: a long trough with a low place in the middle where the water gathers, and terraces cut up both flanks to the level of the ventilation adits. It is cold. Cold enough that your breath goes ahead of you like a scout. Cold enough that the seep water on the north flank has built pale organ-pipes of ice down the wall from the weep-holes, and the crews have to knock them off every few weeks before they close the drainage slots.

### The Floor

The floor is poured in bays, each bay a hundred metres square, each with its own date stamped into the surface at the corner. You can read the works like a calendar by walking along it. The old bays — three years, five years old, which here is old — have gone soft-edged and stained, mineral bloom crusting the joints, black mould in the tooled grooves. The new bays are appalling in their sharpness. Arrises like blade-backs. Grey so fresh it looks blue. On the newest, the mould-release oil is still on the surface, and the water beads on it and refuses to wet, and the whole bay shines in the lamplight like a lake laid flat and hardened.

The new concrete is warm. This is the part that unsettles visitors more than the cold, or the noise, or the depth. In a cavern where your fingers ache, you come across a two-week-old wall and it is giving off heat, quietly, the way an animal does, and the frost stops half a metre from it in a clean bright line. The crews sit against these walls to eat. They call the heat *the cure*, and they are not sentimental about it; they know it stops. But they also know that a wall which stops curing early has something wrong inside it, and they listen to fresh pours the way a farmer listens to a horse.

Fresh pours do make sound. A settling, granular tick, very faint, but the cavern is a hard place and it carries. In the eighth or ninth day a big bay sometimes gives out a single deep report, like ice on a lake, and the pumphouse dogs stand up.

### The Weather

There is weather, and it is on a schedule, and the schedule is the fans.

Six fan houses stand along the west terrace, squat and windowless, each with its intake throat opening into the main volume. When the shift bell goes, the fans step up, and within twenty minutes the whole cavern has wind — a real wind, three or four metres a second down the length of the trough, cold off the surface, dry as paper. It strips the fog. It puts a chop on the standing water. It hums in every open grating and pulls a low howling note out of the shaft-heads that you can feel in your sternum long after you have stopped hearing it.

Then, at the shift's end, the fans drop back to holding speed, and the cavern breathes out. The seepage catches up. Within an hour the air is at saturation again, and the fog comes up off the water in the low bays and stands waist-high, then chest-high, and the lamp-strings go from lamps to smears to nothing. The ceiling begins to rain. It is not much of a rain — a slow, cold, wide-spaced dripping from the lattice of ducts and cable-trays overhead, each drop having gathered on steel for an hour to make the fall — but it falls the whole eighty metres and it lands hard, and it lands *everywhere*, so that the fogged hours have a sound like a forest after a storm, and you cannot tell in the dark which drips are water and which are the tick of curing.

Twice a day, then: a dry loud windy day, and a wet blind dripping night, each about six hours. Nothing above ground governs it. The seasons of this place are made in the fan houses, and the men who run them have the standing of weathermakers, which is to say they are blamed for things.

### The Water

Water is the argument that the works is having with the world, and the works is winning slowly and at great cost.

It comes in from everywhere: hairline joints, construction seams, the tie-rod holes, the mass of the concrete itself sweating out of the aggregate. It is collected in slots at the base of every wall, and the slots feed graded channels in the floor — a whole cast hydrology, gutters and weirs and grit-traps, running west and down. The channels are beautiful in a way nobody intended. The water in them is glass-clear and dead cold and moves fast, and where a weir drops it into the next channel there is a small white noise that you can stand beside and hear nothing else.

All of it arrives eventually at the Cooling Fields: four basins in the low middle of the trough, poured with two-metre freeboard, three hundred metres a side, black and absolutely still in the fogged hours. Steam does not rise from them because they are colder than the air; instead the fog lies down on them and pools. In the windy hours they show as a flat pewter sheet with an ice rind at the edges, and the ice rind grows and is broken back by the circulation and grows again, so the shoreline is always a rubble of thin plates chiming against each other. That chiming is one of the good sounds of the place.

The basins are cold because the plant makes them cold. Along the east terrace, in the deepest part of the works, stand the compressor halls — the reason for all of it. Ammonia machines, twelve of them, running the whole time, with a sound that is not really sound at your ear but pressure at your chest: a huge low chugging, four beats to the second, out of phase between halls so that the whole cavern's air gets slow beating pulses in it, a throb that swells and dies away on a cycle of about eleven seconds. Everyone here has learned to talk in the troughs of it. Newcomers get headaches for a week and then stop noticing, and this is how you tell a newcomer: they still flinch at the changeover.

What the cold is *for* is further down, past the Fourteenth Bay bulkhead, and it is not this landscape's business.

### Light

Dim. Deliberately dim, because light is heat and heat is the enemy.

Sodium strings along the terraces at head height, spaced far enough apart that you walk from island to island of orange. Under each lamp a rough circle of visibility perhaps twelve metres across, and beyond that a grey where you can make out mass but not surface. The walls, at that distance, look like walls of anything. There is white-painted phosphor on every stair nosing and every bollard and every duct edge, recharged by passing lamps, so in the dark hours the works is described by a dotted geometry hanging in nothing: the line of a handrail, the corner of a bay, a ladder going up.

And at four points along the length there are the shaft-heads, where daylight comes down eighty metres of concrete cylinder and arrives exhausted, a cold grey column that does not illuminate anything except the falling water in it and a circle of floor. In the shaft-light circles, snow sometimes arrives. Actual snow, from the surface, drifting down in a slow rotation, melting before it lands or not. Crews eat their bread standing in these circles for no operational reason.

### The Living

Under each shaft-head, in the grey, there is grass. Pale, thin, wretched, and unmistakably grass, three or four square metres of it, seeded by nobody, holding onto the silt that has washed into the floor slots. Someone has bricked a low kerb around the patch at Number Two Shaft.

Elsewhere the vegetation is what the wet and the dim allow: moss in the weep-lines, black and green, in vertical stripes down the north flank so that the wall reads like a page. Liverwort in the drain slots, fatter and more confident. A soft brown algal felt in the sunward end of the cooling basins, which the crews scrape and which comes back. Nothing over ankle height in the whole four kilometres except at the mess huts, where two lemon trees stand in oil drums under grow-lamps, doing badly, kept alive as a point of principle.

Animals are rare and each one is known. Cave crickets in the cable ducts, in colonies, going quiet when the fans step up. Rats along the spoil ramps — fewer than you would expect, and small. Two dogs at the pumphouse, working animals, deaf as posts. And since last autumn a heron, which came down Number Three Shaft and would not go back up, and which stands at the edge of the second basin in the fogged hours doing nothing at all, since there are no fish. The crews feed it. There is an argument about whether feeding it is cruel, and the argument is conducted in the troughs of the compressor beat, eleven seconds at a time, and will not be resolved.

### Rules

Do not enter a bay before the seventh day. Do not touch a wall that is not warm and not cold but somewhere between; report it. Do not stand under the lattice in the wet hours without a hat, not for injury, for morale. Do not break the ice organ-pipes on the north flank without checking the weep-hole behind them first — the ice is telling you where the water is, and blind water is worse than seen water.

And when the fans change over, stop working and stand still until you have heard the note go all the way up or all the way down. Not superstition. It is the only time the whole works speaks with one voice, and if there is something wrong four kilometres away, that is when you will hear it.
