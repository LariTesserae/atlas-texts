## The Ninth Throat

**Approach.** Above four thousand metres the mountain is not yet a mountain. It is a proposition — a heap of pale, unweathered spurs pushing out of a snowfield, edges still sharp enough to open a boot. Nothing here is rounded. There is no gravel, no soil, no talus fan of tired stone; only fresh angles and the white cataract-crust of minerals coming out of solution. The wind is thin and cold and largely absent, and in that thin cold absence you can hear, from a hundred paces off, a note coming out of the ground. Not a rumble. A note. Something being held.

The entrance is a slot in the snow-lip where meltwater from the basin gathers and goes in. You go in after it, down a shaft forty metres deep beside the falling column, using footholds that someone cut with a hand chisel a long time ago and someone else re-cut more recently. The sound doubles every ten metres. By the time your feet are wet you are no longer hearing anything; the noise has stopped being an experience and become a condition, like pressure or altitude.

**The room.** It is small. That is the first thing that refuses to make sense: this vast, unbearable roar is produced inside a chamber you could cross in five strides, could touch both walls of with outstretched arms in the narrow places. Four metres wide, perhaps three and a half high, an irregular ovoid with a fluted ceiling. It is entirely full of water except for a single bell of trapped air at the top, punched and held open by the falling column, forty centimetres of breathable space that shudders like a struck drumskin.

The water is meltwater, six or seven degrees, so clear that it seems to be a slight thickening of the light rather than a substance. Sunlight comes down the shaft in a solid bar, splits on the fall, and turns into an even, brilliant, cyan-white illumination that fills the chamber corner to corner. It is bright in here. Absurdly bright. Snow-glare on all sides, from all directions, with no shadow to rest an eye on.

And apart from the light and the water and the walls, the chamber is empty. Vast space between presences, in a room the size of a granary. Nothing drifts. Nothing settles. Nothing is suspended. The water has no sediment because there has not yet been time for sediment. Once every few minutes a single silver bead of air, escaped from the bell, crosses the void from ceiling to nowhere, and it is an event.

**The walls.** They are pale, fibrous, faintly translucent — the tenders call it *gongspar* — and they are growing. Not metaphorically, not on a geological schedule. Visibly. Over the course of an hour spent in the chamber you can watch a rib thicken by the width of a fingernail paring, watch a new fluted blade begin as a white bloom on a smooth surface and become a structure with an edge.

Here is the law of the place: **the sound is the mason.** The falling column charges the water with dissolved mineral, and where the noise is loudest the mineral comes out. In the pressure antinodes of the chamber's standing wave — the exact places where the sound is heaping itself up against itself — gongspar precipitates like frost on a window. In the nodes, the quiet lines, nothing forms; the walls stay smooth and glassy there.

So the room is a cast of its own acoustics. Its shape is a diagram of its voice. The ribs are the loud places made solid. The clean channels between them are silence, preserved.

And of course it cannot hold still. Every millimetre of growth changes the geometry; the changed geometry changes the note; the changed note moves the antinodes; the mineral begins depositing somewhere new and abandons where it was. The chamber is a resonator continuously retuning itself and continuously re-sculpting itself in response to its own retuning, and it has never once arrived anywhere. Blades grow out over their own bases and snap under their own weight — a crack you feel in your sternum, a fall of white shards, and the note drops a third, and everything reorganises. Twice in an hour. Sometimes twice in a minute. Nothing here erodes. Things accrete, and then they break. There is no third option, no wearing-down, no softening. The floor is a rubble of broken fresh blades with edges like flint, lying exactly where they fell, unrounded, unsorted, unaged.

**Sound, properly.** Underwater you cannot locate a source. Sound arrives at both ears effectively at once, and the brain, deprived of its arrival-time trick, gives up. So the roar has no direction. It is not coming down the shaft. It is coming from inside your skull, from inside the water, from inside the stone, from the year 1200, from the idea of noise. It couples straight into your bones through your jaw and your sinuses and the flat plate of your forehead. Your eyeballs vibrate in their sockets and the brilliant chamber smears at the edges. Thoughts do not survive; they are wiped after two or three seconds, so that you find yourself repeatedly discovering where you are, with the same small shock, over and over.

Rising into the air bell to breathe is worse. In the bell the sound is in air again and it has direction again, and the direction is *everything, immediately, at once,* the falling column hammering the surface a hand's breadth from your face. You take three breaths there and duck back down into the merely unbearable.

**Life.** Very little, and all of it improbable.

On the sunward flutes, where the light bar strikes, there is a fur of black-green algae, three or four species, thin as a stain, packed into the acoustic nodes because in the antinodes the growing mineral entombs anything living within days. So the vegetation of this world grows in silence-lines: neat dark stripes tracing out the geometry of the room's quiet. Above the waterline, on one ledge inside the air bell, there is a clump of moss that has been permanently drenched and permanently deafened for an unknown number of years and appears entirely well.

There is one animal. A translucent crustacean, eight millimetres, no eyes and no antennae worth the name — it grazes new gongspar, scraping the softest bloom off the growing edges, and it has no organ that could possibly detect sound, which is presumably why it can live here. You will see two or three in a visit. In some seasons, none.

And there are the birds. Once or twice a year a snowfinch or a chough is taken by the intake in the snow-lip and comes down the shaft, and drowns, and does not decay, and does not sink out of sight, because it is caught against a growing rib and the rib grows over it. There are eleven birds in the walls of the Ninth Throat at various stages of enclosure: a wing still feathered and free at the wrist, a head under two centimetres of pale spar with the beak still dark, and older ones that are only bird-shaped swellings with a suggestion of a folded neck. Fossils made in a season. In this chamber, the dead become geology before they have finished being dead.

**The built.** There is not much, and all of it is worn — which makes it uncanny, because it is the only worn thing in the world here. Everything native is raw and sharp; the artefacts arrived already smooth, from somewhere that has time.

A ladder of forty-one chisel-cut footholds in the shaft, recut at least four times, the recuts distinguishable by the angle of the tool.

At the low outflow — the drain, a fist-and-a-half aperture at the back of the chamber that lets the water out into whatever it lets it out into — a carved sill of imported dark stone, gritty and dull, alien among the spar. It is wedged with three bronze pins, green-black, hammered flat. Their purpose is not decorative. The drain sits in an antinode. It wants to close. It has wanted to close for centuries.

Because that is why anyone comes. If the drain seals, the chamber fills, the pressure has nowhere to go, and eventually the mountain opens somewhere below — and there are pastures down there, and a village, and a lake that would not stay in its basin. So people climb four thousand metres and go down a shaft into a room where thought is impossible, and, working by feel and by counting, with hand chisels and a bronze bar and no possibility of speech, they cut the growth back off the sill. Two hours' work. They cannot talk; there is a set of eleven hand-signs, made large and slow, and a knotted cord on which each visit's pitch is recorded by the length of a hummed string that matches the note — the only measuring instrument, the only archive. Thirty-one generations of knots. The note has fallen by roughly a minor sixth since the oldest knot.

There is one more built thing, near the sill, and it is not properly a thing. It is a person-shaped mass of gongspar, kneeling, about eighteen centimetres thick now, with the socket of a chisel handle still visible in the region of the right hand. The cord records that he was called Ienn, and that his mistake was not letting go.

**Rules of the place, as understood by those who go down.**

Loudness builds. Quiet is the only place anything can live.
Nothing here wears out. It grows until it breaks.
The room is always tuning itself and will never be in tune.
Do not stay still. Whatever stays still becomes wall.
Breathe in the worst place, because that is where the air is.
Cut the drain, count your strokes, do not attempt to think, and go up.

Above, the snowfield is silent and blinding, and the note is a faint held pressure under the boots, and the mountain — unfinished, unweathered, three days old at its edges — goes on making itself out of its own noise.
