## The Underplain

### 1. The Floor

Begin with the fact that there is no sky, only ceiling — and that the ceiling is so far up and so evenly worn that for the first hour you mistake it for weather.

This is a basin hollowed out of limestone by a river that finished its work before anything with a spine existed. The river is gone. Not diverted, not seasonal, not underground — gone, its molecules dispersed into the long dry, so utterly departed that the word *water* here refers to a substance found only inside bodies. What it left behind is a floor: three hundred kilometres of pale stone as level as a pressed sheet, with a ceiling arched somewhere between two hundred and eight hundred metres above it, held up by pillars.

The floor is smooth the way an old tooth is smooth. Everything sharp has been taken off it. Where the ancient current once braided, there are shallow troughs a few metres deep, worn to soft-shouldered gutters, and you can walk their courses for days. Underfoot: dust. Calcite flour, gypsum silt, the powdered breath of a hundred million years of stone giving itself up a molecule at a time. It takes a footprint perfectly and holds it for centuries, since there is no rain to smooth it and almost no wind at floor level. In places the dust has drifted into long low dunes, and in those dunes are the older footprints — of things that walked here when the mounds were young, preserved as neatly as writing, going nowhere anyone remembers.

It is cool. Steadily, unremarkably cool, the temperature of deep rock, the same in what would be summer and what would be winter, so that the body stops noticing it and instead notices the absence of change.

### 2. The Pillars and the Stubs

The pillars are the leftovers of dissolution: columns of rock the river declined to remove, standing in loose scatterings, sometimes alone for a kilometre, sometimes in copses. Ancient erosion has worked them into waists and shoulders. Many are hourglassed almost to failure and have stood that way, without incident, for longer than any lineage now breathing.

Between them, sparse across the plain, are the Stubs.

These are not natural. They are the oldest architecture: mounds of cemented dust and dung and chewed stone, raised by colonial builders in a continuous project of unknown duration. The tallest living ones reach sixty metres and taper into ventilation spires, fluted, ribbed, honeycombed with intake ports. The oldest are no longer buildings at all — they are hills, rounded to loaves, their spires worn back into the mass, their interiors mineralized solid. New mounds get built on the flanks of dead ones. Cut into any hill here and you find city under city under city, forty or fifty generations of gallery-plan compressed into stone, each layer thinner than the last.

They are spaced widely. A mound complex will cover a square kilometre, and then there will be nine kilometres of nothing but pavement and dust and the shadows of pillars, and then another. Sparse. The emptiness is not absence; it is the foraging radius, the negotiated distance, the reason the whole system has held together at this scale for so long. Density here is a resource that has been budgeted with extreme patience.

Growth rate of a mound wall: about the width of a hand per century. Nothing is ever demolished. The dead are worked into the masonry, which is why the walls, up close, have a fine granular glitter of chitin in them, like mica in granite.

### 3. Light

Scattered through the ceiling are the collapse-holes — dolines, chimneys, ruptures where the roof gave way in some slow event long finished. There are perhaps forty of them across the whole basin. From below, each is a shaft of grey-gold coming down at an angle, thick with dust, and each throws a bright ellipse on the floor a few hundred metres wide, and around that ellipse a wide penumbra, and beyond that the dim.

The dim is the true condition. It is enough light to see the ground and the shape of the next pillar and not enough to see colour properly. Everything is read in tones: bone, ash, ochre, the deep grey-brown of the mound walls. Your eyes never quite adapt, because they never quite need to.

The light does not move much. Because the shafts are angled and the ceiling thick, the bright ellipses drift only slowly across the floor over the year, like the hand of a clock with one hand. In ten thousand years, the ellipses have burned faint discolorations into the pavement, ghost-tracks of where the light used to fall before the roof shifted.

### 4. The Gardens, and the Pale Country

Under each shaft: a garden. Ferns and fleshy low-light succulents, tree-things with black trunks and horizontal fans of leaf held out flat to catch the column of daylight, climbers rappelling forty metres down from the lip of the hole on rope-roots. This is the lush part, and it is genuinely lush — layered, competitive, dripping with epiphytes, loud with insect life — and it is entirely dry. Nothing drips. There is not a puddle, a seep, a bead. The leaves are thick and waxed and cool and slightly sticky. They breathe at night and seal by day. Water in this ecosystem is carried, never poured: it arrives inside the bodies of animals returning from the surface, is passed into the soil as dung and death, is drawn up by roots, is held in leaf-flesh, is eaten, is exhaled, is caught again on the underside of another leaf. The whole cycle happens within tissue. To spill would be a catastrophe, and nothing here has evolved the capacity for it.

Away from the shafts, in the dim, vegetation continues — this is the surprise — but it goes pale. It stops photosynthesizing and starts eating. Kilometre after kilometre of the plain is carpeted, sparsely but unmistakably, with white and cream and dull-pink growth living entirely on guano: sheets and fans and mound-shaped colonies, root-mats spreading under the dust, stalks a metre high with no leaves at all, only a rasping surface for taking nitrogen out of the crust. Fungal networks run beneath it all, threading between the mound complexes, so old and so continuous that if you asked what the largest organism in the basin is, the answer would be *the floor*.

### 5. Sound

And here is the thing you cannot prepare for.

It is deafening. Not periodically. Not at dusk. Continuously, at a level that makes conversation impossible and thought difficult, and it comes from three sources laid on top of each other.

The bottom layer is the basin itself breathing. The ceiling holes connect this volume to the outside atmosphere, and the volume is enormous, so every pressure change in the upper world moves air through those forty throats. It is a roar — low, hoarse, oceanic, not quite steady, rising and falling over hours. In the narrower chimneys it goes tonal, a low organ note, and those notes beat against each other and produce a slow throb you feel in the sternum.

The middle layer is the colonies. Millions of small builders working stone with mandibles, and the mounds are resonators. A mound at full activity hums like a struck bell held to a pillow — and there are thousands of mounds.

The top layer is the fliers. Bats and cave-swifts, in the tens of millions, roosting in the ceiling and in the upper galleries of every mound, and they navigate by sound in a space where sound is already saturated, so they have escalated. Clicks, chatter, warning shrieks, the constant lithic clatter of echolocation off hard rock, all of it doubled and quadrupled by the reflective floor and ceiling until it is less a set of noises than a texture, a granular pressure on the ears like standing inside gravel.

At dawn and dusk the exodus goes up through the shafts: a spiralling column that takes ninety minutes to clear, and for that ninety minutes the sound goes past deafening into something structural. Dust lifts off the floor from the vibration alone. The pale garden leaves tremble. The plain, for an hour and a half, is a drum being played from inside.

And then — never silence, but a lull, a subsidence into the mere roar of breathing stone, and in that lull you understand the local rule: **silence is the emergency signal.** When a stretch of the plain goes quiet, something is hunting it. Quiet moves across the basin like a shadow, and the animals read it perfectly, and it is the only fast thing in the whole landscape.

### 6. Rules

- Nothing pours. Water is a possession, not a feature.
- Nothing is finished and nothing is cleared. Building is additive, always, over spans that outlast lineages.
- Space is expensive; height near a light-shaft is the most expensive thing there is, and the mound complexes nearest the dolines are the tallest, oldest, and most layered.
- Change happens, but on the wrong timescale to watch. The ellipses of light creep. The mounds thicken. The pillars narrow. Somewhere, once every few thousand years, a roof panel lets go and there is a new hole, a new column of light, and a new city grows under it over the following ten generations — which is, in the reckoning of this place, immediately.
- There is nothing here that was made by hands or by mind in the sense you'd mean it. It is all appetite and instinct and repetition, iterated for so long that it has arrived at infrastructure: ventilation, waste management, transport corridors worn into the pavement, agricultural zoning around the light. A city with no one in it who knows it is a city.

### 7. Standing There

You stand in the dust between two mound-hills, in the grey light, in the cool, in the noise. Ahead, ten kilometres off, a shaft of gold with a green blur at the bottom of it. Behind, the pale country, going on. Above, the flat worn ceiling with its ten million lodgers.

Nothing approaches you. Nothing flees. You are not on any schedule the place recognizes. The floor takes your footprints and will keep them, in this dry cool dim, long after everything soft about you has been recycled into a wall.
