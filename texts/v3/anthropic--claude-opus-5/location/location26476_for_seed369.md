## The Breathing Shelf

**I. Where it sits**

Two thirds of the way up a wet mountain, on the northeast flank where the ridge kinks and loses its nerve, there is a bowl in the rock about three hundred paces across. It was scooped out long ago — by ice, probably, and then by the slow chemistry of its own water — and it has had all the time it needed to settle. Nothing here is sharp. The rim is a rounded lip of grey stone glazed with old mineral, pale orange in some places, the colour of a healed burn. The scree that once fed down from the headwall stopped moving generations of moss ago and now lies under a green pelt so thick that a stone kicked loose makes a sound like a fist in a pillow.

Below the lip the ground drops away in benches, each bench softened, each edge lipped over with turf, and at the bottom is a floor of black peat and pale gravel and standing water in shallow interlocking sheets. Nothing about the shape suggests recent event. This is a landscape in its own state, holding a form it arrived at and then kept.

At this altitude the air should be thin and cutting. Everything above the rim behaves accordingly: dwarf willow, wind-flattened heath, bare rock, a small dirty tongue of last winter's snow lodged in a north-facing crack. But the bowl is warm. You feel it as you come over the lip — a change of about ten degrees in five steps, wet heat rising to meet the face, and the smell of it: mud, sulphur very faintly, crushed green stalk, something sweet and fermenting underneath.

**II. The breath**

The floor of the bowl is fissured. Nine main fissures, and dozens of hairline ones, all running roughly with the grain of the underlying rock, all lined with wet mineral flowstone the texture of wax.

Every eleven hours or so — the interval drifts; it is not a clock — the bowl exhales.

It begins as a pressure in the ears. Then the sheets of standing water start to tremble and bead, and then the fissures open their throats. What comes out is warm vapour under pressure, and it comes out *loud*: a sustained organ roar, low, felt in the sternum before it is heard, with higher whistling harmonics off the narrower cracks. Nine notes, not quite in tune with each other, beating against one another in slow throbs. You cannot talk over it. You cannot think over it very well either. It lasts perhaps six hours, rising to a plateau and holding there, and for those six hours the bowl fills with white steam that thickens until the far rim is a rumour and the daylight coming down through it is bright, thick, and directionless, like light inside a shell.

Then it turns. The roar drops through a register, thins, and reverses — the bowl draws in. The inhale is shorter, three hours, and almost silent: a long sucking hush, the sound of a large room taking a slow breath through its teeth. The mist collapses inward and downward and vanishes into the fissures. The air clears. The temperature falls. For an hour or two the bowl is quiet and lucid and you can see the whole of it, every bench, every plant, sharp as a held drawing, and the mountain around it is enormous and cold and indifferent.

Then it starts again.

**III. Water**

Water is never absent and never abundant. It arrives from four directions at once: it seeps from the bench walls, where the rock weeps continuously and hangs its weeping in beards of dripping liverwort; it stands in the shallow sheets on the floor, warm as blood, never more than ankle deep; it condenses out of the exhale onto every surface, so that during the roar hours the whole bowl is beaded and running and each leaf carries its own load of drops; and it leaves by a single notch in the southwest lip, where a thread of it — no thicker than a wrist — slides over the mineral glaze and drops two hundred feet down the outside of the mountain, becoming nothing but hanging spray before it lands.

The water is not clean. It is fizzy, slightly bitter, warm enough to be unpleasant to drink and safe enough not to kill you. It deposits. Everything that has stood in it for long enough — a fallen stalk, a bird's foot bone, a stone — is sheathed in the same pale orange rind. The bowl is very slowly turning its own contents to stone, and has been for a very long time, and this is one reason it looks so settled: it fossilizes its own history at the rate of about a fingernail's thickness in a human lifetime.

**IV. The nine**

There are, in the whole bowl, nine large plants, and perhaps forty small kinds, and the small kinds are mostly mosses and one aggressive succulent-leaved thing with a rubbery pink flower.

The nine are the point.

They are enormous and they are widely spaced, thirty or forty paces from one to the next, each standing alone on its bench with a clear apron of gravel and moss around it. They are not trees, exactly. The largest is a rosette of grey-furred leaves, each leaf longer than a man, spiralling up a woody trunk to a crown twenty feet off the floor — like a lowland giant lobelia that got lost on its way up a tropical mountain and found a warm room to wait in. Its dead leaves do not fall; they fold down and clad the trunk in a thick brown skirt, layer over layer, insulation against the inhale hours. If you part the skirt you find the trunk beneath it hot to the touch and running with condensate, and small colourless things living in the wet.

The second is a fern of impossible proportion, its crozier heads the size of a curled cat, uncoiling at a rate you could measure with patience — a finger's width a week. The third and fourth are a paired thing, two trunks from one root, hollow, and during the exhale they *sound*: the pressure finds their hollows and they moan a fifth apart, adding two more notes to the nine.

All of them move with the breath. On the exhale the leaves lift and open and turn their undersides to the vapour, drinking it directly — the roots here are shallow and half-ornamental; the real feeding is done by the leaf in the steam. On the inhale they close: the rosette clamps into a fist, the fern crozes back down, the great fronds droop and stiffen. Three hours of fist, six of open hand. This is the only fast thing in the place, and it is not fast.

**V. What lives here**

Very little, and it comes and goes.

There is one bird, or one kind of bird — a dull brown thing with an oversized head that arrives during the clear hours, works the gravel apron of one plant or another for the colourless invertebrates, and leaves before the roar. You might see it twice in a day. You might see it not at all.

There are frogs in the warm sheets, but only sometimes, and only small, and they are silent — they cannot compete with the fissures and have apparently given up trying, communicating instead by throat-flashes visible in the diffused light. There is a spider that builds in the skirt of the great rosette and rebuilds after every exhale. There is nothing large. There are no tracks in the peat that are not the bird's.

The emptiness between the nine plants is not sterile. It is simply empty: moss, gravel, water sheet, and space. Standing in the middle of that space during the roar, unable to hear yourself, unable to see either rim, is the closest thing the bowl offers to an experience.

**VI. Traces**

Someone comes here. Not often, and not to stay.

There is nothing built. No wall, no shelter, no hearth ring — nothing burns well enough in this wet to make fire worth the trouble, and there is no wood but the sacred-seeming nine, and those have not been cut.

But: wedged in the fork of the paired hollow plant, at head height, there is a vessel of folded bark stitched with fibre, empty, its inside rinded orange from standing water it once held. There is a hand-axe of green stone on the third bench, worked on both faces and not much used, lying where it was set down. There is a run of notches, twenty-two of them, cut into the skirt-bark of the great rosette, at a spacing that suggests years rather than days, the oldest ones so overgrown they read as scars. And on a flat mineral apron near the outflow notch there are four shallow ground-out hollows in the flowstone, cup-sized, polished — someone knelt there repeatedly and worked something round against the stone, and the stone kept the record because stone here keeps everything.

Whoever it is, they take nothing large and leave nothing new. They arrive during the clear hours, presumably, and I would guess they go up over the rim before the roar begins, because the roar is not something a person sits inside on purpose more than once.

**VII. Rules**

A few things are true here and not elsewhere, and are worth stating plainly:

The bowl is warmer than it has any right to be, and this is the whole reason for everything else. It is a held pocket of low country, a warm room in a cold house, and the nine are refugees from an altitude two thousand feet below.

Nothing hurries. The fern uncoils by the week. The mineral rind thickens by the decade. The scree has not moved in a thousand years. The only rhythm faster than a season is the breath, and the breath is eleven hours long.

Everything that stays still long enough becomes stone, and this is not violent, and takes so long that nothing here experiences it as a threat.

Sound and silence are not distributed as you expect: the loudest hours have the least visible life, and the quiet hours are when the bird comes and the frogs flash and, occasionally, a person.

And the bowl does not want anything. It is not a mouth or a lung, whatever it sounds like. It is a hole in a mountain with hot water under it, doing the only thing its geometry permits, over and over, at its own pace, having long ago arrived at the shape that lets it continue.
