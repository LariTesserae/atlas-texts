## The Standing Field

### Ground

Start with the caprock, because everything else is a guest on it.

It is a pale conglomerate — river gravels from some vanished lowland, lifted whole and cemented by silica into a single sheet, then raised until the sheet became a country. Walk it and you walk on the rounded backs of ten thousand cobbles set in white matrix, each one polished flush with the surface by an unhurried abrasion. The rock does not crumble. It *retreats* — evenly, a hair a century, the way a bar of soap goes down.

The plateau is broad, three days' walk across if you walk badly, and it is not flat. It is *composed*. Long shallow benches step down toward the west in increments of a few meters, each riser weathered to a slope you can ride up. In the middle distance stand the stumps of former buttes, worn to low domes the color of bone and old cream, spaced widely enough that each one owns its own horizon. There is no clutter here. There has been time enough for clutter to be sorted, dissolved, and carried off, and what remains is the residue of that sorting: the durable things, standing far apart.

Between the benches lie basins — closed, shallow, floored in cracked clay. When they hold water they hold it briefly and they hold it *entirely*, a mirror of two hundred meters and four centimeters, and the sky in them is more convincing than the sky.

### The Pins

Across all of it, in a grid whose spacing is roughly eleven paces and which has never once been found to misalign, stand the pins.

They are filaments. Two meters tall, thinner than a pencil, black in the way a hole is black — not a color but an absence of return. They do not sway. Struck with a stone they make no sound at all, not even the tap of the stone, and the stone comes away undamaged and slightly cold.

Every pin is colder than the surrounding air by exactly four degrees. Always. At noon in the hot months, when the caprock will scald a bare palm, the pins are four degrees under the air. In the dark before dawn, when the air has sunk toward frost, they are four degrees under that, too. Nothing powers them. Nothing warms them. They simply hold the offset, and the world arranges itself around this small and permanent impropriety.

And because they do not erode, and the plateau does, each pin now stands on a little pedestal of its own protected ground — a column of soil and pebble a hand's width across and, in the oldest quarters of the field, as much as a meter tall. The pedestals are ranked in perfect rows to the horizon like a cemetery of needles, and their heights are a contour map of how much country has left. In the east, near the scarp, the pedestals are knee-high. In the sheltered western basins they are barely a knuckle. You can read fifty thousand years off the field by walking it and watching the ground rise around your ankles.

### The Drink

Water arrives here in a manner that took the herders three generations to stop calling a miracle and start calling a schedule.

There is little rain. Thunderheads assemble in the afternoons of the wet season, tall and superb, and let down curtains of virga that evaporate a thousand meters up. The rain is *real*; it simply never arrives. Twice a decade it does, and the basins fill, and the trench-river in the north runs brown and quick for nineteen days and then stops mid-sentence, leaving strings of green pools in the scoured bedrock that last until the following year.

The rest of the water comes from the pins, and it comes upward.

In the hour before sunrise the air over the field goes still and slightly luminous, a faint milk that is not fog. Then the dew that lies on the caprock and on the grass leaves the ground. Not evaporating — *rising*, in visible droplets, slowly, all of them at once, a fine sparse rain going the wrong way at the speed of a walking beetle. The droplets find the pins and coalesce on them and run down, and by full light every pedestal has a dark ring of soaked earth around its base, and in that ring is everything green on this plateau.

The herders call it the drink. It takes about eleven minutes. During it the light is horizontal and every rising droplet catches it, so the whole field becomes a slow upward snowfall of sparks, silent, from horizon to horizon, and then it is over and the day is merely bright.

### Light and the Lens

Bright is the wrong word and the only one. At this altitude the air is thin enough that the blue overhead is nearly black, and the sun is not so much a light as an event. Shadows have edges you could cut yourself on. The white caprock throws the glare back up under your hat. Everything is legible to an exhausting degree: you can count the ribs of an animal at four hundred meters, read the grain in a rock you are not going to reach today.

By all rights it should be freezing here. It is warm. It is *pleasant* — shirtsleeve warmth at noon, cool enough for one blanket at night, in a place that should be tundra.

The reason is at the zenith. On very clear afternoons, if you lie on your back and let your eyes unfocus, there is a seam in the sky — a faint circular discontinuity, an inch of arc where the blue on one side is a shade greener than the blue on the other. It is enormous and it is very far up and it caps the entire plateau. Heat goes up through it slowly, at a rate that has been chosen. That is all anyone can say about it. The pins are its footings, or its sensors, or its roots. Or the lens is a side effect of the pins and no one built it as such.

Small consequence of the lens: shadows lag. Not much — perhaps two seconds. Watch a hawk's shadow cross a pale bench and you will see it arrive a beat after it should, still catching up when the hawk has already banked away. Nobody minds. It is like the delay of thunder, and about as informative.

### Silence

The field is quiet in a way that unsettles new arrivals more than any of the rest of it.

Sound does not carry. It does not echo, either — there is nothing for it to bounce off, and when it does meet a butte it comes back thin and stripped of its high end, as if returned through cloth. Speak at conversational volume and a person twenty paces off will see your mouth move. Shout and they will hear you, faintly, and turn. The air carries a suspension too fine to see, something the pins shed or maintain, and it eats the upper registers. Grass moves in wind without hissing. A rockfall on the scarp makes a soft *whumph* like a heavy book closing in another room, and then nothing.

The herders sing here anyway. They have developed a way of singing, very low and very slow, that survives the passage. It sounds like the ground is doing it.

### The Living

And yet the field is thick with animals. This is the part that visitors do not anticipate.

Because water is predictable to the minute and to the centimeter — a ring of damp earth around every pedestal, eleven paces apart, forever — the plateau supports a density of life its rainfall says it should not. The vegetation is sparse in cover and unbelievable in reliability: a low blue-green bunchgrass in a collar around each pin, a fleshy rosette plant that stores the drink in its base, orange crustose lichen mapping the domes, and in the pool-strings of the trench-river a startling ribbon of proper riparian green — sedge, dwarf willow, a fig that has been growing out of the same crack for longer than the ruins have stood.

The grazers are small and stone-colored and come in loose herds of forty or fifty, working the pedestal-collars in a rotation so regular the grass never fails. They are silent. Their hooves are padded. They do not call to each other; they signal with the ears and with a white flash on the rump, and a herd changing direction does it the way a school of fish does, without a sound and without a leader.

The birds are silent too. They are not mute — a chick will cheep, hopelessly, and be found by something. Adults have given it up. They communicate in flight, with wing-patches that flare white and then vanish, so that the sky over the field on a busy morning is full of soundless flickering semaphore, whole conversations conducted at three hundred meters between two specks.

The predator is a long-legged cat the color of the caprock, and it does not need to be quiet, because everything is, and so it hunts by patience and by shadow, and by the two-second lag, which it appears to understand and use.

Insects everywhere. Beetles in the damp rings. A bee that nests inside the hollow of an old pedestal and navigates by the grid. At dusk, moths in numbers that would be a roar anywhere else, and here are a soft pressure against the skin.

### The Marks

People have been here. People come here still, but not many and not often.

There is a stock enclosure on the second bench, a waist-high wall of stacked caprock cobbles laid without mortar, a hundred paces around, three-quarters standing. Inside it, the pedestals have been worn flat by generations of hooves and the grid is a ghost.

There is a hut with a roof — the only roof for a day's walk — and the roof is a woven mat of pins, thousands of them, harvested from somewhere and laid crosswise in a lattice, and it is the coldest shade on the plateau and the water runs off it into a cut stone basin at all hours, and no one knows who first thought to try it.

There are cairns. There is a doorway standing by itself on a dome, cut from a stone that is not local, framing nothing, and the ground inside the doorframe is greener than the ground outside it by an amount that is small and consistent.

There is, on the western rim where the benches step down out of the lens and the air goes suddenly thin and cold, a single low structure of fused white material, seamless, about the size of a chapel, with no opening. Sand has banked against its north side and grass has taken the bank. A cat has been sleeping on its roof, on and off, for years — you can see the polished patch.

### Rules

Water goes up before dawn and down all day.

The pins are always four degrees colder than the air.

Shadows arrive late.

Sound does not travel, so nothing here has a voice worth keeping.

The ground goes down; the pins do not; the difference is the calendar.

And whatever set all of this running has been finished for so long that it is no longer machinery. It is terrain. The birds nest in it, the erosion has come to terms with it, and the herders, walking their animals up the benches in the horizontal light with the sparks rising all around them, do not look up.
