## The Lidded Valley

The land you are standing on is not the top of anything. Two hundred metres above your head there is another floor — the plateau, with its dry grass and its wind — and it is still up there, mostly intact, except that a district of it the size of a small county sagged one autumn about ninety years ago and came down in slabs, and the slabs jammed.

That is the whole geology of this place, and the whole reason for the city.

The cap rock is a hard grey sheet, and when it dropped it broke along joints already present in it, into plates the size of fields. They fell, tilted, caught on each other, and locked — a jam of tables leaning shoulder to shoulder, held by nothing but their own wedging over the hollow beneath. Between them are gaps. Through the gaps comes daylight.

So: a valley with a lid. Four or five kilometres across at the widest, longer than it is wide, narrowing at both ends into darkness where the plates lie flush. Air pale with rock dust. And ranked across it, tilting slowly through the day, thirty or forty columns of light standing from ceiling to floor like the pillars of a building that has no walls.

## Raw ground

Nothing here has been worn.

This is the first thing a visitor's feet learn. The floor is fresh fracture — angular spall, blocks the size of houses, blocks the size of fists, all of it edged. The stone beneath the cap is a pale welded ash, soft enough to cut with a cord and sand, and it breaks in shells and blades. Ninety years is not long enough for rain to round anything. There is no soil. There is no dust that is not the city's own making. There are no gullies, no channels, no fans, no beaches — none of the shapes water makes when it has had time to learn the ground.

Water here has not yet learned the ground. When rain comes through the gaps it falls in vertical columns, hits the rubble, and vanishes into the interstices, or else finds a level place and simply *sits*, in flat sheets a hand deep, going nowhere, because nowhere has been carved for it to go. The sheets last for weeks. They are perfectly still. They hold the light-pillars upside down.

The people call the flat water *dead water*. It is bitter, heavy with lime from the young rock, and undrinkable. It is used for grinding.

## The drip

The drinking water comes from the ceiling.

Warm plateau air slides in through the gaps in the late afternoon and meets the underside of the cap, which is cold, always cold, holding the temperature of buried stone — a steady eleven or twelve degrees, cool enough that you sleep under wool in midsummer. The air gives up its moisture. Dew forms on the rock ceiling in a grey sweat, gathers along the low edges of the tilted plates, and at dusk it begins to fall.

Every evening, for two hours, it rains indoors from a clear ceiling. Not everywhere: along lines. Each plate has its drip-lines where the slope runs, and those lines are the most valuable geography in the world. They are mapped, inherited, litigated. Under each one hangs an apparatus of cords, split-cane gutters, and slabs ground dish-shaped, feeding stone cisterns. A household's standing is measured in cord-length: how much of a drip-line it may hang.

The nine largest storm-columns — the gaps directly overhead where actual rain comes through — are named, and the districts are named after them, and children are said to be *of* Bright Gap or Longspill or the Crooked Third. When surface weather crosses the plateau, the city hears it first as a change of pressure and then sees it as a single grey rope of water descending, roaring in one small circle of the world while everywhere else stays dry.

## The city as infill

There is no room. That is the character of the place more than anything else.

The fallen slabs and the huge blocks take up the floor; the city is what fits between them. Every wedge of space between two leaning masses is walled off with dry-stacked spall and roofed with thin ground plates and lived in, and above that another floor is corbelled out on the same principle, and above that another, until the structure meets the underside of the block and stops. The houses are triangular, trapezoidal, warped — no house is a rectangle, because no gap is. From across the valley the settlement reads as texture: a scale-work of grey and dun filling every crevice of a rockfall, with ladders on the outside, and cords, and small hanging gardens, and no streets visible at all.

The streets are underneath. They are the city's masterwork.

Because the floor is blades, nobody could walk anywhere, and so the first three generations spent themselves grinding roads. Not paving — grinding. Wet sand, and slabs of harder cap rock dragged in harness, back and forth over the same line for years, taking the tops off the spall, cutting a smooth trough two metres wide through the chaos. The Ways are polished. They shine like wet bone in the light-pillars. They wind, they climb over blocks by long ramps, they duck under leaning plates into lamp-lit tunnels and out again. Being smooth, they also drain — the only surfaces in the valley that have been taught what to do with water — and after the dusk drip you can hear the Ways trickling faintly for an hour, the first watercourses this landscape has ever had, and every one of them man-made.

## Quiet, and why

You would expect a stone-working city of forty thousand people to be deafening. It is not. It is the quietest inhabited place imaginable, and this is a rule, and the rule has teeth.

Nobody strikes stone here. No hammering, no chisels, no percussion. The cap above is held up by friction and wedging, and the whole assembly is still settling; the rock is young, stressed, full of closed joints that would rather be open. A hard blow in the wrong place has, twice in living memory, opened a seam and dropped a plate. So the technology is entirely abrasive: cord saws charged with sand, running wet; hardwood wedges driven soft and swelled with dead water until the block parts along its own grain; slurry, leather, patience. The sound of the city's industry is a long, low, wet hissing, a hundred thousand strokes a minute across the valley, no single one of them audible.

Over that, footsteps on polished rock. Water in cane gutters. Voices, but pitched low — loudness is bad manners in the way that spitting is bad manners elsewhere. A shepherd's whistle would carry two kilometres in here.

And under everything: the ticks. The rock talks as it settles, small dry reports at unpredictable intervals, sometimes a groan from a whole plate, sometimes a sifting sound as spall drops out of a joint. There is a guild of Listeners who sleep with an ear to a ground pit and know the difference between a tick that means nothing and a tick that means move your family by morning. They are the only people permitted to be woken at any hour. Twice the city has been abandoned in a night and rebuilt fifty metres over. The old quarters are down there in the dark, crushed flat, still full of cisterns.

## The living part

Ecology has barely arrived and knows it is early.

There is no soil, so all soil is manufactured: ground rock flour, ash, night waste, and above all bird dung, composted in stone bins for two seasons and sold by weight. Where a light-pillar strikes the floor, there is a garden — a walled bed built up on the rubble, and in it onions, a bitter blue-green cabbage, hyssop, beans on strings that climb toward the light and stop when they leave it. Under one of the wider gaps stands the only tree, a fig, ninety years old and eleven metres tall, growing straight up its shaft of sun; it is roped, tended, and eaten from by right of lottery. Everywhere else the vegetation is the pioneer kind: pale mosses lining the drip-lines in long green stripes down the ceiling, a black lichen taking the first hold on the fresh faces, and a grey fern that grows in the fracture cracks where fine dust has caught.

The animals are few and consequential. Swifts nest in the roof gaps — thousands of them, wheeling out at dawn to hunt the plateau air, back at dusk. Their droppings are the fertility of the entire valley and their arrival in spring is the year's great event, and their nesting cracks are as carefully owned as drip-lines. There are pale crickets that came down on their own. There are spiders. Bats in the dark ends, not many; the insects to feed them are not here yet. No fish in the dead water — nothing has yet found a way in. No grazing animal, because there is nothing to graze; no horses, no oxen; everything is moved by rope, ramp, and human back, which is another reason the Ways were worth three generations.

## What goes up

The plateau is still farmed, up there. Grain comes down through the storm gaps in baskets on long lines, and in exchange the city sends up what it is uniquely able to make: flatness. Ground quern stones, whetstones in nine grades, polished slabs for thresholds and altars, and grey-black mirrors of cap rock rubbed to a shine over months, which the surface people prize and cannot make, having no reason to spend a year rubbing anything. The city exports smoothness to a world of rough surfaces. It is the only industry a place with no erosion could possibly have — the inhabitants doing by hand, block by block, what weather has not yet started to do.

The dead are given to the seam. A crack that the Listeners judge to be closing is opened wide enough, the body is laid in with a cord and a cup, and the rock is left to finish. Within a decade the joint tightens and there is nothing to see. It is considered a decent arrangement: the land is still being made, and everyone here contributes.
