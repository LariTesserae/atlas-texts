## The Slake Works

You come to it low, because there is no other way to come. The land here has no ambition: a shallow warm marsh going out flat in all directions until the vapor takes it, so that the horizon is not a line but a softening. Wading is possible in most seasons and never advisable. The water is the temperature of a held hand.

The Works stand on a made rise — three meters of brick rubble, slag, clinker, and old barge ballast, packed and settled over so long that grass-analogues have skinned it and the edges have slumped into scallops. Erosion has finished arguing with this place. The corners of things are rounded. The brick has gone soft at the arrises like old soap; the mortar stands out in ridges because the brick has worn faster. Nothing here is falling down. Everything here has already fallen as far as it intends to, and stopped, and been tucked in by moss.

The footprint is small. Eight streets, if you count the two that are only catwalks over open water. You can walk the whole rise in twenty minutes, though you won't want to walk fast.

**Heat.** It is thirty-eight degrees and the air is at ninety-some percent, and those numbers mean that the air will not accept your sweat. You give off heat by conduction only — by touching cooler things. So the people here touch things constantly: they lean on the north faces of walls, they hold the iron handrails of the condenser galleries, they sit with their forearms flat on wet stone. The whole social grammar of the settlement is arranged around who has rights to which cool surface at which hour. Work happens in the four hours before dawn and the two after the afternoon cloud collapses. The rest of the day belongs to the machines, which do not mind.

**Light.** The Works make their own overcast. Nine pump-stacks and the great chimney exhaust vapor all day into an air that cannot take another gram of it, and so it hangs, a low white lid at forty meters, self-renewing, and the sun arrives as a diffuse brightening somewhere overhead. It is the light of a long late afternoon that never resolves into evening or noon. Shadows exist but have no edges. At the rise's boundary the lid thins and you can see, far out over the marsh, a band of genuine daylight lying on the water like something being kept from you.

Against this, the lamps: caged carbon arcs on the gantries, bluish, buzzing, spaced far apart. Each throws a bright disc of maybe eight meters and then gives up. Walking the tram street at night is a matter of crossing from island to island of light through long warm dark, and the dark is not empty of sound — it is very loud — it is only empty of things.

**Sound.** The baseline is water leaving surfaces. Every roof here is pitched into gutter, every gutter feeds a header, every header a stand-pipe, so that the settlement is a single instrument for the collection of dew, and dew is falling all the time, from every eave and rail and leaf and cable. That gives a fine continuous ticking, like rain that never varies. Over that: the Nine. The pumps run at a slow common beat, a fat percussive thud you feel in the sinuses, with the flywheel-whine layered above it and, every eleventh stroke or so, a sharp iron knock from Number Four that no one has fixed in living memory and which the crews use as a metronome for everything from bread to prayer. Over *that*: the spillway. The unused condensate — the water that has touched the wrong thing, that has failed inspection — goes off the eastern edge in a flume and drops two and a half meters onto slag, and that roar is the loudest thing in the world here and can be heard, they say, four hours' wading away.

The fog does something to sound that the engineers have measured and not explained. Dense vapor banks drift through the streets on the intake cycle, and inside them sound arrives late and slightly wrong. You will hear the eleventh-stroke knock from a bank of fog after it has passed you. You will hear a crew's voices from the place they were standing several minutes ago. It is not an echo. Newcomers find it unbearable. Everyone else finds it useful: you can tell how thick the air has gotten by how much of the last minute you're still hearing.

**Ecology.** Everything green here is lush and nothing green here is fertile.

Moss is the ground truth. It is centimeters thick on every horizontal and most verticals, dark red-green, holding so much water that walking on the brick paths is like walking on saturated carpet, and it has to be stripped from the collecting roofs weekly or it drinks the yield. Stripped moss goes into the flume. On the walls it grows in mats until the mats can no longer hold their own weight and let go — you hear it happen: a wet slap, somewhere, several times a day.

Above the moss, and *sparsely* — this is important, the place is not a jungle — stand the tree ferns. Perhaps forty of them on the whole rise, one to a courtyard, one to a street-corner, each with three or four meters of scaly trunk and a crown that fills its allotted space and touches nothing else. Between them: air. Emptiness with structure. Horsetails colonize the flume margins in dense green pipe-thickets and are cut back every fortnight. In the flooded cellars, algal mats float in slabs, and liverworts crust the tunnel arches in overlapping green thumbprints. There is one large tree, wind-shed and ancient, at the western end, whose species no one agrees on, and its pollen falls in the dry hour and lies yellow on the black water.

None of it seeds. There are no pollinators. Everything reproduces by spore, gemma, runner, or the hands of the crews, who propagate by cutting and have done so for so long that every tree fern on the rise is genetically one tree fern, standing in forty places.

**The absence.** There are no animals.

Not few — none. No flies in a marsh in the heat. No mosquitoes at dusk in air like this. No frogs, no rails, no water-snakes, nothing sculling in the shallows, no rats in the grain store, no dogs, no swallows working the vapor for insects that aren't there. The silence of the fauna is not audible, because the place is deafening, but you feel it as a kind of vertigo. Your body keeps waiting for the sound-layer that ought to be there — the small chirring baseline of any wet hot lowland — and it does not come, and does not come.

The reason is the vent salt. The chimney's fine mineral haze settles over the rise and out about thirty meters into the marsh, and nothing with a tracheal system or a gill crosses it twice. At the levee margin there is a rime. It is grey-brown and slightly glittering and about a finger deep, and it is made of the bodies of insects, arriving out of the marsh forever on the wind, dying at the line. It is swept every morning into barrows and tipped down the flume. The barrows have their own name and the sweeping has its own name and the people who do it are not otherwise remarkable.

The people themselves — sixty-one, currently — are the only animal life the Works permit, and they are exempt for reasons no one is sure of.

**Product.** The Works exist to make unlived water.

Heat drives it out of the marsh; the condenser galleries take it back down; it is collected before it has ever fallen as rain, never having been in a river, a root, a cloud that crossed land, a body. Water with no history. It goes into glass demijohns wrapped in horsetail matting and out by barge, twice a week, to buyers who need water that has not been anywhere, for processes about which the crews are incurious. The animal exclusion is not incidental to this. It is the product specification. A settlement that would be a paradise for mosquitoes has been engineered, at great expense and over a very long time, into a place where nothing can breathe but the people who tend it.

**Rules and cycles.** The Works breathe on a four-hour cycle: intake, saturation, dump. During dump the whole rise steams from its gratings at once, the fog thickens to arm's-length, the sound arrives late, and everyone stops where they are and waits, hands on iron. Around fifteen hundred the lid collapses of its own weight and it rains — hard, briefly, vertically, at blood temperature — and for about forty minutes afterward the air is almost breathable and you can see the real sky, and this is when the second shift eats outdoors and the tree ferns unroll new croziers you can watch move.

The marsh has a pulse that is not tidal, since there is no sea within reach, but is regular: a slow rise and fall of eleven centimeters over about nine days. The cellars flood and drain accordingly. No one has found the mechanism. The pumps are set to it.

And the water runs uphill in the north flume. It has done so since the flume was built, which was long enough ago that the fact is not discussed with visitors, only demonstrated, once, with a floating chip of teak, and then the conversation is changed.
