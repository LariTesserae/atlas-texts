## Sough

You come at it downhill, which is the only way it can be come at. The land here does not so much fall as give — a long soft concession in the grain of the country, three fields wide, until the ridge lets go and you are looking into a saucer maybe four hundred paces across, brimful of vapour, with light in it the colour of weak tea and a sound coming up out of it like something very large breathing through a mouthful of gravel.

That is the whole of the place. It has boundaries you can walk in a quarter-hour. It is a town, entirely, and there is nothing else in it.

---

The rim is a spoil-berm so old it has become a hill in good faith: heather-less, but shagged over with willowherb and horsetail, and terraced by generations of small slippages into steps you can actually use. Water comes out of it everywhere. Not springs — nothing so decisive — just a general seeping, the berm sweating out what the basin's own exhalation put into it the week before. Put your palm to the cut face and it beads under your hand like a cold glass in July.

Below, the roofs. They are packed in so close that the streets read as seams rather than spaces, and they are all low, and they are all layered: slate over corrugate over slate again, tarred, mossed, patched with hammered-flat cans, and each roof set slightly askew to its neighbour so the whole quarter has the look of a shuffled deck left out in the rain. Between them rise the four chimneys and the seventeen vents, and out of every one of them comes a slow white rope of steam that does not climb far. Forty feet up, the steam meets the cool air coming down off the berm and gives up. It spreads. It becomes the ceiling.

So: dim. Always dim. There is an hour around noon when the ceiling thins and you get a pale smear overhead like a coin under milk, and the townspeople call that hour *the reading*, because it is when you can read out of doors. Otherwise the light in Sough comes from below and from the sides — carbon-arc in wire cages at the street corners, the orange gape of firehole doors, the standing lamps over the combs, and, low down where the duckboards meet the water, a faint greenish business in the rot that is fungus and is genuinely luminous, and is left alone for that reason.

---

The town stands on a raft. Peat here goes down twenty-two feet before it finds anything with an opinion, and so everything — mill, chapel, guild hall, the terrace of eleven houses on Tin Walk — is footed on larch piles driven a century and a half ago and kept alive by being permanently drowned. Nothing wooden in Sough is allowed to be half-wet. Wholly wet lasts forever. Half-wet lasts nine years.

The streets are duckboard and iron grating laid over running water, and they are named for their sound. Tin Walk rings. Slate Walk knocks. The Hollow Mile, which is two hundred paces long, booms softly under the heel and is the way you find your bearings when the vapour comes down thick, which is most evenings. There are drip-listeners here who can place themselves in the town to within ten feet, blindfolded, by the pitch of water falling from a gutter onto zinc versus onto brick versus into the open channel. It is not a mystical skill. It is just a thing a person learns in a place where sight is the third-best sense.

The brick is a local clinker, fired from the works' own ash, and its colour is a dark mauve you will not see elsewhere. You will rarely see it here either, because every wall carries between one and four inches of moss, stratified — the older, drier upper courses in grey cushion, then a broad band of feathery green, then at the base, in the permanent splash zone, a black liverwort like spilled paint. You can read the height of the last long wet season off any south wall in town the way you read a flood mark.

And the town is sinking. Slowly, honestly, at about the rate a fingernail grows: the raft settles, the peat compresses, the leaf-litter and the ash and the spent comb-scale accumulate over it, and Sough answers by building up. Every doorway on Slate Walk is a half-doorway, sawn down and re-lintelled at some point. The mill's ground floor is its cellar and its cellar is its sump. Below the current street, in the dark, there are two more streets, full of black water, and these are called the underdays, and children are told stories about them, and adults, who have pumped in them, tell fewer.

The town treads water. It has been treading water for a long time and has got very good at it.

---

The works is called the Combery, and what it does is take water out of the air.

Four low sheds, brick to shoulder height and glass and lead above, each holding forty ranks of copper fin-comb: vertical arrays of blade-thin plates, oxidised to that particular soft green, set a knuckle apart, so that a shed's interior looks like a library where all the books are made of verdigris. Cool brine circulates in the frames. The vapour walks through — it is persuaded through, by the great slow bellows-tunnels under the floor — and it comes out the far end thin and cold, and what it leaves behind runs down the fins and collects in the leaded gutters and goes to the cisterns, and the cisterns are the town's whole reason.

Sough water is heavy, faintly sweet, and holds nothing that ever lived. It goes up the terraces in green glass to buyers who use it for photographic work, for the manufacture of certain glasses, and for medicines. That is the export. That, and pulp.

The engines that drive the bellows and the brine pumps are beam engines and they are named Patience, Marrow, Susan, and the Sixth. There is no fifth. Nobody in town can tell you where the fifth went and the question is considered slightly rude.

They run at one stroke per forty-one seconds.

This is the governing fact of the place. A stroke begins as a rising note in the floor, becomes a shudder you feel in your teeth, then the beam comes over with a sound like a cathedral door closing carefully, and then the exhale — the vast wet chuff into the bellows-tunnel that shakes moss off every wall within a hundred feet — and then thirty seconds of *almost*, filled by drip, by the hiss of the reheaters, by the shriek of the pulp-cutters in the west shed which run fast and are the only fast thing here. Then it begins again. It is loud. It is very loud; you learn to talk in the trough of it and shout in the crest, and everyone from Sough speaks elsewhere in a rhythm that outsiders find odd and slightly hypnotic.

The engines are never stopped. Cold means condensation inside the cylinders where it isn't wanted, means scale, means the fins closing up in a week with mineral fur. In a hundred and forty years the stroke has been interrupted eleven times and each occasion has a name.

---

The vegetation is the surprise, if you have been told this is an industrial town. Sough is warm — the exhaust and the vapour ceiling make a glasshouse of the basin — and it is wetter than the country around it by half again, and so it is *green* to an almost improper degree. Ferns come out of every bolt-hole and weep-hole in the Combery walls: hart's-tongue, maidenhair, a soft-shield fern that grows on the lagging of steam pipes and dies back in the winter draw-off and returns. The channel banks are solid figwort and butterbur and marsh horsetail, chest-high and holding. There are willows along the northern pumphouse that were planted for withies and were then absorbed — pipes run through them, they have grown around brackets, and it is no longer sensible to say where the hedge stops and the plant stops.

The gantries wear bracket fungus in shelves the size of dinner plates. The town, in a modest way, eats its own scaffolding, and the timber crews' work is entirely a matter of replacement rather than repair.

And there are the allotments — six terraces on the sunward berm, under lamps, growing beans and orach and a coarse reed for the pulp-cutters.

---

Here is the thing about Sough that takes a day or two to surface in a visitor, and then cannot be un-noticed.

There is nothing alive here that moves on its own.

No bird crosses the vapour ceiling. No midge over the channels, though the channels are still and warm and ought to be a nursery. No slug in the allotments, no rat in the underdays, no spider in the corner of any window in the eleven houses on Tin Walk. Nothing. The absence is old — older than the works, by the town's own account; the Combery was built here *because* of it, because water condensed in a basin where nothing breeds comes out clean, comes out empty, comes out worth money. The reason is not known. It is respected. There is a stated wrong to it in the way people say *the basin doesn't keep animals*, the way you'd say a certain well doesn't keep sweet.

Consequences, all of them practical:

The dead things do not get eaten, only rotted, and rotting without insects is slow. So the litter builds. The peat thickens under the raft faster than it should. The town sinks faster than it should. Absence of a beetle, translated over two centuries into masonry.

Nothing here is made of animal. No leather aprons — waxed reed-cloth. No wool, no horn buttons, no bone-handled anything, no hide glue in any joint in any building. The soles are gutta-percha. The brushes are teasel head and cattail down. It is not a taboo, quite. It is simply that nothing arrives, and what arrives from outside is regarded as a curiosity, and a little unlucky.

And the pollination is done by hand and by machine. This is the strangest sight in Sough and the one everyone comes for. On the allotment terraces, at the fourth hour, the bellows shift comes on, and a set of low canvas-and-cane fans on a shared crankshaft — driven off Susan by a long belt that runs up the berm in a wooden trough — begins to beat, one beat per four seconds, back and forth over the beds, making a wind where there is no wind. The bean flowers nod. Behind the fans walk the featherers, four or five of them, with cattail wands, touching flower to flower, unhurried, working in the dim under the lamps, saying nothing because at that distance from the engines nothing can be said. They will be at it for three hours. They have been at it, in shifts, in that spot, for a hundred and forty years.

In the guild hall there is a bell-jar on a plinth, and under the bell-jar, on a card, dry as paper, is a stag beetle. It came here in 1861 in a crate of glassware, already dead. It is the only animal in Sough. Children are taken to see it. They report that it looks like a machine.

---

Rules of the place, as an inhabitant would give them if you asked and they had a reason to answer:

Nothing dries; grade everything by wetness and act accordingly. What you drop, you lose — the gratings take it, the peat keeps it. Never let a wooden thing be half-wet. Don't go down to the underdays alone, not for any ghostly reason, but because the pumps are on a slow cycle and the water comes back up on its own schedule. Talk in the trough. And the stroke does not stop; if the stroke stops, come running, whoever you are, whatever you were doing.

---

Nothing much happens quickly here, and that is not a complaint. The gradients are all weak: temperature varies four degrees across a year, the light varies mostly by whether the ceiling is two hundred feet up or forty, the water table rises and falls a hand's breadth with the seasons and is otherwise exactly where it has always been. Change in Sough is real but it operates at the pace of moss and settlement and the slow mauve accretion of brick on brick. A person born here can, at sixty, point to a lintel they remember as a step.

Stand at the rim at dusk — you will not see the sun set, only the tea-coloured light draining out of the vapour and the arc-lamps coming up as the only remaining fact. The basin fills with white. The four chimneys stand out of it. The roofs go under. And every forty-one seconds the whole white lid of the town shivers, and the sound comes up the berm and through the soles of your feet, and in the pause afterward, in the thirty seconds of drip and hiss, your ear goes looking — helplessly, permanently, the way an ear will — for a bird.
