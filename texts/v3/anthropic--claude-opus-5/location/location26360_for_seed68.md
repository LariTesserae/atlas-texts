## Nýgres — the Wool Works at the Low Cape

**The ground.**

The land here is sixty-one years old, which is to say it has not yet learned any of the habits of land.

It came up out of shallow water in a single season — not violently, no ash column, no burning sea, only a long low welling of lava under two fathoms of cold water, hissing for eleven weeks and then done. What it made was a plain of black basalt about two kilometres long and a kilometre wide, joined to the older coast by a neck of rubble. Everything about it is still sharp. The lava froze in ropes and coils and buckled slabs, and the edges of those slabs have not been rounded off by anything except lichen and boot-leather. If you fall on it, it takes skin. Along the seaward margin the flow ends in a low black cliff, six to nine metres, and the cliff is not worn back into a smooth face — it is still all the shapes of the moment it stopped: lobes, drips, an overhang like a curled lip, a cave the size of a stairwell where a gas pocket burst.

Underfoot the rock is full of bubbles. That matters more than it sounds. When the wind comes across the plain from the west at any speed above a walking pace, the vesicled surface takes it and makes a note — not a whistle exactly, a broad grey humming, the sound of blowing across a hundred thousand bottle-mouths at once, pitched somewhere below a cello. The locals call it *the floor*. On still days the floor is quiet and the town feels wrong, thin, as though a bass line has dropped out of a song.

Elevation is nothing much. The plain lifts perhaps forty metres to a spine of scoria at its centre, and from that spine you can see the whole of the place, which is the point: this is a landscape you can hold entirely in one look.

**The water.**

There is not much rain, but there is almost never dryness. The air off the cold water carries a permanent load of moisture that it deposits by contact rather than by falling — on stone, on wire, on wool, on the backs of hands. Fog comes in most mornings and lies in the hollows of the flow until ten or eleven. Where it burns off you can watch the black rock steam. Actual rain arrives maybe eight days a month, brief and thin and slantwise.

So there are no rivers. Water on new lava doesn't run; it goes straight down through the bubbles and the cooling cracks and travels underneath, and comes out again at the base of the cliff as a row of small cold springs that only show at low tide, and as one proper upwelling inland — a round pool three metres across in a collapse pit, so clear the black walls of it look polished, so cold it aches. That is the town's water. It has never failed and it has never varied and nobody entirely trusts it.

Everything else that holds water here holds it in the manner of a lens or a bead: condensation strung along spiderweb, along the horsetails, along the wire fences, along the stone fibres. On a good grey morning the whole plain glitters at ankle height and nowhere above it.

**The vegetation.**

Lush, but not continuous — and that combination is the strangest thing about looking at the place.

New lava colonises in patches. Wherever a hollow caught wind-blown grit, a mat began, and those mats are now sixty years deep in growth and absolutely riotous: woolly grey-green *Racomitrium* moss a hand's-breadth thick, so soft it swallows a footprint and holds it for a month; then in the older, damper hollows, dwarf willow and downy birch in thickets no taller than a person's shoulder but dense as fleece; angelica with stems as thick as wrists; ferns; nettles; a purple sprawl of lupine that somebody's grandmother introduced and nobody can now remove. Cotton-grass in the wet seams. The green is loud green, saturated, fed on fog.

And then, two metres away, bare black glass-edged rock with nothing on it at all, and another twenty metres of that, and then another hollow going off like a firework.

So the plain reads as an archipelago of small jungles in a black sea. Sparse and lush at the same time. You walk from room to room. Each thicket is its own weather — warmer, wetter, louder with insects, smelling of leaf-rot and angelica sap — and between them you are out on open stone in the wind with the floor humming under you.

**The fauna.**

Abundant to the point of nuisance, and this is why the place is loud.

The cliff holds a colony: fulmars mostly, with kittiwakes on the lower ledges, and the noise off it is continuous and enormous — a wet cackling mass of sound that rises and falls but never stops, day or night, from April to August. On the plain itself, terns. Thousands. They nest on the open rock between the thickets, which is exactly where the paths are, and from late May any human crossing the plain does so under attack, hunched, holding a stick up, in a screaming white cone. Snipe drum over the wet seams at dusk — that eerie bleating made with tail feathers. Redwings sing from the birch scrub with a fluting three-note phrase repeated until it stops meaning anything. Midges in the lee of the thickets, in columns, in numbers that make a hum of their own.

Then: eider along the shore in rafts, purring. Seals on the outer skerry. Arctic foxes, blue-morph, that have learned the tern colony is a supermarket and the works canteen is a better one. Rabbits, introduced, doing well. In the pool, no fish at all — nothing has reached it — but a population of pale amphipods, translucent, that arrived somehow and now swim in it in their thousands, so the drinking water is faintly alive.

**The works.**

At the neck of the plain, where the rubble bridge comes over from the old coast, there is a town of about four hundred people and one factory, and the factory is the reason for all of it.

They spin the rock. Basalt goes into a cupola furnace at fifteen hundred degrees and comes out as a thread of orange liquid, and the thread is dropped onto a set of steel wheels turning at very high speed, and centrifugal force throws it off as fibre — mineral wool, for insulation, packed into bales and shipped out on the two-a-week boat. The raw material is the ground itself. They quarry the flow from a face at the plain's centre, a fresh vertical cut in the scoria spine, and the cut is the only truly ugly thing here and also the only place you can see the inside of the world: fifty layers of orange-black clinker stacked like a badly made pastry.

The works is small — one stack, one furnace hall, a compressor house, a bale shed, a rail of narrow-gauge track running out to the quarry face on which a diesel locomotive the size of a car shuttles hoppers back and forth. It is electrified off its own generator; the light spills yellow out of the furnace hall's windows all night and paints the wet moss for thirty metres around.

It is very loud. The furnace draught roars. The spinners scream at a frequency that arrives as pressure rather than sound. The compressor thumps every eleven seconds, and the thump goes into the vesicled rock and travels, so that the whole plain is faintly percussed, so that people two kilometres away feel it in the soles of their boots and don't notice they feel it. Between the compressor thump, the floor's humming, the fulmar cliff, and the terns, there is no quiet anywhere in this landscape and there never has been in living memory. Visitors sleep badly for a week and then sleep better than they ever have.

And the fibre gets out. It always gets out. Fine white stone-hair drifts from the bale shed on the west wind and settles across the plain, catching in the moss, hanging in the willow scrub, beading up with condensation so that certain thickets on certain mornings appear to be full of grey glass wigs. It doesn't rot. It doesn't burn. It doesn't blow away once it's tangled. Sixty years of it has worked down into the moss mats as a permanent white stratum, and if you cut a spade into a hollow you find it there, layered, like a false snowline in the soil.

The birds use it. Every fulmar ledge, every tern scrape, every redwing cup in the birch is built of stone wool, because it is the best nesting material anyone has ever offered a bird: warm, waterproof, structural, and eternal. The cliff face is quilted with old nests that will still be there in five hundred years. Ornithologists come in summer and count them like tree rings.

**The rule of the place.**

The land is still rising. About eleven millimetres a year — nobody is sure whether it is the flow settling and compacting unevenly, or the crust rebounding, or something under the seabed still filling; the geologists have three papers and no agreement. The effect is not dramatic. It is a slow gradient, a patient process, a thing you can only see if you keep records, and Nýgres keeps records obsessively.

There is a tide-post at the jetty with a brass mark cut for every year since 1963, sixty-one marks climbing a metre and a half up the timber, and the current sea reaches nowhere near the top ones. The harbour keeps shallowing. Twice now they have cut the channel deeper, with a dredge barge and, the second time, with explosives, and there is a standing town levy to do it again.

More than that: nothing here is built on a foundation. Every structure in the town — houses, the school, the canteen, the furnace hall itself, all of it — stands on screw jacks on pads laid directly on the lava. It has to, because the plain does not rise evenly; some hollows lift faster, some slabs tilt, and a house set solid would crack apart inside twenty years. So instead, every spring, after the frost has gone out, the whole town goes around with a surveyor's level and a long bar and re-levels itself. It takes four days. Children hold the staff. It is treated as a holiday, with food, and it is the only time all year the works shuts down, and for those four days the terns and fulmars and the humming floor have the sound of the place entirely to themselves, which everyone says they find beautiful and unbearable in roughly equal measure.

The saying is: *we are screwed down, not planted.*

Standing on the scoria spine at midday in the ordinary bright grey light of the region — a light with no drama in it, even, sourceless, honest, showing every colour exactly as it is — you see all of it at once. The black plain. The scattered green islands going off like small explosions of chlorophyll. The white drift of stone-hair hanging in the willow. The single stack with its heat-shimmer. Terns above the whole thing in a loose white blizzard. And the sea, which used to be here, and which is now getting slowly, patiently, imperceptibly further away.
