## The Weeping Shelf

### 1. Approach

From the lowlands there is a wall the colour of old teeth, and it does not end. It runs to the left until haze eats it and runs to the right until the curve of the world does. Two thousand metres of it, in places three. Water comes off the lip in a hundred thousand places at once — not falls, mostly, but *seeps*, so that the whole face glistens and steams and lets down a permanent drizzle that never quite reaches the bottom. The cliff sweats. Caravans at the base travel under oiled canopies, in a warm mist that smells of iron and boiled eggs.

Climb the switchback ramps — they are cut into the cliff and then, over centuries, cemented back over, so that half the road is a tunnel through its own dripstone — and you come out on top into hard bright air, and the plateau begins, and it does not stop for a thousand kilometres.

### 2. What it's made of

Everything up here is stone laid down by water: travertine, tufa, gypsum crusts, banded aragonite. The rock is pale — bone, cream, ochre where iron got into it, grey-black where sulphur did. Where it has been freshly wetted it is the colour of a candle held up to a lamp, and you can see three or four centimetres into it.

The land is a staircase built by dumb chemistry. Hot water comes up out of the deep, loaded with dissolved limestone; it cools, it degasses, and it drops that limestone wherever it slows. So the plateau is terraces — rimstone pools, *gours*, scalloped basins with lips a hand's-breadth high or a house high, stepping down and across in fans and lobes tens of kilometres wide. From a high point they look like contour maps of themselves. From inside them you are walking on a floor of white saucers, ankle-deep, warm as bathwater, each one holding a still round of sky.

Between the terrace fields: dissolution country. Old rock, rained on for a very long time. Flutes and blades and sinkholes, dry valleys, natural bridges, karren so sharp it cuts boots. Nothing here is being made; it is being taken away. The two processes have been trading the same material back and forth long enough to come to terms. The Shelf is not growing and not shrinking. It is turning over in place, one grain at a time, and has been doing so since before anyone's records begin, which is a long while, because the records here are very good.

### 3. Air and water

The Shelf makes its own weather and it is not complicated weather. Geothermal heat pushes warm humid air up all day; it hits the cold thin sky at altitude; it condenses. So there is a lid of white cloud over the plateau from roughly the third hour to the ninth, and beneath the lid it is warm, close, bright with diffuse glare, and everything drips. In the late afternoon the lid tears and the sun comes through hard and low and the whole terrace field ignites — a million pools all going gold at once — and this is the hour that everyone here treats as the real hour of the day.

At night it rains, softly, nearly always. Not weather; plumbing.

The water is warm, alkaline, faintly bitter, and full of minerals. It will kill you slowly if you drink it untreated and cement your kettle if you boil it. Every household has a still. Every district has a condenser tower. The rooftops are all sloped to catch the honest rain, which is the only water anyone drinks.

### 4. The base of everything

There are no plants. Not none-as-in-few; none. No wood, no leaf, no grass, no root. Nobody here has a word for *forest* that isn't borrowed from lowland trade-speech and mispronounced.

What there is instead is the **mat**.

Every warm pool, every seep-runnel, every wet flat surface on the Shelf is upholstered in microbial mat, and it is layered like a book: a top skin of green-black photosynthesizers, then a purple layer, then a rust layer, then a black anoxic sludge that smells of the underworld. It grows a few millimetres a year. It is tough as leather when dried and it can be cut in bricks with a spade.

That is the whole primary production of a continent — a film, thinner than a coat, over half a million square kilometres of warm wet rock. But half a million square kilometres is a great deal, and the mat never stops, and it never has a winter.

Above it, the invertebrates. Brine shrimp in the salt pans in densities that turn water into soup. Larval flies packed shoulder to shoulder in the runnels, a hundred thousand to the square metre, so that the runnels look like they are lined in dark felt until you touch them and the felt boils. Stilt-crabs, palm-sized, grazing the terrace lips in files. Something like a shrimp and something like a woodlouse and something no one has bothered to classify because it is simply *the small grey grazing thing* and there are trillions of it.

And above that: **birds**.

### 5. Overwhelming

The Shelf holds more bird biomass than any other place. It is not close.

They are mostly waders and filter-feeders — long-legged, long-billed, built for standing in warm shallow water and sieving. Colonies occupy whole terrace fields: a hundred square kilometres of standing birds, so dense that from the air the pools look feathered rather than watered. There are flocks that take four hours to pass overhead. There are colonies whose collective sound has been measured and found to be constant to within a few percent across the entire year, day and night, decade after decade — a sound like heavy surf with voices in it, a white roar that rises and dips but never, ever stops.

You do not get used to it. You stop noticing that you haven't got used to it. People raised on the Shelf speak loudly everywhere they go for the rest of their lives, and are known abroad for it.

The birds nest on the terrace rims, which they reinforce with their own droppings and which the water then cements, so that the rims are partly bird-built and the birds are partly rim-built, and this arrangement has been going on so long that the pattern is baked into the geology in bands you can read in any road-cut.

There are predators — a lean hot-blooded runner with a beak like a hatchet, various sky-thieves — but they are noise in the signal. The signal is: mat, fly, shrimp, bird, guano, mat.

### 6. Aggrade

The cities sit on the drier karst spurs between terrace fields, where the water is beneath you rather than around you. There are perhaps forty of them. The largest is called **Aggrade**, which is a geological term and also an instruction.

It is built entirely of stone, bone, shell, chitin, feather, and metal. Not one stick of wood. Walls of sawn travertine, roofs of gypsum plate on iron ribs, window-panes of selenite where they can't afford glass. Rope from feather-quill fibre and mat-cord. Furniture cast in a hard yellow-brown plastic rendered from insect shell, which every household smells faintly of when the weather turns. Insulation of packed feather-down, which is the cheapest material in the world here and would be a luxury anywhere else.

The fuel is **mat-coal**: fossil microbial mat, black and lustrous and slightly sulphurous, mined in seams out of the old terrace stacks. It burns hot and dirty. Along with it, direct geothermal — bore-holes and steam mains and long copper condensers running down the hillsides like ribs, feeding turbines and pumps and the great calcining kilns that turn travertine into quicklime, which is the Shelf's chief export and chief building material and chief cause of white-lung.

So: chimneys. A skyline of them, brick-banded, canted slightly where the ground has crept. Steam plumes standing straight up in the morning calm and lying flat by afternoon. Ore-trams on cable runs across the sinkholes. Guano works downwind of everything, where nitrate is boiled out for fertilizer and explosive and shipped down the cliff ramps to a lowland world that needs both. Pump-houses everywhere, because the water table is a metre down and rising slightly every year, and the city's whole relationship with the ground is a negotiation about drainage.

The streets are washed constantly, by machine and by hand, because if you don't wash a street here it turns to rock.

### 7. The slow rule

This is the thing that shapes every life on the Shelf, and it is not dramatic, and it never stops:

**The ground rises.** Between four and nine millimetres a year, depending on the district. Everything wet gets encrusted; everything encrusted gets thicker.

So Aggrade is built in layers, and everyone knows it, and everyone plans for it. Ground floors are constructed as sacrifices — high-ceilinged, plainly finished, generously doored — because in a hundred and fifty years they will be cellars, and in three hundred they will be solid. The good rooms are on the second and third storeys. Staircases are added to the outside of buildings as the outside slowly comes up to meet the first-floor windows. Every important doorway has, carved beside it, a column of small horizontal marks with dates: the flood-marks of the patient stone.

There are streets in Aggrade whose paving is nine metres above their original paving, and beneath them, in the fill, is the whole earlier street: shopfronts, gutters, an iron bollard, a dog's footprint, all of it turned to travertine and perfectly preserved, because that is what travertine does. Miners cut lime out of their own great-grandparents' neighbourhoods. It is considered a normal profession and only mildly morbid. The city's historians are, functionally, quarrymen.

Nothing here is urgent. A civic project may be planned over four generations, because the deadline is set by chemistry and chemistry is honest about its schedule. The calendar has a unit for *the time it takes a doorstep to become a step*.

### 8. The one strange thing

The mats change colour. Not seasonally — there is no season. In waves.

A given terrace field will hold steady for years, olive-black, and then over some weeks shift to a deep rust, and hold that. And the field next to it will shift a month later. And the one beyond that, a month after. The wave moves across the continent at roughly eleven kilometres a day, always in the same direction, always taking about four months to cross from the eastern scarp to the western, and it comes about once every three years, and it has been doing this for as long as there have been observers with dates.

Nobody knows why. It has been studied. There are papers. The chemistry of the shift is entirely understood; what is not understood is the *scheduling*, since there is no plausible signal that could travel that slowly and that steadily across that much disconnected water.

The people of the Shelf call it **the long blush**, treat its arrival as a minor festival, and do not lose sleep over it. In a landscape where the ground is quietly turning your house into a fossil, a stain that keeps an appointment is not the strangest thing you know.

### 9. Standing there

Late afternoon. You're on a terrace rim outside the city, on a spur of white rock, ankle-deep in warm water that is depositing a microscopic layer of itself on your boots.

The cloud lid has torn. The light comes in flat and gold, and it goes down into the pools and comes back up again, and there are ten thousand pools in front of you, stepping away in curves, each rimmed in bone-white, each floored in rust and green-black, each holding birds. Steam lies in the low ground in long soft bars. Behind you the chimneys of Aggrade are pushing up their straight columns, and the sound of the pumping engines — a slow four-beat, *hah, hah, hah, hah* — comes through underneath the roar of the colony, which is the sound of a sea that has no shore and no tide.

Nothing green. Nothing rustles. There is no smell of leaves, no shade cast by anything that grew. The air smells of sulphur and wet lime and hot iron and, faintly, of the ammoniac warmth of an enormous number of animals.

Everything you can see is the residue of water doing something slow and doing it forever. In three hundred years someone will stand nine metres above where you are standing, on a rock that has your boot-marks in it, and it will look almost exactly the same, and it will be exactly as loud.
