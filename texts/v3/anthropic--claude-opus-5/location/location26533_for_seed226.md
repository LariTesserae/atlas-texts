## The Welt

At this altitude the air is thin enough that you taste the wet in it before you feel it. Cloud lies below, a white floor with slow holes in it, and above the cloud rises a single dome of grey-blue rock about six hundred paces across — smaller than a village's field, though it takes an hour to cross because nothing on it is level and nothing on it is finished.

The dome is new. Not new as a landslide is new; new as bread is new. It is a blister of stone still swelling out of the mountain's back at the rate of two hand-widths a year, and everything about it declares its youth. There are no valleys. There are no ridges to speak of, no gullies, no talus fans, no smooth places. The rock surface is a chaos of unbroken curves and razor edges: glassy bulges where it cooled fast, ropy coils, hollow bubbles the size of a cook-pot that crunch underfoot like eggshell. Nowhere has water yet worn a path. Nowhere has frost yet found a joint. The whole dome is raw, first-draft geology — the rock equivalent of a face before expression has creased it.

In places, if you put your palm flat, it is warm.

### Water that does not know where to go

Rain comes most afternoons, and every night the cloud rises and condenses on the stone until the dome sweats. Water is everywhere here, and this is the strange part: it goes nowhere. A mature mountain sheds water the way a hand sheds sand — down grooves it has spent ten thousand years learning. The Welt has learned nothing. Water arrives on its back and sheets, spreads, doubles back, finds a shallow depression and sits.

So the summit of this cold high place is a scatter of perched bogs. Blind ponds with no inlet and no outlet, black-bottomed, skinned with ice at dawn. Sheets of standing water half a thumb deep spread across whole acres of glassy rock, shivering, catching the sun so that the dome flashes white when the wind crosses it. Marsh-edge conditions at four thousand paces up: sedge tussocks with their feet in cold clear water, sphagnum swelling in the seams, everything saturated, nothing draining.

Water that stands here does not stay harmless. That is the first thing the people will tell you, before your name.

### The rule

The rule of the Welt is this: **the first line is the only line.**

Whatever route water takes across new stone the first time it flows, it keeps forever. The rock accepts one instruction. A trickle that finds its way down a fresh bulge will deepen along exactly that path for the next ten thousand years and will not be persuaded elsewhere, no matter how much later water is poured to argue with it. On any older mountain this is merely how erosion works, slowly, statistically. On the Welt it is law, immediate and absolute, and the law is visible: where water has gone once, the stone is already a finger deep and hardening into its groove; where water has never gone, the surface is untouched forever and will shed a flood without taking a scratch.

Which means the Welt can be *written*. Which means someone must.

### The town

That is what the built thing is for.

Spread over the dome — over all of it, edge to edge, so that from the cloud below it looks like a city crown — is a work of dry-laid stone masonry of extraordinary density and extraordinary emptiness. Channels. Thousands of paces of them. Gutters cut a hand deep and lined with fitted slabs. Aqueducts on stubby piers walking across a bulge on twenty short legs. Stepped weirs, each step a palm high, so that falling water is broken and rebroken and cannot punch out a plunge-pool where none is wanted. Cisterns roofed with corbelled stone. Sluice gates of hardwood hauled up from the forest three days below, greased with fat, swollen tight. Settling basins. Splitter-forks where a stream is divided into three lesser streams, each sent off along its own decided course. Distribution combs of thirty parallel grooves, no wider than a finger, fanning water thin across a slope that must be kept from ever concentrating a flow.

Between and above the waterworks: the dwelling-town. Terraces of dry stone, courses laid without mortar, every block a spall struck from the dome itself, so that the settlement is made entirely of the mountain's own removed material — the town and the channels are one act, the negative and the positive of the same chisel. Stairs everywhere, hundreds of flights, because there are no natural slopes to walk. Retaining walls holding pockets of hauled soil, each pocket the size of a bath. Wind-lees. Roofed galleries where the stone dries out enough to sleep. Storehouses. A bathhouse, one, generous, echoing. Chambers whose purpose you cannot guess, walled and floored and empty, with a stone bench and a drain.

It is urban. It has infrastructure, plumbing, quarters, zoning, a plan. It has room for two thousand people.

Twenty-two live here.

### Density

You can stand in the middle of the town at midday and see no one. You can walk a thousand paces of stepped street and pass no one. The scale of the place is small — you could shout across the whole dome if the water were quieter — and yet you will spend a whole day and meet three people, each alone, each far off, each doing something patient with a string and a chisel.

This is not ruin. Nothing is fallen; nothing is overgrown that isn't meant to be. The town is maintained, swept, greased, mortarless and true. It is simply mostly *ahead* of its inhabitants. The gutterwrights build for the dome the Welt will be in nine hundred years, when it has risen another three hundred paces and thrown out new lobes east and west, and when — if the lines have been written correctly — it will carry standing forest and permanent villages and a river with a name. The empty chambers are for people not yet born. The unfinished aqueduct on the north side ends in mid-air because the rock it will reach has not yet emerged.

Twenty-two people, and a work-plan measured in millennia, and a rule that forgives no error. So they work slowly. A gutterwright may spend a season on nine paces of channel, checking the fall with a bowl of water and a thread, cutting a hair at a time, because water let in early would be a decision no one could ever unmake.

Their tools are hand tools: copper chisels that go blunt in an hour on new stone and are mostly kept for wood; better, splinters of the dome's own hardest glass, hafted, struck with a stone maul. Fiber rope. Wooden wedges soaked to split rock. Bone-and-thread levels. Charcoal for marking. No wheel would be any use here. Everything is carried.

### Loud

The Welt is not quiet. It is one of the loudest small places you will ever stand in, and almost none of the noise is alive.

Water first: every channel is a voice. The stepped weirs chatter; the distribution combs hiss like rain on leaves; the fall on the south lip roars into cloud and never stops, so that the town has a sea in it always. Under the roar, close in, the sound of stone drains gulping, of overflow slopping, of a thousand small threads of water talking in cut rock.

Then the dome itself. New rock cooling and swelling makes noise. All day and all night the Welt *ticks* — sharp, bright, glassy pings, single and unpredictable, some so close it seems to come from the sole of your boot. At dawn, when the ice-rim on the pools lets go and the sun hits the wet, the ticking becomes a fusillade. And two or three times a month there is a deep report from somewhere below, a struck-drum sound with no echo, and everyone stops, and afterwards someone walks a long slow route with a bowl of water to see what has changed.

Wind, over so much bare curved rock, hums. Some of the older combs and grooves have begun to whistle when it comes from the west, which the gutterwrights consider an unintended and rather beautiful mistake in the score.

Human sound is almost none of it: the double knock of maul on chisel, carrying far; a voice, once, calling a number.

### Green

For a place with no soil, it is startlingly lush.

Nothing grows in earth here; everything grows on wet stone, and the stone is always wet. Every north-facing curve is upholstered in moss six inches deep, so saturated that walking on it is like walking on a soaked sponge. Liverworts plate the shadows in overlapping green scales. Filmy ferns, translucent, one cell thick, hang in the spray-zones by the falls in curtains you can read through. In the seams and joints: cushion plants, sedge, saxifrage with pink flowers no bigger than a grain, and a low twisted shrub the people call thumbwood, which puts out one new branch every eleven or twelve years and is used only for tool handles and grave-marks.

The channels are furred green inside, and part of the work is scraping them; the moss grows fast enough to change a fall by a hair in one season, and a hair matters. There is a green vocabulary of failure here: where moss thickens, water slows; where water slows, it spreads; where it spreads, it may find a new first line.

Nothing here has roots that matter. It is all a skin, a lush wet pelt on new rock, and it arrived astonishingly fast — faster than it should have, faster than spores in this thin air can explain. Green seems to want the Welt. The gutterwrights write it down and do not theorize.

### Animals

Rare. There is no soil, so there are almost no insects; without insects, little else.

Some ponds hold a translucent shrimp-thing with a black eye-spot, no one knows how it came. Midges in the bathhouse steam. A white spider that lives in the joints of dry stone walls and eats the midges.

And the bird: a small slate-coloured bird with an over-large beak, which nests only in gas-bubbles in stone less than fifty years old, so that it must follow the Welt as it grows, abandoning the older town for the newer edge. Perhaps forty pairs. Their call is a hard tick, indistinguishable from the sound of cooling rock, which is either mimicry or camouflage or, as the people prefer, courtesy.

Far below, at the treeline, there are goat-things. They do not come up. There is nothing here to eat.

### Time

The dynamic of this place is glacial and it is a gradient, not a cycle: the Welt only ever rises, only ever grows outward, never subsides. Nothing repeats except the days.

A year on the Welt: two hand-widths of new rock at the crest. Perhaps eighty paces of new channel cut and proved. One or two new lobes of raw stone breaking out on a flank, which must be surveyed and gridded and left absolutely dry — sheeted with waxed cloth if need be, tented, guarded — until the plan for it is drawn and agreed and cut. One funeral, maybe, or none; the dead are wrapped in moss, laid in a shallow scoop on the crest, walled around, and left for the mountain to rise past them and take them in. Enrocking, they call it. In a century they are a seam.

And every generation, a small ceremony that outsiders find hard to sit through, in which a new channel is opened for the first time: the wooden gate lifted, water let into stone that has never known water, and everyone watching the thin bright thread run down through eleven hundred paces of cut rock, taking, forever, the shape twenty-two people spent nine years deciding on.

It makes almost no sound at all, against the roar. It is the loudest thing that happens here.
