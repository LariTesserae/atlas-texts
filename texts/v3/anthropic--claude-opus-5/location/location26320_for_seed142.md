**The Sixth Draught, on the Long Flat**

Start with the ground, because the ground is the oldest thing here and everything else is a footnote to it.

It is a tableland the size of a province, four kilometers up and almost perfectly level, and it got that way not by being lifted flat but by being worn flat while high. Whatever range stood here has been reduced to its own root. What remains is a floor of grey-brown mineral, close-grained, faintly waxy to the touch, polished in broad sheets by an abrasion that finished its work long ago. There are no boulders. There are no gullies, because there has never been running water in any memory the rock keeps. There are shallow pans a hundred meters across, no deeper than a body is thick, and low swells you would not notice except that walking one leaves you slightly out of breath. The only true relief is the rim, far off in every direction, where the Flat stops and the unlit air begins.

The air is warm. Not hot — the warmth of a room that has been occupied all day. It is the same warmth at your ankles as at your head, the same at the third hour as at the twentieth, the same in the lee of a wall as in the open. This is the first thing an outsider fails to understand: there is no weather here, because weather is a machine for moving heat from one place to another, and on the Long Flat the heat has already finished arriving. Equilibrium is not a mood in this country. It is the local physics.

The heat comes from underneath. The world's interior is still exhaling, slowly, the way a kiln exhales for days after the fire is drawn, and the Flat is the lid of it. Overhead there is a ceiling of ash so fine it has never learned to fall — a suspension held aloft by the same still warmth, thickening and thinning over centuries, blotting out whatever sky is behind it. So: darkness. Complete enough that the eye eventually stops trying and starts to listen instead.

---

The city is scattered across roughly sixty kilometers of the plain, and calling it a city takes some generosity, though calling it anything else takes more. Its works stand far apart, so that between them you walk twenty minutes on bare polished rock with nothing at all in the direction you are facing, and then the ground begins to tremble faintly in the soles of your feet, and then a wall is there.

The buildings are low, thick-walled, rounded at every edge. Some of that rounding is design and some is age. They are built of blocks quarried out of the city that stood here before, which was built of blocks quarried out of the one before that — you can read the strata in a cut wall like a section through sediment: dressed stone, then brick, then a fused grey slag with the ghosts of older bricks inside it, then dressed stone again. Nothing has been imported to the Flat in a very long time. There is nothing to import it from and no reason to.

Between the works run the lines: conduit galleries in stone housings, rail beds with the rails worn to half-thickness and burnished silver-bright, flue trunks a man's height in diameter, laid on piers, insulated with rammed ash. They cross the emptiness in dead-straight runs that meet at low masonry knuckles where a valve chamber sits, or a gauge house, or a bench. There is a great deal of infrastructure and not much occupancy. It has the proportions of a town's population distributed over a valley's worth of plant.

Everything is sealed. Every seam is lipped and packed; every door is a double door with a dead space between; every window — there are windows — is stopped with a fitted plug of the same grey stone as the wall, taken out only when a plate must be read by lamp, and put back at once. Light is stopped up here the way water is stopped up elsewhere, and for the same reason. Light is heat leaving. Heat is the whole economy. To carry an open flame across the Flat is not a hazard, it is a theft, and it is prosecuted as one.

So the illumination available is this: the dull sullen red at the throat of a furnace, glimpsed through a slit for the second and a half it takes an attendant to check the colour; the faint self-glow of a casting cooling in its sand, going from cherry to plum to nothing over an hour; the pale bluish phosphorescence that certain of the older slags acquire after long lying, enough to show a doorway's edge if your eyes have had two hours to come around; and the gauge-lamps, which are shielded to a needle of light no wider than the mark they are meant to illuminate.

You learn the city by hand. Handrails are grooved with counting notches. Junctions are marked by cast tokens set into the pavement, readable through a boot sole. The attendants — perhaps two hundred of them in the whole Sixth Draught, walking their circuits — read pressure by putting a palm on a pipe and a temple on the palm.

---

The sound is a single note, so low and so constant that it has to be pointed out to you before you can hear it. It is the sum of every bearing and flue and column in the district, and it has not changed pitch in the lifetimes of everyone here, which is how the attendants know that nothing is wrong. Against it: the occasional tick of a gauge stem. The scuff of felt-soled boots. A ratchet, four clicks, somewhere half a kilometer off, arriving thin and clean because there is nothing between here and there to absorb it. Every eleven hours or so a charge drops through a chute with a sound like a swallowed cough. Then the note again.

The works run without hurry and without pause. Ore is drawn from the old city below, smelted, cast, and the castings are carried out along the rails to the reduction houses, where they are broken and returned to the furnaces. Nothing is exported. Nothing is consumed except the temperature difference itself, and the whole plant — foundries, blowers, the long cooling terraces on the north side where the metal lies out in the dark giving its warmth back to the sky — exists to draw that difference off the cooling rock slowly, cleanly, at exactly the rate the rock cools.

This is the strange part, and the residents do not find it strange. The Sixth Draught produces nothing. It was designed, once, to produce something; the older strata are full of moulds for objects nobody now can name. Somewhere along the way the production stopped mattering and the *rate* began to matter, and the works were re-tuned, generation by generation, patient adjustment on patient adjustment, until output and intake matched to the fourth decimal. The city is not an industry. It is a governor. It holds the last gradient on the Flat and lets it down gently.

The water is kept in the Assay House: three hundred and forty-one litres, in sealed copper, weighed every ninth day. It is not for drinking — the attendants take their water bound in the mineral cakes they chew. The three hundred and forty-one litres are the standard, the thing every measurement is finally measured against, and their weight has not changed by more than a few grams since the ledger began. When a young attendant is sworn in they are shown the copper and permitted to put a hand on it and feel that it is exactly as warm as everything else.

---

Life, such as it is: the tallowers. Pale, soft-shelled, palm-sized, blind, they lie flat along the warm sides of flue trunks with their undersides pressed to the metal and their backs to the cool air, and they live off the difference. There is no plant anywhere on the Flat and never has been; the tallowers eat the same thing the city eats. You find them scattered, one every few dozen meters on a good pipe, and you leave them alone, because they lie thickest where a lagging has failed and an attendant who learns to read them saves himself a great deal of walking.

Rarer, and considered fortunate: the walkers. Six-legged, waist-high, slow as a minute hand, moving along the rail beds and taking tallowers with a motion nobody has ever actually seen. One may pass a given junction twice in a decade. Attendants stand aside and let them go by and mark the date.

And once in a long while — the records show eleven occurrences — a column of the overhead ash descends and stands on the plain, some hundreds of meters high, and moves across the Flat for a day or two in a straight line, against no wind, since there is no wind. It does not disturb the works. It has never been closer to explanation than that.

---

Nothing here is changing, with one exception, and everyone knows the exception.

The rock is cooling. Not perceptibly: something on the order of a fraction of a degree in a thousand years. But the ledgers go back far enough to show the curve, and the curve is real, and the whole tuned apparatus of the Sixth Draught — its two hundred attendants, its sealed windows, its worn silver rails, its single unchanging note — is a hospice arrangement, built over unimaginable time, for a warmth that will eventually be gone.

They are not in a hurry about it. They have calculated the date, and it is written in the Assay House above the copper, and it is far enough off that the number is treated less as a prophecy than as a courtesy: a thing worth knowing precisely, the way you would want to know precisely the weight of the standard, or the pitch of the note, or the exact spot on the rim where the Flat stops and the dark air starts, so that you can walk right up to the edge of it in the dark and stand there, warm, and not fall.
