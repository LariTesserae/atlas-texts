## The Throat, Pocket Four

**Approach.**

The mountain should be white and is not. At this height every other summit in the range carries its ice like a helmet, but this one stands out black and bare-boned, because the air on this side of the divide has no moisture left to give it. Snow cannot fall here. Snow cannot even exist here — a flake carried over the ridge goes to vapour halfway down, and the vapour is gone before it reaches the rock. What the mountain has instead of a glacier is a sound. You hear it two hours before you arrive, a low sustained note coming up out of the stone, and you take it at first for the wind on the arête, and then for blood in your own ears, and then you stop pretending and admit that the mountain is humming.

Near the top of the south shoulder the rock opens. Not a cave — a flue. A natural chimney of jointed basalt that runs from a fracture-field low on the mountain's flank up through some four hundred metres of rock and out through a gap under the summit cornice, and because the pressure at the bottom is always higher than the pressure at the top, air moves through it. Always. At the same speed. The seasons here are a difference of nothing; the flue does not know about weather. It has one note and has had it since before anyone came.

The garden is not in the flue. It is in a pocket off the side of it, four metres by six, one wall of dressed stone and three of raw rock, entered by a slot you must go through shoulder-first with your pack passed after you.

**Inside.**

There is no room. That is the first fact and it does not soften. The pocket is packed from floor to ceiling with growth — not a jungle's tangle, which has hollows and galleries in it, but a solid mass, a filled volume, the way a suitcase is full. Fibres in the air. Bracts pressed flat against the rock. Stems that have grown into and around each other until they are one laminated body with the wall. You do not walk in. You insert yourself, and the growth closes on you at the back and the sides, dry and rasping, and holds you at the pressure of a firm handshake all over your body at once.

Nothing here is wet. There is not a droplet, a film, a dampness, a smell of damp, anywhere in the mountain. The plants — call them plants; nobody has proposed a better word — are dry all the way through. Break a stem (you shouldn't) and it snaps like a cigarette and shows a core of grey powder with a faint sparkle in it, mica-fine. That powder is their circulation. It is driven up and down the stems by the vibration of the note, a peristalsis of pure sound, and if the note stopped the powder would settle in an hour and the garden would be dead by morning.

They live on charge. The flue carries dust — feldspar, olivine, the ground-up interior of the range — and the dust strikes their fibres endlessly and strips electrons from them, and the potential between root-in-stone and leaf-in-air is the engine that lets them pull nitrogen and carbon dioxide out of the thin cold air and lay them down as lattice. Sulphur takes the place water would have had. Their tissue is a sulphur-carbon glass, olive-black, tough as horn, and it has never needed a drop of anything.

In the dim you can see them by their own light. Every fibre tip carries a thread of corona discharge, violet going to pale rose, no brighter than a struck match seen across a field, but there are millions of tips and the total is enough. The chamber glows the colour of the inside of a shell. Where a bract crosses another bract there is a small continuous spark and a small continuous tick. Multiply that by the packed volume of the room and you get the second sound, laid over the mountain's note: a hiss like sand poured on paper, unvarying, never once pausing to breathe.

**Species, such as they are.**

Quill-moss, on the cold floor, a pelt of grey needles standing exactly nine centimetres.

Spool-fern, which draws its own silica and winds it in coils, so that the walls are furred with tiny bright springs.

Bellwort — hundreds of pewter bells on wire stems, all leaning at the same angle, twenty-two degrees off vertical, downwind. They are the shape of things that ring and they have never rung, because the wind that bends them has never once changed direction or strength. They were pushed to that angle while they were soft, and they hardened there, and that is their permanent posture and the only posture any of them has ever had.

Foil-bract, in sheets, in the highest corner, folded like something unwrapped and put back badly. When your light passes over it, it shows a dull rose from the strontium in it.

Nothing eats any of this. There is nothing here that eats. No animal has ever lived in the Throat and none could: nothing that needs water can be here for more than a quarter of an hour, and nothing that can go without water has found its way up. So there is no burrow, no dropping, no chewed edge, no web, no track, no hunger anywhere in the room. The garden has never been touched by an appetite. It shows.

**The station.**

The dressed wall is the giveaway. Behind it, set into the flue itself, are three iron wind-wheels on a common shaft, each about the diameter of a cart wheel, turning at a speed you can count if you pick one blade and follow it: a shade under two revolutions a second, and they have not varied by a hair. The shaft goes through the wall on a plain bearing, and behind the bearing sits a dynamo the size of a beer keg, and behind the dynamo, four cables, and the cables go into a hole in the rock and down, and nobody has ever found where they come out. They are still live. Not much — enough to raise the hairs on your wrist if you pass your hand across the terminal block. Enough that the garden's growth is densest around the cables and thinnest at the far corner, which tells you what the garden thinks the cables are for.

The iron is not rusted. There is no water to rust it. It is polished instead, worn by dust into soft rounded shapes, every corner gone, every bolt head reduced to a low dome, the maker's raised lettering on the dynamo casing rubbed down to a legible ghost you read with your fingertips. This is what erosion looks like when it has finished: everything that could be taken has been taken, and the wind now passes over the remainder without effect, and what you see is final form. The station will look exactly like this in ten thousand years.

Scattered, in the growth: one leather glove, dried to the shape of the hand that left it, hard as wood. A brass plate screwed to the dressed stone: **STATION 4 — DO NOT STOP THE WHEELS**, and below, scratched by a fingernail in the dust-glaze of the plate, in a different and later hand, *we didn't.* A tin cup, empty, upright, held four feet off the floor by fern-coils that have grown through the handle. Six sets of initials on the smooth stone by the slot, all of them at chest height, none of them dated.

**Rules.**

The note does not change. Not with the hour, not with the year. There is no dawn here to speak of and the light in the pocket is the plants' own light plus a grey suggestion from the slot, so that time does not so much pass as fail to start.

You cannot speak in here. The tone sits at the base of the voice and swallows it. People who have worked the Throat communicated by squeezing an arm — one for yes, two for out — and by scratching on the polished rock.

The place takes water. It takes it from you, immediately, greedily, and without malice. Your eyes glaze and burn in five minutes. Your lips split in ten. At fifteen the inside of your nose bleeds a little, dries as it comes, and the crust is grey with mineral dust. You go out at twenty and drink a litre and sit for an hour feeling like something wrung.

And here is the thing that makes people come back. Where your breath went — where the moisture from your mouth touched the growth — the garden answers. Within seconds there is a flush of new tissue, white, soft, wet-looking, sprouting fast enough to watch: a small joyful obscenity of growth, the only rapid thing in the mountain. It lasts under a minute. Then it stiffens, whitens further, goes brittle, and shatters, and the fragments are taken by the wind and gone down the flue, and what is left behind is the garden exactly as it was. Same nine centimetres of quill-moss. Same twenty-two degrees on every bell. Nothing added, nothing owed. You can put your face in and breathe and watch it happen and come back a year later and find no mark of yourself anywhere except your initials on the stone.

That is the character of the Throat. It is a fixed point. It absorbs whatever you bring, converts it briefly into something extravagant, sheds it, and returns to itself. The wheels turn and drive a dynamo that feeds a cable that goes nowhere anyone can name. The garden grows on the leakage and stopped growing at some point no one recorded. The note continues. It is not waiting for anything and it is not sleeping and it is not, in any sense the word usually carries, going on. It simply is at rest, at the top of a black mountain, in a room the size of a pantry, filled to the walls, loud, cool, glowing faintly violet, and absolutely dry.
