## The Loud Room

Enter by the crawl at the north, on your belly, through warm mud that smells of wet lime and, faintly, of ozone. You will hear the place before you see it — first as a pressure in the sinuses, then as a sound with no edges, a sound like a crowd, like surf, like a foundry, and by the time you can see light bleeding around the last bend the noise has become something you have to lean against.

Then the crawl opens and you are standing on a travertine balcony sixty meters across, in a chamber shaped roughly like a lung, and the whole of it is white.

---

**The fall.** From a shaft in the ceiling — thirty meters up, its lip smoothed into a fluted throat by a very long time — a river comes down. Not a jet; a braided, ragged, disintegrating column that widens as it drops, shedding sheets and threads and finally a hanging fog of itself. It falls fifty feet and then stops.

Not into a pool. There is no pool. At a level roughly at the height of your ribs, measured from the chamber floor, the water simply ceases, and in ceasing it turns into light and noise.

The plane where this happens is called the sill, by everyone who has ever needed a word for it, and it is flat and level and invisible and absolutely exact. Water arrives at it moving fast and leaves as brilliance — a hard white glare with a green undertone, like sun through shallow sea — and as the roar, which is not the roar of falling water but something drier and more even, the sound of enormous smooth work being done. Whatever engine does this is not visible. There is no housing, no seam, no cabling in the rock; people have looked for two thousand years. The chamber's walls are ordinary limestone, weeping, mineral-crusted, cave-normal. The sill is the only unnatural thing in the room and it has no thickness at all.

---

**Above the sill** the air is soaked. Condensation runs down every surface; the walls glisten; stalactites are fat and active; the balconies are terraced calcite, pale gold, built in overlapping fans and dammed pools like the shelves of a rice paddy. The pools are warm — blood-warm at the edges, hotter near the center of the room — because the sill throws heat as well as light, and the spray recycles it. Standing on the upper terraces is like standing in a laundry: your clothes go heavy in ninety seconds, your hair drips, your eyes sting with the salt of your own face.

**Below the sill** the floor is dry.

Not dry as in "sheltered." Dry as in mathematically dry. If you hold your hand up through the sill your palm comes out wet and your wrist stays powder-dry, and the boundary on your skin is a line you could draw with a pen. The lower chamber — a low, bright, oddly domestic room, four meters of headroom, floored in fine white flour — has never had a drop of water in it. The flour is calcite dust, the residue the sill declines to convert, and it lies in dunes and drifts, undisturbed except where something has walked.

This is the first rule of the place, and the locals put it plainly: *water comes in and does not come out.* There is no stream leaving this cave. A river's worth of water arrives here every hour and is paid out entirely as light, sound, and warmth, which leak away up the shaft and through the fissures until, on cold nights, the hill above appears to be exhaling a soft glow from a crack near its summit. There is a shrine up there. It is very small.

---

**The pulse.** The river above is a braided seasonal thing, and it does not deliver steadily. The chamber therefore pulses, on cycles of minutes and cycles of hours.

At full surge the room whites out. The glare goes from bright to unbearable, shadowless, and the noise stops being sound and becomes a hand on your sternum; you can feel your teeth in your jaw. The spray becomes a driving warm rain that comes at you from below as much as above, because the column's disintegration throws water *upward*, fountains of it, mist rising in slow columns to the ceiling and falling back. Terraces flood; the little dams overtop; the whole upper chamber becomes one moving surface of water going in every direction. Anyone up there gets down, or gets under the sill, or leaves.

Then it slackens. Over ten or twenty minutes the column narrows to a rope, the light warms from white to amber to a honeyed dusk, and the sound comes apart into components — a hiss, a low organ note in the shaft, then, astonishingly, individual drops striking individual pools, a plink, a plink, and somewhere in the dark of a side gallery something chirping. In the lulls you can hear yourself breathe. The lulls are what people come for.

In a storm season the pulse never comes. Then the room screams for three weeks and the mud in the crawl liquefies and nobody goes in.

---

**Life, such as it is.** The light is intense enough to grow things and the water is generous, but almost nothing lives here, and the reason is the turbulence: the terraces are scoured, flooded, and blasted too often for anything to establish. So the vegetation is a scatter, not a carpet. In the lee of a boulder on the third terrace, a hand-sized cushion of something like liverwort, brilliant emerald, thriving. Six meters away, bare wet stone. Long streamers of a black-green filamentous alga in the deep pools where the current is slow, waving. A single fern in a crack near the crawl, absurdly healthy, two meters tall, its fronds shredded on the upwind side. Nothing else on the terraces at all. In the lower dry room, nothing grows — nothing can — but the calcite flour is faintly nutritive and there are patches, near the edges, of a white crust that may be a fungus and may be a mineral and behaves like both.

Fauna: rare. You may sit for an hour and see nothing. Then, at a lull, the crickets start — pale, long-limbed, blind, they live in the dry room and come up to the sill to graze whatever the light deposits on its underside, hanging inverted, a scatter of them along an invisible ceiling. There is a small toad-like animal, three or four of them in the whole cave, that sits in a warm pool with its eyes shut for what appears to be years at a time. In the shaft, high up, swifts nest — they come in from the outside world, and how they navigate the roar and the glare is not understood, but they do it, and once or twice a season one comes wheeling down into the chamber, flies straight through the light, and goes out again.

And that is the census. A room bright as noon, warm as a bath, wet as a mouth, and nearly empty.

---

**Traces of hands.** People have been coming here a long time and they have left almost nothing, which was probably intentional.

Cut into the second terrace: eleven steps, worn into shallow scoops, the treads reglazed by calcite so that they are now a kind of fossil of steps. A doorframe in the west wall — dressed stone, mortised, standing alone, with no wall on either side of it and no door, framing bare rock. In the dry room, a low bench of poured concrete-like material that has not degraded in any way, and on it four ceramic cups, stacked, and a fifth on the floor, unbroken, half full of white dust. Footprints in the flour that are not recent and are not going to be erased. Names, a few dozen, scratched into the dry wall in five scripts, one of which nobody reads.

Set into the stone at the back of the dry room, at the height of a standing adult's chest, there is a rod. It protrudes about a hand's breadth. It is not metal and not stone; it is the color of a fingernail and slightly warm, warmer than the rock, and its exposed end is worn into a smooth dished shape by the touch of hands. It does not move. It does not do anything. Every visitor touches it. The names on the wall are clustered around it.

The bench, the cups, the doorframe and the steps are perhaps two thousand years old. The rod, and the sill, and whatever relationship they have to each other, are older by an amount nobody has been able to establish, and are in perfect repair, and will presumably still be here converting a river into light long after the limestone above has worn down to a plain.

---

**Rules, for those going in.**

Speech is useless above the sill; learn the signs, or write in the dust. There is a curved stone hood on the fourth terrace — clearly built for this — and if you put your head into it the noise drops to a murmur and you can hear a companion who does the same, which is the only conversation the room permits.

Do not sleep on the terraces, however warm and however long the lull has lasted. Sleep in the dry room, on the flour, in the light, which never dims enough for real darkness and never brightens enough to prevent sleep, and which will make you dream of open water.

Nothing rots here — the dry room has no bacteria worth the name, and the wet chamber is scoured too clean — but everything wears. Leave nothing you would mind having polished away over centuries into a shape without meaning.

Do not attempt to pass an object down through the sill quickly. Slowly is fine; hands, tools, rope, all fine. Quickly is not. There is a scorch on the ceiling of the dry room, high in one corner, where somebody long ago threw something, and the mark is small and neat and black and everybody who has looked at it has decided not to repeat the experiment.

And when you leave, go at a lull, and stop in the crawl and look back once. From the mouth of the crawl the column is a white pillar in a golden room, standing on nothing, roaring quietly, holding up nothing at all — and the room, which is small, which is one room in one hill, feels for a moment like the whole working interior of the world.
