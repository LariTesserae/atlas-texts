## The Pane

You come down into it at the hour the surface calls midmorning, through one of the throats — a funnel of dissolved rock forty metres wide, its walls fluted like the inside of a shell. Halfway down the air changes: cooler, thicker, tasting faintly of struck matches. And then the throat opens and you are standing under a sky made of stone, and it is *bright*, brighter than the desert you left, and you go blind for ten minutes and cry salt water onto white ground.

The ceiling is the reason. Fifty metres of fibrous gypsum, satin spar, laid down in a single monstrous sheet when this was a lagoon and the world was young. Its crystals run vertically, needle beside needle, all the way through. Light goes into the top of that slab out on the sun-hammered karst plain and comes out of the bottom here, diffused, scattered, without direction. There is no sun below. There is no anywhere the light comes *from*. It simply exists at every point of the ceiling equally, a mild white blaze, and nothing in this world casts a shadow.

That is the first thing to understand about the place. Nothing casts a shadow. Not the herds, not the towers, not you.

## Ground

The floor is gypsum too, and salt, and the pale chalky residue of a limestone that acid ate out from below a hundred million years ago. It is soft. You can score it with a thumbnail. You can carve it with a stick of bone. Rain — the little rain there is — takes it away in solution, grain by grain, patiently, forever.

So the land here has no edges left. Everything has been rounded off. The old escarpments are long ramps. The old hills are lozenges. The chamber runs perhaps thirty kilometres from wall to wall, and across all of it the relief is gentle, dune-soft, worn to the last stable slope a dissolving stone can hold. It is a landscape that finished happening long ago and has been settling since, the way a body settles into a chair.

There are no plants. Not a moss, not a lichen, not a root. Green never got down here and will not now. What grows instead hangs from the low seeps along the northern wall: the *veils*. Sheets and drapes of bacterial mat, white and grey and rust-coloured, some of them thirty metres long, feeding on the sulphur that comes up in the water and building their whole existence out of it without ever once needing the sun that is pouring down on them from the ceiling. They sweat acid. Every so often a droplet lets go and falls, and if it lands on your arm it leaves a small white star. People walk the seep country under stiff hats of woven mica.

## Water

Damp, not wet. The chamber breathes: warm air comes down the throats by day and meets the cool stone, and condensation forms across the entire underside of the pane, so that the whole ceiling weeps. Not rain — drip. A steady, scattered, arrhythmic falling of single drops from a sky thirty metres up, all day, all night, everywhere at once, each one making a sound about as loud as a blink.

Below, the water collects in shallow pools with margins crusted white, and in a slow river the colour of milk that has cut itself a groove three metres deep and wanders it like a bored animal. Twice in a generation, the surface river finds a new sink and comes down a throat all at once, and then for four days the chamber contains a flood, and after the flood the map is different.

## The City

It was never built. It was subtracted.

Because the stone is soft, the people who came here in the beginning did not stack anything; they carved. Streets first, then dwellings out of the blocks the streets left standing. And then their children re-carved what the damp had smoothed, and their children after them, in the same places, for something on the order of ten thousand years.

The result covers perhaps a fifth of the chamber floor and it looks, from the mouth of a throat, like a city left too long in the rain. Which is exactly what it is. Nothing has a corner. Every doorway is a soft oval. Every column has thinned in the middle like a spent candle. The friezes are legible only as a faint rhythm of bulges — you can tell that something was carved there, and that it was repeated, and no more than that. The oldest quarters are down to knee-high stumps arranged in the pattern of streets, a field of white molars in white gums.

And the streets themselves have gone *down*. Feet wear stone; water follows feet; the two of them together have driven the main avenues sixty, eighty, a hundred metres below the level of the buildings that line them. So the city is now inverted. The dwellings stand up on tables and pillars of untrodden rock, and the roads run in canyons between them, cool and dim and floored with running water, and you get from a road to a house by a ladder of pegged bone, and from one house to the next by a bridge of the same, spidery and continually being replaced. In the deepest avenues you can look up and see the pane far above as a bright ribbon between the overhangs and think, for a moment, that you are outdoors at last.

The tables get thinner every century. Eventually one goes. This is normal.

## Population

Sparse. Perhaps three thousand people in a city built for a hundred thousand, scattered across it in twos and threes and never grouped, like the last chess pieces in an endgame. Whole districts have been empty since before anyone's great-grandmother.

They have no metal, and no use for it — you don't need bronze to work a rock you can cut with a spoon. Their tools are bone, mica, and hardwood traded up the throats. Their light-craft is the exception, the one place their technique goes strange and fine: they cut prisms and blocks of clear selenite and set them in the floors and walls to pipe brightness into the deep avenues, and a good light-cutter can put a warm patch of noon in a room a hundred metres under stone. This is the closest thing they have to wealth.

They wear slit-visors of blackened bone. They soot their cheekbones. They squint. Their skin is the colour of the ground because the ground is powdered onto everything.

## The Rule

Nobody shouts.

Not out of piety, though there is piety in it now. The pane is being dissolved from above at a rate of perhaps a hand's width in a lifetime, and its underside is pitted and blistered and hung with pendants, and where it thins it becomes a drum. And a drum, before it fails, *sings*. The fibrous crystal rings when the load shifts in it — a very high, very thin note, right at the edge of hearing, sometimes lasting hours, sometimes a week, sometimes only long enough for one child, playing alone, to look up.

So the city is quiet in order to hear the ceiling. It is a whole civilisation's worth of silence spent on a single act of listening. There are Listeners appointed to each quarter, older people mostly, who sit still through their shifts with their eyes shut in a bright room, doing nothing, being paid in food to do nothing. There are noise laws older than the oldest legible carving. Grain is ground wet, on hide. Ladders are lashed, never hammered. Children are taught to laugh with their mouths shut, a small convulsion in the chest, no sound. Arguments are conducted in a language of finger positions on the other person's forearm.

And when the note comes, the quarter empties in an hour, in silence, and everybody stands out on the flats and watches, and then the panel comes down. Half a square kilometre of ceiling arriving all at once. It is the loudest thing in the world and it happens perhaps every eleven years.

Afterward there is a permanent hole in the light. The fallen sheet leaves a raw ragged aperture, and through it comes a shaft of real, direct, unfiltered sun — a solid white bar leaning across the chamber, unbearable to look at, painting one blinding lozenge on the floor that crawls with the hours. It is the only shadow-caster in the world and everything around it is thrown into hard black relief for a few hours a day, which the people find frightening and beautiful and unlucky, and around which the rubble field is left permanently unbuilt.

The rest of the district, deprived of its pane, goes dark. And so the city migrates. Over centuries it drifts across the chamber floor, following the light, abandoning the dimmed quarters to the herds. This drift is why the old city is so much bigger than the living one, and why everything you walk through is somebody's grandmother's ghost of a street.

## Fauna

Life down here is abundant in the way of a place with one enormous food supply and very little competition for it.

The veils feed everything. Clouds of midges breed in the acid pools, so dense at dusk that they read as smoke; they have no sound, no whine — selected out of them long ago, along with everything else's voice. Blind white isopods the length of a forearm graze the mats in ranks. There are pale eels in the milk river with silvered eyes the size of coins, hyper-adapted not to darkness but to glare. There are spiders that hunt by ground-tremor and have no ears at all, and a soft-bodied thing that lives in the pane's blisters and drops on what passes beneath.

And there are the walkers: long-legged browsers, waist-high at the shoulder, the colour of wet chalk, with wide padded feet and flexible lips, moving in herds of two or three hundred along the seep line, eating the veils down and letting them regrow. They are perfectly silent. Their feet make less noise than the dripping. A herd crossing the ruins at midday is a slow white flow through white streets under white light — a thousand animals passing within fifty metres of you and the only way you know is the smell and a faint sense of pressure in the air.

The people eat them, when they can, and use their bones for everything.

## Weather

There is no weather. There is drip, and there is the slow tidal change of the ceiling's brightness — a shallow noon, a two-hour dusk as the surface sun goes, and then the long afterglow, because the crystal holds light and gives it back all night, fading from white to grey to a dim pearl by the coldest hour, never quite to black. In ten thousand years nobody in this chamber has ever seen a star or full darkness.

And under that unwavering mild light the whole place is coming apart at speed: the pane thinning, the tables toppling, the streets deepening, the pools shifting, the river changing its mind, the herds redistributing themselves after every collapse, the districts going dark and the people gathering their prisms and moving on.

An ancient landscape, finished, settled, at rest — and inside that rest, everything is falling, quietly, all the time.
