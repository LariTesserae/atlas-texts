**The Warm Plug**

It stands up out of a white flat like a thumb pressed into dough from below — a young crag, maybe three hundred paces across at the shoulders, four hundred tall. Everything about it is fresh. The rock still remembers being liquid: you can read the flow-lines in it, the pulled taffy grain, the glass skin on the south face where it cooled fast and cracked into plates the size of doors. Nothing has had time to round off. Every ridge is a blade. Every joint is square. The scree at its base is angular, unpolished, still ringing new — though nothing here rings.

There is no water. Not scarce; absent. No rain has ever fallen on this rock and no rain ever will. There are no clouds in the glassy hot sky, no dark stain of damp under any overhang, no mud, no rust, no rot in the sense that wet worlds mean rot. The flat below is not a dry lake — it was never a lake. It is a salt-and-ash pan laid down out of the air.

What the crag has instead is breath.

The plug is hollow-hearted and still hot in its roots, and from a hundred fissures near its top it exhales a warm, heavy, sweetish gas: carbon dioxide, sulfur, and a fine hydrocarbon mist that smells like almonds cracked on a stove. This is the weather. This is the water. The exhalation is steady as a sleeping animal's, a little stronger at midday when the rock's outer skin expands, a little weaker at dawn. It has been doing this for perhaps four hundred years, which is the whole age of the world here.

---

The vegetation lives on that breath, and so it is not green.

There are trees — squat, thick, packed shoulder to shoulder in the upper third of the crag, wherever the fissures run. Their bark is pale grey and crusted with sulfur bloom in canary and rust rosettes, which flake off underfoot with the feel of eggshell. Their leaves are heavy coin-shaped plates, silver on top, tallow-yellow beneath, hung edge-down in dense overlapping curtains. They are not gathering light. They are shedding heat and catching wax. Some are the deep bruise-purple of an old plum; some are almost white. In the lower groves the leaf-plates grow the size of dinner platters and layer six or seven deep, so that walking under them you move through a still, warm, tallow-lit interior, a room made of hanging metal.

The trees root barely at all in the ground. They root *upward and inward*, sending grey gill-roots into every crack that breathes, wrapping the vent mouths like fingers around a cup. The oldest of them have grown down over their vents in ribbed hoods, and the gas comes up through the trunk itself.

And they are full of oil. Not sap — a thin pale oil, low-volatile, faintly sweet, which is the blood of everything on this rock. Snap a twig and it wells slowly, and keeps welling, and does not dry. Cut a leaf-plate and the oil pools in the cup of it and sits there for days without changing, because there is no water to feed the quick busy decay of wet worlds. Things here do not putrefy. They stiffen, candle over, and become furniture.

The lushness of the place is real but it is a lushness of mass and layering rather than of sap-green vigor: pale thickets stacked in terraces, throats of foliage crammed into every warm fissure, the whole upper crag furred and shaggy and dense enough that you must turn your shoulders to pass.

---

At night, or rather in the four hours before dawn when the crag gives back its heat to the sky, the hydrocarbon mist condenses. It does not fall as rain. It falls as wax — a slow soft snow of white flocculent particles, drifting down in absolute stillness, settling on every leaf-plate and wall and sleeping back. By first light the whole crag is frosted, dead white, and looks abandoned. Then the sun comes over the rim of the flat, the rock warms, and the wax melts into oil and runs down the leaf-curtains, plate to plate, into the cupped hollows, into the drinking-basins, into the fissures. The trees take it back. The animals come out and drink.

This is the entire hydrology of the world: a daily fall of wax, a daily melt of oil. It is why nothing here is ever thirsty and nothing here is ever wet.

---

There is a city.

No one made a tool to make it. It was chewed.

The builders are hand-sized, six-legged, flat-bodied, matte grey, with a broad plated face that works as trowel and rasp both. They graze the pumice, grind it in a gullet-mill, mix the powder with tree-oil and their own gum, and lay it. That is all they do, all day, for the eleven or so years they live: a mouthful, a placement, a smoothing, another mouthful. There are perhaps two hundred thousand of them on the crag.

What they have built covers the plug's upper two-thirds in a stratified crust six to nine feet thick — terraces, ramps, spiral stairs with treads the height of a knuckle, roofed galleries, arcades, bridges spanning the sharp clefts. The masonry is warm grey and slightly greasy to the touch and takes fingerprints. It is laid in visible courses like sedimentary bedding, and because the crag has been slowly rising and cracking the whole time, you can read the city's strata the way you read the rock's: the oldest walls at the base of any structure are simple and fat-jointed; higher up the work gets finer, the arches shallower, the ornament (there is ornament, though it may not be intended as such) more repetitive and confident.

And the flues. The most striking thing on the crag from a distance is the chimneys: hundreds of tapered masonry stacks, one to eight feet tall, planted over the vents, each with a lipped mouth and a hooded windward side. The builders open and close them with plugs of gum through the day, throttling the breath of the mountain, warming a nursery here, cooling a gallery there. It is an infrastructure. It has no mechanism. It is the same act as building — a mouthful of paste, patiently placed, and then patiently removed a season later.

Nowhere in the city is there a wheel, a lever, a blade, a spark. The only fire this world has ever known is the one still cooling in its own basement.

Set into the walls, everywhere, at every level, are the bodies. Because nothing rots, the builders' dead are not disposed of but incorporated — laid on their sides in the courses like cobbles, oil-candled, grey on grey, so that a gallery wall at close range is a mosaic of small stacked backs and folded legs, thousands upon thousands, perfectly preserved, four hundred years deep. It is not a monument. It is just what is done with a thing that is already the right size and shape and will not spoil.

---

The fauna is abundant and it is quiet.

Air this high is thin, and thin air carries sound badly; the tallow leaf-curtains drink what's left; the powdery mortar and the pumice dust swallow footfall. The result is that the crag is the quietest crowded place imaginable. You can stand in a gallery where forty thousand animals are moving within thirty feet of you and hear a sound like sand being poured slowly from one cupped hand to another, and nothing else.

Nothing here calls. There is no advantage in it. Communication is by touch, by scent, by drumming vibrations directly into the masonry — which you feel in your soles rather than hear.

Among the fauna: long-toed hoppers, knee-high, sleek as seed-pods, that browse the leaf-curtains and are herded by the builders into upper pastures and milked of a thick oil from glands behind the jaw. Gliders with fringed membranes that step off the summit crags and pass overhead in total silence, close enough to see the vein-work in the wing. Bell-snails the size of a fist whose banded shells look exactly like something that ought to chime and never do, grazing sulfur crust off the bark. Bark-lice in glittering carpets. A slow flat grazer, wide as a table, that lives in the lowest warm galleries and has never in its life been outside; the builders keep it, cut oil from it, and lay their walls around and over it as it sits.

And the great ones: three or four ancient hooded things, each in its own crevice near the summit, mostly root and mostly mineral by now, half-mortared into the city, breathing gas straight from the fissure, incapable of moving, alive, and warm.

---

The rule of the boundary is thermal and it is simple. Oil thickens when it cools. Below a certain contour — you can see it, a hard line where the pale thickets stop and the bare fresh glass begins, about two-thirds down the crag — the wax of the night fall no longer melts in the morning. It accumulates. Anything that walks down there stiffens: first the legs slow, then the joints candle over, then it stops, standing, and the wax builds on it. The lower slopes are studded with these — hoppers, builders, a glider that came in too low — white, furred with wax, upright, each one a little taller every year. Nothing goes down. Nothing comes up. The white flat below is not a place; it is simply what is outside.

So the world is this one warm crag: sharp-edged, unweathered, four hundred years old, three hundred paces wide, breathing sweet gas out of its own hot heart, wearing a shaggy silver-and-plum forest, encrusted with a chewed grey city stratified like rock and studded with its own dead, packed to the seams with silent animals, frosted white each dawn and melting each morning into oil.

It changes, but at the pace of a fingernail. A chimney raised over a decade. A gallery abandoned as its vent goes cool, and refilled with wax, and forgotten under new courses. A tree taking eighty years to hood its fissure. A crack opening in the south glass with a single dry tick that no one hears, letting out a new thread of warm breath — and the slow grey turning of two hundred thousand faces, over the following season, toward it.
