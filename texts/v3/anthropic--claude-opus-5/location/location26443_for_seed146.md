## The Blister Country

Begin with your hand. Put it flat against the floor and it will be warmer than you are — not hot, not dangerous, just relentlessly warmer, the way a large animal is warmer. Now put your other hand up. The ceiling is there, a little above your head, and it is warm too, though less. Between those two warmths is everything: a room of air perhaps two and a half meters tall, and it runs for four hundred kilometers in every direction.

That is the first thing to understand. This place is enormous and it is also low. It has the dimensions of a continent and the headroom of a cellar.

---

The rock is pale ash-tuff, chalky to look at and faintly greasy under the fingers, shot through with sheets and lenses of black glass. It came together fast: ash fell, ash welded, and then a body of magma pushed in underneath and began to give up its gas. The gas had nowhere to go. It lifted the ash beds in sheets, one after another, like air getting under the leaves of a wet book. When the pressure equalized, the leaves stayed lifted.

So you have laminae — dozens of them stacked, each one an open space of its own, separated by a floor-and-ceiling of tuff sometimes only a hand's width thick. Where the gas didn't reach, the rock stayed continuous and stands as pillars: irregular, close-spaced, five to fifty meters apart, holding the whole stack in a grid nobody planned. You are always within sight-reach of a pillar. You are never in the open.

Everything here is sharp. There has been no water to round a corner, no frost to split one, no root to pry. The blister-walls still carry the shatter-texture from the moment they parted, edges you can cut a hand on, glass shards standing up out of the floor like teeth that were never worn. It is a young country in the geological sense and it will not age, because there is no agent of aging left. Nothing is scheduled to happen here.

The one exception: the trackways. Where feet have gone, the tuff is polished to a soft ivory sheen, and the polish is exactly as deep as it will ever be. It reached its depth long ago and stopped, because the number of feet is fixed. More on that.

---

There is no water. There is water. Both are true and the distinction is the local religion.

Nothing pools, nothing seeps, nothing drips; there is no dew, no ice, no vapor you could call weather. But the tuff itself is a hydrous rock. Water sleeps in the mineral lattice, one molecule at a time, chemically committed, going nowhere. The whole continent is soaked and bone-dry simultaneously. To drink here is a masonry problem: you find a zeolite seam, you split it, you press the fresh face to your mouth-plates and take what comes off — a breath's worth, a thimble in a good hour. This is why the fauna have wedges instead of teeth. This is why the sound of the world, when there is sound, is a faint irregular *tk. tk.* from ten thousand directions at once, the sound of things quarrying their own thirst out of the floor.

---

The air moves and has always moved in the same way. Heat rises from the sill at the bottom of the stack; the low leaves feed it into the pillar-gaps; it slides sideways for a hundred kilometers, cools by a degree, comes down again through a chimney it made when the country was new. These cells are fixed. There is a wind in the eleventh leaf that has blown north-by-a-little at the pace of a slow walk since before anything walked in it, and it has never gusted, never dropped, never turned. It carries dust — fine welded-ash flour, the only crop — in a permanent haze at about knee height, and it deposits that dust in the same lee-drifts it has always maintained, and takes back exactly as much as it leaves.

This is the country's weather. It is real weather, continental in scale, complete with a dust season that lasts forever and covers the whole of it.

---

It is dark. Almost entirely. Say four hours after sunset in a room with the shutters closed — except that there is no sun, no shutters, and no outside; the "almost" comes from below. Down in the two lowest leaves, the floor is hot enough to hold a dull red that you would not notice by day and cannot ignore here: a sullen underlight that makes the pillars into black legs and throws no shadow anywhere it would be useful. Climb three leaves and it's gone.

The rest of the light is *made*. This tuff and its glass are triboluminescent. Split a face and it sparks — a brief cold green, gone before it means anything, one flash per fracture. So light is not a condition here. It is an event, like a cough. When a big splitter works a seam, it flickers, and everything near it that has eyes gets a stuttering half-second glimpse of the world, and then the world is gone again. There are places, at the base of certain pillars, where something has been splitting the same seam for a very long time, and the dark there is grainy with intermittent green, like the last of a fire.

Mostly, though, you navigate by warmth and by touch and by the strung threads.

---

Now the fauna, and they are the point of the place. Abundant is the correct word. Not swarming — nothing swarms, nothing hurries — but if you crawl a hundred meters in any direction you will pass or touch or be touched by two dozen living things.

They are layered, which the architecture invites. Understand that a leaf's floor is another leaf's ceiling, so every surface is inhabited from both sides.

**Prisers**, hand-length to arm-length, flat as slate, all edge in front. They live wedged in the laminae joints and pry. They are the water economy. They are also the light economy, incidentally.

**Grazers** in the dust wind — dish-shaped, sieve-mouthed, walking upstream at the wind's own speed so that they hold position forever relative to the pillar behind them. They filter the flour for the organics that have been circulating in it since the beginning, taking a little, passing a little.

**Threaders**: long, segmented, thinner than a finger, which live in the inter-leaf gaps where the rock nearly touches, and cross between leaves through hairline fissures. They are the only things that experience the country as a volume rather than a floor.

**Listeners**, squat, with two plate-membranes held up like open hands, which do not move for weeks and are attending to the *tk. tk.* of everyone else's quarrying — a distributed map of who is eating where.

And **the sitting kind**, which have no name because naming them is a category error. They are shaped like part of the floor, they are the temperature of the floor, they do not move in any timescale you can observe, and they are unquestionably animal: warm inside, fed by something, defended by a very slow chemistry that will scar your hand if you press. There are millions of them. You have already sat on several.

---

Here is the law, and it is the strangest thing about this country, stranger than the dry water or the darkness that must be manufactured.

The number is constant.

Not the biomass, not the species mix — the *count*. Every living thing in the Blister Country, summed across all leaves and all forms, is a fixed integer, and it has not changed. Nothing is born in the ordinary way. When something ends — a priser crushed in a settling joint, a grazer that finally stops — then somewhere else, within a few hours and usually at one of the dust lee-drifts, flour gathers, packs, warms, and stands up as something. Not the same thing. Often not the same kind. The country replaces the *unit*, not the individual.

Consequently there is no ecological time here. No boom, no crash, no succession, no evolution that anyone can detect. The pressure that shapes life elsewhere — too many mouths — has been switched off since the beginning. The result is not paradise and not stagnation. It is finish. This ecology arrived at its answer immediately and has been holding it, at full occupancy, for the whole duration.

---

Which brings us to the marks.

Scattered — genuinely scattered, you can walk two days between them — there are made things.

Cairns, first: stacked tuff slabs, waist-high, always beside a trackway, always at the point where the trackway must choose between two pillar-gaps. Someone, or several someones, has been maintaining a route-grammar across a continent by hand.

Then the thread-lines: cord spun from a fiber that comes off the sitting kind, strung pillar to pillar at chest height, sometimes running for kilometers, knotted at intervals in a code. You walk with a hand on the line and count knots and you know where you are. Some lines are old, sagging, furred with dust. None are broken. Someone repairs them.

Then, occasionally, a *room*: a blister-space partly walled off with dry-laid slabs, floor cleared of glass, a low doorway with a lintel dressed square by patient percussion. Inside, nothing much. A grinding hollow. Blades of black glass, beautifully knapped, the best work in the country. A hammerstone of native copper, cold-hammered, never melted.

Never melted, because nothing here burns. There is no fuel — no wood, no oil, no plant matter of any kind, never has been, this country skipped that entire chapter. No fire means no smelting, no ceramics, no glass-making, no lamp. The technology of this place is locked at cut-stone-and-cord not because its makers are simple but because the chemistry closed that door before they arrived and did not leave a key. They have had unlimited time and total stability in which to perfect what is possible. The blades are exquisite. There will never be a second age.

And then the tallies. On certain pillar-faces, dressed smooth, there are marks — thousands, tens of thousands, in disciplined ranks, cut with glass, refreshed where dust-abrasion has softened them. They are not a calendar; there is nothing to date. They are not a history; nothing happens.

They are the count. Someone is keeping the number. Someone has been checking, for a very long time, that the total is still what it was, patting the floor in the dark, feeling the warm still bodies of the sitting kind, adding nothing, subtracting nothing, and cutting one more mark to say: *verified. Unchanged. Correct.*

---

Sounds, in order of frequency: the hiss of the flour against glass teeth. The *tk* of quarrying. Footfall on polished tuff, which is almost nothing. A thread-line thrumming once when a threader crosses it. Very rarely — and this is the loudest thing that occurs — the *whump* of a lamina joint settling somewhere over the horizon, a sound with no echo because the ceiling is too low to give one back, just a pressure against the ears and then the return of the enormous, warm, populated quiet.

Nothing here is dying. Nothing here is waiting. It is not a ruin and not a wilderness.

It is a place that got everything settled early and has simply continued, at temperature, at full census, in the dark, correct.
