## The Cold Room

You would walk over it. Most do. In the low fen country, where the land lies flat as spilled water and the drains run straighter than any river should, there is a place where a limestone shelf lifts a hand's width above the peat and then cracks open. The crack is roofed by the collapsed remains of something built long ago — a sluice-head, perhaps, or a boundary marker made too ambitiously — three slabs of pale stone that fell against one another and locked, and have not moved since anyone's grandmother's grandmother.

Between the slabs there is a gap the width of a shoulder. Below the gap there is a room.

Call it four paces by six, if you could stand in it, which you cannot. Its floor is a foot below the fen's water table and yet it is not flooded; it is only damp, everlastingly damp, the damp of a cellar in February. Its walls are chalk and flint. Its ceiling is the sky, seen through the gap, edged by the three fallen slabs, and the sky is the only thing here that ever changes, and it changes only in colour.

### The light

The first thing is the light, and it is wrong in a way that takes some time to identify.

There is a great deal of it. The hollow is brilliant — glaring, even, so that you squint going in — and none of it is warm. No direct sun reaches the floor; the gap faces north and the slabs lean the wrong way. What arrives is sky-light, whole-sky light, and it strikes the chalk and is thrown back and thrown back again, until the room is filled with a shadowless white radiance that comes from every direction at once. Nothing here casts a shadow. Nothing has a lit side and a dark side. Every surface is described in full and equally, the way things are described in fog, except that the air is perfectly clear.

The chalk floor is what the inhabitants call — in writing, since they do not call anything — *the day-stone*. It holds the light and gives it up slowly. At midnight the floor of the Cold Room is still faintly, uselessly luminous, a grey-white glow with no heat in it whatsoever, enough to work by.

### Cold

Cold pools. This is the physical fact the whole place is built on. Air chilled against the chalk grows heavy and sinks through the gap and stays, unable to climb out, and it has been staying for a very long time. There is no wind in the Cold Room. There has never been wind in the Cold Room. A dropped hair falls straight.

The temperature is a few degrees above freezing in high summer and a few degrees below it in deep winter, and the difference is small enough that the moss does not bother to acknowledge it. Frost lives permanently on the north sills, not growing, not retreating: a standing crop of white feathers, harvested and regrown at a rate so exactly matched that the pattern of feathers on a given ledge is the pattern your predecessor would have seen.

Your breath, going in, hangs. It hangs longer than it should — a slow white ghost of itself, settling rather than dispersing, sinking gradually toward the rooftops and going out among the towers like weather. This is the first sign that you have come somewhere with its own arrangements.

### The town

Every surface is occupied. This is the second thing.

The walls of the hollow are terraced from floor to gap in ledges four fingers deep, and on every ledge stands building. Split flint laid dry, course on course, the blade-faces turned outward so that the walls are glossy blue-black and mirror-smooth. Roofs of snail shell, of mussel shell brought from somewhere, of single overlapping scales of some fish, of chalk sawn into tiles thinner than a fingernail. Stairs no wider than a match, cut into the living chalk and worn into shallow curves. Bridges of woven root. Chimneys that have never carried smoke, and serve as ventilation shafts, and are stopped in winter with plugs of moss.

The town is layered. Dig anywhere — you cannot dig; the inhabitants would prevent you, patiently, by standing in the way — and you would find a floor beneath the floor, and beneath that a course of flint set in a style nobody uses now, and beneath that another. Six or seven towns deep, each one built on the roofs of the last, until the lowest course is not building at all but the original cracks of the limestone, tidied.

And yet nothing is ruined. There is no rubble in the Cold Room. No collapsed wall, no abandoned quarter, no burnt terrace, no scaffolding either. There is no wind to push a wall over and no frost-thaw cycle to split it and no sun to bake it and no flood to undercut it. The town reached its final shape a long time ago and then simply held that shape, the way a pebble in a stream-bed holds its shape: worn to the form that erosion has no further argument with. What maintenance there is is invisible — a tile turned, a joint re-packed, a stair-lip re-cut by a hand's width over a decade.

Nothing is under construction. Nothing is planned. The Cold Room is finished, and has been for some time, and this is not a tragedy there.

### Water

There is no spring, no stream, no standing pool except one, in the north-west corner, the size of a thumbprint, black, seven inches deep, unmoving.

All the rest of the water in the Cold Room is water in transit down the walls. The chalk weeps. The flint sweats. Fog forms at the gap when the fen air rolls over on autumn nights and comes down in a slow grey collapse and beads on everything. Condensation is the whole hydrology: it gathers on the polished flint faces, runs, is intercepted.

The interception is the town's principal industry. Strung between roof and roof, across every street, are nets of combed animal hair — badger and hare and the coarse grey of pony — hung in shallow catenaries, each with a leader-thread running to a gutter, each gutter running to a cistern of drilled chalk. Fog-combs. Whole districts are shaded by them. The hair is oiled once a generation. A well-tended comb yields, over a night of proper fog, perhaps a spoonful, and a spoonful is a great deal here.

Above the combs the moss grows: *lush* is the only word. Two-thirds of the hollow's surface area is moss and liverwort and the frilled green of hart's-tongue fern uncurling from wall-joints, and every plant is doing well, and none is growing much. There is not enough warmth to grow fast and there is not enough dryness to die. Cushions of moss the size of a fist may be two hundred years old, and are mown — with flint sickles, by hand — into hedges, bulkheads, thatch, pasture.

### Silence

The Cold Room is the quietest inhabited place you will ever put your head into.

Moss absorbs. Depth absorbs. Windlessness gives nothing to rattle. The fen above is itself a place of low sound, and what little arrives — a sedge-warbler, a distant pump — comes down the gap thinned to nothing. Inside, the loudest recurring event is the fall of a drop from the underside of the north slab into the thumbprint pool: about once every forty seconds, a *tick* so small it feels internal, as though you had heard it in your own ear.

The inhabitants do not speak. There is no evidence they ever did. They have voices in the sense that they have air and throats, but the practice was never developed, or was set down. Instead:

They write on the fog-film. Every polished flint face in the town is a slate; every night's condensation is a fresh sheet. They write with a fingertip, in a fast running script of loops and dashes, and the message lasts until the film beads too heavily and takes it away. Notices, gossip, prices, prayers, and the town's own name — which is redrawn each morning on the great flint face by the pool, always the same nine strokes, by whoever passes first.

Where a thing must be remembered longer, they scratch it. Where a thing must be said now, they touch: a hand on a forearm, a pattern of pressure. Children learn the pressure-alphabet before the written one.

### The living

Fauna is everywhere and nearly all of it is small and slow.

Springtails move through the lower streets in drifts, grey powder that flows around obstacles like liquid, harmless, harvested with flat pans, pressed into cakes. Snails are livestock: kept in walled moss-pastures, milked for the mucus that glues shell-tile to roof-batten, killed rarely, their shells inherited. Woodlice serve as draught animals in the shell-quarries at the pool's edge, harnessed with hair. There are eight kinds of beetle, and the town has opinions about all of them.

Above: a wren nests in the west crevice, and this is the single great danger and the single great event of the Cold Room's year. The wren is a god who does not know it. Its arrival is announced by a change in the light through the gap; the streets empty in the time it takes you to blink twice, and then nothing moves at all, and the town looks like architecture, and the wren, finding architecture, leaves.

A shrew came down in the winter of some ancestral memory and was survived. There are marks about it on the north wall, scratched deep.

### The rule of the place

Nothing here is going anywhere, and this is not stagnation but tuning.

The cold does not vary because cold pools. The light does not vary because it comes from the whole sky. The water does not vary because it is condensation, and condensation is a function of the same unchanging chalk. The population does not vary; each household of the Cold Room holds a fixed number of hearth-stones, and the number of hearth-stones in the town is the number that the fog-combs will water. Births are matched to deaths with the deliberateness of a craft. There is a written record, on the deep-scratched wall, of the total: nine strokes and a cross-bar, unaltered in two hundred years.

They know the fen exists. Some have gone up through the gap and come back, and their accounts are scratched, not written on fog — the long horizontals of the drains, the terrible openness, the wind that moves things without being asked to. It is understood as a place where nothing is finished.

If you put your face to the gap at noon on a white overcast day in November, you will see it all at once: the brilliance without warmth, the black-blue flint stacked in terraces, the hair-nets sagging with beads like strung glass, the moss hedges, the frost feathers standing on their sills, the small figures neither hurrying nor idling on stairs the width of a stitch, and your own breath descending slowly over the rooftops as fog, and, on the great flint face by the pool, nine wet strokes drying.

And you will hear one drop fall.
