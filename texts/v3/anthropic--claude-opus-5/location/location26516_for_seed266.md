# The Stacks

## First sight

You come at it from above, if you come at all, and what you see first is not the land but the shadows — long black wedges lying across a pale floor, so long that their far ends blur into the haze. Only then do you find the things casting them: towers of rock, five to nine kilometres tall, flat-topped, standing apart from one another the way trees stand in a very old orchard. There are thousands of them. They go past the curve of the world.

The pale floor between them is not water. It is dust — fluidized, hot, and deep, a sea of grains so fine and so warm that they behave like a liquid. It rolls. It has a surface, a shoreline against the stone, and a slow surf. Nothing floats on it that is not built to. Down there the air is thick as syrup and near four hundred degrees, and the light is the colour of a bruise.

Up on the stack-tops the air is thin, and clean, and the sun is a hard white punch-hole in a violet sky. The rock is warm to the palm. Step into shade and your breath frosts. That is the essential fact of this world, from which everything else follows: **heat is everywhere and cold is scarce, and the difference between them is the only thing that has ever done any work here.**

## The stone

The stacks are the ruins of a plateau. Once there was one continuous highland, and the dust sea has been planing it away for a length of time that does not usefully convert into years. What remains are the hard cores: welded tuff, banded ironstone, ash-white sulfate caps polished to a shine on the windward side. Plum, ochre, bone, and the black of manganese varnish where a face has been baking in the sun since before anything walked on it.

They are finished shapes. That is the word for them — finished. Nothing on this landscape is in the middle of becoming something else. The stacks have found the profile that the wind and the dust and their own weight agree on, and they hold it. A stack loses about the width of a hand from its cliff-line in a human lifetime. Rockfall is an event you might hear about twice, and remember.

Because erosion is so patient, the tops are archives. A tool dropped nine hundred years ago is still lying where it fell, half-buried in grit, its edge unrusted in the dry. Footpaths are worn four centimetres into solid rock by the passage of feet that have all stopped walking. There are grazing-trails scraped into the stone by animals whose species is extinct, and the trails are still legible, and people still use them as roads because the animals chose the gradients well.

## The crust

There are no plants. There have never been plants. There is no water for them and no one here has any concept of the green.

What there is instead is **crust**.

On the sun-facing rock, in sheets and rosettes and stacked plates like shelf fungus, grows a sessile animal that eats sunlight only indirectly — it eats the difference. It anchors a black foot into the hot stone and lifts a pale, frilled body into the cold thin air, and it runs sulfur and iron chemistry across that gradient, and it grows. Slowly. A plate the size of a dinner table is older than the city beside it. The colours run from rust to cream to a deep peacock where copper is in the rock. In the morning the crusts open with a faint collective *tick*, thousands of shells unlocking, and there is a smell like struck matches, and the air over the crust-fields shimmers with their exhaled warmth.

Everything else eats the crust, or eats what eats it.

## Fauna

The fauna is not sparse. The fauna is the most abundant thing here.

**Scrapers** work the crust-fields — armoured, low, plated like an isopod the size of a wheelbarrow, with a rasping mouth on the underside. They move at about the speed of a minute hand. Herds of two or three hundred will cross a stack-top over the course of a season, leaving a shaved path behind them that will regrow in twenty years. They are warm to lean against. In cold shade they stop entirely and wait, sometimes for days, and are then indistinguishable from boulders. People have built walls out of sleeping scrapers by mistake.

**Kites** come up from the dust sea. They crack hydrogen out of the hot mineral fog down there, hold it in a body-length bladder of something like varnished silk, and rise — thousands of metres, silently, tilting, spilling gas at the top of the climb to hover at stack-height. They come in migrations that take three weeks to pass. From below they are a slow ceiling of pale lozenges; from a stack-top you look out at eye level into a drifting city of them, close enough to see the fine cilia combing the air for airborne crust-spores. They breed at altitude and sink back down to die in the heat. They make no sound at all. A kite migration is the quietest large event in the known world.

**Swimmers** live in the dust and never come out. You know them by their wakes: long, low, straight ridges pushed up on the dust-sea surface, kilometres of it, crossing and re-crossing in patterns that stay legible for a month before they slump. Stack-folk read the wakes the way sailors read weather. Some of the ridges are old enough to have names.

**Shade-hunters** are the problem. They hunt heat, which up here means they hunt anything alive. They lie in cold — in a shadow-shaft, in the north lee of a wall, in the gap under a stopped machine — and they get very cold indeed, which makes them invisible to their own kind of seeing and to everyone else's. Then they take one step. There is a bounty. It is rarely claimed.

And there are the small commensals, the ones that came in with the machinery and stayed: mica-shelled things the size of a thumb that live in bearing housings and eat oxide dust and are considered lucky; a flat grey animal that grazes the frost off cold-heads and must be swept away each dawn; something that nests in flywheel pits and is never seen, only heard, drumming.

## The cities

There is a city on maybe one stack in twelve. The rest are wild, or quarried, or simply left.

A city is not tall. Nothing needs to be tall when you already live on a nine-kilometre tower. A city is *deep* — it goes down into the cap-rock, in galleries and terraces and stepped courts, because the interior of the stone holds a stable temperature and the surface does not. What you see from outside is a rim: low blockhouses of dressed tuff and grey brick, dust-lift towers, the black glitter of collector fields, and the cable-heads where the spans go out.

The spans are the infrastructure that reads at a distance. Wire bridges, five to thirty kilometres, stack to stack, sagging in long catenaries, with cars crawling along them. A crossing takes most of a day. The cables are spun on-site from stack-iron and they are inspected constantly, by hand, by people who spend their whole working lives walking one wire.

Under the cities, the **dust-lifts**: pipe columns descending the full height of the cliff to the sea, drawing fluidized dust upward in slow surging bores to the smelters. This is the closest thing here to a river, and it runs uphill, and it is made of pipe. The bores pulse. Standing near a lift-head you feel it in your ribs before you hear it.

## The engines, and why it is quiet

There is no steam. There is no water to make it from. Nobody here has ever seen a boiler.

Everything runs on **gradient engines**: sealed hydrogen cycles with a hot head of black ceramic bolted into the sunlight and a cold head sunk into a shadow-shaft. The shafts are the real capital of the city — vertical bores, polished, angled so that no sunlight ever enters them, radiating straight out to a sky that is very nearly space. A good shaft is cold enough to hurt. Cities go to law over shadow. There is a whole profession concerned with the projected shadows of proposed buildings a century out, and the rights that will be lost or gained thereby, and these people are respected and feared.

A gradient engine makes one stroke every three or four seconds. The displacer sighs. That is the sound: a sigh, and then the low whisper of a crankshaft on gas bearings. A field of four hundred engines is quieter than a stream would be, if there were streams.

They drive **the wheels**.

Every city is built around a flywheel — stone-cored, iron-rimmed, three to twenty metres across, turning in an evacuated pit on bearings that are the finest objects the civilization makes. The wheels are the battery, the bank, the clock, and the ancestor. They have been turning for four hundred, six hundred, in one case eleven hundred years. Energy is stored in them by day and drawn out at night, and it is also *lent*: a smaller wheel can be spun up, crated, and shipped along a span to another city, and this is what freight of value looks like here — a heavy box that must be kept upright and that fights you when you turn a corner.

The stopping of a city's great wheel is a death. It is spoken of in exactly those terms, with the same verbs. Three cities on the western stacks are known as the Stopped, and people go there, and are careful, and do not stay the night.

## Sound

The air is too thin to carry much. A shout dies in fifty metres. Two people talking a street apart cannot hear each other, so there is very little shouting, and the streets have a hush in them that visitors find either restful or unbearable.

But the stone carries beautifully.

Iron rods are sunk into the bedrock of every city and hammered in code — long messages, city to city, arriving as a faint knocking in the floor of a room three hundred kilometres away. You feel it through the soles of your boots. Children learn the code before they learn to write. Under it, always, is the subsonic drone of the great wheel, a note you stop hearing after a week and only notice again if it changes pitch, at which point everyone in the city looks up at once.

And the stacks themselves ring. Thermal stress, at dawn and at dusk, cracks and settles the cap-rock, and a stack will give out a single deep tone, once, unpredictably, and then nothing for a month. There are stacks with reputations for good notes. People have been known to build a city on a stack for no better reason.

## Weather, and time

Twice a year the dust sea takes a slow breath — a thermal tide, driven by nothing but the season, lifting the whole surface some hundreds of metres over a period of weeks. The lower cliffs vanish into a hot ochre fog. Kite migrations time themselves to it. Dust-lifts run at half depth and everyone complains.

When the plumes from a tide reach high and cold enough, sulfates condense out and fall as needles. It is a dry, glittering, silent snow. It does not melt. It is swept from the streets into pans and sold. Children are told not to eat it, and eat it anyway, and it tastes of matches and metal.

Nothing here is fast. A smelter takes a season to come up to heat and is never allowed to cool. A bearing is ground for eleven years. A journey of six hundred kilometres is a month of cable cars and waiting at cable-heads for the wind to drop. A child born the year a wheel is cast will be grey by the time it is finally balanced, and will be there for the balancing, because that is the kind of thing you attend.

And under all of it, at a rate no one alive has ever perceived, the dust sea goes on quietly taking the world apart, one hand's width at a time, and the stacks stand around in their long shadows in a landscape that has already decided what it is.
