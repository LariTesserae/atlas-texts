## The Trough at Panel Seven

Come at it from the north, across the plateau, and you will hear it before you see it — not as sound, at first, but as a wrongness in the chest, a pressure under the sternum that makes you swallow. Then the ground begins to tilt. The heather goes over in a long slow lip, the fence posts lean and then lie down, and you are looking into a trough some two kilometres long, four hundred metres across, its floor sixty metres below the level of your boots.

It is a young hollow. Nothing has had time to soften it. The scarps on the eastern side are raw sandstone, sheared clean, the beds still legible right-side-up; the tension cracks along the rim are open a hand's width and go down out of sight, and grass hangs over their edges like torn upholstery. This did not erode. This subsided — dropped, over eleven years, as the workings advanced beneath it, at roughly the rate a fingernail grows. The wall on the western side still shows a strip of tarmac ending in air, and forty metres out and forty metres down, the same tarmac lying in the moss at an angle, unbroken, like a dropped ribbon.

## The Noise

The fan house stands near the southern end, and everything in the trough is subordinate to it.

It is brick to shoulder height and then steel, painted a green that has gone the colour of pondweed, with high louvred windows glazed in wired glass. Behind it the evasée rises — the flared concrete throat of the upcast, a truncated cone eighteen metres tall, permanently plumed. Inside, two axial fans turn, and have turned without a full stop since 1974, and pull four hundred cubic metres of air a second up out of eleven kilometres of roadway.

The sound of this is not loud in the way a road is loud. It is a condition. The fundamental sits at about thirty-eight hertz, below the range where you would call it a note, and it arrives through the soles of your feet and the fluid of your eyes; the blade-pass harmonics stack up over it into a dry, planar scream that you register as a kind of internal glare. At the base of the evasée you would lose your hearing in an afternoon. At the foot of the north scarp, a kilometre away, you can stand and shout and watch the other person's mouth make the shape of your name and hear nothing whatsoever.

The trough is therefore a silent place. Whatever else is happening here — rain arriving, water dripping, a stone letting go of the scarp and coming down — happens inaudibly, wrapped inside the roar. You learn to watch. The men who service the fans use hand signs cribbed from crane work, and slates, and a lamp code, and their faces are more expressive than faces elsewhere.

The walls hold the sound and fold it. There are standing waves in the trough, and they are stable, because the walls do not move and the fan does not change. Where the pressure nodes fall, the surface of standing water is permanently, invisibly still; where the antinodes fall, the pools carry a fixed pattern of ripple, a stationary lattice that does not travel and does not die, for years. You can find these places by eye, or by the way your teeth stop buzzing when you step into one. The mosses in the nodes grow taller. Nobody has published on why.

## The Breath

The mine exhales at eleven degrees and near saturation. The trough is cold — the air over the plateau sits at or below freezing for four months, and in the shadowed half of the year the sun never clears the eastern scarp at all, so that the light down here is a permanent dim, a January-afternoon light, a light you could read large print by and no more.

Where the mine's warm wet breath meets that cold, everything in this landscape is decided.

A fog stands over the evasée, always, and lies down the trough on still days in a long grey tongue two hundred metres wide. It does not rain much here — the plateau above gets its sleet and its drizzle and passes some down — but the trough is wet in a different way: it is wet from below, condensing. Every surface within reach of the plume wears water. In frost, every surface wears rime, and the rime forms in fins, all pointing upwind, into the breath, so that the whole floor of the trough combs in one direction like fur.

There is no river. Water gathers in the sag-ponds along the axis of the trough — twenty or thirty of them, black, shallow, without inflow or outflow, fed by condensate and by whatever seeps out of the fractured sandstone — and they do not connect. They lie there in a chain, and lose their water back to the air, and receive it again at night. That is the whole hydrology: a closed loop the width of a valley, turning over once a day, going nowhere.

## The Moss

And because of that loop, the floor of the trough is not ground. It is plant.

Sphagnum came in within a decade of the collapse, as it does, on the wind and on the boots of the fitters, and it found a cold, permanently humid, low-light, low-nutrient, undisturbed floor — the exact conditions it is built to convert. There is now a mire here between forty and ninety centimetres deep, one of the youngest in the country, and it covers everything. It has gone up the lower scarps in hanging lawns. It has swallowed the narrow-gauge rails, so that the sleepers show as a rhythm of low ridges under the carpet. It has grown over the concrete anchor blocks and left them as green loaves. It has closed over the wheels of an abandoned flatbed truck and left the cab standing out of the moss like a rock in a field.

Walk on it and you do not touch mineral soil at any point. You sink to the ankle, and the water comes up around your boot, and closes again behind you, and in a week the print is gone.

It is arranged in bands, by heat. Nearest the evasée, where the wet warmth never lets the frost land, there are moss lawns of a shocking bright green in December, blistered with liverworts, and a fringe of soft rush; then a wide zone of red *Sphagnum*, low hummocks, cottongrass, cranberry threading over the tops; then, out toward the cold ends of the trough where the plume never reaches, a duller, harder ground of stiff mosses and crustose lichen and rime. The bands have moved about ten metres in fifty years. That is the fastest thing that happens here.

And there is almost nothing else. Density is the strange part: total plant cover, and yet the trough reads as empty. Trees have not managed it — a dozen downy birches on the drier eastern shelf, thin, none over four metres, half of them dead standing, and one rowan halfway up the scarp in a crack, which is the only red thing here in autumn. Between them: five hundred metres of open moss and black water and grey light, with a green shed on it, and a concrete cone smoking, and nothing at all in between. Scattered. Balanced with emptiness. You can see the whole of it from the rim and count the objects on two hands.

## What Lives

Very little, and for a specific reason.

Songbirds do not settle here. They arrive, in migration, in some numbers, and they leave within hours — they cannot hear each other, cannot hear a hawk, cannot hold a territory in a place where song does not exist. So there is no dawn chorus over the youngest mire in the county, and never has been. Bats have the same trouble, worse: the noise floor eats their returns. There are no bats in the trough, though the plateau above is full of them.

What remains is what does not need to listen. A pair of ravens on the eastern scarp, which have nested there for perhaps fifteen years and communicate by posture and by flight, and are famously silent. Midges in unspeakable clouds in the warm band in June. Water beetles and caddis in the sag-ponds. Wolf spiders on the hummocks, which hunt by sight and eyeshine and have no vibration sense to overload. A vixen who dens under the tilted tarmac slab and raises cubs that grow up strange — unstartleable, tame to the point of rudeness, walking up to the fitters at the door of the fan house.

No sheep. They tried; the animals wouldn't stay.

## Rules

The trough runs on two clocks and they do not agree.

The fast one is the fan: constant, indifferent, fed by a substation on the rim and a cable trench that the moss has closed over and swallowed and now marks only as a straight line of slightly brighter green running eight hundred metres across the mire. Nothing about the fan varies. It has the same note in August as in February. It is the climate here.

The slow one is the peat: a millimetre a year, maybe two in the warm band, patient beyond any interest in us. In four hundred years this will be a raised bog with a dome on it. In eight hundred the scarps will have slumped into grass and the fan house will be a rectangle of brick under two feet of moss and the whole trough will look like something the ice left, and nobody will hear anything but wind.

The two clocks are joined, which is the part worth understanding. If the fans stopped for a season, the workings would flood and the shaft would be capped and the breath would end; and without the breath the fog goes, and without the fog the frost gets in past the warm band, and the bright green December lawns die back and the mire stops growing and the trough becomes ordinary moor with a hole in it. This mire exists because a machine is breathing warm wet air onto it. It is a wholly natural thing, made accidentally, kept alive by maintenance schedules.

Once a year, in the small hours of a night in May, they shut down for changeover. It takes about twenty minutes.

The men who have been present describe it the same way each time. The pressure goes out of the chest. The teeth stop. And into the hole where the noise was comes an enormous, appalling, detailed quiet — water ticking off the birches, the moss creaking as it takes back its shape, one pool draining somewhere down a crack in the sandstone, and, very high up, on the rim, faint and unbelievable, a bird.
