## The Warm Level

You come to it down a stair of poured concrete that gives way, forty meters in, to steps cut directly into rock — and the rock changes as you descend. Up top it is a dry biscuit-colored limestone, tidy, cut by the same masons who did the church. By the third landing it has gone soft and greasy-looking, the color of a wet cigarette, and there is a paste on the walls like wet plaster that has never set. Do not steady yourself against it. The paste is gypsum, and under the gypsum the wall is being eaten.

The air arrives before the room does. It comes up the stairwell in a slow column, thirty-nine degrees, saturated, smelling of struck matches and warm meat and something sweetish underneath like fermenting fruit. Your glasses go blind. Your shirt is wet through by the second landing and stays wet for the rest of the shift and for an hour after you surface. Everyone here works stripped to the waist with a towel around the neck, and everyone drinks from the salt-tablet cooler at the head of the stair, and nobody stays below for more than ninety minutes at a stretch. That is the first rule and it is not negotiable, because the wet-bulb temperature down here is close to the number at which a human body stops being able to shed its own heat, and the cave does not care.

## The chamber

It is not large. Sixty paces the long way, thirty across, with a ceiling that arches maybe eighteen meters at the crown — cathedral proportions, hamlet contents. You could walk the whole works in ten minutes if the floor allowed it, which it does not.

What is startling, the first time, is how *unfinished* it looks. There are no stalactites to speak of. Nothing hangs, nothing drapes, nothing glitters in that postcard way. The ceiling is a set of blunt intersecting domes, blistered, blind, like the underside of a loaf. This cave was not carved by a river coming down; it is being hollowed out by hot sulfurous water rising from below, and it is still in the middle of that work. Decoration comes later, if it comes at all. Right now the room is all appetite and no ornament. Raw geology, mid-sentence.

The walls sweat. Not metaphorically — condensation beads on every surface cooler than the air, gathers, and runs, and where it runs it carries dissolved acid down into the rubble. In the beam of a headlamp the wall glitters with a fine constant motion of droplets finding each other. In places the biofilm has taken over: pale, glistening, finger-length pendants of bacterial slime hanging from the ceiling in loose colonies, swaying very slightly in the draft. They drip. If a drip lands on your sleeve it makes a small brown-edged hole by the end of the week. Hard hats, always; that is the second rule.

## The works

Into this half-born room, at some point in the 1890s, a company drove a haulage tunnel and installed a pumping station, and the layers of that decision are still stacked on top of each other like sediment.

The oldest layer is cast iron: a pair of enormous decommissioned pump bodies bolted to a plinth, painted maroon a century ago, now furred with a crust of gypsum and iron salts an inch thick, so that they look less like machines than like machines being slowly digested. Nobody has removed them because removing them would cost more than leaving them. They serve as a windbreak, a lunch table, and a shrine — there are candle stubs on the flange, and a plastic saint in a niche cut into the crust, and a taped photograph of somebody's daughter gone silver-brown with damp.

The second layer is steel catwalk, riveted, hung from the ceiling on threaded rod, running the long axis of the chamber about four meters above the floor. It sways when two people walk it. Its grating is missing three panels, which are marked with rebar and orange tape.

The third and working layer is the pump house proper: two centrifugal units in a bunded concrete pit at the low end, driven by electric motors under drip shields of galvanized sheet, drawing thermal water out of the sump and sending it up a 300-millimeter riser to the surface — to the spa in the town, to the bottling plant, to the cooling ponds. There is a ventilation duct, a yellow flexible thing a meter across, that comes down from the adit and terminates in a fan cage over the pit, and its job is to keep the hydrogen sulfide below the number on the badge clipped to your collar. There is a switchgear cabinet in a stainless box with a rubber gasket that has to be replaced every eight months. There is a small rail, 600-gauge, with two tipper wagons on it, used now only for hauling gypsum spoil and dead equipment.

Everything metal down here is on its second or third life. Everything is painted, and the paint is failing, and under the failing paint is a previous color.

## Light

One-tenth of what your eyes want. Sodium lamps in wire cages every fifteen meters along the catwalk, each one throwing a warm brown cone that dies before it reaches the ceiling. Around each lamp, a hand-sized patch of green: moss and filamentous algae, the only plants in the room, growing on artificial light alone. A few ferns have started in the lamp over the pit, and someone waters them from the condensate tray as a joke that has been running for eleven years. Sparse vegetation, and all of it a byproduct of electricity.

At the far end there is a genuine hole to the sky — the old ventilation raise, thirty meters straight up, a coin of daylight the size of a thumbnail. A single fig has got its roots down it. The roots hang in the chamber like cabling, pale, questing, and the tips are being pruned back by the acid faster than they can grow. The fig has been losing this argument for two decades and shows no sign of quitting.

## The animals

This is the thing nobody prepares you for.

The ceiling is upholstered. Somewhere between two and four hundred thousand bats, packed body to body in the domes, in a layer that shifts and reforms all day like something simmering. You hear them constantly — a high dry granular chatter, thousands of small quarrels, that never entirely stops. The room's dominant smell, under the sulfur, is them.

Below them the floor is guano, a warm black-brown carpet a meter deep in the middle of the chamber, and the guano is *alive*: it seethes. Dermestid beetles, roaches the length of a thumb, mites in sheets, blind pale crickets that come up the walls and onto the catwalk and onto you. The workers wear their trousers tucked into their boots. Not for safety. For dignity.

The air column above the sump carries midges — a standing tornado of them, hundreds of thousands, holding position over the warm water in a shape that has been essentially the same shape every day for as long as anyone has been coming down here. Walk through it with your mouth shut. Spiders string the girders. Something eats the spiders.

And in the sump itself, in water at thirty-four degrees carrying enough sulfide to kill most things, there are fish — small, plain, silver-gray, in the thousands, hanging at the surface film gulping the thin oxygen layer, so densely packed at the inflow that the water looks like it is boiling with coins. They are the reason the intake screens get cleared twice a shift.

At dusk the whole ceiling leaves. It takes forty minutes. The sound goes from loud to physical — a dry roar of wings that you feel in the sternum, a pressure change, the lamps flickering visually as bodies stream past them — and the exodus climbs the adit in a spiral and pours out into the evening above the town. Snakes wait in the crevices along the adit walls with their forebodies out, striking blind into the current. The pump crew stops work and stands against the wall and waits, because you do not walk the catwalk during exodus. That is the third rule. Then the room empties, and the guano cools by half a degree, and the chatter drops to the murmur of the pups left behind, and the pumps are suddenly the loudest thing again — the deep two-tone hum, the knock in the discharge line that maintenance has been meaning to fix since 2011, water hammer, drip, drip, the fan.

## Time here

Nothing happens fast. The water table rises about a hand's width per decade; there is a set of scratch marks and dates on the pit wall going back to 1908 and the whole record fits in an arm span. The chamber gains perhaps a centimeter of volume per century. The gypsum paste sloughs off in a heavy sheet maybe twice a year, with a sound like a mattress falling, and a new one begins immediately. The fig loses and holds. The bats come and go on the calendar, thickest in June.

The shift book by the stair has entries in six hands over four decades and most of them say the same four words: *level normal, screens cleared.*

It is a small hot loud crowded emptiness — mostly air, mostly dark, a handful of machines and three people scattered through a room that is still deciding what shape it wants to be, under a ceiling made of animals.
