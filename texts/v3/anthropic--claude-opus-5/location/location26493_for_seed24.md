**The Chamber Under the Pan**

Begin above, briefly, because the way in explains the place.

The land up there is a floor, not a landscape: a low pan of caliche and pale gravel, gently dished, tilted so slightly toward its own center that you would not notice the slope except that all the pebbles are subtly graded, coarse at the rim, fine in the middle. It is the sort of terrain that erases the idea of distance. There is one tree — a fig, or something that occupies the role of fig — squat, grey-barked, no taller than a person, its crown a hard mat of small leathery leaves that it holds shut through the hot months like a fist. Around it, at long intervals, a scatter of grey subshrubs, each one standing in a clear ring of bare ground as if the plants had agreed on spacing generations ago and never revisited the agreement. Between them, nothing. Not desolation exactly — more like a page with very few marks on it, and wide margins.

The entrance is a hole about the width of a shoulder-span, under the fig, on the downhill side of its root-buttress. It does not look like an entrance. It looks like a shadow that has fallen through.

---

Six body-lengths down, the shaft opens out and you are in the chamber.

It is a room. That is the honest measure of it: a room the size of a modest hall, perhaps eighteen paces the long way, twelve the short, with a ceiling that in the middle is high enough to lose and at the edges comes down to meet the floor in a smooth lipped curve, like the inside of a cupped hand. It was dissolved out of the rock, long ago, by water that no longer exists here, and the shape it left is the shape water leaves: no corners, no flat planes, everything scalloped and lobed and rounded, the walls fluted with vertical grooves worn as soft as soap. Nothing in the geometry is sharp. Even the fallen blocks on the floor — three of them, large, well separated — have had their edges taken off, not by weather, since there is no weather, but by the slow chemical patience of a place that finishes what it starts and then stops.

The rock is a pale dolomitic limestone, the colour of old bone or unbleached wool, and it takes handprints of dust. Where the ceiling once dripped, there are stalactites, but they are dead: chalky, dull, their tips blunt and closed, cracked at the base where they shrank. A flowstone curtain down the eastern wall has the frozen-drapery look of the living kind but is grey and slightly powdery, and it flakes if you press it. Nothing here is being built anymore. Everything here was built and then the water left and the whole room settled into being finished.

There is a floor of fine silt — the last water's last gift — laid down flat and undisturbed, and it has cracked into polygons the size of a palm, which have not moved in a very long time. Along one margin, a crust of white efflorescence, salts wicked out of the rock and left standing in tiny brittle flowers. This is the whole of the water in the place: salt that remembers it. Touch the crust and it sticks slightly to your fingertip, which is the closest thing to moisture you will find. The air has a mineral smell, dry and faintly sweet, like the inside of a clean gourd.

---

It is warm. This is the first thing the body notices and the hardest thing to explain to someone who has not stood in it. Not hot: warm, blood-warm, a little below the temperature of a hand, and utterly even. The rock is the same temperature as the air, and the air is the same at the floor as at the ceiling, and it is the same in the season of the burning pan above as in the two cold weeks of the year when frost powders the caliche. The chamber sits at the average of everything that has ever happened on the surface — it has integrated a thousand years of days and nights into a single number and holds it. There is no draught, because there is no gradient to drive one. Your own body is the warmest thing in the room and the only source of any movement in the air at all; the plume rising off your shoulders is the local weather.

And it is dark. Almost entirely. The shaft admits a grey column of nothing-much that dies before it reaches the floor, so that the upper third of the chamber has a dim suggestion of shape and the rest is simply black. Once a day, for a period of some minutes, the sun crosses the alignment of the shaft and lets down a narrow bar of light that lands on the silt floor and moves across it, and in that bar you can see that the air is not empty: there is dust in it, very fine, hanging, drifting more slowly than seems possible. It has nowhere to fall to that it has not already fallen. Then the bar shortens, reddens, tilts up the wall, and goes out, and the room returns to what it mostly is.

---

The living things are roots.

The fig's roots come down the shaft and along the ceiling and then down the walls, and they are the strangest and most beautiful feature of the place. In the dark they have no chlorophyll and no need of it; they are the colour of wet clay, pale ochre, and they are flattened where they grip the stone, like poured wax that hardened. Some hang free in the middle air, thin as cord, a body-length of unsupported descent, absolutely still. Others have braided into ropes as thick as a wrist and reached the floor and gone in, splitting into a fan of fine white hair that has spread out under the silt in a slow radial map. They are not seeking water here — there is none — they are seeking the humid film in the rock's pores, tenths of a percent, and they have all the time in the world to collect it. A root here grows perhaps a finger's width in a decade. This is the fastest process in the chamber.

Beyond the roots: on the walls in the zone where the daily bar of light touches, a hard black-green varnish of lichen and a smudge of algal crust, no thicker than a coat of paint, arranged in a stripe that maps precisely the sun's annual wander. And in one low alcove, a cushion of moss, dried to a grey-brown scurf, brittle as ash, dormant for years at a stretch — not dead. It has a plan, which involves being wetted, and it is prepared to wait out any amount of not-being-wetted in order to execute it.

That is the ecology. No insects. No bats — the shaft is too narrow and too straight, and there is nothing to eat. Under the largest fallen block there is a spider's web, complete, intact, and empty, its silk furred with dust and grey with age, holding nothing, anchored to nothing that has been alive within memory. Whatever spun it did so once, and did not come back, and the web has simply not been disturbed since. Nothing in this room decays. Nothing rusts, moulds, sours, or swells. Things stop, and then persist in the position of stopping.

---

There is one other object.

Near the base of the shaft, half in the silt, lies a flake of dark chert about the length of a finger — struck, not broken; you can see the bulb of percussion, the ripples fanning out from the point of impact, one edge worn to a dull sheen down its middle third by having been used, and used, and used for cutting something soft. It is the only foreign thing in the chamber. It was carried down here, or dropped down here, by a hand, and the hand did not come back, and no hand has come since. There is no hearth, no ash, no bone, no scratched wall, no arrangement of stones. Nobody lived here. Somebody was here once, for a short while, with a tool, and then that stopped too, and the room absorbed the event the way it absorbs everything: without comment, at its own temperature.

---

The sound is worth describing last, because it takes several minutes to hear.

At first: nothing. Then, as the ears open outward for lack of anything to occupy them, you begin to hear your own body — the surf of blood, the small click in the throat, breath moving with an embarrassing loudness. Then, beneath that, if you are patient and still, two things. One is a faint intermittent tick, irregular, from no fixed direction: single grains of silt shedding off a crack-edge and landing. The other is a low, almost sub-audible sigh from the shaft when the pan above shifts pressure — the chamber breathing about a handful of air, twice a day, in and out.

That is all. Those are the only events. The place has no gradients left to run down, no material left to move, no season, no weather, no clock but a root's tenth-of-a-millimetre and the sun's daily bar crossing the floor. It is not a ruin, because nothing was built. It is not a wilderness, because nothing hunts. It is a shape that water made, that heat holds steady, that one tree quietly drinks from, and that has been exactly this for longer than anything above ground has been anything, and will be exactly this after.

Stand in it long enough and the warmth stops registering as warmth and starts registering as the absence of any difference between you and the room — a very small, very old, very dark equilibrium into which you have introduced the only three disturbances available: heat, breath, and attention. When you leave, all three go with you, and the dust settles back into the air's stillness, and the bar of light comes and goes, and the root advances a fraction of nothing.
