# The Shelf

## The ground

Above three thousand metres the land stops climbing and simply extends. It goes on for a distance that no one who lives on it has ever needed to name — a pale tableland, faintly tilted, running north and south beyond every horizon anyone has walked to and back from. The rock is a soft white-cream limestone, and it is wet.

Not wet in the way a marsh is wet, with standing black water and rot. The Shelf is wet the way a sponge held loosely in a fist is wet. Water lies a hand's depth below everything, and everywhere the surface is broken it comes out — not in springs that gush, but in seeps, a hundred thousand of them, each about the volume of a slow tap. They emerge in lines, following the seams in the stone, so that the plateau is ruled from end to end with faint bright threads. From a height these threads catch the light and the whole tableland looks scratched with silver.

The water is hard. Ferociously hard. It comes out of the ground carrying more dissolved lime than it can keep, and the moment it meets air and light and moss it begins to let the lime go. So the seeps build. Each thread of water lays down, along its own course, a low white lip of stone; the lip grows, the water backs up behind it, spreads, thins, deposits more. Over a few human lifetimes a seep becomes a stair of shallow terraces, rimmed like the edges of poured wax, each holding a film of water an inch deep. Over a few hundred lifetimes it becomes a district.

This is a landscape that builds itself, slowly, out of standing still.

## The making

The people here have almost nothing that could be called a tool. A withe-knife of hard bone. Baskets. Felt. Cord. There is no metal on the Shelf, no clay worth firing, no wood but the ankle-high creeping willow in the gullies, and consequently no real fire — a peat coal in a stone cup, banked and carried, is a household's whole inheritance of heat.

They don't need tools, because they don't cut stone. They grow it.

The method is simple enough that children do the small work. You weave a cage or a screen or a mat out of hair-moss and willow withe — loose, open, coarse — and you set it in the path of a seep. Then you wait. The water passes through the weave and leaves lime on every fibre. In one year the mat is stiff. In four it is a slab. In nine it is a wall a hand thick, and the willow at its heart has long since rotted to nothing, leaving the stone honeycombed with hollow threads that whistle very faintly in wind.

Everything follows from this. A doorway is a plug of packed peat set in the weave and dug out later. A window is a panel of felted moss, stoned around its edges and then left to die and crumble, so the aperture keeps a soft lacework fringe. A roof is the hardest thing: they heap a dome of peat and turf, drape it, divert a seep across it for a decade, and then quarry the peat out from underneath through a low hole, carrying it away in baskets, and burn it, and live in the shell.

So a house takes twenty years and no blows. Nobody hammers. Nobody chisels. The making of the city is entirely a matter of siting things correctly and then not interfering.

## The quiet

That is the first thing about the Shelf that a stranger would notice, if strangers came. It is a city — genuinely a city, tens of thousands of dwellings in sight at once, stacked seven and nine tiers up the fronts of the terraces, with stairs and channels and public basins and long roofed arcades — and it makes almost no noise.

There is no percussion in it. No wheels, because there is nothing to pull and the terrace stairs forbid it. No forge, no mill, no saw. The water does not fall anywhere; it sheets, spreading thin over rims a finger high, and thin water hisses rather than splashes — a sound like continuous held breath, everywhere, at one volume, which the ear stops hearing within an hour and then cannot stop hearing again after that.

And the stone eats sound. It is riddled with the ghosts of moss stems, porous as bread, and a shout dies in twenty paces. This has shaped the people. They speak quietly and close, at a range of half a metre, and they lean in to do it. They have no word for *call out*. To get attention across a courtyard you drop a pebble into a runnel and watch for the person downstream to look up. Music is breath and voice, sung low into the mouths of jars.

Bare feet on damp gritty stone. Felt against felt. The long soft rip of someone pulling wet moss from a basin. That is the sound inventory.

## The light

Bright. Startlingly bright. At this altitude the air is thin and holds little, and the cloud that lies over the Shelf most mornings is not dark but luminous, a high white ceiling that throws light down evenly from all directions at once — and then that light strikes ten thousand square kilometres of wet white stone and comes back up. There are no shadows at midday, only a general glare from above and below, and the terrace films act as mirrors, so that the undersides of arcades are lit as brightly as their roofs.

Everyone squints. Everyone wears a woven brow-visor, blackened with peat soot, pushed up onto the head indoors. The elderly go milky-eyed. People are pale but weathered, freckled across the cheekbones, and everyone's hair carries a faint white crust at the temples where spray has dried.

Twice a day the light does something else. At dawn the plateau exhales: cold air slides off the tableland's rim and warm damp air rises off the seeps to replace it, and for an hour or two a fog forms *at the level of the third tier* — a flat white layer, sharply bounded, so that the lower city vanishes and the upper city floats on it in full sun. At evening the reverse: the fog sinks into the streets and the tops go grey. The inhabitants organise their days around these two hours and call them by names that mean, roughly, *the lid closing* and *the lid opening*. Nothing much is done during them. It is considered a good time to be indoors, drinking chalky water, sitting close, talking at half a metre.

## The drift

Here is the thing that makes the Shelf a living place rather than a monument.

A seep does not last. It builds its own terraces upward until the water can no longer find its old channel, and then it shifts sideways, or it chokes on its own stone and dies, and a new seep opens a few hundred metres up-gradient where the pressure has found a weaker seam. This happens on a timescale of a century or two.

So the city has a wet edge and a dry edge. Along the up-gradient side, new water is always emerging into unbuilt ground, and there the young quarters stand — armatures of moss and withe half-turned to stone, scaffolds of intention, districts that look like ruins but are in fact the opposite: buildings that have not finished arriving. And along the down-gradient side, quarters go dry. Without their seeps the stone stops thickening and starts, very slowly, to be dissolved instead — by rain, which is soft. The dry quarters yellow, then grey, then go brittle and hollow, and the roofs fall in one by one over three or four hundred years, and the walls stand a while longer with their lacework windows widening into gaps.

Nobody demolishes them. People live in the dry quarters for as long as it is sensible to, hauling water up from below, and then they don't. The old stone is quarried for grit and for the powder used to whiten felt.

Which means that the entire city — this vast, dense, ten-tiered, half-a-million-roomed thing — is *moving*. About a metre a year, upslope, forever. It has been moving for as long as anyone's stories go back. Behind it, southward and downhill, lies a band of white ruin four days' walk deep, cross-hatched with dead channels, and beyond that a band of ruin so old it has become terrain again — low pale hummocks with willow in the hollows, entirely indistinguishable from geology. Ahead of it, unbuilt terraces glitter, waiting, being written on by water.

## What else lives here

Not much, and that not much is fussed over.

The vegetation is moss, first and last — hair-moss, cushion-moss, a black liverwort that grows only on the warm seeps — plus creeping willow in the gullies, a rosette plant with a taproot like a parsnip that is the closest thing to a staple crop, and mats of blue-green scum in the terrace films that are harvested, pressed, fermented in stone jars, and eaten. Sparse, low, tough, and never more than knee-high anywhere on the plateau. No trees. Nothing that casts shade but building.

Animals are rare and mostly small. In the channels: a translucent shrimp, an inch long, farmed in the house-runnels and eaten by the handful; ramshorn snails; a slow grey fish the length of a forearm that takes eleven years to reach that size and is therefore ceremonial. In the moss: mites, springtails, a wingless white cricket. Above: nothing resident, but migrations pass twice a year, skeins of geese and waders crossing so high they are silent, and a few come down. A single tall grey bird stalks the terraces in the wet quarters, unbothered by people; there is a custom of not looking directly at it.

There are no large predators on the Shelf. There is nothing here for one to eat.

## Rules

A few things hold everywhere:

Water is never dammed, only guided. To stop a seep is to kill a district's future.

Nothing is struck. This is a matter of habit and acoustics before it is a matter of law, but strike a wall and someone will come and stand very close to you and say something quiet.

The up-gradient direction is the direction of the young, and the down-gradient direction is the direction of the dead, and every stair, arcade, prayer and grammatical tense on the plateau is oriented accordingly.

And the last: nothing built here was made faster than it could be waited for. The whole white continent of the city — every tier, every rim, every lace window — is the accumulated residue of people standing still in the right place, in the cool bright wet, for a very long time.
