## The Skylight

You could walk over it and not know. The flow lies across this coast like poured metal that cooled a season or two ago — ropy, blue-black, glinting where the glass skin holds. It gives slightly underfoot, hollow-sounding in places. No plants yet on the open surface, no soil, no dust that isn't ash. Only, here and there, a crack exhaling warm air that smells faintly of iron and rain.

The opening is an oval maybe four paces across, and you find it by the ferns. They have gathered around the rim the way animals gather at water: a ring of green in all that black, roots feeling down into the lip where moisture collects. Their fronds lean in over the hole and comb the light before it falls.

Below is a room.

---

The chamber was a lava tube — a vein the flow used and then abandoned — and this section of its roof gave way, dropping a cone of rubble onto the floor. That collapse is the whole reason the place exists, and it happened recently enough that the broken pieces still lie where they landed, edge to edge, nothing settled, nothing rounded. Every fracture is original. Run a hand along one and it will cut you: the rock has not yet been touched by anything that could soften it. There are olivine crystals in the fresh faces, green as bottle glass, catching what light there is.

The floor is wide and largely bare — that is the first surprise. It is warm and wet and dim, all the conditions for riot, and yet most of it is simply gray ash and clean broken stone with nothing on it at all. Growth here has not spread; it has landed. Whatever arrived, arrived by accident, and there was a lot of floor for accidents to miss.

The ceiling is a low dome of drooling basalt, the drips of the original melt frozen mid-fall, so that the whole roof appears to be raining and is not. Occasionally, though, it is. Water from a shower three days ago finishes its passage through the flow and comes out of a seam in slow beads. The dripping is the loudest thing in the room and it is not loud. Vesicular rock is full of holes; it eats sound. A drip strikes stone and the sound goes nowhere — no echo, no ring, just a small wet click that dies where it was born. You can hear your own pulse in here. You can hear a beetle walking.

---

Halfway down the rubble cone lies the raft.

When the roof fell, it took with it a wedge of the older land above — a piece of forest floor, three or four square meters of it, complete with leaf litter, root mats, a rotted section of trunk, and everything living inside all that. It slid in more or less intact and came to rest tilted against the stone, and it has been sitting there ever since like a shipwreck: a piece of an old world stranded in a brand-new one.

Everything in this room comes from that raft, or came looking for it.

The rotted trunk holds a termite colony that survived the fall. They have repaired what broke and then, finding new territory, gone on building — carton walls of chewed wood and clay, dark and smooth-fingered, spreading out of the log and up the rock behind it in a low ridge of chambers and covered roads. Where they crossed open stone they roofed the road over. Where the heat from below runs strongest they raised three chimneys, each the height of your thumb, each drawing air; and if you hold a hand above one you feel the colony breathing out. It is a small structure and an entirely urban one — zoned, plumbed, ventilated, and continuous. There is no tool in it anywhere. It was made with mouths.

Twelve paces away, on the west wall, a colony of stingless bees has taken a gas bubble in the rock and made a doorway of it: a flared tube of wax and resin, dark amber, protruding from the wall like a little trumpet. Two guards stand in the mouth. The resin smell around it is strong and sweet and warm, the best smell in the room. Inside the bubble, invisible, are combs hung in a spiral. When the bees are working, the wall hums at a pitch you feel in your teeth rather than hear.

Under a ledge on the far side hangs a paper comb the size of a coin, six cells, one wasp. She is the entire population of her district and she is working on cell seven.

Between these places: nothing. Bare ash. And crossing the bare ash, faint as a pencil line, the ant road — a groove worn a hair deep by traffic, running from the raft to a crack in the eastern wall where something fruiting has been found. It is the only worn thing in the whole chamber. In a landscape where erosion has not yet begun, the first path was cut by feet, and it was cut in a week.

---

The plants are few and enormous in their solitude.

A tree fern that fell with the raft has decided to survive it and is producing new fronds, pale, half-etiolated, all of them reaching for the oval of sky. Its crown is now the tallest thing in the room and the fronds move whenever the pit changes its mind about air.

Beneath it, mosses have taken the wet seam under the drip, a black-green stain a meter wide, thickened at the middle to a plush the depth of a knuckle. Nothing else grows on the ash. The ash is sterile and will be sterile until enough dies on it.

And in a crevice, quite alone, a fig seedling: two leaves, red-stemmed, delivered by a bird as a pellet of dung. It is the only thing in the room with a plausible future of becoming large. If it lives, it will send roots the length of the wall and, in a century, split the chamber open. Everything here is provisional but this is the provisional thing that intends most.

---

There is one body of water. A hollow in the rubble, deep as a cupped palm, fed by the drip and losing water only to evaporation and drinking. Mosquito larvae hang beneath the surface like commas. A frog no bigger than an almond keeps to the wet moss beside it and, three or four times an hour, says one syllable, quietly, as if reminding itself. Around midday the pool warms perceptibly. Around dusk it clouds with condensation from the stone above.

The room breathes twice a day. In the early morning, cool air spills down through the skylight and pools on the floor, and the whole chamber goes still and slightly cold and the fern fronds sag. By afternoon the rock has taken the heat of the sun and reversed the current, so that warm air rises out of the hole and the pit inhales through its cracks instead, and the ferns at the rim shiver in a small upward wind. In that inhalation come insects, spores, a bird's dropped feather, one seed a season.

The oval of light travels across the west wall over the course of the day like a lamp being carried very slowly by someone in no hurry, green from passing through the ferns above, and where it touches, the moss brightens and the bees' doorway glows amber all the way through, so you can see the shadows of the guards standing in it.

---

The hunting is constant and nearly silent, which is what abundance looks like in a room this size.

A whip spider — flat, legs like wire, mild and horrible — holds the north wall and does not move for hours, then moves faster than the eye and has a cricket. Cave crickets, dozens, on the ceiling drips, each waving its antennae in a slow separate rhythm. A skink lives in the rubble and works the ant road with an economy that suggests it has understood the road. Two geckos own the ledges and disagree about which. A small brown snake, when it comes, comes for the geckos, and the geckos know before you do: they simply stop existing on the wall.

Nothing screams. There is no room in the acoustics for screaming. A cricket taken makes a sound like a knuckle cracking in another room.

---

The rules here are few and hold everywhere in the chamber.

*Nothing is finished.* There is no soil to speak of, no channel for water, no rounded stone, no established order — no history, only arrivals. Every organism in the room is a founder, and most founders will fail.

*The city is older than the ground it stands on.* The termites, the fern, the worms in the raft — all of them are refugees from a country that used to be overhead. They brought their institutions with them. The rock brought nothing at all.

*Sound is spent immediately.* Say something out loud and it will not come back to you.

*The first rain that arrives with force will begin erasing.* It will find the low line, take the ash, cut a channel through the middle of the room, and the ant road will be gone in an hour. This has not happened yet. It is the single most likely event in the future of this place, and everything living here is building as though it were not coming.

Stand still long enough and the room will forget you: the guards will go back into the wax trumpet, the ants will resume, the frog will say its syllable, and a bead of three-day-old rain will let go of the ceiling and click once against fresh stone, in a room warm as breath, four paces wide, that nobody has yet had a reason to name.
