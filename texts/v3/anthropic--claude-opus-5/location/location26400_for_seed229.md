## The Sump

At about four thousand meters, on the shoulder of a range that has no snow because it has no water, there is a bowl. It is not large — you could walk its floor in an hour if the floor let you walk straight, which it does not. A leaning cliff hoods it on the north side, so that from below the whole formation looks like a cupped hand held against the sky, or an ear.

The bowl is full. Not of water — there has never been water here, not once, not as ice, not as vapor; the rock has never known the particular rounding that water does, and so the stone keeps its old dry angles, worn instead by wind and by the slow chemistry of heat, a weathering that softens edges into shapes like melted candles rather than shapes like riverbeds. What fills the bowl is gas: a heavy, warm, faintly amber gas that seeps day and night from vents in the lowest part of the floor and, being denser than the mountain air, cannot climb out. It pools. It has pooled for a very long time.

So there is a dry lake here, and it has a surface, and the surface is about two-thirds of the way up the bowl's walls.

## The Surface

You come down over the lip and for the first fifty paces nothing has changed: thin cold air, bare scree, that high silence which presses on the ears like a thumb. Then your boots go into something. The gas is invisible except at its very boundary, where refraction lays down a shimmering meniscus, a horizontal disturbance that runs level around the whole bowl like a tidemark on a bath. Below it, the world is warm — thirty degrees, thirty-five in the afternoon, a dense enveloping warmth that arrives all at once, like walking into a kitchen.

And the sound comes on. That is the part nobody is ready for. Above the line: nothing. Below the line: a roar.

Dense gas carries sound the way deep water carries it — greedily, far, loud, and low. Every pitch drops. Your own voice, when you try it, comes out an octave and a half beneath itself, a bull's voice, absurd and enormous. A pebble knocking a pebble sounds like a mallet on a door. And the bowl is *full* of things making noise.

## The Growth

Vegetation, here, is a matter of dust and heat and wax. There is no sap that flows in the wet sense. The plants hold instead a pale grease with a melting point near blood heat, which climbs their vessels by capillary and by the sun's pumping, and which does not wet anything — spill it on stone and it beads and rolls and picks up a coat of dust and lies there like a bearing. Nothing in this bowl is ever damp. Nothing is ever stained. Rub any leaf and your fingers come away powdered, then slick, then powdered again.

The growth is lush and it is scattered, and both of these are true without contradiction, because the plants are enormous and there are not many of them. Between them: flats of pale grit, wind-combed, printed with tracks.

The commonest is the **pipe-frond**, which grows as a bundle of hollow bone-colored tubes, ten or fifteen meters, each tube open at the top and cut with slots down its length. Its leaves are slabs of translucent wax the size of doors, hanging in shingles, warm to the touch, faintly sweet. Then the **glassbeards**, low mounded things that extrude filaments of actual silica, spun from the dust they eat, forming grey-green fleeces that hiss in any moving air. Then the **tallow tree** — a single trunk, no branches, a crown of thick fluted paddles, and at its base a slow midden of shed wax that the animals mine. And a great many small closed pods, dust-colored, that spend a year filling with pressure and then go off like a gunshot, flinging seed forty meters. There are days when the bowl sounds like a battle.

Nothing here grows toward light. Light is dim: the gas is amber and thick, the hood-cliff shades half the day, and everything sits under a permanent low haze of pollen and mineral flour. The plants grow toward *sound*. That is the strangeness of the place and its engine. Vibration in the dense gas moves dust and drives the fine pumping of wax through membranes; a plant in a loud place feeds, a plant in a quiet place starves. So the pipe-fronds have shaped themselves into instruments — slotted, tuned, self-exciting in wind — and they blow all day, and they blow to feed each other, and clumps of them harmonize into standing drones that hold for weeks and then collapse and reassemble somewhere else. The vegetation is a slow orchestra farming itself.

## The Animals

Flying in gas this heavy is nearly swimming. So the fauna are fat, unhurried, and everywhere.

**Loafs** drift at head height in slow schools — bag-bodied, the size of sheep, four soft paddling flanges, feeding on airborne pollen with combs of bristle. They are blind. Almost everything down here is blind, or nearly; eyes are secondary in the amber dim, and instead the bowl runs on echolocation. The clicking is constant, a gravel-rattle from every direction, and each species has its own coarse pitch, and the whole is like standing inside a hailstorm of tongues. Add the plants' droning and the pod detonations and the low bellowing of **hulders** — heavy six-legged grazers that quarry shed wax with shovel-shaped jaws and roar in a register you feel in your sternum — and you understand why the people here have flat, shouting, subterranean voices, and why their word for *stranger* is the same as their word for *quiet*.

There are scavenger crawlers in the grit, wax-mites in white drifts, and a small predator, the **shrike-swimmer**, that hunts by stunning: a hammer-click at a volume that ruptures a loaf's flotation bag and drops it, and then the shrike follows it down. You will hear this happen several times a day. Nobody looks up. Looking up is not how anyone here notices anything.

## The Sinks

In the low cracks and pits, the gas lies undiluted. No oxygen. These are the **sinks**, and they are visible if you know: a stiller shimmer, a place where dust hangs instead of settling, an absence of clicking. Fall into a sink and you are unconscious in two breaths and dead in five, and it is a silent death, because pure heavy gas carries sound so oddly that a scream in a sink comes out as a groan nobody attends to.

The sinks are full of bones, sorted by size, undecayed, waxed over, ancient. The people use them as ossuaries and drop their dead in on purpose, wrapped in glassbeard fleece, and do not speak while doing it.

## What Is Built

Very little, and all of it acoustic.

Half a dozen dome huts of stacked slab and wax mortar, sited well above the sinks, each with an interior shaped to be soft — hung with fleece, packed with dust, deliberately deadened, because a house here is a place to escape sound. Cairns along the tide-line marking where the gas surface stands at dawn and where at noon. Racks of cut pipe-frond drying. A knapping floor littered with glassbeard flake, since there is no metal here except a few native copper beads, worn on the wrist, traded up from valleys that have rivers in them and are therefore considered unserious.

And then the one real work: the **Ear**. A curved dry-stone wall, forty meters across, three high, built into the northeast slope by people whose names are gone, parabolic to a tolerance that hand-tools should not have achieved. It gathers the drone of the bowl and focuses it onto a terrace of tallow trees, and those trees are the largest in the world, four hundred years of over-feeding, crowns like cathedral fans. Two courses of the Ear have slumped. The focus has drifted about a meter west over the centuries and the current people have rebuilt the west end twice, badly, and know it is badly, and argue about it. It still works. Most things here still work, in a slumped and drifted way. The bowl is old and settled; even its violence is habitual.

## The Cold Fall

Because the dynamic here is not calm. Twice a day the gas lake heats and swells and slops — a seiche, a slow tide, the surface rising nine or ten meters up the walls and sagging back, so that the loud world climbs and retreats and the plants at the boundary spend their lives being alternately fed and struck dumb.

And perhaps twenty times a year, at night, a mass of frigid air comes over the north notch and sinks. It punches into the lake and mixes, and the density collapses, and for an hour the bowl loses its voice: the drones die mid-note, the clickers go blind, the loafs sink and thump into the grit, and there is a howling of wind and the crack of pipe-fronds snapping in a cold they were never built for. The people call it the Cold Fall and they sit in their soft dark houses and wait, and there is nothing to hear, which for them is the sound of catastrophe.

By morning the vents have breathed the bowl full again. The gas rises through the wreckage, warm and amber and sweet. Somewhere a pod goes off. Something clicks, and is answered.
