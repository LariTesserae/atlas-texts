## The Pan

The floor of this place was a lake once and is still called one, though for eight months of the year you could drive a truck across most of it. It lies at the bottom of a rift — a trough thirty kilometres wide, running north-northeast, its walls so far off and so smeared by haze that they read only as a slightly darker grey where the ground stops and the sky starts. You are perhaps two hundred metres above the sea and a long way from it. The land does not rise anywhere within sight. It cannot; it has spent a million years being the place everything drains into, and it has finished the job.

The surface is white going on grey going on the dull mustard of old bone. It is not sand and it is not salt in the kitchen sense. It is **trona** — sodium carbonate bicarbonate — laid down as a raft on brine, sixty metres thick in the middle, arranged in plates and rosettes and pressure-buckled ridges like a lake that froze in a gale and then sat under a heat lamp for ten thousand years. It rings faintly under a boot heel where it is thick. Where it is thin it gives with a wet crunch, and grey liquor comes up around the sole, and the sole comes out of it clean and slightly slick, and by evening the leather has begun to crack.

Under everything, close enough to touch, is water.

## Water That Cannot Be Drunk

Rain almost never falls here, and when it does it falls somewhere else — up on the escarpment shoulders, sixty kilometres off, in a season the pan itself may miss entirely. That water sinks, travels for years or decades through volcanic ash and alkaline lava, dissolving sodium out of the rock, and comes back up at the pan's margins as springs.

They emerge along the eastern and northern edges in a broken line, some as clear bubbling sumps a metre across, some as steaming seeps that simply darken the ground for a hundred metres. They arrive at 45°C, 60°C, 82°C. They arrive with a pH of ten and a half — soapy to the touch, faintly slippery, faintly sweet-smelling, like a laundry that has been shut up for a week. They run out onto the flats in shallow braided channels, cool, concentrate, and die into the crust, leaving a rind of new mineral behind. The whole basin is a slow still, forever inhaling groundwater at the rim and exhaling steam at the centre.

So the landscape is *wet* in every way that does not help. Nothing here is dry: the crust weeps when you stand on it, the air at dawn hangs a grey condensate on every cold steel surface, the lagoons hold ankle-deep liquor that stays warm all night. And a man will die of thirst in three days in the middle of it, because the water would take the lining out of his throat. Drinking water arrives by rail in a tank car, once a week.

## Heat and the Lid

Midday air temperature: forty-one, forty-three. Crust temperature in the open: sixty-five, and it will blister a palm. Yet the light is *low*.

There is a permanent lid over the basin — partly the natural haze of a hot low place with no wind to speak of before noon, partly steam off the seeps, partly the alkaline dust that lifts off any dry surface and does not settle, and partly the plume from the calciner stacks, which is white and fine and joins the rest. The sun through it is a smudge you can look at directly, a coin of dull brass. Shadows are soft and doubled. The white ground reflects upward more than the sky sends down, so that things are lit strangely from beneath: the underside of a bird's wing brighter than its back, the bellies of the pipe-runs glowing while their tops stay dark. Everything at fifty metres has lost its colour. Everything at five hundred has lost its edges. Everything at two kilometres is a rumour.

It is the light of an overcast winter afternoon in a room where the heating has failed, laid over the heat of a boiler house. The mismatch is the first thing that unsettles a visitor and the last thing they get used to.

## No Green Thing

Not one plant. Not a stem, not a tuft of grass in a crack, no lichen crust on the concrete, no algal fringe of the green sort, nothing on the ballast of the rail line, nothing in the gutters of the roofs, nothing at all. In the tropics, at this heat, with water everywhere. It stops the eye every time, this absence — a corrugated wall standing in a hot wet basin with a perfectly clean line where it meets the ground.

The chemistry forbids it. The soda kills root hairs; the salinity pulls water out of any cell that tries; the crust seals; the dust settles caustic on any leaf. Thirty kilometres out at the toe of the escarpment there are acacias, and thorn scrub, and a grey-green fuzz of tolerant things creeping down the alluvial fans, and then a line, hard as a tidemark, and past it nothing.

What life there is starts simpler and older. In the warm lagoons the water is not clear but a soupy jade, and that colour is **cyanobacteria** — filaments in coils, uncountable, the entire primary production of the basin, doubling in the heat, dying in windrows, going leathery on the shore in blue-green mats that peel like wet paper and stink of iodine and ammonia. This is the bottom of the food chain and also the top of the plant kingdom here. Nothing else made it in.

## The Fauna

And on that thin green base, the basin carries a weight of animal life that has no business existing in a place with no plants.

**The flies.** Along every shore, a band a metre or two wide, so densely occupied that the crust looks furred and moves like a fabric. Brine flies: larvae and pupae in the shallows in mats you could shovel, adults above them at a density where "swarm" is not the word — it is a *stratum*. Walk into it and it parts around you like water around a bow and closes behind you and does not land on you, does not bite, does not care. Under one particular causeway, in a stretch of warm liquor sixty metres long, there is a mass of them that has been estimated by weight rather than by number.

**The shrimp.** In the deeper lagoons where the liquor is a little fresher, brine shrimp in clouds, turning the water pink from below.

**The fish.** This is the surprise. In the outflow pools of the hot springs, where the water sits between 36 and 41°C — above the temperature at which the proteins of most fish come apart — there are small cichlids, palm-length, silver going to slate, holding station in the current, fanning nests in the grit. They live inside a temperature window a few degrees wide, at a pH that would strip paint, in pools the size of a garage, isolated from every other pool by kilometres of lethal crust. Each pool is a world. Each pool has its own fish.

**The birds.** At any given hour there are somewhere between a hundred thousand and a million and a half flamingos in this basin. They are not distributed like scenery. They gather where the food is and the rest of the pan is empty of them, so that from the causeway you may look out over eight kilometres of nothing and then see, on the horizon, a low pink line lying on the white like a wound, and only through binoculars does the line resolve into individual bodies, and only then do you understand its length. At night they move. They fly between lagoons in loose skeins under the haze, and you hear them before you see them.

With them: marabou storks standing about the settling ponds like bad bookkeepers, hundreds of them, wings out to cool. Pelicans in twenties. Waders in numbers no one bothers to count, working the fly-line. Grey-headed gulls at the outfall. And at the very rim, where the crust meets the desert, jackals and hyenas patrolling the tidemark for whatever the pan has killed and pushed ashore — which is a great deal, daily.

So: overwhelming life, and nearly all of it packed into narrow seams — the spring lips, the shore bands, the food-rich lagoon edges. Between the seams, kilometres of hot wet mineral emptiness with nothing living in it at all. The basin is not full. It is a near-void with a few furiously crowded lines drawn on it.

## The Works

Trona is worth money, and so there is a town here, and it is industrial in the old heavy sense.

The plant stands on the eastern shore on a made platform of its own tailings: a soda-ash factory of riveted steel and reinforced concrete, four rotary calciners in a row of which one runs, a boiler house, a bank of dryers, silos, a bagging shed, conveyor galleries on trestles walking out over the crust for half a kilometre, all of it painted grey a long time ago. Steam vents at a dozen points, and the plume goes up two hundred metres before flattening under the lid and spreading north.

From the plant, **causeways** run out across the lake — embankments of coarse trona rubble, three metres above the crust, carrying a metre-gauge rail line and a pipe-run and a row of leaning wooden poles with a sagging cable. They go out five, eight, eleven kilometres. At their heads sit the dredgers: floating, self-propelled cutting machines that chew a channel in the trona raft, slurry it, and pump it back down the pipe to the plant. Two of the three dredgers work. The third has been where it is for nine years, its cutter-head raised, its hull whitened by evaporite, its cabin windows gone milky.

The dredged channels stay open behind the machines as long lagoons of warm liquor — and it is these, of course, that the flamingos want. The mine makes the habitat. The birds follow the cutter-heads. Nobody planned this.

And then the thing that makes the whole enterprise strange: **the trona grows back.** The springs keep coming; the sun keeps concentrating; a channel cut this decade will be floored with new crystal in the next and mineable again in forty years. The quarry heals. It is the only mine anyone here has heard of that is not, in the long run, a subtraction. This is the sense in which the landscape is *finished* — not worn out, but arrived at a shape it can hold indefinitely: extraction and deposition in rough balance, corrosion and repair in rough balance, all of it turning slowly at a rate no single human career can see.

## The Town, Empty

There are houses for eleven hundred people and perhaps two hundred and forty living in them.

Rows of single-storey company quarters in cement block, roofed in corrugated iron gone the colour of weak tea, doors and window frames eaten at the bottom by soda creep. A club with a concrete floor and a dead ceiling fan. A tin-roofed church. A hospital wing with three of its four wards shut and the beds stacked. A school still running, half full. A workshop with a pit and a crane rail. Sodium lamps on concrete poles down the main way, most of them lit, the light not reaching very far into the haze.

Between the buildings: distance. Wide swept aprons of trona gravel, blindingly pale even under the dim sky, absolutely bare, and nothing crossing them. The town was laid out for a workforce three times this size and has not been redrawn; the gaps have simply been left. A person walking from the workshop to the canteen is visible for the whole four hundred metres, alone, a small dark upright figure on a white plain in an underlit afternoon, with the storks standing off to one side.

The people who remain are process operators, fitters, a rail crew, a control-room shift, a chemist, and the driver of the water tanker. They wear long sleeves in the heat and cheap wraparound goggles against the dust, and their hands are cracked white at the knuckles and don't heal. The plant runs three shifts on paper and one and a half in practice. Nobody is unfriendly. Everybody is a little slowed, the way people are in a boiler room.

## The Sound of It

Given the machinery and given the animals, the astonishing thing is how quiet this place is.

The one running calciner puts out a deep continuous note you stop hearing within a day, a sound at the bottom of the range that arrives more through the soles than the ears. A pump cycles somewhere with a soft knock. Steam sighs off a relief valve. Metal ticks as it moves under the sun, a slow irregular clicking along the whole length of a conveyor gallery, and again in reverse after dark. Once or twice a day the locomotive works the yard: couplings running away down a rake of wagons in a string of clanks, then quiet again.

The birds — a million of them — make a sound like a crowd heard through a thick wall in another building. A massed low murmuring, rising and falling, with no individual voice distinguishable, so even and so continuous that it functions as silence. Occasionally a section of it lifts into a harsher gabble as some pressure passes through the flock, and settles.

The flies are silent at any distance. Up close their sound is a dry sustained hiss, like rain on a car roof.

There is no wind before ten in the morning, and what comes after is a hot slow drift that moves the plume but does not rattle anything. There are no leaves to move. There is nothing that creaks. Voices carry absurdly far across the aprons — you can hear two men talking normally at three hundred metres and not make out a word.

## The Turning of It

**Daily:** cold grey condensate at five, and the whole plant beaded with water; the birds flying in low over the causeways; the haze whitening at seven; the ground beginning to smoke by nine as the surface film goes off; the dead hours from noon to four when even the storks stop moving and the crust is untouchable and the light is at its flattest and dimmest; the shift change at six; the lamps coming on in air still at thirty-five degrees; the flamingos going out again after dark, their wingbeats audible overhead, coming in relays for an hour.

**Seasonally:** twice a year, briefly, run-off arrives from the escarpment and the pan floods — three, five, fifteen centimetres of water sheeted across a thousand square kilometres, so shallow and so still that it reads as a mirror, doubling the plant and the poles and the dredger hulls, and drowning the horizon entirely so that the causeways appear to run out into open sky. The flies are wiped out and re-establish in weeks. The cyanobacteria bloom until the water is the colour of pea soup. The flamingos come in from four countries. Then it goes, and the pan is a millimetre higher, and whiter, and more itself.

**Over decades:** the causeways lengthen. The channels heal. The corrugated iron thins from the underside. The trona goes out by rail, four hundred kilometres to a port, in white bags, and becomes glass somewhere else.

## Rules of This Place

1. Everything alive here lives inside a narrow band — of temperature, of chemistry, of distance from a spring lip — and outside that band the same water that sustains it kills it within minutes.
2. There are no plants. What performs the work of plants is single-celled, ancient, and green enough to look like an oil slick.
3. The mineral regenerates. Nothing extracted here is permanently gone.
4. The buildings do not.
5. Heat and dim light coexist; the lid does not lift.
6. The largest crowd in the basin is made of animals, and it is quieter than the smallest machine.
7. You may stand in the middle of a town built for a thousand, in a landscape holding a million bodies, and see nothing move for a quarter of an hour but steam.
