## The Drowned Flat

Fifty metres above, there is a lid.

You would not call it a surface. From below, in this water, the sea's ceiling is a grey suggestion — a place where the darkness thins slightly, the way a bruise thins at its edge. Light enters there and is eaten as it descends. By twenty metres it is green. By thirty it is the colour of old bottle glass held in a cellar. By forty it has given up being colour and become only a direction: *up is where there used to be light.* At the floor, where we are, there is a residue of it, enough that the mud is not black but a very dark brown-grey, enough that a hand held out would be a shape rather than a nothing. Not enough to cast a shadow. Nothing here has cast a shadow in eight thousand years.

And the floor is flat. Not flat like a table — flat like a country that has stopped arguing. It runs out in every direction for the size of a valley, the size of a county, past any point where a rise could be expected, and every so often it declines by half a metre over a kilometre and that decline is the ghost of a river. There were rivers. You can still read them if you travel far enough along one, the way you read a word by touch: a long shallow trough, a bank of coarser gravel on the outer bend, a floor of stiffer clay. They carried meltwater once, across a plain that was land, and then the plain drowned and the sea took over the work of moving things about, and the sea is not interested in valleys. The sea planes. Eight millennia of tides have gone over these rivers with a fine abrasive and nearly rubbed them out.

This is the plain's final form. It has no more shapes to lose.

---

The water is not water. That is the first thing the body would report. The water is *loaded*. In the bottom ten metres — the layer that hugs the mud like a fog that never lifts — the sea is a suspension of fine grey silt so thick that visibility is arm's length, then hand's length, then nothing. There is no room between presences. Every cubic centimetre holds its grains; the grains hold each other up by sheer crowding; the light that gets down here is not absorbed so much as *jostled to death*, handed particle to particle until it forgets which way it was going. Reach out and you cannot see your own fingers. Reach out and you can *feel* how full the water is, a soft continual sanding of the skin, a gritty pressure like being inside a thing rather than in a fluid.

And it is moving. That is the second thing.

Above, on the lid, a gale has been standing for four days. It has combed a swell into the whole depth of the sea and set the entire water column rocking, and down here that rocking meets the spring tide, and the two of them together have started a benthic storm. The current on the bottom runs at a walking pace, then a running pace, then slackens, then reverses over six hours, and it carries the plain with it. Sand waves three metres high — long, low, symmetric, ranked to the horizon that does not exist — travel a hand's breadth an hour in calm weather and metres in this. Their crests smoke. On their flanks, ripples run in ranks like fish scales, and on the ripples, smaller ripples, and on those, the crawling of individual grains: a hierarchy of restlessness all the way down to the size of a fleck of mica. Whole dune fields shoulder past each other in the dark. Trenches open on the lee sides and fill within a tide. Nothing here is where it was yesterday and nothing at all has changed.

That is the rule of this world, and the plain would tell it to you if it had a voice: *violence that conserves flatness.* Everything moves. The shape stays. There is no relief left to destroy, no high ground to cut down, no low ground that will not be filled by the next six hours. The turbulence is total and it is completely without consequence. It is the energy of an anthill applied to a page that has already been wiped clean.

---

It is quiet.

That is the strangest thing, if strangeness is allowed here, and hardly any is. All that motion and the sound of it is almost nothing: a hiss, very low and very wide, the hiss of a hundred billion grains touching each other, coming from no direction because it comes from all of them. Under the hiss, at the bottom of hearing, a slow groan as the swell above leans on the whole mass of the sea and lets go. A knock, once in a while — a cobble rolled and set down. And then, in the pauses, when the tide goes slack, an actual silence so complete that the body starts producing sounds to fill it: the pulse, the small tick of the joints. The storm is not loud. The storm is a whisper the size of a county.

---

Reach into it. The floor comes in three materials.

**Shell gravel.** Great pavements of it in the troughs, packed edge to edge, no gap anywhere: cockles, dog whelks, thin razor blades, all of them worn to opaque discs and crescents by the endless polishing, dead and dead and dead, a currency of former animals. Kick it and it clinks like poor coins under a blanket.

**Sand.** Grey, well-sorted, without patience.

**And peat.** This is the good material — the odd one. Where the sand waves have travelled off a patch and left it bare, the plain shows black. Fibrous, compressed, dense as a wet book: the floor of a forest, drowned and pickled. Alder roots still in place, still radiating outward from a stump the diameter of a dinner plate, and the stump still holding the shape of grain. Hazelnuts, whole, in handfuls. Reed rhizome. A stratum of leaf litter you could take apart leaf by leaf if you had a thousand years of slack tide, which you do not; you have about one tide, and then the sand comes back over the top of it and it is gone again for a century.

---

Living things: rare, and each one an event.

A brittle star, lying on the shell pavement with its arms curled in against the current, holding position by being small and low. A hermit crab dragging a whelk shell across a ripple crest, achieving perhaps two metres a day. A dab, buried, betrayed only by the rim of sand-fringed lid around one eye; when it goes, it goes as a puff and a flicker and is instantly unfindable. A sea mouse — a bristle worm the size of a thumb, its flank hair iridescing bronze-green if any light at all reaches it, which mostly it doesn't, so the iridescence happens unwitnessed for the whole of its life. Once, high up, a single cod going somewhere on business, its shape less seen than *inferred* from a displacement in the murk.

Plants, barely. No kelp — too dark, too deep, too mobile. What passes for vegetation is: rusty tufts of hydroid, animal but shaped like weed, growing on the one hard thing available; drifting scraps of red algae torn off some far bank and passing through; long ribbons of dead eelgrass wrack rolling along the bottom like tumbleweed in slow motion; and, on the peat where it is freshly exposed, a whitish bacterial bloom, a film, feeding on eight-thousand-year-old carbon and dying when the sand returns. Sparse. Enough to say that the plain is not sterile, and no more.

---

And then, in the black peat, the tools.

They come up when the dunes move and they go back down when the dunes return, and nobody puts them there and nobody takes them away. A flint core, glossy grey-brown, its flake scars as crisp as the week they were struck. Blades — small, narrow, a dozen of them scattered within a pace, the debris of one afternoon's sitting down and working. A red deer antler with the tines removed and a hole bored through the beam, socketed once for a haft, the wood long gone. A row of hazel rods, still standing, still in the mud where they were pushed, curving in the arc of a fish trap: a weir set in a river that has been ocean for two hundred and eighty generations. Nearby, a patch of cracked stones with a lens of charcoal beneath, which is not a structure, which is only a fireplace, which is the entire extent of building in this landscape — one hearth, effaced, on a plain the size of a country.

There is no one to have made them and no successor to find them. The hands are the only human thing that ever happened here and they happened in the dry.

---

The plain does not preserve and does not destroy. It *buries and unburies*, forever, on a schedule set by wind four days old and by the moon. Give it a flint blade and it will show it for a tide and hide it for a century and show it again. Give it a river and it will erase the river slowly enough that the erasure is not visible in a lifetime, only in the arithmetic. Nothing accumulates. Nothing culminates. The saturation is total — water packed with sediment, floor packed with shell, peat packed with the pressed bodies of a forest — and the turbulence is total, and the two conditions produce, together, a landscape of perfect and permanent stillness.

Then the gale above lies down. Over a day the fog of silt settles out of the bottom water, and for a few hours the plain is visible: a grey field, ribbed with ripples, running out into a soft green-brown dark in every direction, absolutely level, absolutely occupied, absolutely quiet, forty-eight metres under the lid.

Then the tide turns, and it all lifts, and begins again.
