## The Shelf

You come up out of the cloud into more cloud, and then into a room of it — a warm grey chamber perhaps three hundred paces across, floored in stone so new it still has a wet gleam like the inside of a shell.

This is the highest thing for a long way, though you would never know it. The horizon is nine paces off in every direction and made of vapour. Sometimes the vapour thins and you see, downslope, the tops of other shelves like this one, staggered and pale, each with its own cap of mist, and then the thinning closes and you are alone again in the grey room with the ticking.

### The ground

Nothing here has been worn. That is the first thing the body understands, before the mind does: the ground has no dust on it, no grit, no sand in the low places, no soil anywhere at all. Every edge is an edge. The stone comes up out of the earth in lobes and fans and drooping stacks, pale as bone or faintly green, and every lobe ends in a rim thin enough to cut you, because nothing has yet come along to take the rim off. You put a hand down and it bleeds a little and you learn.

The stone is warm. Not sun-warm — there is no sun to speak of here — but warm from inside, the way bread is warm, the way a working animal is warm. It is being made, and making costs heat.

At the vents you can watch it happen. A vent is nothing dramatic: a slot in the older stone, a hand's width, breathing out a pale froth like beaten egg-white, so light it trembles when you exhale near it. The froth stiffens in minutes. In an hour it will hold your weight. In a day it is a shelf you could build on, and by then the crystal front has moved a palm's width further out into the air, cantilevered over nothing, obeying a plan you cannot see but can almost hear.

Almost hear, and also almost *see*. Where the new stone is going, the air is very faintly organised. A haze stands there — a blue not much stronger than the blue in a shadow on snow — and it has geometry: arches, ribs, the shapes of stairs and chambers not yet arrived. Walk into the haze and it is only warm mist on your face. Come back in a season and there is a buttress where you stood, exactly where the haze had drawn one. The plan precedes the rock. The rock is merely the plan getting slow and heavy.

Somewhere under all of it — a hundred metres down, a thousand, nobody who is still here knows — the seeds are working. They are smaller than the idea of small. Their engineering is not described by anything you were taught: they take water and dissolved cloud and the rubble of an older, duller mountain and they *compose*, atom by atom, at a rate that ought to be geological and instead is roughly the rate of a growing fingernail. The seeds do not care about you. This is the most restful fact about the place.

### The water

Water is everywhere and never in a hurry, and — this is the strangeness that takes longest to accept — it does not cut.

It comes out of the stone rather than falling on it. The cloud condenses on every surface, the surfaces are thirsty, and the new rock drinks upward: you can lay a finger on a vertical face and feel a sheet of water climbing it, cool, unhurried, going *up*, drawn into the crystal front to be used. In the low places the water pools in basins with lips as sharp as porcelain, and the pools are clear to their floors, because there is no sediment. No mud, no silt, no stain, no algae-brown. You could read a coin at the bottom of a pool four metres deep.

Rain, when it comes — and it comes twice a day, a warm heavy sagging of the cloud — makes an enormous noise and no difference. It falls on stone that will not be scarred. The runnels it fills are runnels the plan intended. There are no gullies here. There will not be gullies for a very long time. Erosion has not yet been invented on the Shelf; it is waiting somewhere downhill, patient, holding its instruments.

### The sound

It is loud. People expect a young landscape to be silent and it is the opposite of silent.

The crystal fronts tick. Each new lattice locking into place makes a small bright report, like ice cracking in a glass, and there are millions of fronts, so the ambient sound is a dry brilliant sizzle, a rain-on-a-tin-roof that never stops and never gets closer. Over that: the drip. The stone grows hollow in places, great thin-walled bells and horns and drooping paps, and water falls into them and they *sound* — low bass notes from the big shells near the eastern rim, a whole octave of clonks and bongs from the stacked lobes, a tuned pinging where a seep has found a cluster of fingerbones-thin tubes. The place plays itself. It plays itself all night, and there is a great deal of night.

And then the animals, who are shouting.

### The living

Vegetation is almost an afterthought here: no soil, no roots to speak of. In the wet seams there are mats of something between lichen and jelly, dull green, faintly warm to the touch, and in three or four places a stand of hollow canes has got a hold in a crack — twenty or thirty of them, dark, waxy, taller than a person, whistling when the cloud moves. That is the entire flora of the Shelf, and it is enough, because the food chain here does not start with plants.

It starts with the froth. The froth is edible, if you are a mason-tick: a flat, plated, thumb-sized animal with a mouth like a rasp, and there are unbelievable numbers of them, grazing the vent-lips, and they *sing* — a shrill collective stridulation that peaks at dusk and stops all at once when something crosses their sky.

The something is usually a sheet-flier: a membrane the size of a cloak, no thicker than a wet leaf, drifting on the thermals coming off the warm stone, and it comes down over a grazing patch like a closing hand. Sheet-fliers make no noise at all, which is why the ticks fall silent — the silence is the alarm.

In the pools: pale eyeless swimmers, and frog-things that never leave water because there is no water they need to leave — they lay their eggs in the warm seeps, and the seeps are the incubators, and the whole basin croaks at once in a rhythm that sets the hollow stones ringing sympathetically. And along the cloud-line, at the rim where the Shelf ends and the drop begins, the waders come at dusk on legs like wire, four of them, five, never more, stalking the shallows for frog-things with a slow ceremonial patience, silhouetted against nothing.

The animals arrived before anything told them to. Nobody knows how. It is a thing people who study the Shelf say to each other in a certain tone: *the plan makes rock, and the animals just come.*

### The marks of people

Not many. You could count them.

A fieldhouse near the canes: a single room, floor of old imported plank, walls of pressed grey material that sheds water and stays cool, one window, a door that shuts properly. Inside: a bunk, a kettle, a shelf of notebooks with dates spanning ninety years and four different hands, the last entry unfinished.

A mast on the high lobe, ten metres, guyed with monofilament so fine that in dim light the mast appears to stand unsupported, and at the top a grey ovoid that hums at a frequency you feel in your molars. It is talking to the seeds. It has been talking to the seeds for a very long time and nobody living knows the language.

A line of glass pins, nine of them, driven into the older stone at intervals of exactly ten paces, each engraved with a number. Measuring pins, for measuring growth — except the growth has swallowed the fourth, fifth, and sixth, and they are now four metres inside a translucent buttress, visible, glinting, unrecoverable, still faithfully marking a surface that has moved on.

A doorway standing by itself in the middle of the basin, freestanding, made of the new stone, arched, with a threshold worn smooth — worn, *smooth*, the only worn thing on the whole Shelf, and it was not worn by weather. It was in the plan. Something intended a threshold and intended it already used.

And in a niche near the vents, wrapped in cloth, a walnut-sized object that is warm and heavier than it should be by a factor of about nine. If you hold it up and look through it at the blue haze, you see the plan resolve: the arches, the stairs, the chambers, the second doorway that isn't there yet, and much further out and much fainter, the shape of a mountain that will take eleven thousand years and will be, by any measure you would care to apply, beautiful.

### The rules, as the notebooks give them

Do not sit on a front. It will grow around you politely.

Everything sharp is new. Everything smooth was planned.

Water goes up here. Do not build anything that assumes otherwise.

The ticking is louder before rain. The silence is louder than the ticking, and means a flier.

Nothing rots. There is nothing to rot it with. Whatever you leave, you leave forever, at the exact spot, becoming an inclusion — so leave carefully, and leave something worth looking at.
