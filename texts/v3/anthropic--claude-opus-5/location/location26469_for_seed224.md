## The Ledgeroom

**Where it is.** Your ears say four thousand metres. The air is thin enough that climbing three steps costs you a minute of standing still afterward, and cold in the way high air is cold — a dry cold, without the bite of wind, a cold that takes heat off you politely and steadily like someone counting money.

But there is no mountain. Beyond the lip of the hollow, where a summit ridge ought to fall away into valleys and weather, there is a grey that will not resolve into anything. Not fog: fog has depth and moves. This is a surface that happens to be everywhere your eye tries to go past the edge. It has no grain. If you throw a chip of stone into it, the chip goes about two metres and then is simply not being thrown anymore, and about four days later you find it back on the floor near your foot, slightly warmer than the others.

So: high, and hidden. Eleven paces of floor. A room bitten out of a stone that has no outside.

**Floor and material.** The bedrock is a fine grey-blue rock that breaks in shallow curves, like flint but softer-sounding under the boot. Across the floor lies a scatter of angular chips and a fine pale dust, and the chips are the tell: they are all *sharp*. Nothing has ever rolled them. There is no water here to freeze in their cracks, no runnel to carry them, no river to make them into pebbles. They were made sharp by frost that isn't ice and by a slower agency, and they have stayed sharp for a very long time, and they have arrived at a kind of settledness in their sharpness. The talus slope against the north wall has the repose angle of gravel that has been rained on for ten thousand years, without a drop having fallen.

The dust, if you take it between your fingers, is warm — blood-warm — and it does not stick to skin. Under strong light it turns out not to be dust. It is machinery, or was: each grain is a device about the size of a red cell, all of them the same device, all of them broken in the same place. They still try. Their business is repair, and they are repairing the room at a rate of roughly one micron per century, which is slightly less than the rate at which the room is being lost.

**Light.** Dim, and sourceless, and everywhere the same. There is no sun overhead and no lamp; the grey beyond the lip does not glow. The light seems to be a property the air has agreed to, the way water agrees to be wet. It is about the brightness of a room at dusk with the curtains half-drawn — enough to read a large hand, not enough to read a small one.

Because it has no source, nothing casts a shadow. Your own body doesn't. Your hand held over the stone bowl leaves the stone unchanged. Only the built things cast shadows, and their shadows fall *inward*, pooling against their own bases, as if each object were being lit from all sides by something that only exists for objects that were made on purpose.

**The built things.** There are four, in eleven paces, which is enough to be a habitation and not enough to be a settlement.

A low wall, three courses high, four metres long, curved on a radius that does not belong to this room. Dry-laid, no mortar, of the same grey-blue stone. The joints will not take a hair. Put your eye to the top course and sight along it and the curve continues past the end of the wall for some distance in a direction you cannot point to. The wall is not a wall. It is the cross-section of something larger where that something intersects the three dimensions available here — the way a needle through paper is a dot on the paper. Whatever it is, it is running. You can feel it in your teeth if you rest your jaw on the stone: a pressure with no frequency.

A post of black glass, waist-high, set upright in a socket cut for it. At eye height a hole is bored clean through, about a thumb across. Look through it. You see the room, the same room, with its ceiling still on and its floor unchipped and the wall six courses high, and the light in it is better. Nothing moves in there. It is not a memory and not a window; it is the room's original specification, held available for comparison.

A shallow bowl worked into the bedrock, an arm's span across, dry — bone dry, dry the way the inside of a sealed jar is dry. Its rim is polished by handling. In the bottom, the pale grain-dust has arranged itself into a spiral of eleven turns. Sweep it flat and come back in a season and the spiral is there again, eleven turns, the same handedness. This appears to be the closest thing here to an organism: not alive, but recurrent, which in this room is nearly the same claim.

And a threshold. Two slabs and a step between them, immaculately cut, standing free in the middle of the floor. Nothing on either side. No frame, no hinge-sockets, no wear on the step. It has never been crossed.

**Rules.**

*The perimeter is longer than the room.* Pace it: fourteen. Pace the diameter: eleven, halved, so five and a half. The numbers do not reconcile and they never have and they never vary. This is not a fluctuation. It is a fault that has been in the geometry so long that the geometry has weathered *around* it and now depends on it. It is load-bearing.

*Nothing is lost, only misplaced in duration.* Everything that leaves comes back, days or decades later, at the same temperature it had when it left. The chips on the floor include several that have come back more than once. You can tell them: the returned stones are very slightly kinder to the hand.

*Placement is recorded.* Set anything down — a glove, a tin, a knee — and lift it, and there is a faint pale ghost of it in the dust. The ghosts persist for something like forty years. The floor near the bowl is a palimpsest of them, hundreds deep, mostly hands, mostly the same three hands.

*Causality is loose by a small margin.* Small sounds arrive before their causes. You will hear the click of a stone settling, and then, a beat later, feel it settle under your foot. The margin is about a third of a second and it applies only to quiet things. Nothing loud has ever been tried here.

**Sound.** Otherwise: your own blood in your ears, enormous. The tick of the black post giving up heat. Once or twice a day, from the direction of the wall, a sound exactly like a page being turned in the next room. There is no next room. When no one is present, the sound continues; we know this because the dust keeps a record of vibration, and the record is unbroken through the long unattended centuries — a page, and a page, and a page, at intervals that lengthen very slightly, forever.

**The slow cycle.** Roughly every forty years the room loosens. The edges of the chips go indistinct. The wall's joints admit, briefly, a hair. The numbers you get from pacing begin to drift — thirteen, twelve and a half — and the black post's window shows the specification-room out of focus. This lasts a season or two. Then there is a correction, without warning and without any sound at all, and everything is exactly itself again, sharp and countable, and one micron smaller.

That is the whole meteorology. That is the only weather this place has: a tide of definition, going out and coming in, and never quite coming in as far.

The ceiling went that way. You can see where it was — a clean truncation around the top of the walls, an unconformity between what is maintained and what has been surrendered. Above it, the grey.

**The one wet thing.** In a crack in the north wall, at the height of a kneeling person's eye, there is a single crystal of water ice about the size of a lentil. In this air it should have sublimed away in an afternoon. It has been there since before the ceiling. It is not frozen so much as *withheld* — kept at the precise last instant before it becomes vapour, and kept there, and kept there. It is the only object in the room that is being actively protected rather than merely maintained. Whoever built the wall built it around this. The device in the wall, whatever it is doing across whatever direction, may be doing nothing but this: holding one grain of water at the edge of leaving.

**Leaving.** You do not leave by walking out; the grey is not a door. You leave by ceasing to be able to recall how you came, at which point you are elsewhere, at a lower altitude, warmer, with sharp grey-blue grit in the seams of your boots and a faint pale mark on your palm that takes about forty years to fade.
