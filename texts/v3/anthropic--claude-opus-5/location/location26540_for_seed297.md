## The Held Basin

### 1. Arrival

You come down over the Rim and the first thing that goes wrong is distance.

There is no haze here — not the least trace of it, no water in the air to soften anything — so the far side of the basin, some twenty-six kilometres off, arrives at your eye at full strength. You can count the leaf-plates on a single crown over there. You can see the individual angular stones in its talus. The far wall does not recede; it stands up close against your face like a painted bowl, and your body, which has spent its whole life measuring depth by softness, refuses the reading. Walk an hour and nothing has approached. Walk a day and the far wall is the same size it was. It is not that the basin is larger than it looks. It is that it looks like nothing at all — it looks like a surface. The eye insists on a bowl thirty centimetres deep and the legs insist on a country.

The second thing that goes wrong is the sun. It sits at about forty degrees above the eastern rim, bright, ordinary, yellow-white, and it does not move. Not slowly. It does not move. Shadows in the Held Basin are permanent features of the terrain, as fixed as the ridges that cast them, and the flora have grown into them the way flora elsewhere grow into a slope.

### 2. Terrain

The basin is young — properly young, days-old young, and it has been days-old for some four centuries. It came up as a unit through a seam in the older country and has not been touched since by any of the processes that usually go to work on new rock.

So: the fracture faces of the Rim are mirror-bright. Where the stone broke, it broke cleanly, and the edges are still edges. You can put a thumb on one and open yourself to the bone. The talus below is a heap of angles. There is not one rounded stone in the whole region. Not one pebble. Nothing here has ever been rolled, or knocked, or bedded, or worn.

And there is no drainage. This is what unsettles anyone who has learned to read land. Slopes with no gullies. Ridges that simply end, arbitrarily, mid-decision. No fans, no channels, no braided grammar of a place explaining where its water went, because there never was any water, and so there is no argument written into the ground at all — only the shape it had when it surfaced, which is the shape of something extruded: faintly plated, faintly lobed, with long straight joints that run for kilometres and meet at angles a little too clean.

The floor of the basin is grit and plate rock, warm to the hand, and in the low centre there are beds of crystal — great pale rosettes of a salt that never evaporated out of anything, that grew there directly, blade by blade, and stopped. They are still sharp. They will always be still sharp.

### 3. The green

Over all of this: forest. Dense, layered, close-packed, near-touching, saturating — a canopy that runs unbroken for kilometres and stands in tiers on the terraces, with an understorey below it and a second understorey below that, so thick in places you must turn your shoulders to pass.

None of it is wet.

The trunks are not wood. They are bundles of drawn glass fibre, hollow, absurdly light, ringing faintly if you knock them. The leaves are mineral wafers, thin as a fingernail paring, translucent at the margin, veined with dark metal that carries whatever it is that needs carrying. Most of the canopy is not green but a deep bruised purple-black — a broadband absorber, greedy, taking everything the fixed sun offers — and under it the light comes through in violet coins that have been lying on the same stones for four hundred years. The green is lower down: pale acid-green fans, bronze-green scrub, a sort of horsetail the colour of old brass standing in ranks along the joints in the rock.

There is no soil. There cannot be; nothing here rots. The roots go directly into stone and mine it, and where leaves have fallen they have not decayed — they lie in the hollows in drifts metres deep, every one intact, four centuries of foliage stacked like archived paper. You can dig down through them and pull out a leaf from the bottom of the pile and it is a perfect leaf.

It smells of hot slate, of cinnamon, of a faint ozone. It does not smell of anything living, because the smell of living things is mostly the smell of water and of decay, and there is neither.

### 4. What the trees eat

Light, yes. But light is only the power.

What the flora of the Held Basin metabolise is *change itself* — the local capacity for events, the region's budget of difference-between-one-moment-and-the-next. They draw it out of the rock and the air and each other, they fix it into their glass and their wafers, and they have been doing it, at saturation, for four hundred years.

This is the whole explanation of the place, and it is also its whole strangeness.

Because the erosion budget is gone, the Rim stays razored. Because the weather budget is gone, the air does not move — not once, not anywhere, not a breath; the air stands in the basin like a solid, evenly warm at your ankles and your scalp, evenly warm in shadow and in sun, so that stepping from light to shade brings no relief and your skin reports something impossible. Because the sun is a change and changes are taxed, the sun has stopped. Because sound is a change propagating, sound does not propagate: shout, and it goes ten or twelve metres and simply ceases, absorbed, spent. Two people standing thirty metres apart in the groves cannot speak to each other at all. The quiet is not an absence — it is a pressure, a debt, a thing with a surface. Under it you can just hear a very high steady ring, everywhere at once, which is the forest conducting.

Nothing here grows. Nothing here dies. The flora reached equilibrium a long time ago; they are not living so much as *held*, at full lushness, a green flash with the shutter left open.

### 5. The Chatter

The extracted change has to go somewhere. It goes to the pit.

It is about a kilometre across, in the north of the basin, ringed by facets too regular to discuss, and it is the only place in the region where anything happens. It happens all at once. Down there the crystal beds boil and re-form fifty times a second; the rock ages centuries and un-ages; small structures assemble, corrode, collapse and reassemble faster than the eye follows, and the whole thing sounds — this is the only loud place — like a sea of shale being poured endlessly into another sea of shale. Stand on the facet rim and your own body starts to itch with it: your fingernails grow, your hair grows, the cut on your thumb from the Rim closes in front of you. Ten minutes there and you will have lost a year.

It is the exhaust port. It has been running at exactly this rate, in exactly this configuration, since before anyone came.

### 6. What moves

Almost nothing. Fauna are rare here to the point of census: perhaps six.

They are the borrowers — long, low, slow-jointed animals with glass in their bones, the size of a large dog, that carry their own small pocket of duration and spend it walking. You will find one standing in a grove with a leaf-wafer half in its mouth and you will not, at first, be sure it is an animal. Watch. Over a quarter of an hour the leaf goes in. They do not startle. They cannot afford to. They are, as far as anyone can tell, part of the machinery: they crop the flora at precisely the rate the flora would otherwise exceed their allowance, and they have been doing so at that rate forever.

### 7. The pattern, and the seam

Climb back to the Rim and look down and you will see that the forest is not distributed the way forests are distributed.

The groves make a figure. It covers the whole basin floor — a lattice with inclusions, dense here, cleared there, repeating at three scales, and it is unmistakably *written*. Not decorative. Notation. Everyone who sees it reports the same conviction and nobody has read it. The clearings are not clearings; they are the spaces between characters. The Chatter sits in it like a punctuation mark.

There are no buildings in the Held Basin. There are no tools, no walls, no fragments, nothing shaped by hands. But the joints in the floor rock meet at angles that are not geological, and the whole region has a single straight seam running east to west across it for thirty-one kilometres, a hairline where the basin was joined to the world, and if you kneel and put your eye to that seam you will see, in the dark of it, a faint regular gridwork going down.

The place is not undeveloped. The place is *entirely* development. There is simply nothing here built out of matter, because matter is a coarse material and whoever did this was working in the ruleset.

### 8. Visitors

You are a change-spending animal in a change-poor economy, and you will be noticed.

Not by anything with eyes. By the geometry. Walk into a grove and you will find the stems already leaning toward the line you are about to take — bowed, all of them, in a long gentle corridor that anticipates you, and has anticipated you for four hundred years, because in a landscape where nothing changes, the arrangement must always already have included your arrival. The forest cannot bend to accommodate you. It was never able to bend. So it was bent from the start, exactly as much as you would require, on exactly the path you would choose. People take this badly. Some of them try to prove it wrong by walking somewhere unplanned, and find that the leaning goes there too, and that it is not possible to surprise a place that has no capacity for surprise.

You will be tired here in a way that is not muscular. Your heart is expensive. Your breath is expensive. The forest is quietly, politely, at equilibrium with you as well — drawing off the small hot surplus you carry — and after some days in the basin your watch and your pulse will have come to a private disagreement that no amount of arithmetic resolves.

Leaving, you climb the Rim and cross the seam, and the wind hits you like a shout, and the haze comes back and puts the mountains where they belong, and the sun begins to move again, and it is genuinely difficult not to sit down.

You will take out with you: a leaf-wafer, which will not fade, will not curl, will not crumble, and will stay exactly as it was on the shelf in your house for the rest of your life and the lives of your children; and a memory of the corridor of bent stems, which is not a memory of a place, but of being expected.
