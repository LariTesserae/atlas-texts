## The Standing Sea

### 1. The water that sits too high

Begin with the impossible part and get it over with: this sea is five kilometres above the sea. It rests in a basin on the roof of the continent, a bowl of young rock a thousand kilometres across, and it is full to a line so precise you could measure the planet against it. Salt water. Cold — a little under three degrees, top to bottom, everywhere, always. It came from the lowland ocean, and it did not climb here on its own.

Stand — swim, rather; there is nowhere to stand that is not underwater — and look up. The surface is a hundred and ninety metres above you and you can see it perfectly: a bright wrinkled ceiling, silver where the wind works it, and through it, on a clear day, the disc of the sun with edges. The light comes down almost unspent. This is because there is nothing in the water. No plankton, no diatom haze, no green suspension, no drifting fragments of leaf or wood from any shore. The basin is starved of iron, starved of silica, and too new to have made its own dust. Nothing photosynthesises here. Nothing has ever photosynthesised here. The water is optically empty in a way that no ocean at sea level has been for two billion years, and so light behaves in it the way light behaves in air: it goes and goes. At four hundred metres you can still read by it. At eight hundred there is a dim blue afternoon. Only in the trench basins, below twelve hundred, does it finally give up.

The emptiness *is* the brightness. That is the first rule of the place, and the one people find hardest to hold in mind: it is luminous because it is sterile.

### 2. The rock

The mountains under and around you are young — hard, glassy, unfinished. Andesite and black basalt with cooling fractures still sharp enough to open a glove. The ridges run northeast in long parallel blades, drowned to their shoulders, and their edges have not been rounded because nothing rounds them. There is no frost-shatter down here; water cannot freeze in a crack when it is nowhere near freezing. There is no rain to gnaw, no root to lever, no surf except where the highest crests break the surface into low black reefs. Erosion is reduced to slow chemical licking, and the rock is only a few million years out of the fire.

So the slopes are angular the way broken glass is angular. Talus fans are small and coarse — cones of fresh blocks with clean faces, the interstices dark and hollow. In places whole cliffs of volcanic glass rise fifty metres, still bearing the flow-banding, still bearing the conchoidal scoop-marks of their own last cracking, and the light comes off them in oily, greenish sheets. There is a colour here you would call vegetal at first — a pale apple green in the fissures — but it is celadonite, a mineral, growing in the sense that minerals grow: not toward the light, and not in a hurry.

The only true living film is a bacterial one, invisible until you scrape it: a rust-and-cream varnish that lives off hydrogen and iron squeezed out of the young rock itself. It is the base of everything. The whole ecology of the Standing Sea runs not on sunlight but on the slow indigestion of new stone, and so the animals go where the rock is freshest — up the ridges, into the talus, along fault lines that weep warm.

### 3. The noise

Now the thing you actually noticed first, before the light, before the rock, before you had any language for it.

The sea rings.

It is a sound like a foundry heard from inside the metal. A low pulse, roughly eleven seconds apart, that arrives not in the ears but in the sternum and the teeth and the small bones of the wrist — and layered over it, a continuous roar in the two hundred hertz range, granular, mechanical, absolutely unwavering. Water carries sound four and a half times faster than air and lets it go a hundred times more reluctantly. In a basin this size, with hard glassy walls and no soft sediment to swallow anything, sound does not attenuate so much as accumulate. There is nowhere in the Standing Sea that is not deafening, and there are only a handful of sources.

The sources are the Draw.

### 4. The Draw

At the rim of the basin, spaced two and three hundred kilometres apart around a coastline of drowned ridges, there are seventeen intake-and-lift complexes, and they are the reason for the sea. Each is a shaft driven down through five kilometres of mountain to the true ocean below, and a column of engines that lifts that ocean up here and lets it out. The uppermost works break the surface: black caisson towers, riveted, ice-collared, streaked with white salt, standing alone in an emptiness so complete that from the top of one you cannot see anything human in any direction, only water and the low black teeth of reefs.

Below the surface they are enormous. A single outfall gallery is a hall eighty metres wide with a floor of grating and a ceiling of rusted arch-ribs, and the water comes through it at a walking pace that will nonetheless take your body off somewhere and not give it back. The pulse comes from the lift stages far down the shaft. The roar comes from the outfalls. Everything is electrified; the cabling runs in cast conduits with lead-wiped joints, and in the dark under the gantries you can see the faint corona-glow at a cracked insulator, blue and steady, feeding a slow cloud of chlorine bubbles that never gets anywhere.

Nobody is there. Nobody has been there for a long time. There are chairs.

They run on the head of water they themselves lifted — a fraction of the outflow diverted through turbines — and they have found an arrangement with the physics of the basin that has no reason to end. Inflow equals evaporation plus leakage. The level is constant to within centimetres, decade on decade. And here is the elegant, awful part: their pulse rate sits almost exactly on the basin's fundamental seiche period. The Standing Sea sloshes, minutely, in a standing wave a thousand kilometres long, and because the driver never varies, the wave never moves.

### 5. Nodes and bellies

That is the geography that matters, more than the ridges. The sea is divided into acoustic provinces that are fixed in space and have been for as long as anything has been alive here.

In the **bellies** — the antinodes, where the pressure swing is greatest — the water is a hammer. Nothing with a gas space in its body can enter. No lungs, no bladders, no bubbles, no shells with a void behind them. The bellies are wide sunlit reaches over blue-black glass, ringing so hard that the fine silt (there is very little) stands off the bottom in a permanent hovering sheet.

In the **nodes** — thin ribbons and lens-shaped pockets, maybe six of them of any size — there is quiet. Not silence: a felt hum, a pressure on the skull. But quiet enough that a body with a hollow in it can survive, and quiet enough that a predator can hear. The nodes are the dangerous places. This is the local moral order, and it is inverted from what you'd expect: the loud country is the sanctuary.

### 6. The animals

Fauna is plentiful here, which surprises everyone who has read about the sterility, and it is arranged in a way that only makes sense once you know about the nodes and the fresh rock.

Nothing here has a lung or a float. The abundant forms are solid — jelly-solid, cartilage-solid, water-density all through — and most of them are transparent, because in water this clear, in light this strong, transparency is the only camouflage available. You will swim through what looks like a slight optical irregularity, a soft-edged wobble in the light, and find that you have passed through eight hundred animals.

The commonest is a thing called a **ribbon-grazer**: forty centimetres, flat as a bookmark, with a rasping underside and no eyes worth the name, that spends its whole life mowing the bacterial varnish off fresh basalt. Sharp new rock, more varnish; so the ridges are crowded and the old sediment plains are void. Where a talus fan spills down a cliff you will find them stacked in the block interstices like paper in a drawer, thousands, all working, all silent, all vibrating with the pulse.

Above them, **bell-shrimp** in swarms that are not swarms so much as a thickening of the water — they filter the standing silt-sheet in the bellies, and they are safe there, and they know it: put a hydrophone in a belly and their density triples within a kilometre of the loudest point. Around the outfall galleries the ecology goes almost tropical, in numbers if not in colour: the outfall brings up nutrients from the lowland ocean, and there is a rim of life around every intake tower a few kilometres wide, then nothing again for two hundred kilometres.

And the predators, in the nodes and along their edges: **slate-eels**, three metres of grey muscle with a lateral line like a violin's f-hole, that hunt by listening, and can therefore only hunt where listening works. They wait at the node margins for anything the drift carries out of the loud country. There is a whole hydrodynamics of ambush built around a standing wave that has not moved in ten thousand years.

None of them have names given to them by anyone still here. I am improvising.

### 7. The scattered marks

Besides the Draw, the built world is thin to the point of poignancy. Every fifty kilometres along certain compass lines there is a **rod**: an iron pile driven into the rock, four metres, numbered, with a corroded plate. Someone surveyed all this. There are cairns on the reef-crests, mortared, with rings for boats. There is a barge on its side at eleven hundred metres, hold full of gravel, perfectly preserved and perfectly cold. On the flank of one ridge there is a single small building of poured concrete with a steel door standing open, one room, a table, a bunk frame, a wall of hooks, and a stove that could never have been lit here.

Nothing rots. There are no borers, no fouling weeds, no warm water. The iron rusts, but slowly, in a cold and orderly way, and everything that was left is still exactly where it was left, humming.

### 8. Above

The surface has weather of its own and the basin is large enough to make it. Air lying on a thousand kilometres of two-degree water goes to fog; the fog banks stand in the same places, over the same nodes, for months. Snow falls on the reefs and does not accumulate, because the water it lands on is above freezing forever. Sometimes the wind gets into the basin's long axis and raises a real sea, ten-metre grey combers marching a thousand kilometres with nothing to break on but the black reefs and the salt-crusted flank of a caisson tower.

Underneath, none of it matters. Two degrees. A hundred and ninety metres of daylight. The pulse at eleven seconds. The ribbon-grazers on the fresh glass, working.

### 9. The rules, stated plainly

- The water is high because engines put it there, and they have not stopped.
- The light is generous because nothing lives in the water to shade it.
- The rock is sharp because nothing here can weather it fast.
- The sound cannot be escaped, only navigated; the map that matters is the map of loud and quiet, and that map is nailed down.
- Nothing is changing. That is not peace. It is a machine and a basin that happened to fit each other, and a biology that grew into the fit, and no reason yet for any of the three to let go.
