## The Green Loud

### The floor

Begin with the fact that there is no ground.

There is mud somewhere — a new delta, weeks old in the geological sense, laid down flat and warm and barely above the level of the sea that made it. You can feel the mud through everything, the way you can feel a floor through a mattress. But you will never touch it. Between the mud and the sky lies two hundred feet of interlocked rootstock: a mat of living cable, some strands thin as thread, some thicker than a man is tall, all of it braided, welded, and grown into a single flexing sponge. This is the floor of the world here. It gives underfoot by an inch or two, springs back, and transmits the movement outward as a slow ripple that you can watch travel away between the trunks for half a mile before it dies.

The mat is warm — blood-warm, a little more — and everywhere it is damp rather than wet. Water in this country does not lie about in pools. It is held. The root-mat is a reservoir with a skin on it, and the skin sweats. Run a hand along any surface and it comes away beaded. Push a thumbnail into the older sheetwood and clear sap wells up, tepid, faintly saline. In the low places between root-buttresses the seep collects into channels two feet wide that run fast and shallow and braided, silver, changing course hourly as the roots below them swell and shift. These are the rivers. You can step over any of them. There are ten thousand of them and they are all one river, arriving nowhere, being drunk before they get there.

### The materials

Everything is made of one thing, but that thing has learned a hundred trades.

*Sheetwood* — extruded in slabs by the masonvines, lignifying in about four hours, still soft enough at noon to take a fingerprint that will be permanent by dusk. It is the brick here. Pale amber to liver-brown, faintly translucent at the edges, and it always smells like cut grass and hot iron.

*Cording* — the aerial roots of the boulevard figs, thrown across gaps, taking hold, thickening, taking load. Bridges and lintels and cabling. Some cords have thickened past their function into simple, purposeless columns of tension eighty feet long, humming.

*Glassreed* — hollow, silica-armoured, grows in fused ranks to make walls that pass light and nothing else. Whole districts glow from inside like lamp-shades.

*Kettlebloom* — a cup-flower the size of a bathtub that opens, fills with condensate, closes, and becomes a cistern. Streets are lined with them. They are the plumbing.

*Curd* — the young growth, the stuff of the last three days, pale green-white, squeaking, wet, tearing at a touch. It is at the growing edge of every wall, and every wall has a growing edge.

There is no stone. There is no metal. There is no bone, no shell, no hide, no thatch, no fired clay. There is not one object in this valley that was ever made by a hand, and there are no hands.

### The city

And yet it is a city. That is the thing that takes longest to accept.

It is not a forest that happens to resemble streets. The streets are *deliberate*: they run for miles, they hold their width to within a foot, they meet at junctions squared to a discipline no accident would keep, and where they meet, something like a plaza opens — floored in a low dense sward that will not grow above ankle height, walled in glassreed, with a fig standing at the centre on aerial roots like the legs of a table. The arcades have a rhythm: pier, arch, pier, arch, eleven of them, then a return. There are stairs. There are things that can only be called windows, in the sense that they are openings placed for light at the height where a face would be. There are chimneys — hollow culms rising ninety feet, drawing the day's heat out of the lower storeys, roaring. There is drainage, laid out with an engineer's meanness about gradient.

There is no one inside any of it.

Every room is packed. That is the other thing. The city has districts, blocks, storeys, corridors, and each of those volumes is filled to bursting with more plant: shelf-fungus-shaped brackets of leaf, ropes of vine, standing rafts of frond, a saturation so complete that to enter a doorway you must push, and what you push gives with a wet, offended creak and closes behind you. It builds rooms and then it fills them with itself. It builds a colonnade and then it grows a hedge through the colonnade so densely that the colonnade can no longer be walked. It cannot help it. Architecture and occupation are the same appetite here, running at the same speed, and the speed is the problem.

### The noise

You will not hear yourself think, and after a while you will stop trying, and after a while longer you will find you have not been thinking, and you will not be able to say for how long.

The sound is continuous and enormous and has no animal in it at all. That absence is the first thing that frightens people: a din of this magnitude, and not one throat in it.

It is made of, roughly:

**The grind.** Growth at this rate is audible. Cellulose walls stretching past their yield, millions per second, aggregate into a low groan like a ship's hull under ice, and it never stops, and it comes from every direction including below.

**The cavitation.** Water columns snapping in the xylem, each break a click. There are enough of them to make a hiss like frying, sheeting across the districts as the sun moves.

**The artillery.** Dehiscence. Seedpods here are pressure vessels, and they let go with a flat crack that slaps off the sheetwood and comes back at you three or four times. In the afternoon it is one crack every second or so across the whole valley, sometimes a barrage of forty at once when a whole terrace ripens together. The seeds themselves whistle. You will be struck. It does not much matter; they are the size of peas and they bruise.

**The organ.** Wind across the chimney-culms. Anything from a groan at the bottom of hearing to a shriek that makes your eyes water. Different districts are tuned differently and they beat against each other, and the beat frequency wanders as the city grows and detunes itself, hourly.

**The rain-on-leaf.** More on that shortly.

Together it sits at a level where speech is impossible past arm's length, and where the pressure of it can be felt on the chest as a kind of steady pushing. There is no shelter from it. The interiors are worse; sheetwood is a good resonator.

### The weather it makes

There is not much rain from outside. What water arrives, arrives from the city.

By ten in the morning the transpiration has thickened the air to a white haze at canopy level. By one, that haze has become a proper cloud sitting on top of the district, dark-bellied, lit underneath by the glassreed glow. By three or four it lets go: twenty minutes of vertical, warm, heavy rain that is not water so much as the city's own breath handed back. The noise of it on ten square miles of leaf is the loudest thing that happens in a day, a white roar that eats every other sound and leaves you standing in a blank.

Then it stops. The kettleblooms shut. The root-mat swells perceptibly — you can feel the floor rise an inch under you — and within an hour the surfaces are merely damp again, and the whole valley is thirsty, and the next day's cloud begins to assemble.

The delta is dry country that has arranged to be a rainforest by holding its breath.

### The things that are not animals

Half a mile in, something crosses the plaza ahead of you at knee height, moving with intent, and you feel a great and shameful relief.

It is not an animal. It is a *runner*: a detached rootball the size of a dog that inches along on turgor, questing, sensing light gradient, and will root itself somewhere by evening and become a wall. There is no nerve in it, no eye, nothing that wants. It only behaves as if it does, because behaving as if it does gets it to the light.

The pollinators are worse. There are no insects, so the flowers throw their own: florets that detach and go up on a bladder of gas, drifting in loose flocks of several hundred, banking together on the thermals, glittering. They *look* like starlings. They turn as one, because they are the same weight and shape and the air is doing it. They will settle on you in numbers and crawl, hygroscopically, toward moisture — your mouth, your eyes — with a patience that is only physics.

And there are the mimics: a district where the buds have hit on the shape of a face, and produce it, thousands of times, on every stem, unblinking, all of them wrong in the same way. Nobody knows what it is mimicking. There is nothing here with eyes to be fooled.

### The rules

The valley does not run on the usual causality. It is not that the rules are absent; it is that growth got out ahead of them and they have not caught up.

**One.** *Effect precedes cause, locally.* You will find fruit hanging where the flower has not yet opened. You will find a doorway completed and weathered in a wall that will not be grown until tomorrow. The city is building faster than the sequence can be maintained and it has begun to skip. When the missing step finally happens, it happens quietly, and something you already walked through becomes, retroactively, possible.

**Two.** *Repeated things are the same thing.* The city iterates. Eleven arches, then a return; the same plaza, the same fig, the same eleven arches. Walk the block and come back around to find you have arrived from the wrong side. There are not many identical plazas. There is one plaza, at many places, and it is getting tired of the distinction.

**Three.** *Distance is a growth rate.* The far district is not far in yards. It is far because the city is inserting new material between here and there at slightly more than walking pace. You can walk toward a tower all day, in a straight line, at good speed, and end further from it, and the failure will be perfectly visible: new arcade unrolling in front of you like carpet, curd-white and squealing, six feet a minute.

**Four.** *Nothing here rots.* Decay is a thing done by bacteria and animals and this valley has neither. Anything that stops is simply grown over, incorporated, its material drawn out through the interface and used again. Cut into an old trunk and you find yesterday in it: a length of arcade, a kettlebloom still holding its water, the shape of a plaza, all of it perfectly preserved in the round, still faintly warm. The past is not gone. It is structural. It is load-bearing.

**Five.** *Names germinate.* This is the one that matters. Say a word aloud in this place — shout it, you will have to shout — and the word is a seed. Within an hour there will be something answering to it. Call a street the Long Street and it will begin to lengthen, and it will not stop. Call a thing beautiful and it will make more of that. Call it a city and it will build. The valley has no ears; it has no way to receive this; and it does it anyway, and it has been doing it for as long as anyone has been coming here to describe it, and there is a very serious question about which came first.

### Standing in it

Late in the day the light comes in low and level along the boulevards, through the glassreed, and the whole district goes the colour of a held-up hand. Everything is edged, everything is new — no weathering, no lichen crust, no smoothing, no worn step, because nothing here has been standing long enough to be worn. The city is at every moment about four days old.

The chimneys roar. The pods go off in strings. The runners cross the plaza toward the last of the sun, tilting, urgent, mindless. The floor breathes under you, up an inch, down an inch, warm as a flank.

And nothing lives in any of it. There is no window with anyone at it, no path worn by feet, no hearth, no waste, no cradle, no grave. It has made a place for something, at enormous speed and enormous volume and enormous noise, and it is going to keep making it, and the thing it is making it for has never once arrived.

Unless you are careful, and you shout at it, and it hears you, and it grows one.
