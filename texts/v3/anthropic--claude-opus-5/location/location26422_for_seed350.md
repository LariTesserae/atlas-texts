## The Valley of Second Surfaces

**Where the water stops, twice**

The first thing to fail here is the idea that water has one top.

You come down the shoulder of the basin at knee depth in warm brown flood, and above you — thirty, forty metres up, level as a table, lit from beneath by nothing you can find — is the *other* surface. It is unmistakably a surface. It has ripple. It has the puckered silver skin of water seen from below, the wobbling meniscus around each drowned pipe-stack and each vine that penetrates it. Rain falls from it downward in long shivering strings. Rain also falls *up* into it from the flood you're standing in, in thinner threads, more hesitantly, like something being reconsidered.

Between the two surfaces there is air, mostly. Air at ninety-something percent humidity and blood temperature, air you can drink by breathing hard, air holding suspended droplet-shoals that drift as coherent bodies, that pass through you with the texture of warm gauze. In places the shoals thicken until walking becomes swimming without any transition you could point to. You will find yourself doing a slow overarm stroke six feet above ground you can still feel with your toes.

There is a third surface, deeper, that you only ever see in the reflections of the other two: something below the mud, level and patient, waiting for its turn.

**The floor, newly cut**

Everything underfoot is young and unforgiven. The valley is a delta that has existed, in this configuration, for perhaps eleven days. The rock is black and glassy and *sharp* — pillow lava frozen mid-swallow, its rims still holding the exact shape of the bubble that made them, unsoftened by a single season. Where you kneel, it cuts. The sand between the outcrops has not learned to be sand yet: under the tongue every grain is angular, all edge and no roll, and it squeaks against itself with a sound like styrofoam. Nothing has had time to round. Nothing has had time to lie down.

Certain slabs are still warm from below in a way that has nothing to do with the sun (there is no sun). Put a palm flat on the basalt and you can feel it *tick* as it cools, a slow crystalline clicking that travels up the arm bones and, if you stay too long, into the teeth.

Elevation is almost nothing. The whole basin lies within nine metres of itself, from the tallow flats to the low levee ridges, and it is perhaps four kilometres across — a town's worth of world, a valley's worth, open enough that you can see the far side as a dim green haze full of moving lights, closed enough that the noise never leaves.

**Vegetation: the lush without the eaten**

It is *packed*. Density here is a physical fact: within any given cubic metre you will find eleven to twenty distinct living things, layered, near-touching, jostling, and none of them will be an animal.

The dominant growth is **cable-fern**, which is not a fern. It rises in braided trunks the thickness of a thigh, splits into fronds broad as bedsheets, and reproduces by shedding whole limbs into the current, where they root wherever they snag. Cable-fern grows *audibly*. In a warm hour you can hear a stand of it lengthening — a wet ratcheting, a creaking crackle like ice on a pond but running upward, and beneath it the deep hollow *bong* of internal chambers filling with sap under pressure.

Under the ferns: **loom-reed**, which grows in tight parallel files and weaves. Its lateral shoots cross and lock into mats, then the mats curl and lock into tubes, and the tubes make channels that route the flood where the reeds want it. Whole streams in this valley have been engineered by grass.

Under the reeds: **tallowweed**, low, blue-black, greasy. It exudes a heavy waxy oil that films the flood in interference colours, indigo and bronze and rot-green, and it exudes a great deal of it — hundreds of tonnes across the flats. The oil is flammable and the valley knows it.

And everywhere, in the standing water, the **pressure lilies**: bladders the size of dustbin lids that inflate over two or three days and then burst, one at a time, all across the basin, each detonation a flat wet *CRACK* like a rifle in a bathroom. That is how they scatter. There is no bee to bribe, no bird to feed, no gut to ride. Everything reproduces by breaking, by drifting, by exploding, by being carried.

By being carried, mostly, and this is the strange part.

**The engines, unhoused**

There are engines in the rain, standing in the open with no building around them, and they have not stopped.

Screw-pumps four metres tall, cast iron, turning steadily in trenches the loom-reed dug for them. Root-turbines: vertical-axis drums seated directly in the black rock, driven by flood-race, their shafts running off into the growth on tensioned belts of woven cable-fern bark. Cracking towers of riveted pipe, lashed and splinted with vine, distilling tallow oil into something thinner that runs downhill in open troughs, catching what light there is like a vein of mercury through a jungle. Belt-lines on trestles carry silt in open pans from the drowning edge of the valley to the growing edge, and dump it, and come back. Compressors. Arc-rods on poles fizzing in the wet, spot-welding a hairline seam in a hull that will be finished tomorrow and be gone the day after.

Nothing houses any of it. No walls, no roof, no floor plate. Industry here is a kind of exposed organ system, and it is in symbiosis with the plants: the tallowweed fuels the engines, the engines pump silt and nitrogenous slurry to the cable-fern, the cable-fern grows the belts, the loom-reed digs the races, the engines break the pressure lilies open early and blow their seed across the flats on compressed air. The machines are this valley's insects. They are its pollinators, its dispersers, its scavengers and its tide.

The metal is new. That's what unsettles. The rust is bright orange, forty-eight hours old, sitting on top of paint that still smells of paint. Every bearing is greased. Every belt is tensioned. Every stamped plate carries a revision number — VER 8811, VER 8814, VER 8814-a — and if you find two identical valves and compare them, one will be a revision behind the other, and it will be the one further downstream.

**Scattered evidence of hands**

Almost nothing is built for anyone to be inside. But: a single tin shed on the levee, door swollen shut, roof drumming. A concrete stair of nine steps rising out of the flood to a landing and then nothing, no railing, no continuation, the rebar ends still sheared bright. Three pylons in a line across the basin, carrying no cable, humming anyway. A tiled bathing-tank, warm, spotless, full to the brim, level as a mirror despite everything, twelve metres from a turbine that is shaking the ground.

And the small things. A pair of rubber boots, sole-up, in the tallow. A ledger swollen into a brick of pulp on which one word remains legible: *reconciled.* Levers worn smooth in a way that only comes from a hand — thousands of pulls, a slight asymmetry, thumb-side deeper. Whoever left those wear-marks is not here, in the sense that no one is anywhere here, and the levers have gone on being pulled.

**Dim**

Light comes up from below and sideways: the flood glows faintly, a sick warm amber where tallow oil floats, a colder green in the reed channels, and the arc-welds throw hard blue strobes that print the whole scene on your retina for a quarter second at a time — every frond edge, every rivet, every falling string of rain — then take it away. The upper surface leaks a grey shine. There is no horizon glare, no shadow with a direction, no time of day. You will not learn to tell hours here. You will learn to tell *revisions*.

**The noise**

It is deafening in the technical sense; you will lose some of it permanently. The floor of the sound is the rain on ten thousand square metres of loose metal, an unbroken white roar. On top of that: the turbines, out of phase, beating against each other in slow throbs you feel in the sternum. The screw-pumps' hydraulic knock. Cavitation shriek where the flood is squeezed through the loom-reed's tubes — a needle sound, up at the top of hearing, that never stops. The lily detonations, irregular, close, always startling. The cable-fern's growing crackle. And under everything, a subsonic drone that isn't a machine, that comes from the valley's own iteration, a sound like a very large page being turned very slowly and forever.

Speech is impossible. If you brought a companion you have already lost them.

**The rules, as far as they can be stated**

*The valley drafts.* It does not endure; it is redrafted. Islands rise on a rising basalt sill, are colonised in a day, are cut out and eaten by their own reed-channels, are gone. Every eight to fourteen hours some feature of the basin is revised — a channel rerouted, a stack demoted, a levee unbuilt — and the engines and plants that served the old version simply stop being where they were. You will not see the change happen. You will see, afterward, that it always had been this way, and your memory will be the only thing out of true.

*Duplication runs downhill.* One crate on the upper flats becomes two on the middle terrace, becomes nine, becomes forty identical crates rafted at the outfall, all with the same dent in the same corner. Copies degrade by revision. Do not follow a thing downstream expecting to find it.

*Sound precedes event, sometimes by minutes.* You will hear a turbine tear itself apart and then walk past that turbine, intact, and know something. This is the closest the valley comes to giving warning, and it is not a warning; it's a proof.

*The three surfaces trade places.* Rarely, and without transition, the upper surface becomes the one you are standing under and the lower becomes the lid. Everything not rooted falls or rises. The bathing-tank is always, always, still level.

*Nothing here eats.* This is the deepest rule and the strangest silence in the place: no mouths. No burrows, no droppings, no eggs, no carcasses, no gnawed edge, no flapping. All that growth and all that power, and not one thing that hunts, not one thing that flees. The plants are not defended, and there is nothing to defend against, and they overgrow with the abandon of a garden with no visitors. The machines run their belts for a production that goes nowhere except back into the ground, and the ground gives it up to the ferns, and the ferns break and drift and root, and the pressure lilies crack the air open, and the rain comes down and also up, and the valley turns its page.

You are the only appetite in four kilometres. This is why, if you stand still too long in the ankle-warm flood, the belt-lines slow — just slightly — as they pass you. It's the closest thing to being noticed that will happen. Then the arc-rod strobes, the whole valley prints itself blue-white on the back of your eye, and by the time the dark comes back you're standing somewhere very slightly else, in revision 8815, and the ground is still ticking as it cools.
