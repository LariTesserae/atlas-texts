## The Unstarted Room

There is no route to it. This has to be said first, because everything else about the place depends on it. The room sits perhaps forty metres under a slope of grazed limestone, and the only opening is a seam in its ceiling about as wide as a forearm, which rises three metres and ends. Not in soil, not in another chamber — it ends. Solid rock above, unfractured, no more porous than a tooth. People do get in. They arrive already inside, generally mid-breath, generally in the middle of the sentence they were saying somewhere else. Nobody has usefully explained it. The room does not seem to consider it a problem.

It is small. Four paces by five, if you could take four paces, which you cannot, because of the crowding. The ceiling is low enough at the east end that a tall person stoops, and high enough at the west end that the seam disappears into a brightness you cannot look at directly.

---

**The rock is new.** Not new in the geological sense of young strata — new in the sense of *unstarted*. Every surface is a fresh fracture. The walls are made of conchoidal breaks, glassy scoops and ridges, and the edges where they meet have never been rounded by anything. They are sharp the way a struck flint is sharp. You learn this immediately and you learn it again about forty times, because there are no shadows here to warn you where anything is.

There is no dust. This is the detail that unsettles visitors more than the light does. No sediment on the floor, no talus at the base of the walls, no rubble in the corners, no clay, no mud, nothing has ever fallen from anywhere to anywhere. The floor is a single unbroken shelf of bedrock, and where it meets the wall it does so in a crisp interior angle, like a bowl that has been machined and never used. Caves are made by removal. This one has had nothing removed from it. It is a void that arrived before its own excavation, holding the position, waiting for the water that will one day come and make it into a proper cave — droop the ceiling, round the shoulders, lay down flowstone, silt the floor. That water has not come. It is going to be a very good cave. It has not yet begun.

**The light comes from the stone.** All of the stone, evenly, at roughly the intensity of an overcast noon reflected off fresh snow. Because it comes from every direction at once, there are no shadows anywhere in the room — not under the ledges, not beneath the animals, not under your own hands. You cannot tell how far away anything is. Your fingers arrive at surfaces without warning. Held up, your palm looks like a picture of a palm, flat, unmodelled, faintly translucent at the edges. Visitors describe the sensation as being *inside a diagram of a room*.

**The sound is enormous and it never changes.** It has two layers. Underneath, a sustained tone somewhere near a bowed wineglass, coming from the seam in the ceiling — the room is a resonator and something is exciting it, though nothing moves in the seam. On top of that, stridulation: a dense sawing hiss from the fixed population of the walls, loud enough to be felt in the molars and the sinuses. Together it is about the volume of standing beside a road. You shout to be heard, then stop shouting, because there is no one else.

The important thing is that the sound is *identical*. Not similar minute to minute — identical. It is a loop with no seam, or a waveform with no variation. People who have spent an hour in the room can hum eleven seconds of it accurately decades later, and when they come back — nobody comes back — it would still match.

**Water is present in small, exact amounts.** Condensation stands on the crystal faces in beads that neither grow nor run. There is one pool, in a ground depression at the east end the size of two cupped hands, and it is always precisely full to a line you can see stained on the rock — except the stain is not a stain, because staining is a process, and processes do not happen here. It is a change in the mineral, present since the beginning. The water is cold, clean, faintly sweet, and if you drink from it the level does not drop. If you spit into it, the level does not rise.

**It is cool.** About eleven degrees, wall and air and water all the same, so that touching things gives no information. Your breath fogs, and the fog goes out faster than it should — not dispersing, just ceasing, as though the room were deducting it. This is a general principle. The room subtracts what you add. Warmth, moisture, noise, footprints in dust that doesn't exist. It runs at a fixed total and it defends the total.

---

**The living things are numerous and they are the same living things.**

Chalkmoths, several hundred, coating the north wall in overlapping ranks: wings crusted with the identical mineral as the rock, so that the wall appears scaled. They do not fly. They walk, slowly, in circuits, and each circuit returns each moth to precisely where it began.

Bell-crickets in every crevice, source of the hiss, pale grey-green, more of them than there is room for, packed abdomen to abdomen in the fissures like grain in a sack.

Eleven hundred and four snails, whose shells are grown from the wall and cannot be told from the wall until they move. (The count is not folklore. Four separate visitors have counted them, in four separate centuries, and got the same number, and each thought they were the first to bother.)

Mites in the film of water on the crystals, in numbers that make the film seem to simmer.

And, in the west corner, under the bright part of the ceiling, something the size of a large cat, six-limbed, furred in a grey that gives back the light exactly, asleep. It breathes. Twelve breaths a minute. It has been asleep for the entire duration of the room and it is not thin, and nothing here has ever been eaten.

That is the whole ecology, and it is not a cycle. Nothing eats, nothing mates, nothing is born, nothing dies, nothing decays. There is motion — a great deal of motion, the walls crawl — but no development. It is a tableau that fidgets.

**The plants are sparse and wrong-looking.** Nine rosettes of a pale flat-leaved thing rooted into hairline cracks, and a scatter of moss cushions, maybe twenty. All the rosettes are the same size. All the moss cushions are the same size. Nothing here grew to its dimensions; everything arrived at them. If you look for a small one, or a large old one, you will not find it. This is the moment plants look like when there is no growth to look at.

**Someone has been here.** Against the south wall stands a dry wall of stacked stone flakes, mortarless, two hands high, nine courses, beautifully made. Beside it lies the tenth stone, shaped for its place, not placed. It will not be placed. Set it on the wall yourself and, when you look again, it is back on the floor at the same angle.

Carved into the solid rock beside the wall is a doorway. Hand-sized. Jambs, lintel, a worn threshold. Behind it is rock. It is not a niche or a shrine; it is a door, correctly proportioned, for something the height of your palm, and it has been used enough to wear the sill.

There are four tally marks near the pool, cut with something harder than the wall. Four. Nothing suggests they were interrupted; nothing suggests they were finished.

And in a small pocket above the sleeping animal there is one flake of dark stone with a groove worked into it for a haft, worn blunt on the working edge. The haft is not there. The haft was never there. Whoever wore that edge blunt did it somewhere else, in a place where things wear.

---

**The rules, as they are usually given:**

You can take nothing out. Objects removed are found back in position; the removers report the ordinary sinking feeling of having imagined the whole thing, and then find their pockets torn from inside.

You can leave nothing. Names scratched in the wall are gone before you look up. Blood does not stay.

Injuries received here do not progress. A cut from the wall does not bleed and does not close. It sits open, clean, painless, at the exact stage it was made, for as long as you remain. Outside, it resumes.

You cannot sleep. Sleep is a change of state.

You do not get hungry, and you do not stop being as hungry as you were when you arrived.

And you leave the way you came, which is to say without agency and without noticing, mid-breath, mid-sentence, somewhere on a grazed limestone slope in a light that has direction and a wind that moves and a silence full of small unrepeatable noises, with eleven seconds of a tone in your head that will still be there, unchanged, when you are old.
