## The Lantern Under the Hill

**Where it is.** Ten paces in from the hillside, and eleven paces down. You reach it by a stair that begins in dry grass and ends in water — the last nine steps are underwater, and they go on going down, pale and exact, until they meet the floor. The stair does not come back. There is no far side. This is the whole of the place: one room, cut into the hill, then lined, then sealed. A pocket. You could stand in the middle of it and touch nothing, and still be inside something entirely made.

**The room.** Square-ish, nine paces by nine and a half — the plan was a square, and the last quarter-pace is where the crew hit a harder seam and gave in a little. Vaulted; the vault is a shallow dome laid up in courses of thin brick, each course leaning further in until the ring at the top is small enough to cap with a single cut stone. Every surface — floor, walls, dome, the treads and risers of the stair — is coated in a lime plaster burnished with a river cobble until it holds a low shine, like the inside of an eggshell. It is very new. You can still read where the burnishing strokes overlapped, a faint herringbone under the water's flex, and where a thumb went in and was smoothed over but not quite. The chisel marks in the stone underneath show through at the corners as ghost-hatchings. Nothing has worn. There is no rounding anywhere, no lip on the steps, no soft place. It is a room with all its edges still on it.

**The water.** It is full, or nearly. The water stands about a hand's breadth below the springing of the vault, so the room is water with a thin lens of hot air trapped over it. Swimming in it, you have a low bright ceiling of your own reflection, and above that a real dome you can touch. The water is hot — not scalding, but at the edge of comfort, the temperature of a hand pressed hard against a hand. It comes in from a spring in the hill's hip, alkaline, so clear it is difficult to believe in, so clear that distance stops working: the far wall might be an arm away or fifteen paces, and you find out by swimming.

**The light.** A shaft rises from the crown of the dome to the hillside above, a square bore a forearm across, cut plumb and lined with the same burnished plaster. At the top it is open to the sky. This is not a slot for air; the whole room is built around it. From mid-morning to mid-afternoon a column of sun comes down it and enters the water, and the water takes it and gives it to the plaster, and the plaster gives it back, and everything gives it back again, and the chamber fills with light the way a bell fills with a struck note. It is *bright* — brighter than the shaded hillside above, unshadowed, sourceless, a white light with a faint green in it that comes from nothing but its own depth. Your own hands are lit from beneath. The floor is lit. Your shadow does not lie on the floor; it hangs in the water in front of you as a soft blue absence and dissolves at arm's length. At the true noon of certain weeks in the year, the column strikes the floor dead centre, where a small disc of darker stone was set for it, and for perhaps forty breaths the disc glows like a coin on a forge, and then the sun moves on, and the light-patch begins its slow climb up the east wall.

**The sound.** It is loud in here. That is the first thing and the hardest to convey, because the light suggests stillness and the room is not still. The inflow arrives through a clay chute that discharges from the north wall a little above the waterline, and its fall into the pool is a continuous white roar, magnified by the dome and given back from every plastered face, so that the noise has no direction — it is not coming from the chute, it is the substance the room is made of. Under the roar, a beat: the spring is siphonic and surges, so every hundred and some breaths the flow trebles and the room's voice drops into a bass thudding, and the water level lifts a finger's width, and the trapped air is squeezed and forced out through a vent-hole in the southeast haunch with a shriek like a shepherd's whistle held too long. Then the surge passes; the air draws back in with a wet gulp; and there is a minute of merely roaring. Under all of it, always, the fizz of gas coming out of the hot water — a fine granular hiss on the skin, like a fistful of sand poured slowly onto a drum.

You cannot speak in this room. Voices arrive as shape without word. The people who built it work by hand-sign and by rope: a line strung from the stair-head to the vent has knots in it, and a tug means *rising*, two means *hold*, three means *out now*. On the dry wall of the stair, above the waterline, there are tally strokes in charcoal counting surges, and a knife-scratched arc marking where the light-patch reached at the solstice, and beside it another arc, corrected, and beside that the first one crossed out.

**Rules.** The room permits entry only on the ebb of the cycle; at the surge, the air-lens compresses and the vent screams and a swimmer at the dome is pressed against the brick. The sluice at the base of the west wall — a bronze plate on a wooden screw, the only worked metal in the place, thumb-thick, already skinned green — is opened once a day with a bar to let the mineral load out downhill into a channel; while it is open the water pulls, and the pull is toward a grille no wider than a hand, and the pull is patient and would not let go. So: enter at the ebb, leave before the surge, never on a sluice hour. And carry nothing you cannot swim with. There is no ledge to set things on. Everything in the room floats, sinks, or is part of the room.

**Its inhabitants, such as they are.** No animals. Not one. The intake was screened with a woven basket at the spring-head, and nothing that swims survives the heat anyway. There is no fish, no frog, no water-beetle, no larva, no fly on the water. It is the most striking absence in the place — a room of hot bright water with nothing hunting anything.

Plants, barely. The light-shaft's rim, up in the daylight, carries a collar of maidenhair fern that has found the steam, and a few of its roots have come down inside a hand's length, white and hairlike, waving in the updraught. A caper seedling has taken a mortar joint on the stair, three leaves, tenacious, four steps above the water. And in the two corners furthest from the inflow, where the water is a degree cooler, there are patches of cyanobacterial felt the colour of a bruise — the first living skin the room has grown, ragged-edged, still deciding whether to stay. That is all. Nine paces by nine and a half of brilliant water, and three green things in it.

**What it is doing.** Becoming lined. The water is heavy with dissolved lime and it is laying that lime down, invisibly, everywhere, a coat thinner than a fingernail's growth in a season. Already the sharp arris of the lowest steps has taken a bloom, a faint sugaring; already the tool ghosts at the corners are going soft under their own frost. In ten years the herringbone burnish will be gone under stone the room made itself. In a hundred, the vent will have narrowed to a whistle-hole and the dome will have grown down into a series of stubby fingers and the sluice will be a lump. The builders know this. That is why the tally marks, why the corrected arc. This place is not finished, and it is not ruining; it is at the beginning of a long slow filling-in, and everything about it — its hardness, its glare, its noise, its emptiness — belongs to that beginning.

Stand on the ninth step with the water at your chest, in the roar, in the impossible light, and the room has the feeling of a held breath before a very long exhalation. Above, on the hillside, the grass is dry and there are goats somewhere and the shaft is a dark square in the ground that you could step into without noticing, and no sound comes out of it at all.
