## The Breathing Room

There is no hill to announce it. The plain above is a hard pan of caliche and split stone, the color of old bone, and it runs flat to every horizon under a sky bleached almost white. You could walk over the place and never know, except that in the middle of the afternoon a thread of moisture leans up out of the ground and bends east, and the goats — if there were goats — would turn their heads toward it.

The opening is a slot, three paces long, no wider than a body, softened at every edge. Nothing about it is sharp. Ten thousand rains have rounded the lip into something like a lower jaw; where you put your hands to climb down, the rock is glassy and warm and slightly greasy with algae, and it fits the hands, because hands have been fitted to it for a very long time.

Then the descent, seven meters of it, down a chimney where the stone bulges inward and you go sideways, and the air changes at about the fourth meter. It thickens. It goes from oven-dry to bathhouse. It smells of fig sap, wet limestone, crushed fern stalk, and something animal and sweetish, like a bat roost or a granary.

And then the room.

---

Call it forty paces across, an ellipse, with a ceiling that domes to maybe twelve meters at the center. It is a single chamber and it is complete in itself; the walls do not lead anywhere except down into fissures too narrow for anything larger than a rat snake. It has the proportions of a chapel and none of the ambition. It was made by water leaving, not by water arriving — an old solution cavity in a limestone bed, drained, roofed, forgotten, and then reoccupied by everything.

The heat comes from below. Along the eastern wall the floor is broken into a lattice of hairline fissures, and out of these the earth exhales: a slow, dense, mineral warmth, thirty-eight degrees at the vents, thirty-one at the far side, never varying by more than a degree between noon and midnight, never varying at all between seasons. This is the engine of the room. The heat rises, meets the cold stone of the dome, and gives up its water there in a skin of condensation that beads, gathers, hangs, and falls. It rains in this room every night of the year. Not hard — a fine, irregular, unhurried drip, one bead per handspan per minute, so that the sound is less a sound than a texture, a faint arrhythmic ticking spread across the whole ceiling like static.

The light comes from above, and only from above, and it is not much. The slot admits a single wedge of sun that moves across the west wall between the fourth and sixth hour and reaches the floor for perhaps forty minutes a day in high summer. The rest of the illumination is scatter — green-filtered, twice-bounced, the color of a bottle held to a candle. Enough to read a face by. Not enough to read by. In this dimness everything that photosynthesizes has learned to be enormous and thin.

---

The fig came down through the slot as a seed in a bird's gut, probably two centuries ago, probably more. It grows *upward* on the outside — a stunted, wind-scoured crown on the plain, unremarkable — and downward on the inside, and the inside is where it has spent its real life. Root curtains hang from the ceiling in braided sheets, some as thick as a thigh, some finer than fishing line, and they have colonized the walls, flattened themselves against the wet stone, fused, buttressed, made cloisters and arcades of themselves. Where a root reaches the floor it thickens into a pillar. There are eleven such pillars. They are why the ceiling has not fallen.

Under and among them: ferns in tiers, the lower ones with fronds a meter long and translucent as insect wing; liverworts in overlapping shingles; a soft blue-black moss on the warm stone near the vents, cropped short by grazers; pale climbing vines with leaves the size of a hand, which flower without color because color would be wasted here, and are pollinated by touch and by scent. The scent of them at night is thick as syrup. Density everywhere — nothing in this room stands alone in space. Frond overlaps frond overlaps root overlaps snail. You cannot put a palm flat against the wall without covering four species.

And at the low northern end, the water: a pool the size of a cart, ten centimeters deep at the rim and unmeasured at the center where it slides under a ledge. It is fed by the ceiling rain and by a seep that runs faster in the weeks after a surface storm, which is four or five times a year. Its surface is skinned with something living and iridescent, and it is broken constantly by things coming up to breathe and going back down.

---

Here is the rule of the place, and you notice it in the first minute and cannot stop noticing it afterward:

**Nothing in this room has a voice.**

There are perhaps nine hundred vertebrates in here and they are all mute. The frogs — there are three kinds, and hundreds of them, fat and pale-throated, sitting in the fern axils where the drip collects — do not call. They signal by drumming: a foot pressed and released against a taut leaf, a tap on a hollow fig root that carries through the wood better than air would carry a croak. The geckos hunt the walls in numbers you would not believe, six or seven to a square meter in the good places, and they do not chirp; they communicate with tail-flags and with a slow open-mouth display that shows a violet tongue. The bats — a small colony, two hundred or so, in the highest pocket of the dome — do not echolocate. They fly by sight in the dim, by memory, by the feel of air moving off the leaf edges, and they land on the fruiting fig with a whisper of leather and eat in total quiet. The crickets have wings but no sound-file on them; the ridges are gone, worn off the species entirely, and the males court by standing on a frond and trembling until the frond trembles too.

Why, is not written anywhere. The dome is a perfect whispering gallery; a single sharp noise made here comes back four times, from four directions, magnified. Perhaps for something like a hundred thousand years, anything that announced itself in this room was found. What remains is an ecology conducted entirely in vibration, scent, and light — the flick of a tail, the tremble of a stem, the pheromone plume drifting up the heat column and pooling under the ceiling where the moths read it.

So: abundance without sound. Cascades of movement in near-silence. Snails the size of plums pouring over the wet stone in slow files. A whip scorpion the length of a forearm, feeling its way along a root with legs like antennae. Blind white shrimp turning in the pool. Ants in three castes running a highway up the largest pillar, and a hundred meters of that highway visible at once, and no sound from any of it. Twice a night a rat snake takes a frog off a fern and there is a thrash of maybe two seconds and then nothing, and the ticking of the ceiling continues, and the frogs three fronds away go on drumming their small tattoos.

The loudest thing in the room is the drip. The second loudest is your own breathing, which you will find yourself trying to quiet.

---

People came here. Not many, not recently.

Six steps have been cut into the chimney at its worst pinch, each one shallow and worn to a scoop. At the pool, the rim has been dressed flat over about a meter and a half and there is a groove for a cord, polished, cut by long use. A niche has been hollowed into the west wall at chest height, hand-deep, and there is nothing in it now but a wasp's abandoned cell and a black residue of old lamp soot. Sherds of a thin red-slipped ware lie in the moss below it, four of them, one with a fingernail-impressed rim. And on the largest fig pillar, at the height of a standing adult's shoulder, the bark has been rubbed for so long by so many passing hands that it has gone hairless and dark and slightly concave — a bad hand's-width hollow, the way a doorframe wears.

That is all. No wall, no tomb, no image, no tool left behind. Whoever they were, they came down for water in the dry months, they came quietly because the room teaches quiet within a minute of entry, they touched the pillar on the way past, and they left. The last of them stopped coming long enough ago that a fern has established itself on the topmost step.

---

The room is not static. It is simply slow, and it is in balance, which reads as stillness only to a visitor who stays an hour.

Through a day: the wedge of sun arrives and every gecko on the west wall shifts to intercept it, then shifts away as it burns. Condensation is heaviest just before the surface dawn, when the plain above has cooled — that is the room's rainy hour, and the frogs move up the ferns to meet it. Midday the air stratifies; a warm ceiling layer holds the night's scents in suspension and the moths gather there, hundreds of them, hanging in a haze eight meters up like a slow brown smoke.

Through a year: the fig fruits twice, and both times the room goes briefly frantic — bats, beetles, ants, a civet-like thing that comes down the chimney and is otherwise never seen, all of them in the crown at once, all of them silent, the whole pillage conducted at the volume of a page turning. The dry season shrinks the pool by two-thirds and exposes a shelf of bones, mostly frog and gecko and bird, sorted by size the way water sorts things. The wet season brings the seep, and once every few years brings a real flood through the slot — a brief brown column of water hitting the floor and sheeting outward, tearing away the lower ferns, drowning the low ants, refilling the deep places, and adding another half-millimeter of silt to the accumulated depth of the floor. Everything regrows within four months. Everything has done this several thousand times.

Through longer time: the dome gives up a slab every century or so. The fig pillars catch most of them. The floor rises. The room shrinks. In some future the two will meet and the room will be a seam of black soil in white rock, full of pollen and small bones and four sherds of red pottery, and nobody will hear that either.

---

If you sit down against the warm east wall and stay past your welcome — and the room does not object, has no mechanism for objecting — this is what happens. Your eyes open up and the dimness becomes generous, and you begin to see the number of animals, which is not a number you were prepared for. Sweat stops evaporating and simply sits on you. The drip resolves into individual events, and you find you are tracking them. Something walks across the back of your hand on many legs and you let it. A frond eight centimeters from your ear begins to tremble, very slightly, for perhaps a minute, and then stops, and something has been said, and it wasn't to you.

Nothing here needs you to be quiet. It's just that you will be.
