## Beneath the Grating, Number Six

### The Way In

There is no door at grade, only a cast-iron grating in the paving of a service alley, its bars worn concave by a century of boot soles, and under the bars a smell that people who live above have stopped noticing: warm iron, wet brick, a faint sweetness like scalded milk. Lift the grate — it lifts, the hinge has long since found its resting arc and will not go further than that arc allows — and the throat goes down twenty-two rungs.

The rungs are the first lesson. They are hot. Not searing; the heat of a mug of tea ten minutes forgotten. You climb down with your palms open, taking the rungs at the sides, and your hands come away smelling of pennies.

At the bottom the air stops being air from outside and becomes the air of the place, which is a substance with its own manners. It is thick, at about the temperature of blood plus a little, and it does not move except in slow lobes, a drift toward the warm and a drift away, so that standing still you feel a soft pressure on one cheek and then, minutes later, on the other.

It is dark. This has to be said properly, because it is not the dark of a room at night. There is nothing coming in. Twenty feet of made ground and paving between here and the sky, and the throat's grating admits, at best, a grey rumour on the top three rungs. Below that, the dark is complete and it is *furnished* — you learn the place with your hands, your shins, and the soles of your feet, and after a while with the heat on your skin, which works like a very slow, very coarse kind of sight.

### The Chamber

It is not large. Sixty feet by forty, and high enough that a thrown stone takes a moment to come down. Call it the size of a modest chapel, and the shape of one too: a barrel vault of Staffordshire blue brick, headers laid dark and glazed, running the long axis with three squat transverse arches breaking it into bays. Stencilled at the springing of the second arch, in lead-white paint gone brown, the characters **№ 6**, and beneath in smaller letters a word worn to *—ATING HOUSE*.

Everything in here was made. There is no soil, no stone that was not quarried and dressed, no surface that did not come off a mould or out of a mill. The floor is engineering brick laid on edge in a herringbone, dished toward channels; the walls are brick; the ceiling is brick; the piers are cast iron with a beaded fillet at the base and a fluted collar where they meet the vault, foundry-marked *HOLLAND & SON — DUDLEY*. And yet the whole of it has weathered into something that behaves like a landform. The brick arrises are rounded off. Calcite has come out of the ceiling in the wet bays and hung itself in drapes and slow stalactites down the pipework, so that in places you cannot tell iron from mineral except by tapping: the flange rings, the deposit only thuds. The floor channels have silted with a fine warm grey mud, packed hard, cracked in mosaic where they run dry. Rust has gone as far as rust goes here and stopped, having eaten to a stable brown skin that no longer flakes but sheds a faint powder under a fingernail. Threads are galled shut. Every bolt in Number Six is a decision that can no longer be revised.

### Density

The reason you learn it with your hands is that it is packed.

The volume is laced with pipe. Mains of eight and twelve inches run the long axis at three levels, lagged in plaster-and-hair that has gone to a crust and, where the crust has broken, exposes the black iron beneath in long wounds. Off these, a coral of branches: two-inch, one-inch, half-inch, elbows, tees, unions, drip legs, and a great orderly clustering of small copper capillaries into brass headers, each capillary once running to a gauge somewhere, most of them now running to nothing. There are ladder-frames of angle iron, a catwalk at eight feet with an open-mesh deck that hums a note when stepped on, and everywhere the hangers — hundreds of them, U-bolts and clevises and turnbuckles, so that looking up (if you could look) the vault is not a vault but a canopy, layered and near-touching, with the brick visible only in patches like sky through a thicket.

Passage through it is a matter of learned routes. There are three: the low way along the north channel, on hands and knees, where the mud is warm and dry and the pipes above give off enough heat to make you hurry; the middle way, upright, sideways, turning your shoulders every few paces through a slot between a header and the flank of the No. 2 vessel; and the catwalk, which is easy and which you will not use twice, because the mesh transmits every footfall into the whole hanging structure and the structure answers for a full minute afterward with small tickings from twenty places at once, and the place does not like to be spoken to that loudly.

### Heat, and Where It Comes From

Four vessels stand in the west bay: riveted cylinders, five feet through, lying in cradles, with domed ends and manway covers dogged down. Number Two is the warm one. Its lagging has gone entirely, and its plate is at the temperature where you can hold your palm against it for a count of four. Numbers One, Three, and Four are merely blood-warm, warm as the room.

Nothing is feeding Number Two. This is the first of the place's departures from sense. There is a firebox beneath it, and the firebox is cold and swept, and the flue above is cold, and the fuel road that once served it has been bricked across since before the calcite began, because the calcite runs over the brickwork of that infill in an unbroken sheet. Whatever is warm in Number Two has been warm a long while, patiently, at a temperature that varies over the course of a year by perhaps four degrees — warmest in what would be late winter above, coolest in autumn, out of phase with everything, in phase with nothing anyone has identified.

The heat spreads by the pipes as it was designed to and pools under the vault. The floor is cooler than the ceiling by fifteen degrees, and the dished bays cooler again, so that the chamber has a climate with an up and a down to it, and a body moving from crouch to stand passes through three weathers.

### Water

Damp arrives here two ways.

The first is condensation, which is the chamber breathing. The warm air holds its moisture against the ceiling; the north bays, cooled by the ground beyond the brick, take it back. A drop forms at the tip of every calcite drape, swells over the course of eleven or twelve minutes, and falls. There are perhaps four hundred such tips. They are not synchronised and never will be, and the result is the sound of the place: a slow irregular percussion, four or five drops a second across the whole volume, each landing on brick or in mud or in the sump with a different voice, so that the darkness has a texture and a distance to it, and you can tell where you are in Number Six by the shape of the dripping.

The second is rain. When it rains hard above, the alley gutter overruns into the grating, and for an hour a rope of water comes down the throat and along the north channel, cold, smoking where it crosses the warm mud, and goes to the sump. This is the only event. It is the whole of weather here. Afterward the chamber is measurably cooler for two days and the dripping quickens, and the mud in the north channel is glazed and takes footprints for a week, and there is a smell of the street — soot, horse, coal-tar, whatever the street smells of in whichever century — that fades over the following days like a visitor leaving slowly.

The sump is in the southeast corner: a brick-lined tank ten feet square, uncovered, filled to within two feet of the floor with water at bath temperature, absolutely still except where a drop hits it. It is clear and tastes of chalk and iron. A pump once emptied it; the pump is seized, its packing gone to fibre and its bronze impeller welded to its own housing by a hundred years of gentle mineral, and yet the sump has never overflowed. The level is the level. It has been the level long enough that a tidemark of pale deposit ringed the brick and then, over decades, thickened into a lip.

### What Grows

Vegetation gets in the way any life gets in here, which is by accident and then persistence.

Around the throat, in the grey rumour of the top rungs, there is a felt of moss two feet thick vertically and no thicker, sharply bounded where the light gives out. Below that, nothing green — but in the second bay, where a drip has fallen on the same spot for a hundred years, there is a colony of blanched things. Fern-like: a rosette of pale stalks, wax-white, the shape of ferns but without leaf, subsisting on the sugars in the brick lime and the tar in the old jointing and whatever the rain brings down. They are cold to the touch even in the warm air. They grow perhaps an inch a decade. There are eleven rosettes; there were eleven when the last logbook entry was made, in a copperplate hand, on a page swollen shut with damp.

Elsewhere: threads of something in the lagging cracks, black and fine as hair, that go dormant when the rain comes. A crust of grey on the flue brick that is possibly a lichen and possibly not alive at all.

### What Moves

Eels in the sump. Three, four — no one has counted them at once. Blind and unpigmented, pale as the ferns, thirty inches, moving so little that they are usually taken for lengths of shed lagging until they turn. What they eat is a question nobody can answer without emptying the sump, which nobody will do. They have been there long enough to be older, probably, than the people who mention them.

Moths in the vault. Not many — you might go three visits without one, and then a fourth when one lands on the back of your hand and stays, its feet minutely gripping. Wingspan of a thumbnail, wings without pattern, eyes reduced to specks. They find each other by scent alone in the total dark and their flight is a small dry whirring that stops the moment it starts, because they navigate by touch against the pipes and are always alighting.

And in the cold flue above Number One, high up where a hand can just reach through a broken sight-glass port, there is a warm papery mass the size of a hat that is not a wasp nest and gives, when you put your palm near it without touching, the sensation of being *noticed*. This is the second departure from sense. It has been there four generations of tenders. Nobody has looked, because there is nothing to look with, and nobody has taken a lamp in, because you do not bring flame down the throat of Number Six — that is the rule, and it has outlived its reason, and it is kept anyway.

### The Quiet

For a place built entirely of engines, it is astonishingly quiet. This is what people notice last and remember longest.

The inventory of sound: the dripping, described. The tick of metal in the hours after the rain, as the pipes come back up to temperature, a random popping that can go on for two days. A relief valve on the No. 3 header that lifts perhaps once a fortnight and sighs for four seconds — the loudest thing that happens, and it happens by itself. A hum, so low it registers in the sternum rather than the ear, present always, source unlocated, that a tender in 1889 attributed to "the works" and a tender in 1954 attributed to the substation two streets over, and which was there before either. And, when the alley above is used, a soft granular grinding transmitted through twenty feet of made ground: someone's wheel, someone's heel, distant as weather on another planet.

Speech in Number Six comes out quieter than intended. Everybody does this. The volume is small and the surfaces are soft with mineral and the air is heavy, and a raised voice returns to you thickened and wrong, and so you drop to a murmur and stay there.

### Slow

Nothing here is still, but nothing here is fast. The rate of the place is roughly this: a drop every eleven minutes; a valve every fortnight; a rain every week or two; four degrees over a year; an inch of fern per decade; a millimetre of calcite per human lifetime; one seizure of one bearing per generation. Set against that, a visit is a violence — a bright fast smear of body heat and moved air and disturbed mud that the chamber takes a fortnight to absorb.

Which it does. That is the character of the place, more than the heat or the dark: it is in balance and it will return to balance. The rust has stopped, the level is the level, the pipes hang where they hang because every hanger that was going to fail has failed and been arrested by the ones that didn't. Number Six is not decaying. It finished decaying. It arrived at its own state, and now it holds it — a small warm dark room of iron and brick and drip and blind eel, twenty feet under a street, keeping a temperature nobody requires, at a pressure that reads three-quarters of a bar on the one gauge that still reads anything, and has read three-quarters of a bar since before the gauge's maker went out of business.

Come out. Twenty-two rungs. At the top your hands smell of pennies and your clothes are wet through with your own sweat and the chamber's condensate mixed, and the alley is cold on you, and behind you, under the grating you have set back on its worn arc, a drop falls, and another, unhurried, in the dark, in the warm, keeping the count.
