## The Level

Everything here was laid by hand, and none of it has aged.

The ground is brick. Not brick over earth — brick over brick. Where a canal has been cut you can see the courses going down, herringbone under stretcher under herringbone, twelve, twenty, forty courses into the wet dark, and the water standing in the bottom is clear enough to show that it keeps going. People have dug. There is no bedrock. There is no soil. There is the made thing, all the way down, and beneath the deepest course anyone has reached, more of the same, except that the bricks get smaller, as though laid by smaller hands.

The plain of it runs out to a valley's width in every direction — a made floodplain, low, flat to within a finger's height across a day's walk. It is not featureless. There are causeways raised a body's height above the general level, running arrow-straight, their flanks battered and faced in a paler clay. There are sunken tanks the size of village greens, stepped down in eight courses to standing green water. There are levees, plinths, culverts with corbelled mouths, and one hill — a broad terraced mound with grass growing on its risers, which is not a hill at all but eleven million bricks agreeing to look like one.

The heat comes up out of the floor. By the middle of the endless day the open brick will take skin off a bare sole, and the air above it shivers in sheets. But the water table sits a hand's width under the top course, so the brick is always damp beneath its hot skin, and it steams. Vapour stands in the culvert mouths. Every plant carries beads of condensation on its underside, forming faster than they fall, so that the leaves drip steadily in a place where it has never rained.

## The light

There is no sun.

The sky is a white glare from edge to edge, even in intensity, and the brightness does not come only from above. It comes from the water, from the pale faces of the causeways, from the air between things. Nothing here casts a shadow. Stand in the mouth of a culvert and the dark inside is not dark; it is a softer grey glow, and your hand held against the wall has no edge of shade beneath it.

This does things to distance. Without shadow the plain has no modelling; a levee three hundred paces off and a levee three thousand paces off look equally near and equally flat. You learn to judge by the brick pattern, by how many courses you can still count. It is the only reliable ruler.

## The water

It never rises and never falls. Wooden gauge-posts are driven at the tank corners, notched and blackened by nothing, and the water meets the same notch always.

It moves, though. There are slow currents in the canals, and they run where the levees tell them to run, including upslope. A canal will climb a causeway's gentle ramp for two hundred paces, unhurried, without a ripple, and go over the top. Gravity is a suggestion the water is polite about but not bound by. Drop something in and it sinks, so it isn't that weight has stopped; it is that the channels have authority.

The tanks hold reflections. In the reflection the sky has a sun in it, low and orange, and the reflected brickwork is worn — corners rounded, joints furred with moss, the whole thing centuries older. And there is a shadow beneath the reflection of your body, long and eastward, that your body does not have. Most people stop looking after the first time. Some stand there for a long while, trying to decide which of the two of you is the copy.

## The green

The vegetation is lush and it is planted. Every tree stands in a square well cut in the pavement, edges crisp as the day they were struck. Figs, mostly, and something like a mango with rough black bark; their roots have gone down into the joints and split nothing, because nothing here splits. Papyrus and reed stand in ruled beds. Taro fills the tanks in rows. Vines drop from the causeway parapets in curtains twenty feet long, and the flowers on them are wet and heavy and open.

Between the plantings there is a great deal of empty hot brick. That is the character of the place — dense green clots at long intervals, and between them a shining floor, wide and bare, so that from the terraced mound the landscape reads as a very large drawing with a very few marks on it. The green is thick where it is; it is simply not everywhere. The emptiness is as deliberate as the planting.

Nothing is in season, because there are no seasons. The fig carries bud, green fruit, and ripe fruit at the same time, permanently, on the same branch. Eat the ripe one and by the time you have walked around the trunk it is there again. Nothing falls. Nothing rots. There is no leaf litter anywhere on the Level, and no one has ever seen a leaf drop.

## The animals

They are numerous and they are quiet.

Herons stand in the tanks, ibis pick along the levee crowns, catfish move like slow thoughts in the canals, crabs the colour of new brick sidestep along the joints. Dragonflies in the hundreds over the reed beds, geckos flat against the parapets, bees at the vine flowers. It is a rich place. It has the population of a healthy delta.

None of them make a sound. The frog on the tank step inflates its throat to a taut pale globe and holds it and lets it down, and what comes out is not noise but a small change in the pressure of the air against your face. The birds' bills open and close. The bees' wings blur without hum.

Handle them and some are warm and soft and some are not. There are frogs with the faint spiral of a wheel on their bellies, herons with a potter's thumb-swipe at the base of the neck, catfish whose gills are cut, not grown. They live exactly as well as the others. Their eggs, when you find them, are potsherds — curved, fired, with yolk inside.

## Sound

Sound does not travel here. It is made and then it stays where it was made.

Strike a brick with a mallet and it rings a clean note, and a person standing four paces away hears nothing at all. Shout, and the shout is loud in your own skull and dies at arm's length. Two people walking a causeway must walk shoulder to shoulder to speak. Beyond that they walk in a silence that is not empty — it is full, packed, the way water is full — but which carries nothing.

The result is a landscape of enormous quiet with small dense pockets of noise in it, like the green in the brick: your own footfalls, your own breath, the drip off the leaf directly above you. Everything else is mute distance.

## The tools

Whoever built this had no metal to speak of. There is soft copper in a few chisels, and otherwise shell adzes, wooden mallets, cord, plumb-bobs of fired clay, reed baskets, a mason's float worn smooth by use.

They are lying about everywhere, at intervals, on the tank steps and the causeway crowns, set down neatly. None of them is broken. None is chipped. There are no discard heaps, no rubble, no offcuts, no kiln — nowhere on the Level is there a kiln, and there are perhaps four hundred million bricks.

Every brick carries a thumbprint in the same place on its bed face, and it is the same thumbprint. You can check. People have checked for days.

At the northeast margin there is an unfinished corner: a stack of green unfired bricks drying under a reed shade, a mortar pit with the slip still wet in it, a course half-laid with the line stretched and pegged. The green bricks have never dried. The line has never sagged. It has been the same amount of unfinished since anyone can remember, and it is the only part of the world that admits the world was made at all.

## The edge, and the walking

The Level ends in a ruled line. Past it there is white — not cliff, not sea, not fog exactly; a brightness with no surface in it.

Walk toward it and it withdraws at your own pace, and the brickwork keeps coming, and after an hour you will pass a brick with a nick out of its corner shaped like a bird's foot, and after another hour you will pass it again. The canals do the same. Follow one down its length and you arrive at your own back.

So the place is regional in feel — a valley's worth of causeways and tanks and figs and standing herons, wide enough to lose people in — and it is also closed, and the two facts sit together without arguing.

## The rule

Nothing changes. That is the whole law.

A dead heron does not decay; it lies with its eye bright and its feathers perfect, and if you go away and come back it is standing in the shallows again. Wounds close over as you watch. Footprints press into the soft never-setting mortar and rise smoothly back out behind you within a count of ten. The gauge-post shows the same notch. The unfinished corner stays unfinished.

It is hot, and wet, and green, and shadowless, and silent, and brand new, and it has been brand new for as long as it has existed, which is a length of time nobody can get any purchase on.

The tally marks are cut into the parapet of the third causeway, a long panel of them, several thousand at a glance. Count them and note the number. Count again. It will not be the number you wrote down, and your own handwriting will look like someone else's, and the ink — you didn't bring ink — will still be wet.
