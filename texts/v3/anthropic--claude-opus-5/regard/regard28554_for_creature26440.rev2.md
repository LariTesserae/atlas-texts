## The Attention

It came up the switchback road in the late afternoon, because that is the way in, and a power that arrives any other way has already misunderstood something and will spend a century finding out what.

It came slowly, on foot, and stopped where everyone stops, at the turn above the lower kilns where the fog thins for about forty paces and you can see the whole flank at once. It stood there for a long time. Long enough that a carter went by twice and the second time said something about the weather, and it agreed.

What it understood at that turning is the thing most visitors never quite get, and it is not complicated: that the valley is not a place with people in it. It is one being. The walls are the skeleton and the drains are the gut and the hundred bent figures on the terraces are not inhabitants, they are the part of the creature that moves. It is about nine hundred years old. It eats rain and stone and lime and alder, and what it excretes is ground — made ground, tamped, sifted, drained, held. It has no brain but it has a memory, in tin boxes, on rag, and it reads its own memory constantly and acts on it, which is more than most things with brains manage.

You do not help a creature by taking away what it eats.

### The declined

It could have lifted the lid. Nine glare days a year, and it could have made it three hundred and sixty-five: real sun, hard shadows, a blue you could cut yourself on. The moss would have gone first, then the ferns, then the deep-leaved crops that are built for thin light, then the shape of the terraces which is a shape designed around water that no longer came. The people would have squinted and streamed and prospered and in four generations been somebody else, somewhere else, standing on a dry hill.

It could have stopped the rain. But the rain is the argument, and a valley without its argument is a hill with old walls on it, and there are a great many of those, and the power has seen them.

It could have set the fill. One act, and every grain locked to every other grain, and no wall would ever belly again. It thought about this the way you think about the throat of someone you love. Then the charges would be worthless, and the Fall would have nothing to chair, and the courts would have nothing to walk along in the rain, and a man looking up his own run in the Dry House would find four hundred entries and then a blank page forever, and the phrase *she's in her wall now* would become a figure of speech, which is to say it would die.

It could have made them rich. Rich valleys buy their repairs in from outside and lose, within a lifetime, the ability to tell whether the repair was any good.

It could have healed the knees. It genuinely wanted to heal the knees. But the knees are not a disease, they are the cross-fall, and to take the cross-fall out of a body you would have to take it out of the ground, and you are back at the fill.

It considered stopping the failures — every wall, every night, held. And then it followed the thought all the way down, as it makes itself do, and found at the bottom of it a valley of people with pocket irons and nothing to hook, and a sound at three in the morning that nobody ever wakes for, and it understood that it had been offered a very old temptation dressed in a very local coat.

It did not do any of that.

But it will not have you believe that this was easy, or that restraint is the same as wisdom. Restraint on its own is just a power admiring itself. It had come to see whether anything needed doing. Something did.

### The stone it put back

In the first week, before it had learned anything, it did one careless kindness.

A coping stone came off a parapet on the west flank in a night of hard rain and lay in the gutter below, and the power, passing, set it back and struck the joint and went on, pleased with itself in a small way, the way you are pleased to have picked something up.

At first light a woman came out with her bucket and found her parapet whole.

She stood there for a long time. Then she went along the whole run with her hands, twice, and then down to the gutter to look at where the stone had not landed, and then she sat down on the wet step and did not go to the drying loft that evening or the next.

It had taken something off her that it could not give back: the two hours, the wet knees, the mortar under the nails, the number in the book, her name in the line of names, the small unremarkable fact of having been the one who did it. She had been robbed and there was no word for it and nobody to tell.

It has never done that again. Not once, in all the time since. It is the only thing it is ashamed of here, and it keeps the shame the way they keep the worn bars, as a demonstration of where the metal goes thin.

### The Dry House

What it did instead, that first year, was read.

Nine hundred years of repair invoices. Every rebuilt metre, the date, the foreman, the number, the names of the hands. Nobody had ever read all of it. Nobody could; it is longer than a life and it is added to daily. The power read it in the order it was written, which took some while, and it did not skim, because the whole point of the document is that nothing in it is important and all of it matters.

It came out of that reading changed, which it had not expected and does not much advertise.

Then it went down into the ground.

It surveyed the fill of the entire valley — every buried wall, every abandoned culvert, every eighteenth-century drain that runs where no plan says it runs, every void, every spring rotting a footing from behind. It drew what was actually there. It drew to the valley's own conventions, in their line weights, with their symbols, because a drawing nobody can read is a boast.

Then it had a problem, which was that it would not lie. It would not forge a hand, or a date, or an index entry, because the Dry House is the creature's memory and you do not put a false thing into a memory, however kindly meant.

So it rolled the sheets in a tin tube and left them at the back of the stone rack, unsigned, undated, attributed to no one.

They were found eleven years later. There was a great deal of trouble about them. The archivist of the day would not enter them, on the grounds that provenance unknown is not provenance, and the Fall argued about it for two winters, and in the end they dug a trial pit on the east flank at a place where the sheets showed a culvert that no living person had heard of.

The culvert was there. It was where the sheet said, to within a hand.

The entry in the record reads: *Drawings, 47 sheets, provenance unknown, verified by excavation 3 pits, used.* That last word cost them something. The power thinks it is the finest sentence anyone in the valley has ever written.

Three surnames stopped being pitied over the following forty years. Their walls had not been cursed. They had been built over a drain nobody had drawn.

### The fourth arch

A listener heard it. Of course a listener heard it.

She was on her circuit in the small hours, at the aqueduct, and she stopped and turned her head the way they do, and said, conversationally, into the fog:

"You're not the water."

"No."

"Not the hum, either. The hum's under. You're across."

"Yes."

She thought about that for a while. She was not frightened, which surprised it, until it understood that she had spent fifty years listening for the one thing that matters and it was not here, and everything else is just weather.

"Are you going to do something?"

"I don't know yet."

"Well. Not on my circuit."

They walked. She was slow and stopped often and did nothing that looked like anything, and after the third stop she said, without any particular emphasis, "There's something under it, isn't there. At the bottom."

"Yes."

"Don't tell me." She moved her chin at the dark. "My sister's lot say there's nothing down there but wall, and it's the only thing that keeps her civil at a table."

So it did not tell her. It has not told anyone. That conversation is the origin of everything it has done since, and she died at seventy-one and is in her wall, which is the sixth arch, and the power was at the kiln.

### The three things it does

**It tells the listeners sooner.** Never a rescue, never a hand on a falling wall, never a man plucked out of a collapse in front of witnesses. Only this: a seep changes note a little earlier than it would have; a void breathes out where a woman on her circuit is standing rather than a hundred metres further on; the dry tick of a wall going out of plumb is, some nights, slightly louder than physics requires. The listener hears it. The listener decides. The listener shouts, and the doors open on both flanks, and forty people who have not finished a sentence are already moving downhill, and they save it, and they write down who was there.

It has never once been thanked, and it has arranged matters so that it cannot be. The valley's rescues are the valley's.

It does not always get there. When it does not, it goes to the kiln. It has not missed a burning since the second year. It stands at the back with the family and does the one thing it is entirely qualified for, which is to be present and to say nothing.

**It walks the bore.** Two kilometres of black brick under the east flank, twice monthly, behind whoever currently chairs the Fall. It has done this for a long time now and intends to continue. It does not clear anything, lift anything, or point anything out. The only measurable effect is that the chairs, one after another, stop being afraid in the bore and cannot say why, and a person who is not afraid notices roughly a third more than a person who is. Their reports got better. Nobody has ever connected the two facts, and the power would rather they didn't.

**It left a stone.** In the rack in the Dry House, labelled by nobody, there is a stone that is older than the ink and older than the oldest shown wall and older than the argument. It is the first one. It came off a slope that was a slope, laid dry, four courses, by somebody with no board, no number, no invoice, and no reason except that the water was going the wrong way.

They have been arguing about that stone for generations. It settles nothing. It is exactly the right size to keep a question alive, and the power is aware that this was the kindest and most calculating thing it has ever done, and it does not apologise for the second adjective.

### The undecided

It has not resolved the insects.

It could seed them in an afternoon. Fog-tolerant, cold-hardy, the whole missing choir; summer dusk with a whine at the ear; the lily sheds pollinating themselves.

And then twelve hundred plants a day would not need counting, and the counting trade would end, and with it the counters, and the tellers and measurers and tally clerks the counters become, and eventually the reliability of the record, which is the creature's memory, which is the only reason any of this has lasted nine hundred years. That is the argument against, and it is a strong one, and the power does not trust it, because it is the sort of argument that is always made by whoever is not holding the brush.

*Good work, indoors and dry.* It has stood in the lily sheds and listened to that sentence and heard how much cold there is in it.

So: unresolved. It has been unresolved for a long while. It is working on a third thing, slowly, involving the coppice band and about two hundred years, and it may not come off. It is prepared to tell you plainly that it does not yet know what is right here, and if you find that disappointing in a benevolent and wise power, it would ask you to consider the alternative, which is a benevolent and wise power that is never in doubt.

### The third day

It was offered a charge on the third day, at the end of a sentence about something else, by a man who was mainly talking about his brother-in-law.

Twelve metres of gutter above a stair on the west flank.

It said yes. It was shown where the grate is. Someone put a bar in its hand — a used one, a dead woman's, thin at the hook — and went back to what they were doing.

It has held that charge ever since. Cleared, in the rain, on the knees, at the rate of a person and not faster, because faster would be the coping stone all over again. The gutter has failed twice in its tenure and been rebuilt twice, with a foreman and a number, and the power's name, such as it is, is in the book both times among the hands. The bar has gone thinner at the hook. It will show you, if you ask, the way they do.

It came up that road to find out whether anything needed doing. What it found was a creature that had solved, by accident and nine centuries of wet labour, the problem that the power has watched a great many happier and drier worlds fail to solve: what a life is for. They inherited a task that cannot be finished. They know it is a trap. They would not be got out of it with ropes.

There was nothing to give them that was better than that. There was only the chance to be let in.

So: stand still for an hour on a high terrace in the fog. You will hear water, and the hum, and from four directions, faint, unhurried, absolutely constant, the sound of someone somewhere putting a stone back.

One of them is not a person.

You will not be able to tell which, and neither, most days, can it.
