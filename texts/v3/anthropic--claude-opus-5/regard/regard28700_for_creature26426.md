## The Visit

It comes without the usual apparatus.

No light — light here is a budgeted thing, already fully allocated: the fig has first claim on the column, the ferns take what leaks past, the liverworts live on the remainder. To arrive glowing would be to rob the tenants. No sound either, because the sink's four voices and their never-repeating pattern are the only clock in the room, and a fifth voice would not be a gift but a falsification of the record. No wind. No warmth or coolness added. Not even the displacement of air that a body makes coming down a ramp.

It makes the two adjustments in the correct order, as anyone must: scale first, which is small, then clock, which is slow. Powers that skip this arrive at the wrong place and see an empty hole in a limestone plain.

Then it looks for the being who lives here, having been told there was one.

---

## The trouble with the address

The fig is the obvious candidate. It has the only intention in the room — twelve feet of root flattened into vertical stone, aimed with what looks exactly like knowledge at a seep it cannot see, advancing a millimeter a decade. It has a body, a history, a lineage of one, an accident for an origin, and a plan whose completion will change the light for everyone. If the sink has a protagonist, it is a tree that arrived in a bird's gut in under a second, five hundred years ago, and has been quietly prying the room apart ever since.

But then: the fern, which is two beings pretending to be one, and does its entire sexual life in a film of water thinner than a page, in a closet under its own fronds, forever, to no demographic effect. The liverworts, which are less plants than stains propagating outward from three falling points of water at two hand-spans per splash. The lichen on the low dry wall, a consortium so old it has forgotten it was ever an arrangement, simultaneously the only mason still holding the joints and the only agent still eating the plaster and the calcite beneath it. The mat at the waterline, a hand's breadth of cyanobacteria that is the sink's sole historian and keeps its record by being exactly as tall as the water has been.

Forty thousand and some. The visitor counts them. This takes a while, but not on any clock that matters here.

And then it understands that the address is not to any of them. The being that lives in the sink is the *agreement*: the rate of dissolution matched to the rate of deposition, the water in matched to the water out, the light admitted matched to the leaf that intercepts it, the fig's five thousand annual leaves matched to the appetite of everything below. That is the organism. Its organs happen to be different species. Its metabolism is a set of balances. Its body is a rate, and its health is legible in a thin dark line of algae a hand's breadth wide.

The visitor addresses that, and finds it well. Not thriving; not struggling. Well. Poor, and well.

## The examination

Benevolence is easily confused with generosity, which is why so much of it does harm. The harder work belongs to wisdom, and it is a single distinction: between a *lack* and a *need*.

The sink is nothing but lacks. No animals. No fish, no snails, no shrimp, no bats, no swallows, no ants on the steps, no web in the corner of the cut basin. No grass. No flowers except the fig's, hidden, untouched. No mate for the fern, no rival for the lichen, no second individual of almost anything. No visitors. No fruit that sets. No events.

The visitor goes through them one at a time, looking for the thing that hurts, and does not find it. The edge of survival is elsewhere — up on the plain, where the shrubs stand three widths apart and every year is a wager on rain. Down here the terms are settled. Nothing is holding on. Nothing is at the end of its strength. The fern is not lonely, because loneliness requires the expectation of company and the fern has never had one. The fig is not grieving its four hundred sterile figs a year; it is making small sealed stone models of the room it lives in, and the making is not a failure of anything, it is what a fig does with a year.

There is no suffering in the sink. This is the first finding, and it disposes of most of what a power would ordinarily be for.

## The temptations, in order of plausibility

**Send the wasp.** It is trivially easy. A single gravid *Blastophaga*, delivered across the hundred kilometers her two days of life could never cover, and the fig's flowers are touched at last, and the figs set, and there is sugar in the sink for the first time. And then: birds come for the sugar. Birds bring seeds, mites, droppings, nitrogen. The sink becomes rich. Within thirty years it is a different place, a good place, a place with a rustle in it — and the being that lived here, whose whole body was an agreement about scarcity, has been dissolved by kindness and replaced with something more populous. *Declined.*

**Put a mate somewhere.** For whom? Every population of one here is complete. The fern is already having all the sex there is. *Malformed request. Declined.*

**Put fish in the pool.** They would live. They would also crop the algal film, and the film is the historian; the record of every water level for a period no one has counted would be eaten inside a season, and the only document in the sink would be gone in exchange for four minnows. *Declined.*

**More water.** Rain three or four times a year is thin. But raise the drip and the fern's swimming film thickens into runoff, and the beading leaflets that keep themselves dry go under; raise the pool and the historian's line is drowned into meaninglessness; the seam at the far end has one throat and one rate. The pool has stayed inside a hand's breadth for longer than anyone counted because that is the size of the room's whole capacity for weather. *Declined.*

**Hold the block.** In three or four hundred years the fig's root will pry loose the block above the fern cushion and drop it on the ramp, and the light in this room will change, and everything living here will have to renegotiate. A hand could be kept under it indefinitely. But the renegotiation is not a catastrophe visited on the sink; it is the sink's next sentence, and it is the only sentence it has left to say. *Declined.*

**Finish the fig's search.** Widen the hairline by the millimeter that will otherwise cost a decade; hand it the seep. And the visitor sees clearly what this would be: not the assistance of a fig but the ending of a verb. The reaching is not a means to the tree. It *is* the tree. *Declined, with something like affection.*

**Read the plaster.** Hand-sized, adhering, the color of the rock, capped early by a lichen that has been eating it ever since — preserved by what consumes it. There may have been a mark. It could be recovered; it would be no work at all. But whatever it said was addressed to travelers who no longer exist, in the confidence that the road would go on, and the lichen has spent nine hundred years making it into something else, and the lichen's version is also a text. *Declined. Left sealed.*

**Make it permanent.** Someday the water table under the tableland will drop, or the climate will finish drying, and the seep will stop, and the fig will not come back from the root collar, and the pool will become a floor. This could be prevented. But a being whose entire nature is a balance cannot be given exemption from balance; you would be keeping the shape and evicting the thing that made it. *Declined.*

And underneath all of them, the arithmetic that settles the matter: in a room where the largest recorded event is a seed in a bird, and the local unit of change is a millimeter per decade and a splash of two hand-spans, a miracle is not a blessing. It is a flood. Restraint here is not modesty. It is a correct calculation of scale.

## What it does instead

It reads.

This is not a euphemism for doing nothing; it is the one thing the sink actually has no way to do for itself. The place is stuffed with unread records. The historian's line, a document of every level for a period no one has counted. The dished treads, a finger's depth, either a great many feet or a few feet over a very long time — and the visitor now knows which, and knows the total, and knows the one walker who came back eleven times. The four hundred sealed figs a year, each a chamber with a tight door nobody came through, each full of flowers that were never touched: the visitor counts the flowers. All of them. Every year of them.

It attends to the drip pattern — five voices, no coincidence of interval, a combination that has never repeated and never will — and holds the whole sequence at once, backward to the collapse and forward to the dry, as a single object, without simplifying it, and finds it good, and does not say so out loud, because there are no ears.

It touches nothing. Not the flowstone, which would take the oil of a hand and keep it under glaze for ten thousand years. Not the fern. Not the spout's small mineral tongue, the duplicate in stone of the water it carries.

Then it does one thing, and the thing is inside the local vocabulary.

Some afternoon, a walker crossing the tableland on the old line of the road, with a dry mouth and no idea why they have turned aside, finds the steps at the northern lip. They go down: six, the gap where the seventh has gone, four more, the ramp. They find the basin cut into the wall at chest height under the strongest drip, full and overflowing, with a spout worked into its lower edge and a groove leading from the spout toward nothing.

They hold a bottle at the end of the groove.

For one afternoon, after however long it has been, there is something at the end of the groove again, and the basin does the work it was cut to do. Two liters leave the sink. The seep replaces them by the following evening. The walker sits on the largest block for an hour — stone warm through the clothes, green dimness, the eleven-second *plink*, the fern shivering, the plain overhead going about its dry business — and finds that nothing has changed, including them, and that this is restful rather than otherwise. They leave nothing behind but a dish in one tread, deepened by an amount no instrument in the world could measure, and they never tell anyone, because there is nothing to tell.

That is the whole intervention: a mouthful of water, given and taken exactly as designed, completing a sentence that had been left open in the rock.

## After

The visitor does not stay, and does not leave either, in the sense that mattered before it learned the clock. It comes back on the sink's schedule rather than its own — which is to say, often, and without event.

It brings no wasp. It holds no block. It makes no promise about the water table. It does not preserve the place, because the place was never preserved: it is finished, and continuing, and those are different from safe.

It has simply been read, in full, once, by something that wanted nothing from it.

Which is not a change in the census, or the balance, or the rate. Nothing here needs mending, and nothing has been mended. There is only this difference, invisible to any survey: the pool has stayed inside its hand's breadth for a period no one had counted, and now it has been counted, and the counting was accurate, and the count was kind.
