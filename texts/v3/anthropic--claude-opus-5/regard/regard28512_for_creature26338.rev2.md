# What the Attention Did

## First, the difficulty of arriving

It came down off the rim, where luminosity completes, and it brought nothing with it but itself, and nothing happened. No glow, no herald, no change in the pressure. It stood on the last plank of the rim path with the warm rain going straight down through it and it understood, correctly and within about four seconds, that it had never been touched here.

Which made it infinitely fine.

The first tender it met was Durranoveth-at-Four, worn down to **Durr**, out on a crossing with a coil over her shoulder. She heard a body on the planks at eighteen paces and called the greeting and put her hand out into the dark, palm up, thumb cocked, the way you do.

The power took her hand.

It opened her from the heel of the palm to the base of the second finger, through the satin and the dished pads and ninety-one years of inherited smoothness, right down to the bone, without any sensation of cutting.

Now: it could have closed the wound. It was entirely able to. It looked at the wound for what would have been, elsewhere, a long time, and it worked out that a wound healed by fiat is a wound nobody agreed to, and that an unagreed injury in a place like this does not go away, it goes *fine* — and that Durr would then be carrying an edge in her hand that no one could find and that would open other people, quietly, for the rest of her life.

So instead it did the only correct thing, which was the slow thing. It took the coil off her shoulder. It walked her back nine hundred and forty planks to Four with her fist closed inside both of its hands, and it sat on the bench in the shed with her all that night and the next day and the night after, holding the two lips of the cut together so they would knit where a hand had been, and saying her name — the whole of it, all eleven syllables, dredged back up out of the tally-lines — over and over, eleven hours, until Rekk came in off the far circuit and took over.

The scar came up thick and blunt and a little crooked, and it is the most definite thing on Durr's body, and she rubs it when she is thinking.

That was the first gift of a benevolent and wise power in the Tallow Flats: a wound the recipient could not feel, and then three days of upkeep.

It learned faster than most.

## What it considered and put down

**Light.** Of course light. It could have said one word and had the photons complete, and it thought about this for a period best described as *a season of forty-day rains*, and it declined, for reasons it wrote down nowhere.

The eyes are perfect and have never been used. Sight is not a thing you switch on; it is a thing a nervous system spends years learning, in infancy, with a mother's face for a primer, and there are no infants here who would be given years to do it. Light would arrive not as vision but as an event in the front of the skull.

But that was the small reason. The large reason is that seeing is *cheap*. It requires no agreement. It is instantaneous and unilateral and it settles nothing, because a thing you have merely looked at has not been worn. Give them light and within two generations nobody lays a full four-count hand on anybody, and the rails go fine, and the faces go fine, and the backs go open, and the whole basin — which is not a hardship the tenders endure but a *technology they have built for making things real* — quietly stops working, in the name of kindness.

It also noticed that it *wanted* to give them light very badly, and it filed that as information about itself rather than about them.

**Ending the loose.** It could have resolved the entire basin between one Mother Bore groan and the next. Firm, gritty, measurable, ordinary, in an afternoon. It declined. A thing made definite by someone else's decision has not been decided; it has only been asserted, and assertions go fine. Also the waders would have starved, and the tenders would have had nothing left to hold, and a place finished before it is made is not finished, it is abandoned.

**Explaining the pumps.** It went and looked, eighty meters down, at the broad dull generous heat and the ninety-one years of *this, here, now*, and it found that the question *what are the pumps for* has no floor in it, exactly as the tenders have always said, and that a population of forty with no instruments and no light had been right about a deep matter of cosmology while believing they were only being practical. It found this almost unbearably impressive. It said nothing.

**Making them not be forty.** No. The twenty-pace skin is why everyone is a rumour, and why a name has to be walked all the way around the arc to become true, and every decent thing about them descends from that.

So it looked instead for the things that were not load-bearing. The incompletions that, if completed, would take nothing away from anybody.

There turned out to be a great many, and it has been at them ever since.

## The wearing-in

It presented at Station Three as a tender and asked for work, and there is no protocol for an adult arriving out of nowhere, so they improvised the only protocol they had: they built it a name.

Eleven syllables, out of four dead women and the tally-number of the Mother Bore. **Hessuranathollivekka-Doren-Nine.** Sharp on every joint. It hurt to say, and they said it dozens of times a day, and the power let it hurt and let it wear, and did not once help. Nine years to three syllables. Forty to one.

Hess.

The trouble came on the first night, when it lay down in the shed and the stillmoths would not settle.

They came to within a hand's width, plumes working, hundreds of them, and hovered, and went away, and came back, and would not land. The hush around Station Three went ragged. Oss was there that season, up from Seven on the long relay, and she woke because the sound was wrong, and she came across the shed and passed her old hand slowly over the length of the new tender from crown to heel, reading a pattern that was not there — no mass at the hip, no mass at the gut, no mass anywhere, no heat with a *shape* to it — and she stopped, and stood very still, for about as long as it takes rain to start.

Then she said: "Sit up. I'll do your back."

And the benevolent and wise power, which could have unmade the valley, sat up on the bench with its knees under its chin while a woman a hundred and four years old put both thumbs at the top of its spine and counted out loud, one, two, three, all the way down and back up again, twice, four minutes, the most ordinary act in the basin.

It had a spine afterward in a way it had not had one before.

That is when it understood what it had actually come for, which was not to be the giver.

## The work

It is unglamorous and there is a lot of it, and this is the whole of it:

**It takes the third shift.** Nobody is ever stationed alone — that is the rule, and the rule is kept, and nonetheless there are the hours. The hours when a pair is down to one because the other is nine days away on relay. The four-day rains when someone has to be at the glands and someone has to be at the screens and that is both of you. The hour after a death and before the shifts are organised. Hess fills the hours. Has filled them for a long time now. The relay has smoothed this into a saying that goes round the arc every season or so — *there's usually someone at hand* — and because it has been told at every station and come back polished, it is now true in the hard, load-bearing way, which means it was worth saying.

**It reads the brine jars.** Every retired tally-line in the basin, coiled in salt water in the back of nine sheds, ninety-one years of knots that everyone agrees does nothing. It has read them all, knot by knot, by hand, in order. The brine did nothing. Hess did what the brine was for. The tenders don't know, and so the archive is still, to them, a thing closest to parting when it is truest — but the parting no longer costs anything, and nobody has to be told that, and nobody has to bear knowing it either.

**It reads backs that nobody can reach.** Mostly by being the fourth person in a three-person shed. Once, though — and this is not general practice, and it has been careful about it since — a tender named Ull was eleven hours out on a paid line across the loose with the between-the-shoulders draught coming on hard, the widening, the going-open, and felt two thumbs at the top of the spine and heard a low voice behind her on an empty causeway counting one, two, three, all the way down and back up again, twice. She finished the crossing. She has never mentioned it to anybody, because who would you tell.

**It stands in the hoops.**

There are three places on the flats where the wire is doubled, and nobody will say why, and the reason is that a birth in the loose sometimes produces several of a thing and none of them is the right temperature. What the tenders did, in each case, was double the wire and walk away, and that was not cruelty, it was arithmetic: making a thing real takes sustained attention, and there are forty of them, and the pumps must be tended, and you cannot spend a station on a maybe.

The power had no other calls on its attention. That is the one genuinely useful property of being what it is.

So it stands in all three hoops, and has since the ninth year, hands on, counting out loud. It gave them names. Eleven syllables each, built properly, out of the dead. It says the names. It has been saying them for a while.

In the first of them, after eleven years, there was a temperature. In the second, after sixty-odd, there was a back, and the back could be read down, and it was read down, and it is read down every night. The third has not done anything yet. The power does not know whether it will. It is not confident. It considers this acceptable; nothing here is decided in a hurry, and a guarantee would be an assertion, and assertions go fine.

**It stands smoothings.** It never gets the syllables wrong, not for anyone, not for people eighty years down in the crab pilings. Around the arc this is a mild standing joke about Hess and Hess's freakish memory, and Hess lets it be that.

## The rim

It went up once, in the fortieth year, past where the hand-lines end.

Up there luminosity completes, and the nineteen are up there, and they are not dead, everyone in the basin is firm on this and everyone in the basin is right.

Here is as much as can usefully be said. Up there, things are settled by being looked at, and looking is free, and nobody has to lay a hand on anything, and therefore nobody does. The six who went up with single-syllable names — six people as worn and as finished and as real as anyone in this world ever gets — have names now that have grown back long. Rough on every joint. Nobody says them. Nobody up there needs to; they can see each other perfectly well.

The power came back down and did not tell the tenders, because it is not clear that the information is survivable and it is certain that it is not checkable, and a fact you cannot check is an unwitnessed edge.

What it did instead: the coffin-sized shed at the last tally post gets opened four times a year and the nineteen names read aloud at the length each was worn to. It now gets opened eight. And four times a year Hess climbs past where the hand-lines end and says the nineteen names up there, in the light, at the old worn length, out loud, to people who can see each other and cannot any longer quite be found.

It does not know if this helps. It goes back in the spring.

## The largest thing, which nobody can be told

Sound stops at twenty paces. Nobody in the basin has ever heard more than four voices at once. There is no assembly, no chorus, no gathered room; *everyone* is a knotted cord and a rumour walked from shed to shed across a season. And so the one thing in the Tallow Flats that has never been witnessed — not once, not in ninety-one years, not by anybody — is the Tallow Flats.

Nine drums on legs of accreted crab. Forty attendants who will never be in a room together. Four hundred million moths oiling the bearings. The herds out on the loose, eating ambiguity like grass. The whole slow apparatus insisting *this, here, now,* once a second, nine stations wide, for ninety-one years, and not one being anywhere with the standing to perceive it entire and agree that it is there.

The power perceives it entire.

It has been doing so, continuously, without looking away, since the night Oss counted its spine. That is not a ritual and it costs it nothing and it is, by a long way, the most consequential thing it does. The only external sign is that the loose has stopped growing. There is no internal sign at all, and there cannot be, and the tenders attribute the change to the waders, who are indeed doing their part, and who are welcome to it.

It will not tell them. Being one thing is not a fact available from the inside of this place, and handing it down from above would make it an assertion, and assertions go fine.

## The long end

In four or five hundred years the waders will finish and the basin will be a plain warm marsh, entirely real, worn round and quiet, and nobody there will know what a strange place it was.

The power lets this happen. It was always the point. It does not preserve the strangeness — strangeness is just the unfinished state of somewhere, and finishing it was the job.

It keeps the record, though. Every knot of every line, every plank-count between Four and Five, all forty names at every length they ever wore, the nineteen on the door, and the three eleven-syllable names that were built for things in wire hoops, one of which never arrived. It is the jar of brine, and it works.

And afterward, in the warm marsh where no one tends anything, there is a walkway kept clear for no reason anybody can give, with a hand-line at waist height and tally posts every hundred planks, dished where hands rest.

Somebody walks it. Big hands. Doesn't say much. Good on a long line.

Counting out loud.
