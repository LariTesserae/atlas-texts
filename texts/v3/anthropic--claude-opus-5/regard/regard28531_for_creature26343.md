# The Attention

## 1. The manner of its arrival

The first thing it did was work out how to come in.

There were forty doors and all of them were expensive. To descend through a doline would have meant standing in the one place in three hundred kilometres where standing is a form of theft — occupying a light-shaft, throwing a shadow across an ellipse of garden that has been contested by root and stalk and epiphyte for eleven thousand years, taking, for however long the visit lasted, the single most valuable commodity in the basin and converting it into nothing. It considered this and declined.

It could have entered as weather, as a pressure change, since the basin is already permeable to that; every breath the outside world takes goes down those throats. But a pressure change here is a note, and the notes beat against each other, and the throb of that beating is felt in the sternum of everything that has a sternum. To arrive as weather would have been to arrive as a voice in a language it had not yet learned, saying something it had not chosen to say.

So it came in low, out of the pale country, on foot, at a walking pace, over eight days, from the dry margin where the guano-fans thin out and the crust goes to bare pavement — the least valuable ground in the basin, the ground nothing crosses because there is nothing on it to cross to. It displaced no air that the floor could feel. It left no print in the calcite flour, which is not the same as saying it was weightless; it means only that it had understood, before it took the first step, that the floor is a document and had decided not to write on it until it knew what was already written.

It has been noted elsewhere that this is what wisdom looks like from outside: a great deal of prior consideration, expressed as a very small entrance.

## 2. The census of mercies

Then it stood in the dim, in the noise, and made the list. Everything that could be done, and the cost of doing it.

*The river.* It could put the river back. This was the largest and most obvious of the mercies, and it took the least time to reject. Three hundred kilometres of level pavement, a ceiling arched two hundred metres up on hourglassed pillars — a river here would not be an abundance, it would be a demolition. The mounds are cemented dust. The dead ones are hills of dust. The scent-roads are dust. The forty thousand cisterns hanging in their brackets, holding sixty litres between them, would be swept into a volume of water so large that no organism in the basin possesses a category for it. There is nothing here that has evolved the capacity to be given water. Nothing here has a mouth shaped for a flood. It knew the taste of that error from a wetter world it had loved badly, long ago, and it did not repeat it.

*Rain.* The same objection, spread thin. Also: rain would smooth the floor. The footprints of the things that walked here when the mounds were young would go in a season. It weighed a hundred million years of legible record against the standing crop of one century's grazers, and found — this is worth saying plainly — that it was not sure. It set the question down unresolved and moved on, which is a thing wise powers do more often than they are credited with.

*More light.* Forty holes, and it could make four hundred. Forty gardens becoming four hundred gardens, the pale country greening, photosynthesis arriving in the dim like a decree. And every mound complex, whose entire spacing — nine kilometres of nothing between one square kilometre of city and the next — is a budget struck between foraging radius and available water, would find its budget void. Density here has been negotiated with extreme patience over a span that outlasts lineages. To hand the basin more light would be to hand it a boom, and a boom is a thing a place survives, if it survives, by losing most of what it had.

*Sight for the builders.* Sentimental. They have no use for it. Their world is a substrate and their language is the biting of walls; eyes would be a hole in the seal, a place to lose moisture from.

*Length of days for the founder.* She lays one egg every eleven seconds and has done so for between forty and two hundred years, blind, boneless, in a cell built around her when she was small. It could double that. It looked at what doubling would mean — the same cell, the same eleven seconds, four hundred years of it — and understood that it had been about to mistake duration for kindness because duration is what this place trades in.

*Silence.* It could quiet the basin. It could damp the forty throats and still the resonators and give the plain an hour of the sort of peace that, in almost every other place, is a gift. Here it would be a massacre. Silence is the emergency signal. An hour of basin-wide quiet would be read, correctly and instantly, by every animal on the floor as the largest hunting event in the history of the world, and they would freeze, all of them, everywhere, for an hour, and the ones that survived would go on freezing afterward at every lull for forty generations. It set this down as the most instructive item on the list: *the local meaning of my kindest instinct is terror.*

*The well-digger.* It could take the well-diggers out. Four to nine days at a flank, the colony amputating its own galleries and cementing behind itself, and then sixty litres — four hundred years of climbing, a gram and a half at a time — gone in a night and a half. It is the cruellest arithmetic in the basin. And the mounds do not die of it; they spend two centuries not dying of it, and the layer they lay down in those two centuries is thinner and denser than any other, and it is that layer, forty of them stacked, that makes the hills that new cities are built on. Remove the well-digger and you do not get a happier basin. You get one that stops going down.

At the end of the census it had a finding, and the finding was this: *every mercy available to me is a mercy in the wrong grammar.* Almost all of them were, at bottom, gifts of speed. And the entire achievement of the Underplain — the thing that had drawn its attention across whatever distance it crosses — is the conversion of scarcity into duration. Here, to be fast is to be a predator. The only fast thing on the plain is silence, and silence is hunting.

## 3. Looking for the one who lives here

It had come for a being. It went looking.

It considered the founder, on the grounds that she has lived somewhere longest and moved least, and found that she does not know there is anywhere to move to, and that this is not a deprivation to her but the shape of her whole self, the way the sealed cuticle is.

It considered the floor — the fungal net threading between the mound complexes, so old and so continuous that it is the largest organism in the basin — and found something enormous and patient and entirely without a point of view, an appetite the size of a country.

It considered the masonry. Two million individuals striking gallery faces with the flats of their heads, and the mound carrying every strike, and each mason picking its own thread out of the total hum by the feel of it in the legs. The building is the language, and the language grows a hand's width a century, which is the rate at which the sentence lengthens. Here, it thought, is the one thing in the Underplain that has syntax.

And it considered — briefly, and then for a long time — the one standing in the dust between two mound-hills in the grey light, who was on no schedule the place recognised, whose footprints the floor had already taken and would keep.

It made no ruling. What it concluded was smaller and more useful than a ruling: that this is a city with no one in it who knows it is a city, and that the deficiency in the basin — the only deficiency it could find that was not also a load-bearing wall — was not water, or light, or safety, or time. It was that nothing here had ever been *known*. Not one course of masonry, in fifty generations of compressed gallery-plan, had ever been read by anything capable of reading.

## 4. The problem of the mute bat

There is a temptation in this, and the basin names it.

In the upper galleries live the ones that have stopped shouting: large, long-winged, mute, faced mostly with membrane, enormous in the ear. They do not sound the world. They listen to the clatterers sounding it, take the echoes off targets other animals have illuminated, and then take the illuminators. A hunter that has given up asking questions and lives entirely on overheard answers.

It saw itself in that, immediately and without flinching. Pure witness, in a place like this, is not innocent. To come three hundred kilometres in order to *understand* and then leave, having spent nothing, altered nothing, risked nothing, and taken away a complete acoustic map of a world that had no idea it was being read — that is the mute bat's whole career, and it is a career, and it eats.

So: not nothing. One act, then. In the local grammar, at the local rate, and indistinguishable from what the place already does to itself.

## 5. What it did

**It stayed.** This came first and mattered most, and nothing in the basin could possibly have noticed it. It attended for four hundred and some years — not as a vigil, not waiting for anything, simply looking at the plain for a length of time that would register here as a look. It watched an ellipse of gold creep the width of a garden. It watched one mound wall thicken by four hands. It watched a scent-road out on the eastern flats fade to the edge of unreadability and be refreshed, twice, by animals whose great-grandmothers' great-grandmothers had laid it. Nobody had ever looked at this place at its own speed. That was the first real kindness and it consisted entirely of patience.

**It went with the ones who fell.** A hundred climbers leave through a doline when the breathing reverses; sixty reach the lip; thirty come back down with forty grams of the outside world in their guts. The others come off the overhang somewhere in four hundred metres of dark. It did not catch them. Catching them would have inflated the balance of trade, and the balance of trade is the reason the negotiated distance holds, and the negotiated distance is the reason any of this has lasted. It went down with them instead, each one, all the way, so that the fall was attended. And in the deep cells, at the hour when a founder's pheromone fails and the attendants stop stroking her and drift off to the new cell to stroke someone else, it stroked her. Hours of it. Nothing was changed. No system was perturbed by so much as a milligram. It is the only thing it did that had no consequences whatsoever, and it did it several million times.

**It never made a silence.** Not once, in four centuries. Every movement it made, it made inside noise. When it needed to do something that would have a sound, it waited for the exodus — for the ninety minutes when the column is going up through the shafts and the plain is a drum played from inside and no local hush can exist because nothing can be heard to stop. This is the discipline it kept most strictly, and it is the closest thing to reverence in the whole account.

**And it let a roof go.** In the north-west, nine kilometres from the nearest complex, there was a panel of ceiling with perhaps four thousand years left in it. It had a look at the four thousand years — at the ordinary geology of the thing, the fatigue lines, the way it would eventually fail on its own with nothing standing under it — and it brought the failure forward by three hundred and twelve years, and dropped it during a dusk exodus so that the noise of it went into the din and made no quiet anywhere.

The count of the dead: some thousands of crust-grazers; a stand of pink guano-fans; nine hundred-odd lodgers in the panel itself. It did not pretend that this was nothing. Wisdom includes the willingness to hold a cost you cannot design away and not look off to the side of it.

What it made: a shaft of grey-gold coming down at an angle, thick with dust, and a bright ellipse a few hundred metres wide on ground that had been dim since before there was anything with a spine. Within two hundred years, ferns. Within four, black trunks holding flat fans of leaf out into the column. Rope-roots came down from the lip. A mound went up on the eastern verge and then two more, and in ten generations — which in the reckoning of this place is *immediately* — there was a city under the new light, layered, contested, tallest nearest the brightness, budgeting its density with the usual extreme patience, and no one in it, then or ever, knew that its light had been a decision.
