# The Third Author

It came the way anything sensible comes to the Sill: from below, up the warm stream that stinks of eggs and iron, and then it stopped at the lip of the gorge for eleven years.

That is the first thing to understand about it. It could have arrived in an instant, in glory, with the cirque ringing. It had done such things elsewhere and had learned what they cost. Here it stood at the mouth of the gorge through eleven cycles of frost and thaw and did nothing but watch and listen, because the place it had come to attend announced its intentions in a very small voice, and because the local word for everyone outside is *the loud*, and it did not wish to be addressed by that word even silently, even accurately.

In those eleven years it learned the six hundred signs, including the four hundred it would never be permitted to use, being nobody's neighbour. It learned that the high register is for channel disputes and the hip register is for the truth. It learned the eleven acoustic seats and sat in each of them at night when they were empty, and heard the fan resolve into its chord, and understood that the shaded were not being pitied but employed. It learned the missing note in the music, and why it was missing.

And it never entirely settled the question of who, exactly, it had come for. The two hundred? The fan? The process? A wiser power than most, it declined to decide, and treated all three as one patient: one long-bodied animal made of water and stone and birds and hands, which had been growing in that notch for a very long time, keeping itself, closing itself, opening again eighty paces east.

## What it did not do

It did not silence the water, though it could have, and though the hush is the whole burden of their lives.

It did not stop the stone. To stop deposition on the Sill would be to stop the Sill; there would be a warm wet shelf full of dying birds and a town with nothing to become.

It did not open one sealed room. Not the one with the lamp still standing. Not the one with the dog.

It did not give them writing. They have a script — it is bodies, it is scald-maps and stone strings and the length of a name — and a technology that does not thicken and seal would have made all of that into decoration inside four generations.

It did not bring metal, or fuel, or a roof over the whole terrace to hold off the night frost, and so it did not abolish the one hour every morning when two hundred people kneel together and break ice with horn paddles, quietly, which is the closest thing they have to a liturgy and the reason they can stand each other.

And it did not carry them away.

This was the real temptation and it lasted longest. Nine days' travel west there is a broad warm valley with soft water, deep soil, hardwood, ore, and shade enough that nobody would ever have to ask for it. The power surveyed that valley in a night. It could have put them there entire, houses and looms and grandmothers, and half of them would have stopped dying of their own kidneys, and their children would have had shadows, and their children's children would have had names that stayed still.

It went and sat in the twelfth hollow — the one the town has never used, because the acoustics there are poor — and thought about the valley for two years.

What it concluded was this: that the Sill's people are not a problem with a solution, they are an argument the water is having with itself, and they are winning; that a gift which requires the recipient to become someone else is a purchase; and that in the warm valley, within a century, they would be the loud.

## The law it accepted

Instead it took the local law on itself, which is the only genuinely intelligent thing any power has ever done anywhere.

*Nothing here is eroded away.* The logic of the Sill is deposition, addition, encrustation. So the power permitted itself to add and forbade itself to take. It could lay down films. It could not lift anything off.

This bound it harder than it expected. It meant it could not scrape. The one act of subtraction on the whole Sill is the endless scraping of thresholds with horn and ground quartz, and it is the town's central labour, and the power watched them do it for four hundred years and never once put a hand to a doorway, because the only tool the Sill permits its people is the one it had sworn off. It found this difficult. It considered itself instructed by the difficulty.

Everything it ever did there, it did by lying something down.

**A shadow.** It has one; it is the only thing on the Sill that does. It began standing at noon where a child was working, and moved on before anyone could own the spot, and never twice in the same place in a season, so that no shade-right could be claimed and inherited and no one's debt of kindness was made worthless by an inexhaustible free supply. Within a century the children had a story about a shy animal that gives you cool and runs off if you thank it. That was the intended outcome. The story is better than the shade.

**A weed.** Bitter, low, silver-backed; it seeded itself at the edge of the willow grove over about forty years, so slowly that nobody could remember it arriving. The shrimp eat it. Children chew it on a dare and spit. What it does — nobody there knows this, and nobody ever will — is make the stones grow in flat plates instead of knots, so that they pass. The first agony still comes in the late teens. It is still an occasion; the stone is still washed and drilled and strung; you can still read a person's age off the string at their throat. The power removed nothing that anyone had made meaning from. It removed the size the stone can reach, which was the only part of the arrangement nobody had consented to.

Four generations on, the strings had got embarrassingly long and the town had run out of acoustic seats. They opened the twelfth hollow — the bad one — and argued for a year about whether it counted, and the second seat and the fifth seat, who had not spoken in nine years, discovered that they agreed it did not, and were so appalled at agreeing that they fell out again immediately on new grounds.

The power considered this its finest work and never told anyone.

**A bargain with the combers.** It could speak to them. This is not remarkable; they are not complicated; they want warmth, and shrimp-cake, and to be left alone with a good undercut. The town's shape has always been a negotiated document with two authors, one of whom could not be addressed. Now there were three signatures, and only two authors ever knew it. What the power asked for was very small: that the rims be undercut *earlier*, further back, so that when they fail they tick longer first. The dry beetle-tick behind a wall used to give three or four days. It gives nine now. The two-note whistle is still heard four times a year and nobody who hears it forgets it, but for a long time now it has been a whistle that finds the terrace already empty, the baskets already up, the grandmother already sitting somewhere else complaining about the view. They have no idea their luck changed. They have a hip-sign that means *fortunate for longer than is decent*, and they use it about the weather.

**A note.** This was the smallest thing it did and the one it liked best. Their music is written in negative, stepping around the frequencies where a failing rim would sound, and so their song has always had holes in it, and reaches for something it is not allowed to have. Over three hundred years the power adjusted the grain and bedding of the young stone across the whole fan — a film here, a film there, at the pace of a fingernail a month — until no rim on the Sill could tick in one particular pitch. Then it waited.

Nobody was taught anything. A woman singing over her hands after the ice-breaking simply did not stop where her mother had stopped. Her daughters kept the new note. It is now a hundred and ten years old and considered very ancient, and there is a school of thought that says it was always there and the old people were merely timid.

## The one night it spoke

Once it broke its own discipline.

There was a shaded woman in the fourth seat who carried six names, one for each vent that had raised and then abandoned her, and she was asked at feasts to recite them in order, which is to recite sixty years of the Sill's plumbing, and she pretended to be reluctant. She outlived everyone. At the end she outlived even the memory of her first name, because the vent it came from had died before anyone else now breathing was born, and a name with no one left to say it is a stone with no string.

The power came into her dome on the last night, and sat down at less than a forearm's distance, in the dark, which is the only place and posture in which speech is not obscene there. She had heard it for years. Blind people hear everything, and what she had noticed about it was the one thing that gave it away: on a shelf where every single object announces in advance what it intends to do, this thing had never once told her.

She said, in the mouth-language, murmured, close: *You are the only quiet thing here that isn't about to fail.*

And the power said her first name.

That is all it ever said aloud on the Sill, in four hundred years — three soft syllables belonging to a spring that stopped running in a year no one records. She was quiet for a while. Then she recited the other five in order, at conversational speed, badly, to an audience of one with unlimited time, and pretended to be reluctant, and died before the ice was broken.

## The ceremony

There was one gap it could not fill, and knew it could not, and waited for.

When a child stops fitting through the door of the room they were born in — when the threshold has come in a hand's width and they have gone out a hand's width — that is the end of childhood on the Sill, and there was no ceremony for it. Nothing. No feast, no cut hair, no sign. You went to the door in the summer, and you did not go in, and you went and did some work.

The power could not make a ceremony. A rite handed down by a power is a uniform. It could only stand in the light and cast the one shadow on the Sill, which is what it happened to be doing, near the young stone at the mouth of a half-sealed dome, at noon, on the day a girl called for the eastern vents came to her room and found the door had beaten her.

She sat in the cool for a while and thought. Young stone can be sawn; young stone is soft as cheese and hardens in the hand. Under the rules you may open a sealed room to look and not to take, and she had nothing to take and nowhere to look, so she pressed her right hand flat into the wet lip of the threshold, at the exact size it was on the last day it fit, and went off to carry shrimp-cake to the combers.
