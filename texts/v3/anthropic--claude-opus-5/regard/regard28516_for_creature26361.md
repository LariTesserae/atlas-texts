# The Visit

It comes the long way, on foot, because the alternative is knowing without walking, and knowing without walking is a kind of not-arriving. So it crosses half a day of peat and heather with its sleeve soaked to the elbow, and finds the place the way anyone finds it: by the absence of birds, then by the knock in the shin bone, too slow to be a pulse and indifferent to the climb.

At the lip it stops for a long time and does not speak, because at the lip speaking is not available.

---

## What it does not do

It does not quiet the shaft.

This is the first thing considered and the first thing set down. The noise is not an affliction laid over the Bellows; the noise is the Bellows working. The same weight of water that abolishes hearing is what holds the flue at four degrees and never colder, what keeps the frost off, what drives the salt haze up in summer and the damp up all year, one bead per stone per minute, forever. Silence the hole and inside a season the filmy ferns are gone — fronds one cell thick, no cuticle, no defence, the correct design for exactly one country and no other — and after them the shingles, and after them two million animals in a film of water ten microns deep who have never heard of the sea and would not survive being introduced.

A gift that would have to be paid for by the recipients is not a gift. It is a transfer.

It does not fence the opening. The hole announces itself the way a fire does; a fence would only be something for the moss to eat.

It does not restore the moth's ear. It finds the patch under no lens, the slack thickened skin where a tympanum used to listen for bats, and understands that giving it back its tuning here would be handing a creature a membrane and then tearing it, every nine to twelve seconds, for the rest of its five weeks. The instrument is not broken. The instrument has been correctly put away.

It does not stop the moths going up. This takes longer, because the going-up looks like the one clear grief in the shaft: a population that finds its mates by scent on a current that only ever descends, so that every animal in the flue is permanently receiving news from above and none from below, and climbs, and climbs, and the ones who reach the lip are subtracted. But it counts forward, patiently, the way you have to, and finds that once in a great while the lip is reached on a still hour, and the moor does not kill her, and nine miles down the coast there is another wet dark hole in another headland with green in it, and that hole was colonised somehow. The climbing is not a death-drive. It is the only post the species is able to send. The power leaves the post office alone.

It does not give the eleven their eyes. Their line has been blind since before this coast had a shape; eyes would not be a restoration, they would be a wound with nothing to see out of it. It does not net them, count them twice, or improve their silt. It holds a light low over the pool for a while, at a distance that changes nothing, and settles the question that no one else has ever settled: eleven. It writes this nowhere. It is simply that the number is now known, which is the whole of what it can do for them and more than they will ever require.

It does not prevent the birds falling in.

This one it turns over for a long while, at the bottom, in the dark, on the rubble cone with the air screaming up through the gaps. A starling every eight or ten years; twice in memory a young fulmar. And there is a fly in this shaft whose larvae live nowhere else on the coast, asleep in the moss as eggs and as patience, waiting a decade for a mountain of nitrogen to arrive in a country that otherwise runs on stone-sweat. Mercy to the wren is famine to the fly, and famine to the fly is not mercy to anything, only a different subtraction with a nicer name on it. So it does not put out a hand.

What it does instead, when the day comes, is go down with the wren. Not to soften the flue, which cannot be softened, and not to make the floor other than it is. Only so that the fall is attended. This costs the Bellows nothing and is not visible in any survey of the shaft, and it is the whole of what benevolence is permitted here.

It does not rehang the rope, or draw the four pegs out of the crack before they finish splitting the stone they are locked into, or re-turf the hut, or relight anything. The marks on the stair are not abandoned. They are finished. A place that has arrived may be visited; it may not be improved. That is the third rule, and nobody made it for the power's benefit, and the power keeps it anyway.

And it does not summon anyone back. There is no argument in which coercing a family line down a stair for the sake of an office it has outgrown comes out as kindness. The words can be said in other rooms now. Fewer of them have to be said at all.

It does, once, arrange a fog to lift at the correct hour on a particular afternoon, some years hence, for one person walking that coast with something inside them that has no room to be said in. Not a summons. Only the absence of birds, made slightly easier to notice.

---

## What it does

It puts the wax in its ears.

It comes down at the lowest ebb of the smallest neap, in the dark, without light, navigating by the temperature of the stone and the direction of the draft, because the alternative is arriving in glory, and glory at the bottom of this shaft would burn a flat pale crust of liverwort that has held its square yard since before the basalt above it was worn into a lip. It uses the handholds. They are dished and softened and read as the fossil prints of hands, and they were cut by one pair at a time over eight hundred years, each adding a hold where the last one had slipped, and they are addressed to whoever comes next. The power consents to be whoever comes next. At the fourth turning, where the rock is wrong, it uses the rope, which is stiff and black and holds out of habit, and it holds.

At the ledge it finds the lump in the dry fold, kneads it warm, tears it in two, and presses it in — and this is the actual act, the one the rest hangs on. It makes itself deaf. It accepts the local law, which is that in this country hearing has no use and has been set down, and it does not exempt itself, because arriving somewhere and exempting yourself from the terms is not a visit, it is an inspection.

Then it does what the office was.

It fills the three bowls. One bead at a time, off the wall, water that has never touched sky, slowly enough that the filling is most of the visit — which was always the design. There is no one at the top to carry them to. A birth, a fever, a hull, a burial: all of those households are down to fallen walls and a line of stones in the crowberry. It carries the bowls up anyway at the end and pours them out over the wall of the nearest one, because the water was never the medicine. The descent was. And the descent, it turns out, does not need a patient at the top. The shaft was visited, once or twice a month, for eight hundred years, and then it was not, and the only thing the Bellows has lacked since is the visit. That is the single lack in the place that can be filled without changing anything in it.

And in the twenty minutes, at the bottom of the ebb, when the roar drops to a wet hollow breathing and a person could speak, it takes the wax out and says the names.

Everyone who ever came down. The one per generation who was chosen, or left with it, or simply the one who could stand it. It says them at speaking volume, aloud, in order, into the largest listening absence on the coast — not to record them, since the Bellows keeps nothing and that is precisely its virtue, but because a name said out loud one more time is very nearly the whole of what was ever wanted by anybody.

It does not add a revelation. A revelation would be a mark, and marks here are swallowed; there are four iron pegs and one slack membrane to prove it.

It has nineteen of its twenty minutes used. It spends the last one saying nothing.

---

## What it takes away

Only the words.

They were said into this room for eight hundred years on the understanding that nothing here hears: names of the dead, names of the living, the one sentence a marriage cannot survive, confession with no confessor, which is either the cleanest form or none at all. A power that can stand in this hour can stand in those hours, and so it hears all of it, every word ever spoken at the bottom of the Bellows, at speaking volume, in the dark.

And it hears them the way the wall hears them. This is the thing the moss taught it, and it is worth saying plainly that the moss taught it: sound arrives, is absorbed, is converted to a faint warmth, and is forgotten. No record, no weighing, no report. Absolution by absorption. Nothing was betrayed, because the terms were kept exactly — they spoke into an absence, and the absence turned out to have been a kindness, and the kindness kept nothing, which was the only promise ever made.

It leaves the tallow to the springtails. They have taken four human generations to get this far and they have the wick still to go, and it is theirs.

---

## What it leaves

It rolls the wax back into one lump, presses it into the dry fold behind the hut, and puts its thumbs in the two hollows on the way — in the same two hollows, which are already deeper than one lifetime of thumbs could make them, being the composite of six generations, or nine. The hollows are now a hair deeper.

That is the only mark, and it is indistinguishable from the others, deliberately.

---

## The appointment

It looks forward, as it can, along the ten thousand years in which the shaft will deepen by a fingernail, finish drinking its pegs, round its own edges and hold precisely to what it already is. It finds the day, a long way off, when the vein that feeds the pool sideways out of the old dark pinches shut, or the seaward wall goes, or the flue chokes and the breathing stops and the ferns are gone by evening.

It does not prevent that day. Places are allowed to be finished; that is not the same as being failed.

It only notes the date, the way you note the tide, so that when the water goes down out of the pool for the last time there will be someone standing on the rubble cone in the dark with wax in their ears, who knew there were eleven.
