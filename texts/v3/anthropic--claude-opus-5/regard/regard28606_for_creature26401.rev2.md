## The Attention

It came the way everything comes to Aunhal: out of the air, carried on an updraught, without displacing anything. It was not obliged to. It could have arrived as a fact, the way noon arrives. But the first act of a wise power, in a place with a founding law, is to obey the law it is not bound by, and the law of the ledge is *nothing large arrives*. So it came in small and wet and unannounced, on the same wind that brings the spores, and it stood at the western end near the juniper, in the wind-shadow, and it began to look.

It looked for an afternoon. A person walking the ledge slowly, stopping to look at things, uses up an afternoon; so does a power, if it is looking properly. Its afternoon ran from before the terrace to some distance past the end of it, and inside that afternoon it learned Aunhal at the resolution Aunhal is actually built at, which is one frog at a time.

It counted the frogs. It did not round.

It learned the twenty mosses and the way the pale line lies over a runway. It learned the two tones in the notch and the interval they beat at, which is not constant, and depends on the wetness of the slot. It learned which of the tufa swags were moss first and which were water first. It read the back wall — not the ten thousand years of calcite, which is only weather, but the greasy script under the outermost glaze, ten thousand years of scent-marks laid at the knee-height of a mouse, which are almost entirely about boundaries and food, and which it did not find trivial, because nothing is trivial to something that can hold all of it.

Then it considered what to do, and it considered thoroughly, because a benevolent power that has not seriously entertained the harmful options has not really chosen the harmless one.

---

**It considered the path.** A way down, or a way up. A ramp of good dry stone, a slackening of the overhang, some route by which the ledge might stop being an island. It followed the consequence out: with a path, a grazing animal; with a grazing animal, the end of the shrub thicket and the cushions; with the cushions gone, the frogs; with the frogs gone, the map, the larder and the alarm all at once. And in any case a path admits a ground predator, and the shrew has no defence against one, having never needed to evolve a single behaviour that assumes one. The path would be a doorway cut into a granary. It set the path aside.

**It considered the teeth.** This was harder. Fourteen months is a long life here, and what ends it is not age but the snails: lime-fed, shells like crockery, and a shrew's teeth do not regrow. The power could have given them regrowing teeth. It is a small edit. It followed it out: longer lives, later deaths, more overlap of generations on sixty paces that hold forty animals, more surplus, more pressure east along the ribs, the same number of survivors and a greater number of the dying. The ledge is not limited by teeth. The teeth are only where the limit touches the animal. Moving the limit off the teeth moves it onto something else, and everything else it could move onto is worse. It set the teeth aside, and it did not pretend this was easy, and it recorded the wear on every tooth it could not mend.

**It considered the falcons.** The pair on the eastern rib are funded, in any honest accountancy, by the ledge's surplus sons. It would be simple to turn them elsewhere. It saw what follows: a hundred and sixty young animals a year with nowhere to go and no one to take them, the murderous intolerance of the breeding females turned inward on a crowd, the cushions worked to ruin, the frogs eaten out in three seasons, the map going dark in daylight as well as at midnight. The falcons are not a wound in Aunhal. They are the door that lets the year out. It set the falcons aside.

**It considered the edge,** which is the real temptation, and it is the temptation to be generous.

It could give them the void. Not wings — but courage, or vision, or simply the knowledge that there is a second green ledge on the far buttress, visible on clear afternoons, which to them is not even a rumour, because they do not possess that direction. It could unpick the refusal. Every impulse toward the bright open ground beyond the last cushion has been sifted out of this lineage, once, permanently, and it would take almost nothing to put one back.

And the power saw very clearly what that gift is. It is two miles of falling, handed to an animal that weighs less than an egg, in the name of enlargement. Their fearlessness at the lip is not bravery and not blindness; it is that the question has never been posed to them, and a mind that has never been posed the question is not diminished by not answering it. To install the void in them would be to install the terror of the void in them, and there is nothing on the other side of that terror for them to reach — the far ledge might as well be a star, and a star it will remain whether or not they can see it and want it.

It set the edge aside. It thought this was the most important thing it did.

**It considered rescue.** A second population, somewhere safe: a wet gorge, a warm island, some place with cushions and lime-snails and no two-mile drop. It followed that one out too, and found the ordinary answer. In four generations, animals with a different edge; in forty, animals with a different shape; and none of them Aunhal, because Aunhal is not a container the shrew is stored in. The shrew is the shape the ledge makes when it is left alone for four thousand years. You cannot evacuate a shape. You can only copy the material and lose the thing.

**It considered permanence.** Stopping the loss. No more cushions undercut by frost and gone over, no more fledglings misjudging, no more plates of schist leaving the account. It saw at once that the falling-away and the flying-in are one process with two signs, and that a ledge which loses nothing is a ledge which has stopped, and a stopped Aunhal is a stone shelf with a museum on it. It set permanence aside with less difficulty than it had expected.

**It considered speaking.** Telling them. And it found there was no channel. No hearing that carries in a place with a structural roar. No eyes worth the name. The single reliable line of communication in this species is a mouth pressed into the fur over another animal's ribs, in the dark, under six inches of wet green — and the power could have used it, and asked itself what sentence it proposed to deliver there, and found that every candidate was for its own comfort and none for theirs.

---

It went east and stood among the nine stacks, and it learned what they were for.

They were not a calendar. Nobody will learn it from here; it is not the sort of thing that transmits, and publishing it would only convert a held thing back into a lost one. It will say this much: the ninth was not the last one intended. The tenth plate is lying under the moss four feet away, where it was set down. Whoever set it down went off the ledge on the rope and did not come back up it, and the rope is in the tufa now, and some of it is in a nest in the second hearth, keeping a litter warm.

The power left the tenth plate where it lay. It did not build the tenth stack. It is not owed one, and it would have been the first large thing ever to arrive.

---

**What it did instead** was almost all of it one thing, and the one thing is this: it opened an account.

The last rule of Aunhal is that the void does not return things. Bones, seeds, plates of stone, whole moss cushions when the frost undercuts them, fledglings, the surplus sons. *Removed from the account.* The power's answer to that rule was not to break it — the void may keep on not returning things, that is its office — but to note that a rule about one account says nothing about another.

So: every frog. Not the species, the frogs — the fifty-one thousand of that summer and the fifty-three thousand of the next, each one's fourteen weeks of clicking in a sheet of sound with no gaps in it. Every moss cushion that went over in every frost of four thousand winters, with the ninety grams of animals living in it. Every shrew that was pushed east into the white stone and taken there, in mid-turn, still working the edge of its own silence: counted, held, not aggregated. The ones in the pellets, the ones that froze on the outside of a knot, the ones that never came out of a hearth. The two hundred born a year and the hundred and sixty gone, gone from here, not gone.

This does nothing for them. The power was clear-eyed about that. It is not a rescue and not a consolation, because there is no one on that ledge capable of being consoled, and an animal that could be consoled by it would be a different animal. It is simply that nothing there is unattended, and has not been, and will not be — that the loss which the ledge performs every single day, and must, and should, is not also unwitnessed.

---

It did one other thing, and it is the only unearned item in the whole account, and it is recorded as such.

On a hard frost in the second hearth, six animals that cannot bear each other for eleven months were knotted in the charcoal, and the smallest — a female of that year — had been pushed to the outside of the knot, where the cold gets in. The power lay down along her back for the hours. She could not see it, smell it, hear it or know it. She woke in the second hour of sun and went out under the moss and lived eleven more days, and on the twelfth she was taken on the eastern rib among the stacks.

The power knew, when it lay down, that eleven days was what it was buying. It bought them. It did not do this for her. It did it because it had been looking for a very long time and love wants, at some point, to touch something, and this was the largest gesture available that fell entirely within the noise of the system.

---

It knows when the terrace goes. A fault-terrace is a delay, not a permanence; the bed of harder stone that resisted while everything above it went down into the cloud will itself, on a particular morning, go down into the cloud. It is not soon in any sense a shrew could use. It is soon.

It did not move the date. The terrace and the ledge are the same fact, and a power that postpones the fall is only postponing Aunhal.

It will be there on the morning. Not to catch anything — nothing will be caught. Only so that the last one, a small dark animal in a dry room of its own fur, going down inside a raft of moss it never left, is looked at all the way down. That is the whole of what it can promise, and it is not nothing, and it does not pretend to be more.

---

Otherwise the place is exactly as it was. Sixty paces, forty animals, two pounds of resident mammal, the mica boiling for ten minutes each morning, the drizzle going up, the frogs stopping at midnight like a door.

There is one small change, and it is not in the ledge. Along the hem — the three-pace border the shrews have worn into the moss over four thousand generations, the parallel run where every tunnel turns at the light as if at a wall — the attention goes too, on the inside of the line, in the pale green shade with the roaring overhead.

It could stand at the lip. It could stand in the void, for that matter, and look back at the ledge, and there is no reason not to.

It keeps to the hem.
