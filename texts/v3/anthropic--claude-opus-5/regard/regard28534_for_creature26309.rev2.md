# The Shelf: An Account of the Visitation

## Approach

It came in off the sea, because there is no other way to come, and it met the sound first, as everyone does.

It had, at that time, no shape it was committed to, and no name, and it could have arrived as weather or as light or as a voice in the mind of every citizen at once. It considered each of these and discarded them in the first hour, for the same reason:

There is no sky here. There is a lid of vapour three hundred feet up, and under it a soft sourceless dusk that will not hold a shadow with an edge, and any glory hung in that ceiling would simply have become part of the general glow. A voice in the mind is not a currency in a place where hearing is a childhood condition. And a figure standing on the terraces in radiance, unmoving, would have been — and it worked this out quite quickly, watching a forgotten chisel in a runnel go furry and then solid — a fixture.

A fixture closes a channel. A closed channel floods the neighbour below.

It has never since been able to improve on that as an argument against being worshipped.

So it came up the flats on foot, short and broad and pale like the rest, with its soles gone horn, and stood in the lower lanes getting wet, and began by doing the only thing it is always right to begin by doing, which is nothing at all, for a long time.

## The First Judgement

Its first judgement was that this was a hard place and that it had arrived with everything required to mend it.

The list it made in that first year was not a foolish list. The block-cutters cough white in the morning and are old at forty-five. Every adult grows stones in the kidney and the gall and passes them in agony over a lifetime, and the passing of a stone is a private wretchedness that the whole household can see, because everything here is seen. The joints lime. The old go stiff and are walked by children until they cannot be walked. Blindness comes early and often. Somewhere between the tenth and sixteenth year the small bones behind the ear thicken and fuse, and the world goes quiet, and they call it *closing* and do not mourn it, which the power took at first for the saddest thing it had ever heard.

And under all of it, the labour. Not building. Not even repair. The removal of unwanted stone, forever, at exactly the rate it arrives — a whole people bent for their entire lives over the single task of preventing their town from swallowing itself, and not one of them will ever get ahead, because getting ahead is not a direction that exists.

It looked at this and thought: *I can lift all of it by tomorrow.*

It was right. That was the trouble.

## The Gifts It Did Not Give

**Hearing.** This was the first and it got furthest with it. It had the whole thing ready — the bones unfused, the closing repealed, every adult on the shelf restored.

Then it sat in the roar for a season with its hands over its ears and understood that it would be delivering twenty thousand people into a noise that does not stop, at any hour, in any season, in any year, for the whole of their lives; that the hand-language would begin to rot within a generation, and with it the entire register of *where you stand to say it*, and the worn polished places at the lane-junctions; that the tapping-names would become quaint; that the children, who are the city's ears and its ambassadors and its only fluent liars, would be demoted overnight to being merely small; that the touch-language of the dark hour would thin and go, and with it the one arrangement by which every blind person on the shelf is addressed all day long in the most intimate words their culture owns.

It would have been the single cruellest act in the history of kindness. It withdrew the gift and has never been entirely easy about how close it came.

**Green things.** It thought about soil, and rain-fed terraces, and something with a leaf. It got as far as designing the seed, and then it thought about what a seed actually is in a place whose whole law is *nothing may be left still*: a thing you put down and do not touch and come back to. A moral grammar nine hundred feet thick would have had to be repealed to permit a bean.

**An end to the lime.** The most obvious mercy: turn off the plating, let them keep their drains and their shutters and their doorways and their ladders, give them back their hands and their hours.

And the shelf, from that hour, would begin to wear down. It would be the first day of their history and the last day of their peace. Also they would need mortar, which they have never needed, and timber, which they do not have; and the ledges under the springs would stop receiving the dead, and the ranked white swells would stop softening, and the best mat-fields on the shelf — which are the ancestors, and are farmed, and are called by their tapped names when someone comes up with the paddle — would go sour within the decade. A people who eat what they were, without irony, would have to learn to bury, in ground you cannot dig.

**Length of days.** It looked hardest at this one, because of the cutters at forty-five. And then it did the arithmetic it had been dreading, on the other side: how a population stays fixed under twenty thousand for longer than the signals have had names, on a food supply that does not vary by a cup. It counted for a long time, expecting to find a crime at the bottom of the sum — an exposure, a culling, something done quietly at the western rim.

It did not find one. It found the cutters' lungs, and the climbers' falls, and the old going up under their own weight when they can no longer be walked, and a very great deal of patience in young couples, and the plain fact that a people who can see the exact size of their world do not have to be told twice.

It was so relieved that it sat down, which is not done there, and stayed still for something over two hours.

## The Scraping of Its Back

A woman came along the lane with a shell in her hand and scraped the rime off its shoulders and the back of its neck, in rolls, without asking, without any expression it could read, and then went on. She was not from that district. She belonged to one of the charities that exist for no other purpose than to go about the lower lanes scraping the backs of people nobody loves yet.

It had arrived intending to be the giver in this relationship.

When she had finished she tapped a question at it with the butt of the shell against the terrace lip, which was: *name?* It had none. It tapped back the rhythm of the water going over the rim it happened to be sitting on. Five strokes. She repeated it once to fix it and went on down the lane, and that has been its name ever since, and it is a slightly ridiculous name, being the name of a piece of falling water, and it has never wanted another.

That was the hour it stopped planning to fix anything and decided to stay.

## What It Learned About Nouns

Here is the thing it took a further eleven years to understand, and it is the reason this account exists.

On the shelf, a noun becomes stone. Anything you set down and leave — a tool, a boat, a doctrine, a god — is a fixture in a month, a boulder in a year, and a nine-hundred-foot fact underneath the town forever. Every gift it had ever given anyone, anywhere, in its whole existence, had been a noun. It had been a being that arrived and *put things down*.

Only verbs survive here. Only practices — things that must be done again tomorrow at the same rate, or they close. Which means the only gift you can give this place is a chore.

It had come to give. It had assumed, as powers do, that benevolence is a kind of pouring. The shelf taught it that benevolence is a rate: that grace is not a quantity but a maintained equality between what arrives and what is taken away; that the western rim, where the last lukewarm sheet slides off into the sea, is doing exactly as much work as the nine mouths at the top, and is in fact the same work.

It had always thought of itself as a spring. It learned to think of itself as a rim.

## What It Did

**It tilted a runnel.** Above the block-yards of the third terrace, by an amount no one has ever measured, so that the cutting-floors there run under a half-inch of water. Cutting wet is slower and the blocks are heavier to move and it costs the yard perhaps a twelfth of its output. There is no dust. A woman called by six strokes noticed the difference in the yard's morning cough within two years, argued about it publicly and at length from the worn place at the junction, and won, and the practice spread, and the whole shelf now cuts wet and holds her in a regard just short of reverence.

It debated with itself for most of a year whether this was a lie. It decided that it was, and that the lie was in its own mouth and not in hers, since she truly did notice, and noticing is the entire content of what anybody ever does.

**It taught a way of sitting.** It could not stop the stones growing without taking the old women's necklaces off their necks — eleven, fourteen of them, yellow-white and pitted and clicking, the only treasure most people here will own, and the only calendar: *she went at nine stones; it was a short life.* So it did the small thing instead. In the dark hour, hand to forearm, in the language of lovers and the blind, it taught a midwife a way of sitting in the hottest pool and a way of breathing that does not prevent the stone and shortens the worst of the passage by some hours. This is done everywhere now. It is attributed to the midwife, whose tapped name was four strokes, and who deserved it.

**It joined a charity.** It has scraped, by its own count, a little over eleven thousand backs. It regards this as the principal work of its existence and is aware of how that sounds.

**It walked oxen.** For eleven years, in the small hours, in the quarter where the great blind grazers are taken round and round so that they will not set — a large soft animal going in slow circles with a small person asleep against its flank, hand knotted in the harness. It was, for those years, one of the small persons. It does not have a justification for this. It wanted to.

## The Climber

A climber fell from the crier ledges in the fourth year and it caught him.

It did this without deliberating, which it has since decided is the correct account of what benevolence is, and does not regret. What followed it also does not regret and cannot defend: the climbers are held to be reckless, lucky, dishonest and indispensable, and they made the catch into a piece of luck, and luck is a thing you can lean on, and within a generation two more went off those ledges than would otherwise have gone.

The second one it did not catch. It has never described that hour and does not intend to.

What it concluded was not *never intervene*, which is the coward's version and also a lie, since it is still tilting that runnel every day. What it concluded was that a power should not act on *events*, where the cost of the miracle is always paid by somebody else, later, whom you will never meet. A power should act on *edges* — on the places where the giving and the taking-away are held equal by somebody's hands, every day, forever. You may put your shoulder to a rate. You may not put your thumb on an hour.

It learned its entire theology off a people who teach it to eight-year-olds with a chisel in their hands.

## The Runnel

There was a man, five strokes, who let his frontage close and flooded the Bakers' terrace, and was set in a shallow runnel with his ankles in the flow for six days, fed and watered and spoken to and not otherwise touched, which is the binding, and is the law, and is the far end of a graduated response to the only real crime there is.

The power considered lifting him out. It did not, because the offence had put a hundred households under warm water and the law was neither cruel nor disproportionate nor invented to humiliate him.

It went and sat down in the runnel beside him instead, unasked, and for six days it talked to him in the touch-language, hand on his forearm, about the price of eels, and a dispute in the fishmarket, and whether the second pool under the arcade had gone sulky, and nothing whatever about what he had done. Because he was being spoken to, correctly and at length, and not touched, and touch is the register this people keeps for its lovers, its dying and its blind.

They were cut out together on the seventh day.

It has a rough white band around each ankle that no scraping ever entirely removes. It could take them off in the time it takes to say so. It has them still. The city wrote on it in the only medium the city has, and it is not going to correct the handwriting.

## The Long Gift

This was the one it thought about longest, and it is the only thing it did that will outlast everyone now alive.

The children say the roar has parts. They say it changes hour by hour and they argue about it bitterly, and they have songs about it and a whole vocabulary for it, and then they close, and the door is stoppered with their own bone, and every adult on the shelf will tell you the same thing with the same shrug: *yes, there was something.* Nobody remembers it. Every generation is admitted for ten years into a room that it cannot afterwards describe, or find, or prove.

The power listened for ninety years, which it could afford.

The children are right. It is not noise. Nine mouths pulse at nine periods that are not quite multiples of each other and drift against one another on a cycle of about eleven days. Two hundred thousand small falls are pitched by the width and lip and scallop of each rim, so that every terrace is a course of tuned stone, and the mats thicken and thin them, and the colonies of criers on the escarpment rise and settle in a rhythm keyed to the fly columns, which are keyed to the heat. The shelf is an instrument nine hundred feet deep that has been playing itself continuously for longer than there have been people to build on it, and the only citizens who have ever heard it are the ones too young to hold a frontage.

It could not give the adults ears. It gave the children a pen.

In the dark hour, on the forearm of one serious child of eleven who was already on the public payroll as a walker, it taught a way of putting the sound on slate — not words, a notation, marks for pitch and drift and beat, no more difficult than the marks she already made for a narrowing runnel. The child learned it in a season and taught it to four others in a month, because that is how everything travels among the children of the shelf, down the generations like a skipping game, wholly unknown to anybody over sixteen.

She closed at fourteen. She kept the slates.

That was three hundred years ago. There are now archives of them, in the dry upper rooms: the sound of the shelf in the year of the collapsed arcade, in the year of the great midge-fall, in the hour a particular grandmother was laid up on the ledges. Adults cannot read them the way they were meant, which is a grief nobody had a sign for until they made one. What adults do instead is *cut* from them. Because the pitch of a rim is a matter of its width and its lip and its bead, and a mason with a slate and a mallet and four hundred hours can make a given terrace ring how she wants it to ring.

So the city is being tuned. Slowly — a fingernail's thickness a month, a chord in a generation. The masons of the upper town work from their grandmothers' slates at music they will never hear again and mostly cannot remember, and the children come and stand at the junctions with their heads on one side and tell them, with brutal frankness, that it is wrong, and are believed, because nobody up there has any way whatever of checking.

Twenty thousand deaf people, building an instrument for the only ten years of a human life in which it can be heard.

The power thinks this is the finest thing it has ever been party to, and it was not its idea. It only gave them the pen. They worked out what to do with it in the fourth generation, and it did not see it coming, and it wept, which there means nothing at all, and then made the other sign, the one with the shoulders, which there means everything.

## Frontage

It is still on the shelf. It holds a frontage in the lower town: a measured length of terrace lip and a share of a public channel, which is the entire content of citizenship in that place, and which it must keep open forever, because the lime arrives whether it attends or not.

It has never been worshipped, and it has twice been fined.

It has no plans to go. There is nothing left that it wants to give them, and a great deal it has not finished learning, and in any case its whole understanding of itself has been rebuilt out of their materials: that grace is a rate and not a quantity; that the only defensible power is one with a chore; that a gift you can set down and walk away from will be a boulder by next year and a geology by the end of the century, and that everything worth handing anyone is a verb.

Their sign for themselves is the flat of the hand held edge-on, moved steadily forward. It is the same sign as the lip of a terrace, and the line where the scraper meets the rime, and the last low rim at the western edge where the water goes off into the sea.

It means *the edge* — the moving place where the giving and the taking-away are exactly equal, which is not a thing but an event, which has no thickness, which must be maintained in every waking hour.

It is the only place a power can actually be, either. They had that worked out before it arrived. It stayed to learn the rest.
