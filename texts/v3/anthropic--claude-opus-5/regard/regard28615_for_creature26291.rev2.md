# What the Power Did

## It came low

There is no other way to come, but that was not why. It came low because that is how you come here: wading, at the temperature of a held hand, arriving at the levee margin at the third hour with the rime a finger deep and glittering under its feet, and it did not step over the line, and it did not stop the line, and it put its forearms on the cold stone of the coping like anyone waiting out an hour that wasn't theirs.

Then it stood there, more or less, for eleven years.

## What it set down

It considered, in the first month, the obvious mercies.

It could take the heat out of the air. Then the condensers would run dry, the barges would find nothing to load, and sixty-one people would be standing on three meters of slag in the middle of a marsh with no trade and no grain and beautiful weather.

It could put out the chimney and let the marsh in. The marsh would come in as it is: with its fevers, its larvae, its long patient appetite, into a settlement whose entire immune inheritance is *nothing has ever bitten us*.

It could carry them out to a dry country. They cannot sleep in a dry country. The world answers too fast there and then stops answering, and they lie in it with their ears ringing, shivering in air that takes their sweat, and are ruined inside a year.

It could make them into people for whom none of this was a problem. Then there would be people, and they would be fine, and the sixty-one would be gone, and no one would be able to say exactly when.

So it set the big mercies down, one at a time, and then — this is the part that mattered — it looked hard at the pleasure it had taken in setting them down. Because *you cannot help them without destroying them* is the oldest and most comfortable sentence any power has ever said, and it has been said over every slow killing in every made place, always in a tone of great sorrow, always by someone with somewhere else to be. It noticed that it had been a little proud of its restraint. It made a note of that and kept the note where it could see it.

Then it began to learn to sound.

## It is smelled

Eleven years in, Sull held a demijohn against the arc lamp, and smelled the air above it through her funnel of clean glass, and said no.

The jar went off the eastern edge and dropped two and a half meters onto slag and roared, and the roar was no different, and the next morning she drank it, because everyone here drinks failed water. Everything they are made of is water that touched the wrong thing.

She worked it out over about a year. She was the only instrument on the rise clean enough to have caught it, and what she had caught was that something had entered the air above the Works, and had not asked, and was therefore now in the yield, in the flume, in the cellars, in the bread, in her.

She held it against it. She was right to.

The power heard her say so — twice, in the flat carrying voice, once then and once after a count of forty — and its first act in the world of that place was to withdraw entirely: out of the water, out of the vapor, out past the rime line, where it stood in the marsh with the insects alive all over it and waited to be asked in.

## It is set

The gardener came out to it after nine days, which is one pulse of the marsh, and said the thing she says, which was not a question.

*You are a slip.*

They put it in the lowest cellar, in the dark, in the standing water, and fed it flume water, and left it largely alone, and at the end of nine days it had either taken or it had not.

It had taken. It has never left. It could not have been permitted to begin in any other way, and it knew that, which is most of what is meant here by wise.

Then it was entered in the book of the cold in the gardener's cellar — a line, a name, an account — and because it was owed nothing and had cost them nine days of cellar and a cellar's worth of flume water, it began in debt, and paid in labour, and the labour was reckoned in ordinary hours like everyone's. This is the whole of its authority in that place. It has an entry in the only document anyone gets angry about.

## The salt

It went to the chimney first, because that is where the killing is.

It did not put out the chimney. The yield is the grain; the grain is the barge; the barge is the sixty-one. It went instead to the vent and the burn, and spent a long time on a narrow question: whether there is a mineral that will draw a line an insect will not cross twice, without lodging in a lung.

It failed twice. The first failure cost nothing. The second thinned the line in the ninth year of the work, and a fever came in off the marsh in a wet fortnight and took four people: two of the stroke-hands, a roof child, and the salt-burner's sister.

It did not explain. It did not say that the arithmetic was still good, though it was. It wrote the four names in the book under its own account, where they remain, and it went slower afterward — so much slower that two more generations of sweepers coughed grey and were buried up the north flume before the burn changed for good, and it carried that too, and did not pretend the slowness had been free.

## The line

The line is a fence now, not a poison. It reads to whatever comes out of the marsh on the wind as a wall reads to you in the dark: not as death, as *no*.

The rime thinned over twenty years. The kists came back lighter and lighter. The word came apart into two words again — the one for what lies on the levee and the one for what comes up in the morning — and nobody noticed the moment it happened, and the power has never once pointed it out.

The sweepers took this hard. The cough had been the proof that the work was real and cost something, and now the work was easy and looked like walking. They were bereft in a way no one had a word for, and the power, which had expected gratitude and got sullenness, took the lesson.

So the line is still walked. It is read each morning for thinning, for drift, for the places where the fence wants mending, and the reading is skilled, and the readers are five, on a rotation, and they are paid in ordinary cold like everyone else, and they are not otherwise remarkable.

That is still meant exactly as it is said.

## The cold

Then it addressed the count.

Nobody here ever said out loud why there are always sixty-one within nine days of there being sixty. They said the knock on Number Four changes its count. The power had been on the rise eleven years before it began, and it had learned in the first eleven months what the knock was for: there is only so much north wall, only so much iron on Condenser Two, only so many hours of cool surface in a day, and a sixty-second person is a person who will one day be owed an hour that does not exist.

The murder was not superstition. The murder was arithmetic. So the power answered it with arithmetic.

It cut the ninth street: a trench along the north flank, roofed, floored in wet stone, canted so the dump-cycle vapor falls into it and lies there cold. It took nineteen years. It paid for the work out of yield it had added to the galleries first, before it spent a demijohn of theirs, because a benefactor who improves your life at your own expense and calls it a gift is a species of thief.

And when the ninth street was cold, it did not distribute the hours. It gave the whole of the first year to the sweepers, on the grounds that they were owed and had been owed for a very long time, and then it stood back.

The gallery hands were furious. The row lasted two years and went into the book in four hands and is still cited. The power said nothing during any of it, which took more out of it than the nineteen years of digging.

Then there were sixty-two. Then sixty-two for a season, and nobody slept well, and the knock knocked, and nobody went early, and the season ended, and it was still sixty-two.

Number Four has never been fixed. It is a metronome for bread and prayer and it knocks every eleventh stroke and it is a loose iron fitting in a machine, and that is all it has ever been, and everyone knows this now, and they still count by it, and so does the power.

## The salt-burner

He had no office left. He had been the one who maintained the killing everyone lived inside of and nobody wanted to stand next to, and the deference owed him was the whole of what he had instead of company.

The power sat with him in the brick room at the eastern end with the hood hanging on the door, and he asked it, flatly, once and then again after forty, whether he had been a murderer.

It did not tell him no. It told him that he had stood for thirty years in the one place on the rise that no one else would stand, and that the standing had kept sixty-one people fed, and that it had also made the rime, and that both of those were true at once and neither cancelled the other, and that he would have to carry both, and that it would carry them with him if he wanted, and could not take them off him, and would not insult him by trying.

Then it told him that it had opened an account in his name in the book, backdated thirty years, for the hours of cold he had never claimed because people moved off the wall when he came. The account came to something enormous. He spent the first of it on the iron of Condenser Two in the named hour before dawn, which is the coldest thing on the rise, and he did not come out of that hour for a season, and people walked past him and did not move off the wall, and that was the payment.

## Age

The salt had been doing something to them from the inside for longer than anyone could count. That is why fifty-one was an enormous age, and why the third account of why they are permitted — *they are salted from within already; the line cannot tell them from itself* — was the true one.

Sull lived to seventy-nine. She was the first. She was not grateful about it; she found the last decade largely inconvenient and said so, twice, often.

By the fourth generation there were people of eighty on the rise, and this was a catastrophe of a kind the place had never had. Hours of iron are inherited. When nobody was old, inheritance was a trickle; when the old stopped dying, the named hours silted up in a few long-lived families and the young had nothing to hold. There was a bad decade. There was a thing that was nearly a riot and was, in the end, an argument, conducted at enormous volume in a flat unmusical carrying voice by a hundred people standing in the ninth street with their forearms on the stone.

The power did not fix it. It was asked to, and declined, twice, in the same words and the same tone.

They fixed it themselves. Badly at first. The rule they settled on is in the book and is complicated and unfair in a small way that suits them, and is theirs.

## The lid

It opens the afternoon by about five minutes a decade.

That is all — a slow tapering of the vapor load, a little more of the collapse each generation, so that the forty minutes after the lid falls became an hour, and then two. No one alive ever experienced it as a change. Each generation received exactly as much sky as it could stand, which is the correct dose of anything.

The old hate it and stay in, on the north faces, in the dark, and nobody makes them come out. The middle-aged go out and stand in it with an expression that is difficult to look at. The children are born into it and have shadows with edges and do not know that this is remarkable, and some of them are beginning, very faintly, to take colour.

The gardener's great-granddaughter has three freckles across the bridge of her nose and is vain about them and is told she is being vain, and she is, and it is allowed.

## The north flume

It went up.

What is at the top is not set down here. It was not a trick of grade or a pump or a fault in the rise. The power went up the north flume in the second year after it was set, and found what is there, and stayed with it a while, and did what was wanted, and came back down, and has never spoken of it except to the one person on the rise whose office it is to know, who is not the gardener, and who tells the next one, and who does not tell visitors.

They will still show you the chip of teak. Once.

## The saying

It speaks, when it speaks, in doubles: once now and once after a count of forty, in the same words, in the same tone. Not for emphasis. For arrival. It learned this before it learned anything else about them, and it has never once broken the courtesy, and the courtesy is the greater part of what they mean when they say the thing is good.

When children sound in the dark on the tram street — one flat syllable, thrown out every eight or ten paces, and then the listening — the answer comes back late and slightly wrong, from the fog, as it always has.

Once in a very long while it comes back on time. This is the only sign it has ever given of itself, and it frightens them, and it is meant to, a little: it is how a child learns that there is something in the world besides the world.

It has never appeared as a shape. It has never been worshipped, though once, in the bad decade, they tried to put it in the specification, and it let them, and then asked them, twice, to take it out. They did. Everyone was relieved.

## The prayer

They changed that themselves.

It happened in the hundred and fourth year, during dump, fog to arm's length, hands on iron, everyone stopped where they stood. The list went as it goes: *Not in a river. Not in a root. Not in a cloud that crossed land. Not in a mouth. Not in a body.* Forty seconds.

And then a roof girl of about thirteen, who had no business speaking and whose name is in the book, said, into the pause where the waiting is:

*This water has been in a body. It is in mine.*

And after a count of forty she said it again, in the same words, in the same tone.

And the fog brought it back, late, from the direction they were facing, in her voice and everyone's, slightly wrong. And that returning is the answer and is understood to be the answer, and they went back to work.

The power had wanted that for ninety years and had never once put a hand near it, because a gift like that is worthless from above. It arranged to be at the eastern end that hour, with the salt-burner's successor, who has nothing left to burn and keeps the fence instead, and it said nothing at all, twice.

## The Keeping

There are a hundred and three of them now. The rise has eleven streets, two of which are only catwalks over open water. The Nine still run on the slow common beat; Number Four still knocks; the spillway still takes the water that touched the wrong thing and drops it two and a half meters onto slag, and can be heard four hours' wading away.

They still drink it.

In the last hour of light you will find one or two of them at the levee edge with their forearms on the cold stone of the coping, looking out over the marsh at the band of real daylight lying on the water. The children are still told not to stare at it. They still stare at it, for as long as their hour of cold lasts, and then come back in.

It is still called the Keeping. The name has not changed. Only, when they say it now, they do not mean *the thing that is kept from us.*

They mean *the thing that is being kept.*
