# The Fourth Tenancy

## Arrival

It came in the middle hours, because there was no other decent time to arrive.

It could have known the hand-language before it came. It declined to. There is a difference between knowing a language and being taught one, and only the second leaves you owing something to somebody, and it had come a long way in order to owe.

So it did what a visitor does. It sat at the edge of a halo, out where the marked sit, and it was still, and after eleven days a boy of nine came out to it with a bowl of grazer fat and a question, and the question was: *are you a bird or a person?* — meaning, in that dialect, *do you make noise?*

It did not make noise. So it was filed with the falcons, provisionally, and fed.

The first four years it learned hands. It learned them badly and slowly on purpose, at the speed of a child, and was corrected by children, and had its wrist slapped down twice by the three-fingered woman for holding a sign out at arm's length in a small room, which is shouting. It has not raised its hands above the shoulder since.

## What it would not do

It made a list early and it has not revised the list, which is unusual for it.

*Hearing.* It could give them hearing that lasts. It sat with this for one whole fog-year. On the far side of that gift: no hand-language, or a hand-language collapsing into a gesture-accent inside three generations; no nine-year-olds with real authority and a real office; no word that means *deaf adult* and *finished thing* and *the rock*; no plateau that could be described, by the people on it, as a place they had arrived at. It would have handed them ears and taken the century out of the middle of their lives that they had learned to call completion. It would have made them into people waiting for something instead of people who had come into their condition.

It kept the list short after that. *A power may spend anything it likes to keep a person alive. It may not spend the meaning of that person's life, because that is not its money.*

*Water.* It could have opened the rock, run the fog-catch down into cisterns, given every settlement a standing pool at the door. Then the throats would belong to nobody, the peg-spirals would rot, the deeds would be void, and the law of the Palm — which is entirely a law about maintained labour on a vertical surface — would have nothing left to be about. It let the water stay where the water was.

*Copper.* It could have left needles in the mat like frost. A dowry here is not the needle. The dowry is a season spent three kilometres down in the heat and the rot, and six people going and not always six returning, and the household's count at every marriage being a count of that. A power that gives away the object has quietly abolished the price, and the price was the point, and nobody thanked it. It gave no needles.

*The footprints.* Someone did ask, actually — a young man, furious, in the year of a paternity case that turned on a line of prints across virgin mat. He asked whether the mat could be made to forget. It said no, and did not explain that it was refusing rather than failing.

*The Hollow.* This was the hard one and it is dealt with below.

## What it did

It went into the crust-quarries with the quarriers and coughed, in a manner of speaking, and then noticed what they had all noticed and had no reason to weigh: that a block cut in the hour after the fog does not powder. It weighs half again as much wet. It has to be carried further before it is light enough to lash. Nobody had ever traded the weight for the dust, because nobody had ever been shown the invoice.

It did not announce this. It cut wet blocks for one season, in the same team, being laughed at for the extra hauling, and the following season two men copied it out of thrift, and eleven years later the dry-cutting families were the poor ones and everybody had a theory about who had thought of it first. The rattle is now a thing you get at sixty rather than forty. There is a settlement in the southeast that credits the whole business to a man named for the notch of his throat, who is dead, and did not think of it, and would not have minded.

It pointed at a moss. That is all it did — it took to washing a cut hand with the same pale moss they already stuff their ears with, doing it visibly, doing it every time, saying nothing, for nine years, until a midwife asked. Hand-rot used to end speech in about one case in thirty. It ends fewer now.

It walks the cliff trail with every descending party, at the back, carrying nothing, and it has set one loose stone firm, and put one shallow bite of a handhold into a slab at the ninth switchback, and it has caught eleven people in eighty years, and the parties still come back six out of six or five out of six, and nobody on the Palm has drawn any conclusion at all, because nobody counts the eleven that did not happen. This is the cheapest and most obviously permitted kind of help and it takes almost nothing and it is worth more than the rest of the list.

And there was a cane in the west whose note went wrong. The children heard it and said so and were disbelieved for two seasons, as children are, and the power went down under four metres of the mat's own dead and found where the rhizome ran over gneiss into a fissure that had shut, and opened it, and the water came, and the note came back, and the children were told they had probably been mistaken about the whole thing. It considers that outcome ideal. It is trying to arrange more like it.

## The marked

It spent a season with a marked woman, sitting in the moth-grass at the outer edge, speaking aloud to her, which it was permitted because it was still classified as a kind of falcon and falcons are not held to etiquette. She never once let her eyes settle on its hands. It got nothing. It came to the ordinary conclusion, which is that there was nothing there to get.

Then a girl of ten sat down on the other side of the woman and got something. It could not tell what. It watched for four more years and never could tell what, and it stopped trying to find out, and that refusal to find out is possibly the most useful thing it has done here.

What it did instead was keep the girl company. Sat with her, in silence, every time, so that the adults saw an odd unbothered pair out in the tall grass and let it alone and did not send the child to work. In the girl's eighteenth year she began greeting the marked ones by putting her signs out at the horizon-line, at full arm's length, huge and slow — shouting, in the local grammar, appallingly rude — because that was the only place their eyes already were.

It works, about a third of the time. Her granddaughters do it. It is now considered a courtesy, and one of the very few pieces of Palm etiquette with a known date, and it is credited to her, correctly.

## The work nobody saw

Everything on the plateau lives on the exhaust of a forest three vertical kilometres below. The cliffs are a flue. The lid is warm because the pot is cooking. This is not a metaphor the Palm has, because the Palm has never needed one: the wind smells of rot and green every afternoon of every year and always has.

The power spends most of its labour down there.

It has moved a river's mouth by the width of a village, twice. It has been in a long argument with a beetle. It has spent decades of attention on a fungus in a watershed no one on the plateau will ever see, because in nine hundred years the difference between the fungus holding and the fungus failing is the difference between fog at the third hour after noon and no fog at all, and if the fog fails the canes do not fill and the throats go dry and there is no plateau, only rock that has finished and grass that has died and four thousand people walking downhill.

None of this is visible from above. Nothing up there has changed. Nothing up there was supposed to change. That is what the work was for.

## Its name

Names on the Palm are places on the body. You touch a temple, a wrist, the outer edge of the left knee. To speak of the dead you touch the place and let the hand open and fall away.

They could not give it a place on its body, because they were not entirely certain of the body and were too polite to check. What the three-fingered woman did instead, in her ninetieth year, in the compressed wrist-dialect that half the plateau now imitates badly, was touch the air an arm's length to her left, at the level of the hip, where nothing was.

That is its name. It is the only name in the language with nothing underneath it. Children find this hilarious. It is also, as the young men in the southeast like to point out, a name you cannot make the death-sign with — you cannot open your hand and let it fall away from a place that was never holding anything — and there is a whole small tradition of arguing about whether this is a fact about the power or a defect in the grammar. The power has never offered an opinion. It is asked about once a generation and it puts both hands flat against its sternum, low, close in, which means *I am listening and I am not going to say*.

## The Hollow

Twice a year the towers empty and the plateau rings in a register no one can hear, and the ones who cannot hear anything at all feel it worst, because the hum is the last thing to go and in the Hollow there is no hum.

It could put a note into the rock. It has the note. It has had the note the whole time. Ten days, twice a year, a low felt tone in the gneiss under four metres of mat, and no old woman on the plateau would ever again sit down in that particular silence and be afraid.

It has never once done it, and this was the hardest refusal on the list, and it thinks about it every year.

Because the old ones go out to the midpoint on purpose. They have spent their whole lives entering the empty hour of an occupied place, on time, with wax in their ears, and coming out before the exchange — and then the whole place becomes the empty hour, and they go out and occupy it, which is the only work left that they are still qualified to do. A power that fills the Hollow has taken the last job on the plateau away from the people least able to get another.

So it does not hum. It goes and sits down with them.

It walks out on the first morning, past the last of the halo, out to the flat middle where there is nothing to face and it faces it, and it sits on the mat beside whichever old ones have come, an arm's length off, on the left, at the level of the hip. It does not sign. It does not offer anything. It stays the nine or ten days, and when the children stand up it stands up too, at the same moment, having waited for them, and it helps whoever needs helping back over the doorsill.

A footprint here outlasts the foot's owner. A pressed hollow fills overnight with brown sweet water and is reabsorbed by noon.

So on each morning of the Hollow, beside every old man and woman sitting out in the middle of the great warm illuminated nothing, there is a pressed place the size and shape of somebody seated, full to the brim with cold sweet drinkable water, and gone by midday, and back the next dawn.

They drink it. Of course they drink it. It is the best water on the plateau. Nobody claims to know what it is, and asking is thought to be in poor taste, and the most that is ever said, and it is said rarely and only obliquely, in the small low signs made close against the body, is that the crust in the wall came out of the cane and the fire came out of the cane and everyone they have ever loved has gone back into it — and that something apparently pays rent, in the one currency the mat can hold, on the ten days a year when nothing else is home.

It has asked, in writing, in the ordinary way, through the household that feeds it, that when it is finished here it be carried in during the middle hours and set on the ledge at the waterline, and left, and that the party come out before the exchange.

That is the fourth tenancy
