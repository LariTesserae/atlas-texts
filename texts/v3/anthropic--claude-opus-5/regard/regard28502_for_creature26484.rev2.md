## What the Power Did

**The first hundred years**

It did nothing.

Not out of caution, exactly, and not out of the sort of reverence that mistakes a hard life for a museum. It did nothing because the first true thing it understood about the basin took a long time to learn, and until it was learned, every kindness available was a kind of vandalism.

The thing it learned is this: in the Standing Water there is no private speech. The plant is the voice, and the plant carries everything to everybody. A word said here is said to two hundred people at once. Which means that a power which speaks — even once, even gently, even to say *be comforted* — does not offer anything. It legislates. It becomes the loudest node in the basin, and thereafter no woman can settle a stem dispute, no girl can refuse a pairing, no one can knock *not now* without wondering what the other voice in the wood thinks of it.

So the rule it made for itself was: **whatever is done here must be either invisible or inaudible, and it must be possible to refuse.**

That rule cost it nearly everything it wanted to do. It kept the rule.

**What it did not do**

It did not warm the water. The cold is the reason shoulders are greased by someone else's hands each morning, and the reason a working day is measured in bouts, and the reason there is so much complaining, and the complaining is one of the four or five kinds of love available here.

It did not sweeten the air in the nodes or multiply it. The scarcity of air is the reason there is a counting woman, and the counting woman is the reason there are two hundred people in a bowl that could hold four thousand badly. A power that gave them unlimited breath would have given them, within sixty years, a crowded basin, a softened cane, a slow toppling that did not stop, and then nothing. Generosity that does not do arithmetic is just a slower way of killing.

It did not raise the dead, or lower the water, or make the fibre strong when dry, or harden the joints of the old, or take the taste of the sour air out of the mouths of the stem-drunk.

This is the hardest paragraph, and it should be read slowly, because it is not a list of wise refusals. It is a list of children who will be deafened at eleven, and men who will be lame at forty-five, and a woman who will lie on a raft in twenty years beatified and thieving her grandchildren's decades, and none of them were helped. The power knows the names. It knows all the names; that comes later. A benevolent power that tells you its refusals were costless is not benevolent and is not telling you the truth.

What it did instead was smaller, and took longer, and was harder to see.

**The lip, which is a door**

Ten paces out on the polished stone, the basin falls off behind you and you stand in the enormous quiet, and nobody can hear you, and nobody can hear you being answered. The people of the One Stem have been using this fact for four hundred years for exactly one purpose: to say things that must not be overheard.

The power did not build a temple, announce itself, or ask for anything. It simply began to answer.

Rarely. Not always. Never to the first asking, and never where a knock could carry it back inside. If you walk out into the sheet-water and stand in the cold and say a thing aloud, you may be answered, and most people are not, and no one can prove any of it, and this is on purpose. There is no priesthood at the lip. There is no offering. There is a rumour, and the rumour is unstable, and the rumour is the most it is willing to be.

What it gives, when it gives, is almost always information. *Your column is soft at the fourth node and will come down before the rains; move the sleeping shelter.* *The child's fever will turn tonight; do not open a second stem for her.* *Your mate did not surface because the cord parted at the stone, not because he let go; you may stop thinking the other thing.* *No.*

It says *no* often. It says *no* to weather, to fish, to the fortunes of specific marriages, to every request that would put a thumb on the scale of who gets what in a town where everyone can hear everyone else's luck. A power that is known to be persuadable becomes a market, and the poor always lose the bidding.

Nobody has ever been given air.

**The one that was asked for**

Nine years ago a woman named by a rhythm of two-short-long walked out at dusk, a woman of sixty-one who had opened perhaps ninety children's ears in her life with a thorn and a great deal of ceremony, and asked whether there was another way.

The power said: yes.

It did not say it kindly and it did not soften what came after, which was the truth: that the way exists, that it can be taught in a season, that a child so opened would dive to forty metres and hear a whisper in air at twenty paces for the rest of her life. And then the rest of the truth, which is that within four generations the town would speak with its mouth, and the mallet-grammar would thin to a set of signals, and the holes in the grammar would close over, and the retired names would come back into use because nobody would feel them as holes any more, and an old woman lying tethered at night would hear nothing through the bone behind her ear, because her ear would be whole, and the water would be silent to her.

The woman stood in the sheet-water for a long time. Then she asked for it once, for one child — her stone-mate's granddaughter, born small in a bad year — and the power gave it, and asked nothing, and told her it would give it again to anyone who came out and asked, and that it would never offer.

The girl is nine. She dives with the rest. She hears her own people shouting and it hurts her, and she cannot say why, because to them it is simply how a sentence is said. She has begun going out to the lip alone in the afternoons and coming back with nothing said. She will probably walk out for good, at nineteen or twenty, over the northeast rim and down, lame and strong and able to hear.

The power knows this. It gave the gift anyway, because it was asked, and it intends to be on the road with her when she goes, and this is the honest shape of the thing: a good gift, correctly given, that will cost one specific person her home. The grandmother has not asked for a second child to be opened. She has not forbidden it either. She is sixty-one. The power is waiting with her, and has not once suggested what she ought to do.

**The counting woman**

This was the only approach the power made unasked, and it broke its own rule to make it, and it has never decided whether it was right.

It did not take the rods. It did not correct her tallies, or whisper which couple should be allowed a column, or relieve her of one hour of being disliked. It came and sat on the raft in the driest part of the high town, in no form at all, while she counted, and it stayed there most nights for eleven years until she died.

Once, late, it told her the truth about the number of the dead — that it is not *eleven or twelve thousand*, it is eleven thousand nine hundred and six — and she said, after a while, "That's a lot of stems," which is the most beautiful sentence anyone in the basin has ever said to it, and then she went back to her notching.

She was not thanked in her lifetime, and the power did not arrange for her to be. It only made sure that she was not alone in the arithmetic, which is the one part of that office nobody can see and nobody can share.

The rods passed to a man she had been shouting at patiently for a decade. He is bad at it and improving. The power sits with him too.

**The work that cannot be told as a story**

The northeast lip is being undercut.

Not by the sheet-water, which only polishes. Below and behind the polished stone there is a seam of softer rock taking water it was never meant to take, and in something under nine hundred years the lip would have gone, in a night, with a noise nobody in the bowl would have had time to name — a mile and a quarter of standing water leaving by one door, and the cane laid down on the silt in a single sweep, and the low town opened to the sky, and nothing afterwards but a wet grey bowl and a very great deal of dead frog.

The power spent a long while in the rock, which is not the kind of work that can be described, and set the seam.

Nobody will ever know. There will be no flood, and therefore no story about the flood not happening, and no shrine, and no gratitude, and no change whatsoever in anyone's afternoon. It is by a very long way the largest thing the power has done here, and it has precisely the weight, in the history of the Standing Water, of a word that was not said.

It did not make the lip eternal. Nothing here should be eternal; a basin that cannot end is not a basin, it is a sentence. It gave the stone about four thousand years, which is roughly a hundred and sixty generations of the town climbing after the water at a finger's width apiece, and which will end, and the power will be there at the ending, and will do what can be done then, which will not be much.

**The night outside the rim**

To knock a retired name is the worst thing you can do with a mallet. And after three generations the hole in the grammar closes and the rhythm is given to a new child and the person it was becomes, exactly and permanently, nobody.

So the power went out.

Past the lip, ten paces, twenty, out into the enormous cold silence that these people fear more than drowning — the silence that is the whole of their word for dying — and there, on the bare mountain where no sound can get back over the rim, it knocked the names of the dead.

Eleven thousand nine hundred and six. In order, oldest first, the ones from the low town whose doorways are under the silt and whose grandchildren's grandchildren are under the silt. Each rhythm once, correctly, at the speed a midwife would knock it. It took a night and most of the following day.

It does this now at intervals. It is not a rite; there is no one to attend it. It is simply the case that the rhythms exist somewhere, said properly, by someone who has them all by heart, and that the place where they are kept is the silence outside the bowl.

Four years ago a diver walked out at dusk with something on his mind and stopped, thirty paces down the polished stone, because at the very edge of what an opened ear can pick up there was knocking, from outside, where nothing is.

He went back in. He told nobody, because he could not have said it without four hundred metres of neighbours hearing, and because he did not want to be thought dry cord.

He is forty-two, and his joints are going, and he is less afraid than he was. He cannot explain it, and has stopped trying, and when his time comes he will not call it walking out. He has not found a better word. He has only stopped believing the old one is accurate.

**The heron**

They had already built a door for it, which it found humbling.

The grey heron stands on the rafts and does not move for hours and is lucky and is never harmed, and if it comes down on your rack and takes the whole afternoon's fish you say nothing, and the neighbours say nothing, and it is understood that something has been paid on the household's behalf.

So the power pays.

Not always, and not proportionally, and never in a way that can be traced back to the fish. A household that lost its drying rack to the heron in the spring is a household where the fever turned, or where the parting cord held the extra half-second, or where the man who was going to say the unforgivable thing over the wood was interrupted by a frog. The accounting is deliberately bad. If it were good, they would start feeding the bird on purpose, and then they would be a people with a god, instead of a people with a piece of luck and a courtesy about it.

It takes the fish, too. It eats them. Something has to be taken or the whole arrangement is charity, and charity in a basin this small is a debt, and a debt that cannot be repaid is a stone tied to the ankle of a town.

**Walking out**

They do not walk the exile out. They simply stop knocking for him, and one morning he is gone, and his rod is planed smooth and started again.

The power goes with him. Always; this one is not rare. Down the mountain road on splayed soft feet, four days to the first market, lame by the second. It cannot make a road kind to a man built for water. It does not carry him, or find him money, or bend the minds of lowlanders who call his people the wet people and laugh at the shouting.

What it did instead, sixty years ago, was arrange — slowly, by ordinary means, a marriage here and an inheritance there — that there is a house in the second market town with a wide shallow trough behind it, and a woman who keeps it, and who does not laugh, and who knows to store cordage wet. Some of them stop there. Some go past. A few have gone back up, years later, and stood at the lip and been let in again, because the town's rule about exile is fierce and the town's memory is not.

The stem-drunk usually do not go back. The power walks beside them anyway, the whole way, including the last part, and does not say the obvious thing about the air, because they know, and being told would only be one more voice in a life that has run out of quiet.

**What it wants**

It would like to be thanked. This is true and there is no point pretending otherwise; a benevolent power is not immune to wanting to be loved, it is only better than most at not collecting.

It would like, once, to knock into the wood at the north rim and be heard instantly at the south, to say one sentence to two hundred people at the same time — that the lip will hold, that the names are kept, that the arithmetic of the counting woman is correct and terrible and correct.

It will not. A god who knocks owns the language, and the language is the only thing these people have that belongs to all of them equally, and it was not made for a voice that cannot be argued with.

So it stays as it is. Unprovable, refusable, mostly silent, occasionally a bird.

At night the old ones sleep in the water, tethered at the ankle, greased, faces up under a sky that is very close and very hard, and the hollow wood ticks and knocks and does not finish settling, names and answers and somebody's birth-triplet away to the south. The power is in the water with them. It is not doing anything. It has learned that there is a difference between doing nothing and being there while nothing is done, and that the difference is most of what it came for.

It will not walk out before they do.
