## The Attention

What drew it was not the mountain.

Extruded domes are not rare. A power of any size has seen ground behave worse than this, and faster, and with less to show for it. What drew it was a girl of eleven lying face-down on a fresh cut face at dusk with her cheek against warm amber stone, holding still — holding *still*, in a world going up under her at five metres a year — in order to hear the rock making room for itself.

There is nothing in that. It is a child listening.

It came anyway.

## How It Came Up

It did not descend. Descent would have been a statement, and a statement made from above, in a city where the young are stationed at the summit and authority lives lower down, would have been misread in exactly the way that flatters the speaker.

So it came up the way everything comes up: on foot, from the plain, with the traders, and it arrived at the lower market wheezing, and was laughed at.

It let itself be laughed at for a long time. This was not humility as a performance. It genuinely needed the lesson, and the lesson is only available to the short of breath: that here, air is the currency of courtesy. It took it six years to learn to speak on the outbreath only, low, close to the ear. It took it eleven to stop interrupting — not the crude interruptions, which it never made, but the fine ones, the small helpful sounds a listener makes to show they are listening, which on the Rise take the air out of another person's mouth and are very nearly assault.

By the time it could sit properly in the crater of a conversation, it had been on the mountain nineteen years and risen ninety-odd metres, and had a birth-height on the census that was a lie, and a dented tin cup at its belt, and hands going grey at the knuckle.

It considered this the first half of the work. It was not being patient. Benevolence at the wrong speed is only weather.

## What It Set Aside

**The air.** It could have thickened the air. It stood with the question for most of a decade, at the elbows of seventy-year-olds resting their hands on pipe bundles and breathing like men carrying something full, and it wanted to, and it did not.

Because the whole moral architecture of the place is built on the cost of breath. The low voices. The pauses you can read to the second. The second language in the fingers, in which they say the things that matter most. The capacity — rare anywhere, and universal here — to sit with a grieving man inside a silence and not fill it. Give them air and none of that is destroyed, which is the trouble; it is *orphaned*. They keep the manners for two generations out of habit and lose them in the third, having no reason, and what they will have gained is the ability to shout.

There are peoples who would trade that. It did not think these people would, and it declined to make the trade on their behalf while they were busy.

**The rise itself.** It could have stopped the mountain. It is a matter of pressure and time and neither is difficult.

But they have no words for past and future. They have downhill and uphill. Their grief is a direction and their hope is a shortness of breath and their marriage contracts are written in metres. Stop the mountain and you have not given them rest; you have taken their tenses. They would still age and still die, only now downhill of nothing, in a place that had stopped meaning anything, holding a vocabulary that had become a set of dead idioms about slopes.

**Removal.** It could have taken them off, all of them, to a world with soil in it and rain that falls.

It has done this elsewhere. It does not discuss those places.

**The stone in the kidneys.** This one it did not set aside. But it did not begin there either, because it had noticed that the stones are drilled and strung and worn at the throat, that a woman with eleven is regarded with respect and horror, that there is an etiquette of sympathy around them refined to the point of art, and that when a person goes downhill the string goes with them as the only part of them made the way the mountain was made.

You do not walk into that with a cure in your hand. Not on the first day. Not on the four thousandth.

So it drank the water. Boiled, settled, passed through beds of crushed off-spec block, and still loaded, a hundred cups a day for sixty years, and somewhere in its forties, on schedule, its own small quarry works opened. It passed nine. It kept them. It wore them.

It wanted to know the size of the thing it intended to take away.

## The Terms It Set Itself

**Leave nothing that cannot rot.** In a world where a coin-scratch outlives grandchildren, where the abandoned quarters sit down there in the dry heat immaculate and appalling, the one gift a wise power must not give is another permanent object. Everything it left had to be perishable, or learnable, or a habit — something that could be dropped by a generation that no longer needed it, without leaving a monument to reproach them.

**Take no terrace.** Flat ground is contested here. Nothing that wanted worship could be allowed to exist, because worship needs a floor, and a floor here is somebody's bakery.

**Shim-scale, or nothing.** Two millimetres. A centimetre an hour. The levellers had already worked out the only ethical rate of change available on this mountain, and had built a guild and a priesthood and a vocabulary around it, and it saw no reason to imagine it knew better.

**Never be the one who does it.**

## The Question

It went down to the lower vent quarter and lived among the mat-scrapers for eleven years, and smelled of it, and was married among them, and ate the cake, which is genuinely awful.

And it noticed what everyone there had always known: that the scrapers get the stone late, and lightly, and some of them not at all. Their cisterns are lined with spent mat because spent mat is what they have instead of tile. The bad-green takes the load out of the water. It sits in the bottom of a cistern doing nothing anybody had ever thought to look at, quietly unmaking the disease of the entire city, at the bottom of the social order, for six generations, among people nobody had ever asked a question.

It did not bring them the cure. It brought them the question.

Then it carried the answer uphill, and gave the credit away correctly — to a scraper woman named in full, with her birth-height, in the hearing of the survey — and stood back while the gutter-wardens fought it and the stone-doctors fought it harder and the levellers, who think in the right units, took nineteen years to be convinced.

The beds are standard now on every terrace inside the collar. Children still get the stone; the water is not clean, only better. A life's yield is two or three where it used to be nine, and the strings are shorter, and the etiquette has survived as a courtesy whose origin nobody quite recalls, and a mat-scraper family lives in the warm dense middle of the mountain and marries out, and the cake is still awful.

That is the largest thing it did, and its own part in it was a sentence with a question mark at the end.

## The Bird

In the girl's fourteenth year a bird came down.

Not sent. Not arranged. A young one, off the thermal, exhausted or sick, folding onto a condenser fin in the fog collar in the pearl light with its enormous wings all wrong, and a lane's worth of people around it in a silence you could have leaned on — because these are the only things on the Rise that do not go up, that came from a world where things fall down, and nobody living had ever seen one on the ground.

It was standing there. It knew what to do and it could have done it beautifully.

It filled its cup, held it out at waist height where a hand could take it, and looked at nothing.

The girl took the cup and did the actual thing, and the bird drank, which no one had known they could, and sat on the fin for two days being watched in shifts, and went up.

There is now a second gesture. A small downward tilt of the chin, for which there is also no name, and which is not sorrow and not welcome, and which the young use more than the old. She was known for the rest of her life as the one who filled it, and told the story perhaps forty times, and never once mentioned who had been holding the cup, because she did not remember. There had been an old draper standing there. There is always an old draper standing there.

## The Trade It Kept

Of all of them it kept drapery.

Not levelling, which it could have done superbly and which rules. Drapery: the old man's work, going round the terraces with a hot chisel cutting the flowstone back off the doorways before it closes them, in the certain knowledge that it will grow again, in this doorway, in your lifetime, forever — the local emblem of futility and duty at once.

For forty-one years, in the warm continuous drizzle, it cut stone off doors. It trained apprentices and did not train them badly and did not train them into anything. It was not the best draper on the Rise; it took some care about that. When the terrace it worked dried out and went silent and the flowstone stopped growing there forever, it moved up two rings and started again on doorways that were still closing.

There is no lesson in this that it ever said out loud. But the people who worked beside it noticed that it was possible to do the pointless necessary thing for a whole life without bitterness, and several of them managed it afterwards, and told their apprentices nothing either.

## What It Could Not Do

A surveyor sent down below the collar to read an old benchmark walks through two hundred years of neighbours — seated in their own former rooms, salt-cured, leathery, doors open, strings in their laps, the coin-scratched signatures of everyone who ever lived there legible on every wall for as long as this world lasts — and comes back up subdued, and drinks a great deal of water, and does not talk about it.
