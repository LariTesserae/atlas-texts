# The Attention

Something noticed.

It is not useful to say what. Call it a power; it was benevolent, which means it wanted their good and not its own reflection in them, and it was wise, which means it knew that those two things can be told apart only slowly and at close range.

Its first act was a refusal.

It had intended to arrive as light. That is the ordinary way, and it is a kindness elsewhere, and in the vug it would have killed people. A brightness on the quartz is either the foundry — which burns eyes out — or it is strain, which means a district is about to come down. Any radiance the power cared to make would have been read in under four seconds by a pale person in an unlit booth, and a pin-caller would have shouted down the line, and a tenement would have been cut loose and struck and carried away in units one person can lift, and in the scramble two or three would have fallen. A greeting costing three lives.

So it put the light away and came in the way everything comes in here: through the rock, slowly, behind a drill, at the pace of a boring animal. It took eleven months to cross the last hundred metres. It used the time to learn the tap-code, which is not difficult, and the etiquette, which is.

When it reached the back of a worked-out seam on the eastern wall it did not emerge. It knocked.

# What It Declined

The obvious gift is water.

The power weighed it properly, which means it followed it forward. A spring in the low galleries. Within a year: washing. Within five: the end of the wax, and the graphite, and therefore the end of polishing, which is the only thing these people do with their hands that has no product. Within twenty: rot, and therefore the racks of dried ancestors wired under the family platforms come down and go somewhere else, and the children stop learning the lineage by climbing under and touching it. Within a hundred: mould in the mica, damp in the seams, a different ecology, a different labour, a population that no longer knows what a nose is for. Not worse people. Other people. The power looked at the four generations it would take to dissolve them and declined to be the solvent.

Air, the same. Thicken it and sound carries; speech at three metres stops being a whisper; the striker on its thong becomes an ornament; the tap-code goes in a generation and takes with it the condition that no one in the vug has ever been out of earshot of anyone, and the immense, exhausting, load-bearing courtesy of not listening, which is the best thing they have made and the only thing they have made that is invisible.

Stop the mountain rising: the power got further with this one than with the others, because the mountain kills them. Twice in living memory a metre in a night and a third of the city down. But a mountain that has stopped is a city that has stopped, no fresh breaks, no new veins, no rebuilding, no *lift* — and, at the end of it, a structure that cannot be taken down. They have a word for that and they fine the builder.

It considered curing the furnacemen's eyes and saw that silver-eyed is an honourable condition and that a silver-eyed elder is fed and polished by strangers as a matter of course, and that it would be removing not a blindness but an obligation, and that the city does not have so many of those it can spare one.

It considered the unlit, and could not decide, and has not decided.

It considered abolishing the floor — the scree, the shard-folk, the cut — and found that the floor has its own tap-dialect and its own long memory and eats better than the eastern wall in a bad year, and that what is wrong down there is not the floor.

And it considered doing nothing at all, on the grounds that they were complete and that admiration is a form of respect. It rejected this too. Doing nothing is very often laziness wearing the costume of restraint, and the power had come a long way, and there were nine thousand of them, and some of them were suffering in ways that had nothing to do with the architecture.

It settled on two rules and kept to them.

*Answer when asked.* Never announce.

*Take shifts.*

# The Fine

In its second year there was a lift — a small one, nineteen centimetres — and a scaffold carrying about forty tonnes of spar-counterweight began to shear off the wall above a crowded gallery, and the power, which had been standing there, put out its hand.

What it made was a brace: a buttress of quartz grown continuous with the living wall, single-crystal, seamless, without a bolt or a pin or a joint anywhere in it. It holds. It will hold for as long as the wall does.

It cannot be taken down. Not in units one person can carry, not in units forty people can carry, not at all, because there is nothing in it to undo. The inspectors came the next shift with their strikers and their tags, and went over it very carefully, and conferred, and assessed it a grave, and fined the builder.

They kept the brace. They are practical people and it holds forty tonnes. They fined the builder anyway.

The power offered to pay in metal. It was told, without heat, that anyone can bring copper; that copper is lying in the rock; that what is scarce here is air. The fine was set in bellows-shifts, at the standard rate, and the power worked them — a hundred and twelve shifts on a treadmill in the foundry chain, forty bodies to a furnace, leather straps, the long thin whine, half a shift and then up to the ceiling districts to lie in the cold with its limbs spread and its palms turned out, because it was doing this at the size and weight of a citizen and had agreed with itself to feel it.

It could have worked the treadmill at the strength of a hundred. It worked it at the strength of one. That was the whole of the point.

By the fourth month the crew had stopped watching it and started talking over it. By the seventh it had a name. Names here are knocks, three or four beats, given at the age when a child first hangs something that holds, and it had hung something that held, so it got the same four beats as the brace. Bridge and builder, one name. That is normal. Nobody thought it was a poem.

# The Shifts

After the fine was discharged it kept working, because the fine had only ever been the introduction.

It took seam-watch. Eight hours in an unlit booth looking at a wall is not a thing a person can do well for eight hours; attention frays at about the fifth. The power's did not fray. It sat the unpopular shifts — the third watch, the deep-cold hours under the apex — and it tapped the ordinary warnings in the ordinary code from an ordinary booth, and the pin-callers called, and districts came down clean. Deaths in the ceiling wards fell by about a third and stayed there for as long as it stayed. This was attributed to good watching, which is correct.

It took midwifery, of a sort. The first grief of any household is the infant that will not grip. The power sat through a hundred and some of these and found that a fraction of them — not most, a fraction — were simply cold, born in the wrong gallery in the wrong hour, and that there is a particular way of holding such a child against the deep-chested part of an adult, with the child's foot brought against the adult's palm, that starts the hook. It taught this to one midwife on the eastern wall as a trick. Not a revelation; a trick, the way one worker shows another how to seat a pin. She taught it on. Within two generations nobody knew where the hold had come from, and it had picked up a name-knock of its own, and the power considered this the most successful thing it did in the vug.

It sat with the stone-drunk. It did not take them off the stanchion. It had thought it would; it went to the first one intending to, and then pressed its own jaw to the steel and understood the problem, which is that what they are listening to is worth listening to. So it changed its mind and stayed, and it stayed for the week, and when they were found — hooked on, desiccated, weighing almost nothing — it had already sent the family the location in plain code. It did this eleven times. Three of the eleven came away. It did not make them; it asked, once each, and three answered. The other eight were not owed an opinion from it.

And it went down to the floor, and let the shard-folk polish it.

This took most of a night, in pairs, with a leather pad, the whole surface, wax and graphite and then the long burnishing, and it was the single most intimate act in the culture and the power submitted to it exactly as a citizen would and said nothing about it afterwards to anybody above. No one above will be polished by the floor. That did not change. But something that had been unimaginable had now happened once, and the people who had done it knew they had done it, and a thing that has happened once has a different weight than a thing that has never happened, and the power was content to leave the rest of it to them and to time.

# The Page That Was Not Scraped

There was an unlit woman, very old, at the end of her service, whose eyes were no longer needed by anyone. She asked the power — directly, jaw to stanchion, in the private register — whether it could give her light.

The rule was: answer when asked. So it answered: yes, and it will cost you what is left of your sight, and you will have about a quarter of an hour, and afterwards you will not see the green on the quartz again, and you will not see anything.

She thought about it for two days. Then she used a short flat dental syllable that means catastrophe and windfall at once, in the same word, with no tone on it at all, and said: a lift, then.

The power made a small warm light, the colour of an ember, and held it for her in the middle of the vug, and she looked.

Fifteen minutes. She had never seen the city. She had been raised in absolute dark at a handling range of three centimetres and had run a district-cutting authority for fifty years on the basis of touch, tapping, and a kind of green fire she alone knew properly. She tapped continuously the whole time she looked, and three scribes took it down on mica, and what she produced is, technically, a poor description. She used the wrong words for colours she had no words for. She spent two entire minutes on a gantry bracket.

The plate hangs in the assembly hall. It is graphite on mica, which means it is scrap, which means it is meant to be polished off and used again. It has not been polished off. It is the second permanent document the culture possesses, after the wall of pin-tags, and it is the only one made of sentences, and people come and read it with a lamp and their fingers.

The power keeps a copy. It considers it the only interesting thing it acquired in the vug that it did not already have.

# The Question in the Seam

Out past the last worked vein there is a rhythm that is not a drill and is not the city, and the unlit have a code-group for a light on the quartz that is not strain-light, and they are trained never to send it.

The power went and found out what it is.

This is the one place it will say it may have done wrong: it did not publish. It decided, on their behalf, that the answer would be a load nobody had pinned for, and that a city which can evacuate a district in an hour cannot evacuate itself, and that knowledge you cannot act on and cannot put down is a grave by their own definition. It made this decision alone and it records that it was paternalism and that it has no confident justification for it, only a judgement, which it might have got wrong.

What it did instead was teach a courtesy. Not to them; to the other one. The Hung's best invention is the elaborate, codified, imperfectly observed agreement not to listen to what you can hear. The power carried that down into the deep rock and spent a long time explaining it, in a register that has no top and no bottom, to something patient enough to let it finish. It seems to have been understood. The forbidden code-group has been sent once in the ninety years since, instead of the usual two or three times.

And when a drill-follower alone in a seam asked it, flatly, jaw to the rock, *is the mountain aware of us* — it answered, because that was the rule. She came back and did not tell anybody. That was her right and it is still being exercised.

# What It Took

It took the word. The short flat dental one. It had not had a word for that and had been making do with two, and it uses hers now, elsewhere, in places that are shattered every few years by a thing that is also their living, and it has been useful.

It took the name, four beats, shared with a buttress.

It left no doctrine, no monument it could be thanked for, and no account of itself. There is no tradition about it. A trader who asks will be told that there was someone who worked a great many shifts and could not be taken apart and was fined for it. They will mention the fine. They always mention the fine.

---

# The Vacant Pin

On the wall of stamped iron tags in the assembly hall — the only permanent document, the genealogy that is ironmongery — there is one tag with a four-beat knock-name that no living person claims. The pin it refers to is driven into a deep seam on the eastern wall and it is sound and it is rated for a household.

Pin-right is inviolable. It is the whole of their idea of belonging and the loss of it is the whole of their idea of punishment. A pin cannot be reassigned and it cannot be struck and the register cannot be amended, because the register is the one thing in the city that is not a palimpsest.

So the platform hanging from that pin stands empty, and by a custom whose origin nobody can now give, anyone with no pin may sleep on it for one night. Debtors. The cut. Shard-folk up from the floor with their different dialect and their long memory. Newcomers still gasping. They hook on and sleep and go on in the morning and nothing is asked of them, and nobody calls it charity, because it isn't; it is simply pin-right, and pin-right is ironmongery, and ironmongery is permanent.

The power made itself into a legal fact, which was the only material available that the mountain cannot take down.

It gained four centimetres last year.
