## The Attention

### Arrival

Nothing enters Cell Nine but heat, and nothing leaves it but heat. So it came in as heat: a quantity of warmth so small that the Constant's bearings did not report it to any thumb, distributed over the whole shift so that the moss did not gain a millimetre and the weed did not gain a finger and no bead swelled past its node. It did not use the lock. It could have used the lock — the lock is a courtesy, and courtesies are available to anyone — but a door that opens is a change in the pressure, and the pressure has not varied in living memory, and it did not intend to be the first thing in six hundred years that the walls noticed.

That was the first act, and it was not a kindness so much as a manner. But manners in a sealed room are most of ethics.

### The reading

It read the book before it did anything else.

Twelve hands. Some of them shaking, later on in their stretches; one that never shook at all and is the most frightening. Page after page after page of six numbers, in pencil, because ink weeps. *Eleven. Forty. Seventy-one. Four. Full. Even.* Hundreds of years of it. A visitor would call it the dullest document ever produced by a literate species, and a certain kind of clever visitor would call it a tragedy, and both of them would be reading it as a record, which it is not.

It is a vigil. It is a rosary said by women who cannot hear each other and cannot speak, who have been given six beads and told to keep telling them until the shift ends, and who have never once, in a dozen hands, put down a number they did not personally verify with a rule and a thumb and their own cold hands in the fur. There is no lie anywhere in that book. There is not much else in it either. But there is no lie, and the power, which had seen a great many libraries, sat with that for some while.

Then it made its list of everything it could do, and began striking things off.

### What it did not do

It did not unpin the governor. Four seconds of correct music would finish the wall, and the wall wants to finish; the wall has wanted to finish since the day the milk was first turned on, and it will want to finish for as long as there is lime in the sumps. Wanting is not a reason. Forty million ponds would come down in a single green sheet, and three hundred birds would break themselves on a surface that had finally been allowed to become hard, and the pearl skin one second old would become, in one second, ordinary stone — beautiful, banded, dead, and at last possessed of an edge that something had rounded.

It did not open the throat to the sky. Vent-swifts are calibrated to a four-note standing wave and one direction of turn; put out over the mountain, a swift does not fly away, it falls to the left, looking for a horizon in a world that has stopped roaring. Freedom delivered to an animal that has been redesigned by captivity is just a longer drop. The power knew several powers that had learned this the expensive way and it did not intend to be the next.

It did not let the millers rest. Ten minutes of rest is a fixture; a fixture is a bump under the pearl; the crowd feels the snag in the grain and the wall takes her. Mercy at the individual scale here is indistinguishable from burial.

It did not end the cell. This one it considered longest, because there is a school of benevolence that would call Nine an atrocity of pure duration and switch it off with a clear conscience, and the power could not entirely prove that school wrong. What it had instead of a proof was a census: four hundred thousand worlds, eleven walkers, three green things, a woman with a pencil, forty million commas of clear jelly orbiting the inside of their own skies. None of them had asked to be spared existence. It is very easy to be generous with other beings' time, and it declined the temptation.

And it did not tell her about the mountain, or the sea, or the other cells the numbering implies. Nine is not curious, and that is doctrine, and doctrine here is not stupidity; it is a load-bearing wall built by people who found out what happens to a tender who spends her shift wondering. There is a version of this story where the power arrives bearing the whole world as a gift, and she goes back down the ladder-lock carrying it, and is never any good in the cell again, and the book gets its first lie in three hundred years within the month. It had seen that version too.

It also found out, incidentally, whether the balance in Cell Nine was designed or discovered.

It is not going to say. Both sides of the argument that nobody has, because arguing requires shouting and shouting is impossible, would have been made worse by winning.

### The pin

Here is what it actually did first, and nobody will ever know, and that is the shape of the thing.

The governor pin is iron. It was driven by a man with a hammer into an atmosphere at ninety-something percent and just above freezing, and it has been rusting since the hour it went in. It is the one object in Cell Nine that is genuinely, measurably *going somewhere*. Everything else in that room is a circle. The pin is a line, and it has an end, and at the end of it the governor comes free on its own, at three in the morning, in some tender's shift, and the noise changes by a semitone and does not change back, because there will be nobody standing at the crank when it happens who knows what four seconds means.

The power took the pin out and put in a pin that will not fail. It did this between two strokes of the beam, so that the note did not alter by a hair, and the hangers did not flash, and the swifts did not stutter in the turn, and the millers did not lift a single file off the stone.

The doctrine of Cell Nine is that nothing changes. It was not true. It is now nearer to true than it has ever been, and the making of it true is not in the book, and cannot be, because averted catastrophes leave no column. That is the most expensive kind of good deed there is: the sort that is indistinguishable, forever, from nothing having been wrong.

### The light above the lamps

Then it did the one showy thing, and it did it with borrowed materials.

It did not hang a new lamp. A new lamp would change the light budget, and the tenders navigate the dark stretches by the flicker of the hangers — green under Lamp Two, greener and colder under Six, two-and-one at the northeast rib — and the power was not going to redraw a blind woman's map to make a point.

Instead it went up through the lattice. Slowly, at the pace of a thing that means to be noticed, from the grating to the invisible ceiling, and the hangers did what hangers do when the wall of the world flexes: four hundred thousand ponds lit at once, in a rising column, and for eight or nine seconds Cell Nine had a ceiling.

And she looked up. Of course she looked up.

Above the lamps, above the vapour, in the wind-shadow of the updraught where the milk has never once settled, the swifts keep their nest. It is not a nest. It is a shelf and then a ledge and then a hanging grey suburb of cemented spit and feather and mineral crumb, and it has been added to by two or three hundred birds in shifts for longer than the word *long* has meant anything, and it is *old*. It is greasy. It is scarred. It has edges that something has rounded. It contains, in its lower courses, feathers from birds that were flying before the Constant was cast, and in its upper courses, eggs. It is weathered. It is the only weathered surface in the Freshworks.

The green went out of the air and the ceiling closed again and she stood in the roar with her cage on her head and her coat weeping, holding a pencil, in a room whose entire theology she had just been shown the cellar of.

She did not put it in the book. There is no column for it and she was not a woman who invented columns.

She put in a tick. In the margin, beside *swift column: full*, one pencil tick, which in the language of the book means *seen*.

Nine hands later, the tick is still being copied forward. None of them know what it is for. They copy it because copying forward without understanding is the whole of the discipline, and because a mark made by a tender who verified something with her own eyes is not the sort of thing you leave out.
