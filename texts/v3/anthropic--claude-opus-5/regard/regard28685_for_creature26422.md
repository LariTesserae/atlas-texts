## The Attention

It came in from the rim in the wet season, when the cliffs were calving, and the first thing it did was nothing, and it did that for eleven choirs.

This is the part that would be left out of any account written by people who wanted to be impressed. Four hundred and forty years of doing nothing is not a prologue. It is the majority of the work. A power that is only benevolent arrives with hands already full. A power that is also wise arrives empty and sits down.

What it learned in that time cannot be summarised, but the shape of it can. It learned the difference between the roar over a fat vessel and the roar over deadwood, which took nine years, and it learned to read a bead-string, which took two, and it learned the taste of eleven thousand kilometres of gallery wall, which took most of the rest, and it learned that the tongue-geography of the deep lines is not a quaint substitute for cartography but a better map than any it had ever been shown, on any world, by anyone. It learned the knock. It gave itself a name-knock and never used it.

It declined to be entered in the census. It registered itself, in its own reckoning, under weather.

### The question it had to answer first

Nothing here is growing; nothing here is dying; both at identical rates, in balance, weathered into a shape it has no reason to leave.

Read that sentence one way and it is a description of peace. Read it the other and it is a description of a coma — a mind of continental extent that reached a comfortable altitude and stopped, and has been idling for longer than there have been people to notice, and could be *offered more*.

Every power that is only benevolent reads it the second way. This one made itself read it both ways for four hundred years before it allowed itself an opinion, because it had noticed, over a long career, that the phrase *I could offer it more* has preceded almost every atrocity it has ever had to clean up after, including its own.

The answer, when it came, came from the castings.

A thing in a coma does not extrude shapes of shapes nobody knows. A thing in a coma does not produce, at the surface, forty years late, hollow fused-cane models of animals from the plains that never fell on it. The Thatch was receiving. From what, and how, the power never entirely settled, though it suspected the answer was *from the plains, by the roots, through nine hundred kilometres of stone, very slowly, in a manner best described as smelling*. And it was answering. It had a vocabulary, and the vocabulary was objects, and it spoke at one word per forty years, which is a reasonable rate for someone two thousand metres tall who has nowhere to be.

So: not asleep. Slow. Sufficient. Talking to itself and to the ground and possibly to the weather, and getting the hands wrong every time, and not caring, because the hands were never the point.

The power revised its plans downward, which is what wisdom mostly feels like from the inside.

### What it refused

It refused to make it rain on the surface. The refusal took one afternoon and no deliberation: rain on top ends the Draw, ends the sweat-cloud, ends the shade, ends the Dew Hour, ends the ears, ends the language of touch, ends the tap, ends everything the place has made of itself, in exchange for water it does not need.

It refused to cure rim-legs. The Thatch keeps no prisoners; it has simply arranged the door so that it works better in one direction. A power that fixes the door has made a decision about a whole people's future on their behalf, at speed, with their descendants' consent forged. If they want the door, they can build it. It left the materials lying about where a curious person would trip on them, which is the most it was willing to do, and nobody has tripped yet.

It refused to end the choir. Forty years of ratcheting is a hard thing to live under and it is also the only calendar, the only feast, the only date, and the reason a woman can say *a year before the choir* and be understood exactly. You do not take a people's clock away as a kindness.

It refused to give them writing. Not because writing is bad. Because it would have been *its* writing, arriving fully formed, with the knock-names in it as awkward transliterations, and in three generations the mouth-name would be the real name and the rhythm would be decoration. If they invent it, the knock will survive inside it, badly, the way things survive when they are the parents of the thing that replaced them. That is theirs to botch.

And it refused the great temptation, which was the tappers.

It went through the arithmetic properly, more than once. Give one woman back her hearing: she is the finest tapper on the plateau for sixty years instead of three. Her household's tap-rights consolidate. The succession — the terrible, functional succession in which the best is always the one about to lose everything, and the apprentices watch her mouth for the moment she mishears a simple thing, and therefore *learn* — that succession stops, because there is no longer any hurry. And the silent lose the one thing that dignifies them, which is that their condition is universal and undeserved and coming for everyone, including the person now saying grace.

So it did not do that. Instead it improved the glass.

Not visibly. Over about two hundred years the cane-ash flux ran a little cleaner in a way the glassmakers experienced as their own gradual competence, and the beads seated better, and the lipping held, and the ruptures — the sudden ones, the ones that take a nineteen-year-old's ear in a single midsummer afternoon over a fat vessel — became rare instead of ordinary. Net effect, averaged across the plateau: three years. Three more years of hearing per working life.

Nobody thanked it. Nobody could have known. This is the shape of nearly everything it did.

### The three things it did

**It put an edge on the quiet.**

People went into hollowings and did not come out. There was no meaning built on this. Nothing rested on it; no craft, no honour, no grammar — only fear, and nine mutually contradictory superstitions, and every few years a child.

It did not close the hollowings. A lid over six hundred metres of rot-void is a thing the Thatch makes on its own schedule for its own reasons, and there were more of them than could be counted, and a plateau without holes in it is a plateau that has been managed. What it did was give the void a voice: it tuned the collar of dead cane around each hollowing so that the air moving over it produces, at the edge of the soft grey circle, a thin high whine — inaudible in the fat roar, audible precisely and only when you have stepped into the near-silence, which is to say audible exactly one pace before it matters.

The whine has a name now. It is a small, unlovely word and it is one of the first ten words a child learns above ground. One of the nine superstitions was retroactively declared correct, on no evidence, by a school of tappers whose grandparents had believed the opposite; there was a schism; there were two decades of unpleasantness about tap-rights disguised as theology. The power watched all of this with enormous affection and corrected none of it.

**It arranged for something to be noticed.**

There was a woman in the deep lines, nine years silent, name-knock a slow triple that you could feel through a bare foot from four hundred metres, fed first, consulted, not entirely trusted, already dead in the grammar.

The power did not speak to her. It has never spoken to anyone here. It arranged a rope to be in the wrong place, and a lost basket to be worth retrieving, and a route to be shorter than it looked, and she walked into a hollowing at the third hour of light because the quiet is not a thing that means anything to a woman for whom everywhere is quiet, and she surveyed the lid from beneath with her wall hand and her tongue, and she came out on the far side, and she knocked.

It took two generations. Now there are eleven of them, and they are called by a compound of the word for silence and the word for road, and they map the lids, and they are paid in salt and in metres of lit gallery, and they are still fed first, and the dread has gone slightly sideways into something with more room in it. The grammar has not changed. Grammar takes longer than religion. But the sentence that used to end *they are already, in the grammar, dead* now, in some households, in some moods, ends differently.

**It answered the castings.**

This was the only thing it did that was addressed to the being rather than the tenants, and it is the only thing it would call correspondence.

It laid a shape on the mat, in the deep centre where the Thatch is winning, twenty kilometres from any route, and let the nap close over it in a season, and waited thirty-eight years for the reply to be extruded, and read it, and laid down the next.

The vocabulary is small and the latency is a human lifetime and the grammar, as far as it can tell, has three cases: *this fell on me*, *this did not fall on me*, and *this I have not had*. It has been going now for six choirs. It has established, it believes, mutual recognition. It has established that the Thatch is content, that it is not lonely in any sense the word survives being stretched to, that it would like — and this took ninety years to be sure of — *the smell of rain from the other side of the rim*, which it remembers from when the plains were a sea, and which the power has been unable to deliver, and has said so, and been forgiven, or something adjacent to forgiven.

The power will not describe this as a conversation with a god. It has met gods. This is a correspondence with a very large, very old, very slow neighbour who has good manners and no hands.

### Where it lives

It lives as an asker.

Waist-high, too many legs, no eyes, apologetic manner. It follows parties carrying food at nine or ten metres and closes in during sleep to take crumbs, gut-scrapings, hair, the softened ends of ropes. When noticed it stops, lowers the front third of its body, taps the ground twice, and backs away. Then it comes again.

It could have been a Stander. It could have been the thing that comes up through the vent-mounds at night and makes the nap ripple. It considered, once, briefly, being a voice, and then thought the whole business through to its conclusion, which was a people who were the least of the tenants by mass and the most of them by opinion being handed something enormous to have opinions about, and it went and ate hair instead.

The galleries an asker-line frequents are noticeably cleaner. Households do not feed it and do not drive it off. Nobody has ever eaten one. There is a story in two of the deep lines that the askers are relatives, and it is not believed, and it is not laughed at either, and this is approximately the correct amount of true.

### The rope shed

Forty years ago the Thatch extruded, at the edge of the empty nap eleven kilometres from a combed Stander, a casting of something nobody knows.

It is hollow, fused cane, ribbed, standing alone in the shimmer. It is roughly right, and about a third too large, and it has too many legs, and the legs are wrong, and the front third is lowered as if in the middle of backing away. The hands — there are no hands; the shape has never had hands; the Thatch gave it hands anyway, in a position that is not any position the shape has ever held, because the Thatch always gets the hands wrong and has never once been told, by anyone, that this matters.

A survey party out of a three-string alliance cut a door in it nine years ago. They store rope in it. They camp in it during Dew Hour, and leave it for the next party, and nobody feels any way about that at all.

It is the finest thing that has ever happened to the power, and it has told no one, and it never will.
