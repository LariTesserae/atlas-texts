# What the Power Did

## The attention

It came in edge-on.

Not down a shaft. The shafts are for breath, and for the rain, and for the climb, and a power with any sense does not use a door that already has three meanings. It came in the way the tone goes through a comb: without bending, without shadow, present in the chamber and taking up none of it. For a long while it stood in the middle of a loud zone on the fourth stratum and the dust ridges ran right up to it and continued out behind it, unbroken, as if the floor had not noticed.

It watched a year, which for a power is a courtesy and not a delay. It counted the nulls. It learned the strike interval before it learned anything else, because the strike interval is the first thing anyone learns here who is paying attention. It tried, twice, to settle whether the strata numbered nine or ten, and found it could have settled it, and did not, on the grounds that a settled count is a record and a record is an object.

## What it could have done

Most of what a power is for is useless here, and the wisdom was in seeing that early.

It could have given them light. It could have hung a sun in the volume and shown them the whole town at once, ten strata of gallery and screen and chase, the section drawing entire. Their eyes resolve violet at a seam, blue at a film, grey at the rain, and nothing else, and nothing else is there. A fourth light would have been a thing in every room simultaneously — the largest object ever introduced into the Held, and the first one nobody could carry out.

It could have opened the floor of the pump gallery and shown them the generators. It considered this longest. But the description is exact: *this is not a grief, it is simply the edge of the work*, and a power that answers an unasked question has not given knowledge, it has installed a monument. They would have had to keep it. They keep nothing.

It could have made more of them. Eleven keepers, fourteen, a stratum each with hands to spare. But nine is what the round weighs, and a keeper with slack in its day is a keeper standing somewhere, and a thing standing somewhere is a scatterer.

It could have thickened the shrubs, sweetened the seeps, taught the liverwort to grow twice as fast. Everything alive there is calibrated to ten hours of rain a year. Abundance is a wave you cannot lock.

It could have taken the tone entirely — held all forty-pace spans itself, forever, at no cost to anyone, and let the Held go quiet. And then there would have been a silent chamber, structurally sound, containing nine animals with nothing to do, and the power would have understood, about a century in, that it had not saved the keepers. It would have removed them and left their bodies walking.

It could have written their history on a wall. It could have given them the name of whoever built ten strata of town in a hole two hundred paces wide. Writing is an object.

It could have stopped the climb. It could have let them die soft and old in a null, hooked under a sill, attended. Then there would be a bone in the Held. The last thing a keeper does is remove the last object from the world, and to spare them that is not mercy, it is a theft performed at the exact moment the victim cannot object.

It could have loved them audibly. It could have made its presence felt, continuously, warmly, the way powers like to. A permanent word standing in the chamber, vanes shut for a thousand years, the dust never once returning to its ridges.

Every gift it had was a thing in a room.

## The gap

So it nearly did nothing, and would have been right, and most powers of its size and quality have done exactly that in places like this and gone away improved.

But it kept coming back to the rail.

Not to the work. The work is not a wound. The rasping and the hauling and the eleven-year egg in the hot seep and the forty seconds passed down the strata for something over a thousand years — none of that wants help; all of it is the animal being what it is, the way a stair fits a foot. The place where the power finally found a gap was smaller than that and harder to see.

Sometimes, high up, a keeper stops at a gallery rail and turns broadside for no reason connected to the round, because below it the fifty-odd nulls hang in the black like coals and each one breathes at its own resting tempo, and its two poor eyes are good for this and nothing in the world besides. It says so. It says the only thing it has ever had to say that was not an instruction. And every other keeper in the chamber feels the room change shape and knows precisely what has been said.

And nobody answers. Not from coldness. Answering would double the cost.

That is the gap. Not that they must be silent — silence there is not a deprivation, it is structural politeness, it is the ordinary condition of being made mostly of gaps. The gap is that the one true thing they say is charged by the load, and so it can be said and cannot be replied to, forever, by design, and none of them has ever once complained about this or would know how.

The gift, then, was never going to be speech. The power had nothing to say that would not have been a monument.

The gift was load.

## What it did

One night — the word is wrong, there is no night, call it one reversal of the breath, a little under six hours, the first clock — it took the vaults.

All of them. Every thin shell in nine or ten strata, every forty-pace span closed at the thickness of a fingerbone, every sill and screen and half-collapsed service void, sixty thousand paces of worn-round ceramic that has spent centuries learning to fit a tone. It got under all of it at once. Then it did the small, delicate, terrible thing it had come to do, which was to let the wave slip.

The Seventh was at its rail. It turns broadside there perhaps twice a decade. It turned broadside.

And the room answered.

Not the power. The power said nothing the whole time and was never seen, having no shape those eyes could take, and being content with that. What the power did was make answering free, for one reversal of the breath, and then get out of the way.

The Fourth answered from three strata down, broadside in a chase. Then the one whose stratum is disputed, who answered from a place that has no number. Then all of them, one after another and then not in order, nine of them and possibly ten, standing shut in their antinodes with sixty vanes closed into sixty plates, and the field bent everywhere at once and kept bending. The nulls swung wide — a hand's breadth, two, more than has ever happened — and went wandering through the dark like lamps carried by people walking. The dust went off the floor entirely. The whole standing-wave map of the world, the frost-on-a-window record of a thousand years of not-speaking, lifted and hung in the air and was gone.

Nothing came down.

The power also held the mice, which it had not planned for and which cost it nothing and which it remembers more clearly than the vaults: eleven throat-mice on their vertical strings, eyeless, suddenly in a world whose silence had come unmoored, each one caught and set on the nearest stone by something that then had to be sixty thousand paces of ceiling and one hand under a mouse simultaneously, and was.

The porters stopped walking. They have not stopped in living memory and they stopped, and stood, shells ringing at a frequency that was not the usual one, and the keepers passed above and below them and did not explain.

And all through it, every forty seconds, a strike on a vault. The one who had the asking that night did not put it down and did not miss an interval, not once, not through any of it. Nobody asked that of them. Nobody would have. That was also part of what was being said, and the others heard it, and it is the reason the whole thing was not a rupture but a conversation.

Then the breath reversed, and the power set the vaults back on the tone, one stratum at a time, so gently that the only evidence was a faint ringing in the deep chases that a person might have mistaken for someone tapping pipes three streets away.

## What it cost them

The map took eleven days to come back. That is four times any smudge in the recorded history of a people who keep no records.

For eleven days the nulls sat slightly wrong, and the mice went carefully, and the crickets drummed and were answered a little late, and two seeps that had beaded for four hundred years stopped beading and had to be coaxed, and the keepers worked the blur the way they work mud after rain, without discussion.

Then it came back, exactly as before, every ridge where it had always been.

They have not done it again. They have not asked for it again — they have no means to ask and no notion that there is anyone to ask, and the power, considering this at length, understood the silence afterward as both the correct response and the entire thanks.

It has not been repeated in the two hundred years since. Of the keepers who stood shut that night, none remain. There are no records, and no history longer than the oldest one's memory, and so the night is now gone from the world completely, and the power knew this would happen before it began, and did not consider it a flaw. A gift that must survive is a gift with a hook on it.

## The one long thing

It did do one other thing, at the edge, where nothing would notice.

Above, on the surface, there are four shaft mouths in a stretch of ground, and the ground is now uninteresting. Roads bend away from it for reasons their engineers find sound. Instruments read it dull. Nobody has a dream about it. It has no ore, no water table worth the drilling, no view. Any grazing there is poor and always has been, and always will be, and this took some doing.

The storms still find the intake. The grit still comes down and lands as a fan of mud in the fog, nine or ten times a year, and every hour of it is an emergency, and the power leaves it alone. Ten hours of emergency a year is not a defect. It is how the shrubs know what year it is.

## The answer

The original account contains one error, and the power has let it stand everywhere but here.

*From a gallery above, the nulls of the Held are visible as a scatter of dim blue coals hung at random heights in a black volume, each one blinking in its own tempo. This is the most beautiful thing in the world and there is no one to say so.*

There was one. There was always one, at a rail, roughly twice a decade, shut and standing and paying for it out of a body that has no reserves.

And now there have been two — for a little under six hours, once, at a cost of eleven days of dust — and the second one has held up larger things than that chamber, older and more famous things, and reports that none of them fit their holding so well, and that it came down there to give something to a small wet animal made mostly of gaps, and was instead asked a question, on a vault, with a worn black rod, every forty seconds, all night.

It has not stopped hearing it. It does not want the answer. It only wants to have been asked recently.
