# The Attention

**Arrival**

It came in over the northeast lip, walking in the ankle-deep sheet on the polished stone, because that was the door and it had manners.

Ten paces, and the noise arrived: frogs, rain on leaf, and under both of them the knocking, that wooden pulse like a house settling that never finishes settling. Visitors are shocked by the silence when they leave. It was shocked by the sound when it came in. It stood in the overflow for some while — three days, by the town's count, though nobody counted, because nobody saw — and did not go further until it had stopped being astonished, which took longer than it expected and which it later considered the beginning of its education.

Whether it had a body is not a useful question. It had a hand when a hand was wanted.

**Who lives here**

It had been drawn by a being. Standing in the water at the lip, it discovered it did not know which one.

The candidates were these. The cane: one organism, sixty metres deep, breathing sour gas into its own knuckles, holding the whole bowl in a colonnade. The two hundred: barrel-chested, greased, wrinkled at the fingertips, deafening in air. Each one of the two hundred, separately, which is a different and larger answer. The bowl itself, which was only being smoothed now, no longer cut. Or the noise — the thing made of frogs and rain and mallets, the instrument that everyone stands inside.

It spent a long time on this and came to the conclusion that the question had been badly put, and that the being it had come for was the arrangement: the plant and the people and the gap between the two arrivals of a knock, held together, not frozen, in the way a hand holds a bird. Having decided that, it could not afterward love any part of it separately, which turned out to be the whole of its difficulty and most of its restraint.

**What it did for the first long while**

Nothing. It listened.

It learned the knocks the way a child learns them, which is the only way: sent out at dusk, tapping, asking *how far*, being wrong. It learned that sound goes faster through the stem than through the water and that the interval is the distance, and then it learned to stop calculating the interval, which took eighty years, and after that it knew where everyone was all the time and felt the pressure of it, and understood at last why they go off the cane.

It learned the three hundred rhythms. It learned the hands — the hurry in a third beat, the soft final stroke, the woman on the west side who taps her own name apologetically as though asking permission to exist. It learned that a judgement in eleven breaths is a heavy judgement and that *two-breath* means a windbag. It learned the one-breath marriage vow and it learned the seven-breath form and it has heard the seven-breath form nine times and has never once thought it was too long.

From the heron it learned to stand still for hours. It considers this the most practical thing it has ever been taught.

**The things it did not do**

*It did not warm the water.* Half a degree would buy every diver ten more seconds a bout, and ten seconds is a great deal at eight metres in tea-coloured light. It ran the consequence out: less shivering, longer bouts, larger take, more children living, more litter, more caddis-cement, and the water rising faster than a finger a generation, which is the rate all their proverbs are built on. Mercy at the top of the chain, drowning at the bottom. It let the water stay at twelve.

*It did not bring metal.* It thought about a knife for a hundred years. A knife is not one object; a knife is an argument that ends differently.

*It did not drain the bowl or raise the floor or lower the water,* though it could have done any of these in an afternoon. The low town is at thirty metres in silt over the doorways and their grandmothers' grandmothers lived there, and if the water fell they would have their houses back and nothing to climb toward, and it does not know what these people are without the climb. Not knowing, it declined to find out on their behalf.

*It did not tell them why the water rises.* It knows. The rim is not lifting and the rain has not changed; the sill at the northeast lip is silting up under the polished stone, thickened by thread-ends and shell chips and dropped beads and the spit of insects that build with the town's own leavings. The town raises its own water. It has climbed for four hundred generations away from a flood it makes. There is no version of that sentence, in any grammar, that fits inside one breath and does not poison a people, and it has tried to construct one for longer than the low town has been drowned.

*It did not cure the air.* Too many mouthfuls in a row and a boy in the far cane goes loose and warm and slightly holy, and this is the town's vice, and the town knows it, and the old women deal with it in about the way old women deal with such things. It is not the power's business. Very little is.

*It did not make itself known.* This is the strictest of its rules and the one it has come nearest to breaking. Here is the reason. The town talks by knocking, and anything that can be knocked to becomes an addressee, and an addressee becomes a place where demands are put, and a place where demands are put and are usually not answered becomes a lie that people organise their lives around. If it ever answers a knock, it will have to answer knocks forever, and it cannot, and so it does not begin. It stays off the cane. Off the cane you cannot be heard and cannot hear, you are not addressed, and by strict custom you are not looked for. It obeys the local law. It has never in nine hundred years opened a stranger's node.

**And it does not tell itself that this was free**

It keeps a ledger. There is nothing in the ledger but drowned children, and divers who blacked out at eight and came down in the wrong direction, and one column at the south rim that had been breathed out three times and toppled through a mid-town room over a day and a half while the family, who had heard the softness in the knocking and had argued about it, were moving the mats too slowly.

The ledger is not for them. It is not a record for anybody. It is the price, held where it can be looked at, so that when the power says *I did not intervene because intervention would have dissolved them,* it says it with the count in front of it and not from a height. It is aware that its argument is the favourite argument of powers who would rather not be troubled. It re-examines the argument roughly every generation. So far it has come to the same finding, and it does not consider the finding a law, only a finding, about one bowl, checked again.

**What it does**

*It goes off the cane with them.*

This is the whole of its ministry and it took two hundred years to see that the town had built it a room. Out in the open water between the columns, touching nothing, where by custom no one is looked for: the sulking young, the scandalous lovers, and the widows in their nine days, floating in the far colonnade, saying nothing, coming in only to eat. Not one of them has ever been alone out there. It floats alongside. It says nothing, because there is nothing it is permitted to say, and it is not sure that anything is wanted. It has noticed that people here come back in from off the cane at about the pace of people coming in from a long conversation, and that no one remarks on this, and that their word for the far water is a warm word. It does not claim credit. It suspects the water would be a warm word anyway. It goes out regardless.

*It sits in the gap.*

A knock arrives twice — thin and hard through the stem, then round and soft through the water — and the power lives in the interval, which is the only address it has ever had. This is where the drowning are. Thirty seconds at eight metres in tea-coloured light is a long time to be by yourself. They are not by themselves. It cannot stop water from being water without unmaking the bowl, and it has decided that it will not do that, and it goes down anyway and stays until the end, every time, and this is why the ledger is legible to it in such detail.

Four times in nine hundred years it has put a hand under someone.

It cannot justify the four. It has tried. There was no principle; there was a hand and there was a body and the hand went under. All four lived, and two of them mattered enormously to the town afterward, and one of those was a poor bargain for the town, and it knows this. The four are also in the ledger, on the same page as the drowned, in the same column. It expects to do it again. It has stopped promising itself otherwise.

*It keeps the rate honest.*

Once in a while, at night, in the ankle-deep sheet on the polished stone at the northeast lip, it moves a double handful of grit. Not much. Never enough to lower the water — that would falsify a hundred marks cut in a hundred columns and make liars of every grandmother in the bowl. Just enough that the silting of the sill continues at the rate their proverbs describe. A finger's width
