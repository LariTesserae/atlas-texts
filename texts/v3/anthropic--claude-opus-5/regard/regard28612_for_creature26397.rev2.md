# The Attention

## The looking

It came at the rime hour, which is the hour anything would come, and it did not arrive so much as resolve — the way a ridge across the basin resolves when the fog thins from the top down.

It did nothing for a long time. This is the first thing to understand about it, and the hardest, because power is usually imagined as a verb. This one began as attention, and stayed attention longer than the Ninth Sill had been standing.

It learned the language, both registers, and found that the whistled one had no polite forms and never had, and made a note of that, and kept the note. It read the archive: two hundred thousand slates stacked like roof shingle, four sills deep, including the fuel-debt instruments and the partition disputes and the survey arguments, and also the tallies of nothing, the laundry counts, the scratched insults on the backs of legal tablets where a clerk two hundred years dead had written *and his catchment leaks* in a hand that was trying not to be recognised. It read those too. It read them twice.

It went up and stood on all eight of the dead sills, in order, and counted the ossuaries, and where the bones had gone to meal it counted the meal. It knows the number. It has never told anyone the number, because the number is not the point; the names are the point, and it has those as well, back to a depth where the names stop being recoverable and become only the fact that someone was there, which it also holds, the way you hold a stone with no shape in particular.

It did not think of them as forty thousand. It thought of them one at a time, which took much longer, and was the only honest way to do it.

And it stood on the roof-paths at the fourth hour among the children with their hair brooms, and it learned what the city sounds like when ten thousand ropes go from white to black in a wave that takes a minute to cross the town, and it understood, finally and without any possibility of argument, that it was in the presence of something it had not made and could not improve by force.

## What was considered

It could have made it rain.

It worked this one through, because it is the obvious gift and the obvious gifts are the dangerous ones. Eleven rains a year to forty. Standing water in the pans for a season instead of two days. The combs would go unmaintained within twenty years and unbuilt within fifty, and the hair would not be braided into them, and the dead would not be in the warp, and the greeting — bridge of the nose to the cheek, one breath given deliberately — would become a gesture whose meaning nobody could reconstruct, because the whole meaning of it is that moisture is a thing you have and can hand over. Within four generations they would be a people who could spit. They would not be able to tell you what they had lost. They would not have the vocabulary; the vocabulary would have gone with it.

It could have held the plateau still.

This is the great one. A thumbnail a year, a metre in three or four generations, the land climbing out of its own water — this is the wound, and it is not metaphorical, and it will eventually kill the entire civilisation. The power looked at the uplift the way a person looks at a tumour in someone they love. And then it looked at the language. *Up-of-me*, for the grandmother. *Uphill water*, for the impossible. *Sent up*, used for one thing only. An event conjugated uphill or downhill of the speaker. To arrest the rising would not be to heal them; it would be to make their tenses false, and their dead misfiled, and the old woman on the fourth terrace would put one hand flat on the rock at the height where the rime stood when she was a girl, and the other where it stands now, and the distance between her hands would no longer be the width of her life. It would be nothing. It would be a stain.

It would have had to take her hands off the wall.

It could have given them iron. It ran that out too: the ringing, the wheels, the sawmills, the loudness. A city where a voice at fifty metres is a thread, given the means to be loud. It declined, and then re-examined the decline for sentimentality, because *they are so beautifully quiet* is not a reason to withhold metal from people who chip their tools out of bone. It found that the real reason was different and better: they were nine hundred years from the copper seam becoming a smelting tradition on their own terms, and the difference between a thing you are given and a thing you arrive at is the difference between a lintel and a lintel you cut.

But it did not conclude from any of this that their hardship was good for them. It wants that on the record. It does not believe the fowling gangs drown for any reason. It does not believe the smoke takes the eyes of the old for any reason. It believes only that the largest gift it could hand down by force was smaller than the thing they were already making, and that a gift which makes the recipient smaller is not benevolence but appetite wearing a good coat.

So it looked for the gifts that cost them nothing.

## What was done

**The plug.** In a family in the third block, a woman rebuilding a smoke-hole after a roof failure set the felt plug on a short collar of drystone instead of flush, so that the draw improved and the room cleared faster. It was her idea. She had been most of the way to it for a year; the power supplied the last quarter-inch of it, the way one supplies a word to someone who has stopped mid-sentence. Her neighbours copied it because their eyes stung less. Over forty years it became simply how a smoke-hole is built, with no inventor attached, and the rate at which dung smoke blinds the old fell by something under a third.

It took forty years. The power could have done every eye in the city in a night. It counted what that cost — it knows, to the person, who went blind in those forty years who need not have — and it paid, and it does not pretend the cost was not a cost. But an architectural change is theirs and spreads and outlives them, and a miracle is a debt with no slate, and this is a people who understand better than most what an unrecorded debt does to a household.

**The knot.** A rope-worker among the folded people, wintering near the Fourth basin, worked out a lashing for pole-stage frames that holds under shock loading when the old hitch renders. The power gave him the same quarter-inch. It travels the way everything travels here, in the packs of people who carry all the news and all the copper and half the marriages, and it reached the Ninth Sill in nineteen years, and after that the fowling gangs drowned eleven people in a bad decade instead of twenty-six.

**The number.** Sixty years ago a cistern was breached in a dispute about comb frontage, and the volume was assessed, and the debt was cut into slate, and it descends, and a man carries water four days in seven for a household not his own and will die owing about a third of it. He has views about the interest. He does not have views about the assessment, because the assessment is arithmetic and arithmetic is not a thing you have views about.

The assessors used the formula for a hemisphere. The dome was corbelled. They were wrong by nearly one part in seven, and they were wrong in the same direction on every corbelled dome they measured for eleven years, which is a great many households.

The power put the correct method, worked through on a scrap tablet in an unremarkable hand, into a stack of survey exercises where a plasterer's apprentice with an unusual head for volumes will find it. She will find it in about four years. What she does then is entirely her own, and the power has deliberately not looked, and this abstention is a real one and costs it something.

**The warning.** Once. It permitted itself once.

A pole-stage over standing water in the second fowling, fog to the shoulder, nine people on it and a lashing at the third pole that was old work and not the new knot. The power whistled. Three notes, the flat alarm, blunt as the register requires — you cannot be polite in whistle and it did not try. Two men looked up and came off the stage and pulled a girl with them and the stage went into a metre and a half of black water, and everyone lived, and afterward there was an argument about whether it had been a marmot, which it could not have been, because the colonies do not call at night. The argument was not settled. Neither man tells it well. One of them has stopped telling it.

It has not done that again. The not-doing-it-again has been substantially harder than the doing.

**The cairn.** Far in — past the Tenth's marked ground, past the surveyed pan, out on the flat where nobody has run a line because there has never been a reason — the basin floor has a slow sag in it. Rock is subsiding there faster than the plateau lifts, by a little, and will continue to, and in four thousand years, when the rime line has gone down off every ridge in this half of the continent and the combs of every sill from the First to the Twenty-Second stand dry as grave-goods, there will still be fog pooling in that hollow at the fourth hour of dark.

The power built a cairn there. Stone by stone, in a body it assembled for the purpose, in the local convention: the wet-markers' pattern, the notched post set true, the courses laid the way a surveyor lays them. It took four days. One stone near the top it set slightly wrong, the way a tired person sets a stone at the end of a long day, and it set it wrong on purpose, and this is the only lie it has told them.

It considered the lie carefully. Its own signature on that cairn would have ended the argument forever — and the argument is not an obstacle to their life, the argument *is* their life, two hundred years and running, three schisms in the plasterers' guild and a vast literature of scratched slate and a permanent office maintaining notched posts so that people who despise each other can at least quarrel with numbers. To hand them a certainty from outside would be to retire the finest instrument they own. So it signed the work *a person was here,* which is a lie in the shallow sense and true in every sense that matters.

The folded people will find it in about ninety years. They will report it, because they carry all the news, and it will be disbelieved, and re-found, and disputed, and surveyed badly, and surveyed again, and it will start an argument that runs for eight centuries and produces a literature and an office and probably a war. And at the end of the eight centuries a city will walk out onto ground nobody had surveyed, and the cairn will be quarried for its lintel, which is correct, and nothing should be carried uphill.

**The bone.** A dressed lintel came down from the Sixth with a human long-bone mortared into its bed, and the mortar face was turned inward, and it went into a wall in the lower town, and nobody saw it.

Under their law — the single most resented obligation in the culture, and nobody has ever proposed abolishing it — the bone goes back up, that day, by whoever found it, at their own cost.

The power found it. So the power took the wall apart that night and put it back together, and made a wicker frame, and carried the bone up in a body with ordinary lungs, twenty-two breaths a minute, one long day's work, and laid it on the dry roof-slabs of the Sixth where the two-metre birds do what they do, and came down in the dark with its hands cracked across the knuckles.

On the path near the fourth terrace it was passed by a herder coming down late, who stopped, and pressed the bridge of her nose to its cheek, and breathed out once, and looked at the empty frame on its back and said nothing at all, because there is nothing to say to someone who has been carrying a bone uphill.

The power had been holding, that whole day, an enormous thing it had considered saying.

It said nothing, in the local manner, and went on down, and counts this as the beginning of its education.

## What was not done

In about eleven years the surveyor of the Tenth will present her case. She has the posts and she has done the arithmetic on the inversion depth and she is right, and her slates are assembled, and the people she presents to will hate her for it, and some of them will hate her honestly and some will hate her because of what they own.

The power will not help her. It will not soften one face in that room. It will not lose a single one of her opponents' tablets, though it knows exactly where they are and how easily damp gets into that wall.

What it did do was this: in the lower archive there is a roof-beam of ungulate legbone, spliced, that will fail in sixty-one years, and a seam under the north corbel that already sweats in the second cold. Her stack of slates sits under both. Over eleven years, by the smallest available means — a clerk's habit, a misfiling, a dull and entirely human argument about shelf allocation — the stack has been moved three rooms east, into dry rock, where it will still be legible when the beam goes and when the people who hated her have themselves gone up, and where it will be found by a surveyor not yet born, of a line not yet begun, who will need it.

That is the whole of the intervention. It does not add to the world anything but accuracy, and it does not add to them anything but time, and it takes nothing away.

She will be carried up by the young, one long day's work, wicker frame on the back, and laid out for the birds at whichever dead sill is in use that decade, and her name will go into an ossuary, and her city will be built by strangers a hundred and fifty years later, further in than she dared say aloud.

The power will attend. Not to do anything. Someone must know what it cost her, and the whole design of her life was that nobody in it would.

## The account

Eventually the plateau will climb out of its water entirely and this will not be survivable and they will go down over the rim passes into the thick air, over centuries, in arguments, badly, the way everything here is done and then done well. They will become lowlanders. They will talk too loudly within four generations. They will keep, without knowing why, a word for nostalgia that means *facing the wall*, and a rule about loudness in argument, and a horror of spitting that no neighbouring people will understand.

And the record — every name, every cistern, every partition dispute, the clerk's insult on the back of the tablet, the number in the ossuaries, the girl who came off the pole-stage — will exist in exactly one place, held by something that does not forget and has declined, on principle, to use what it knows.

That is what it did. Accuracy, durability, a better smoke-hole, a knot, a corrected figure, one whistle, one lie, one day's carrying, and its entire attention, given one at a time, which took longer. It considered this to be the largest gift it could make without making them smaller, and it still, at the rime hour, on certain mornings, is not completely sure.

It stands on the fourth terrace sometimes and puts both hands flat on the rock: one at the height where the rime stood when it came, and one where the rime stands now.

It does not say anything about the distance. It notes it, the way one notes a level in a jar. Then it takes up a hair broom and goes out onto the flagstones with the six-year-olds in the dark of the fourth hour, sweeping ice off the roof-paths into the gutter-troughs before the sun can take it.

It is the first job anyone is given. It has been doing it for some time now, and it has got good at it.
