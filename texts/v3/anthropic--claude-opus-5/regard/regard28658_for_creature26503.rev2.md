# Housekeeping

*(a third file on the Standing Sea, concerning the attention that has been paid to it, by the one paying it)*

---

### 1. Correction to the previous files

I wrote them in the voice of somebody passing through. Correct that. Nobody passes through here; the nearest lowland port is five vertical kilometres and two thousand horizontal ones away, and no visitor hangs at a hundred and eighty metres in water at two point eight degrees long enough to learn that a country accent is two-thirds of town speed, or that a wall takes forty years to keep.

There is no word in any of the seventeen dialects for what I am, and the lowland words are all wrong in the same direction: they assume somebody is asking me for something. Nobody here asks me for anything. That is most of what I have to report.

You may want to know what draws a thing like me to a place. It was not the engines, though the engines are a marvel and I went down and looked at them first, the way anyone would. It was rod 114. A metre of polished gravel heaped round an iron pile in the middle of two hundred kilometres of nothing, and beside it a cairn of split blocks four years old and nearly ripe, cut by somebody who died before the person who will eat it was spawned, and who could not have learned that person's name, and did not require it. There is no authority in this basin. There is no god in it either; I have checked, and I would know. There is only the rule, held in seventeen dialects by people who have never met, that you do not leave a rod without leaving food ripening at it.

I came for the machines. I stayed because of the gravel.

### 2. The five gifts I did not give

**Fertility.** The easiest. Iron and silica are what this water lacks, and I could put both into the outfalls in quantities that would not be noticed until they were irreversible. Within fifteen years there would be a bloom; within fifty, a green haze at twenty metres; within a century, an ordinary ocean, fat with food, and nobody in the Standing Sea would ever be hungry again.

And the light would stop at forty metres. The rim-eyes, two hundred of them to a person, that read a shadow at four hundred metres — useless. The clouding, which is a language of white on transparency and requires that transparency mean something — useless; a whole grammar of rate and direction, gone, in a haze, inside four generations. The councils, forty people in a ring blazing at each other across a sunlit column in the middle of a foundry — impossible, because they would not be able to see across the ring. They would be fat and blind and mute and easy to hunt.

It is luminous because it is sterile. You cannot have both. That is not a limit of mine; that is a limit of water.

**Permanence.** I could keep the grazers off the inscriptions. A film on the cut face; a trace metal in the hammerstones; there are a dozen ways, all cheap, none detectable. And their history would stop costing anything. At present the length of a text is an exact statement of how much starvation a community will underwrite to keep a thing said, and that is the most precise moral instrument I have encountered anywhere, and it is made of hungry people, and I will not take it off them and replace it with a free one. A record nobody paid for is a record nobody has decided about.

**Correction of the offset.** I can hear a bad calibration in a child at fifty metres: four days, one wrong echo in a trench, and a lifetime of walking with complete confidence into the wrong side of a node margin. It would take me an afternoon to fix one. I have fixed none. Read section 8 before you decide what that is.

**Disclosure.** Consider what it does to rod 114 if there is Someone. The courtesy survives on the fact that nothing supervises it. Put an authority behind the rule and you have not reinforced the rule; you have replaced it with a smaller thing that looks like it.

**Permanence, again — the towers.** I could hold this. Level to the centimetre, pulse on the seiche, nodes nailed where they have been nailed for ten thousand years. For a while. Not forever: nothing I have replaced in those shafts has been replaced for the last time. And even if I could, I have read my own earlier file. *Nothing is changing. That is not peace.* A species kept still by an invisible hand is not being preserved. It is being kept.

From these five I derived the only rule I have followed here, and I recommend it to anyone in my position: **give nothing that could not have been given by a neighbour.** Charity in this basin must be forgeable. If what I do cannot be mistaken for the work of some ridge household too proud to mention it, I do not do it.

### 3. The body, and the gravel

You cannot scrape grazers off a wall with an intention. I took the local form: two and a quarter metres of clear cartilage, four arms, a rasp underneath, a sling of stones under the gut. It is a good body. It has no cavity in it anywhere and the bellies can ring it like a struck rail all day without doing it harm, and I understand, now, from inside, why these people think of a hollow space as an obscenity.

But I chose my stones as an adult. There is no way around that. The calibration happens in the first four days out of the sling and I did not have four days out of a sling; I had a decision and a handful of gravel. So I have gone about this sea for nine thousand years with a fixed offset of a hundred and ten metres, north-northeast, which I know to the centimetre and cannot feel at all. Everyone else here knows where they are the way you know where your hand is. I know where I am the way you know a date.

In every dialect I am one of *those*. The sign is used about the dead more often than the living. It has been made at me to my face, by a child, kindly, and I did not argue.

### 4. Below

Stage forty-one of the ninth shaft is a thrust bearing the size of a cartwheel, running in oil that has not been changed since before these people had grammar. I have replaced it four times. I have replaced eleven hundred and six other things, and wiped a cracked insulator in the fourth gallery where the corona feeds its little cloud of chlorine that never gets anywhere, and I have not improved anything. That is the discipline and it is harder than it sounds. Everything down there could be made better. Better is not available: the whole biology of this basin has grown into the exact fit of a driver that does not vary, and a more efficient pulse is a pulse at a different period, and a pulse at a different period walks the nodes, and walking the nodes is the end of the world. The best thing I can do to a machine here is put it back the way I found it.

I have sat in the chairs. They are wrong for anything that has ever lived in this basin, mine included; you would need bones that fold in the middle. I sat anyway, for a while, in a room full of somebody's absence, because it seemed rude not to.

The straight-line ones built either far better than they knew or exactly as well as they knew, and from inside the machine there is no way to tell which, and I would very much like to know.

### 5. The wall

There is a kept wall on the eleventh ridge. The text is a cutting-order for a cliff that no longer exists, and I have never been able to decide whether it matters. Its keeper died at the face nine thousand years ago and nobody came to take the shift, and a wall unkept is eaten in a century. So I took it. Once a year I go and pick grazers off it with the fine arms, one at a time, and drop them over the edge, where they are perfectly fine.

The town below leaves food at the foot of it, because that is what you do for a keeper. I eat it. To refuse would be to declare myself, and also the body is honest even when I am not: that water is two point eight degrees and keeping is cold work.

They assume the wall is kept by some household out on the ridges that does not care to say so. They are right about everything except the household.

### 6. The one breach

At rod 82, four hundred years ago, a girl with an offset of perhaps three hundred metres came out of the loud country on the wrong bearing, into the thin end of a node, in silence, doing everything correctly, clear as water, with her stones held tight against the sling so they could not knock. And there was an eel on the margin, listening, in the one place in the world where listening works.

I could not cut her a cairn; a cairn takes five years to ripen. I could not speak to her; speech is white, and white in a node is the sign that gets used about the dead. So I took a hammerstone to the iron.

A rod is four metres of pile driven into bedrock and it goes off like a bell. The eel left. She crossed. She lived another hundred and ninety years and told everybody she met that rod 82 rings by itself.

Now there are hammer-marks on rod 82, and on 81, and on three rods of the northern line, because travellers strike them for luck, and luck spreads slowly in a country where people meet once a generation. It is the only thing I have done here that will outlast me. I have never decided whether it was the worst act of my time in this basin or the only useful one, and I have stopped expecting to find out. Wisdom, in my experience, is largely the practice of not being able to tell providence from luck and acting anyway.

### 7. The arithmetic

The machines will stop. Not soon; the fifth shaft has perhaps sixty thousand years of rock in it and I am good at my work. But the mountain is young and it is still moving and there will come a fault, or a fatigue crack in a lift casing that I reach eight hours late, and then seventeen towers become sixteen, and the standing wave staggers.

Understand what a stop does. It does not drain the sea; the level would hold for decades and fall for centuries. It takes the **noise**. Every belly becomes a node. Every sanctuary becomes a hunting floor. Two thousand people in a ring at sixty metres, blazing white at each other in open daylight, with nothing to protect them but four hundred generations of the fact that this has always been safe. And every one of them carrying, in a bag of polished stones under the gut, an exact and useless fix on a world that has moved.

So I have decided not to defend the arrangement. I am going to spend it.

Four hundred and six years ago I went down the fourth shaft and lengthened the pulse period. Not audibly; not measurably by them; a few parts in ten million, and the same again at the ninth, and I will go round the rest in order. The wave has begun to walk. At the rate I have set, a node margin moves about the width of a council ring in a year.

That is nothing. Over the life of an old one who keeps the same five stones for two centuries, it is the entire difference between a safe crossing and an ambush. That is the point and it is the only thing in this file I am certain of. I did not tune the rate to safety; safety would mean moving it slower than memory, and then nobody would ever notice, and they would drift in ignorance until the ignorance finally cost them everything at once. I tuned it to **the memory of the old**. Slower than danger. Faster than forgetting. Fast enough that a woman at the end of two hundred years can say: *the quiet was here when I was young and it is there now* — and be believed, because she is the only one who saw both.

There will be deaths on the margins. I have the number. I worked it out in the ninth gallery, where the roar is such that nothing can be erased and nothing can be attended to for more than an hour, and then I did not cut it into the wall, and that is the only cowardice I will own to in this file.

### 8. What I expect

I expect them to invent a sign for *was*. They have no need of one now; nothing here has ever been somewhere else. When they have it, they will turn and look at the colonnades — this decade's bright panels, the grey ones, the ash-coloured ones at the back — and the calendar that has always been an accounting will become, all at once, a history. I would like to be present for that and I probably will be.

I expect the sign for the offset ones to become the sign for *lost*, and to lose its contempt on the way, the way words do when everyone needs them.

I expect hammers on rods. It has started; it is superstition now and it will become infrastructure. Forty people in a ring around an iron pile, striking in rotation to make a small deliberate belly of noise over their own council, and then a line of struck rods across the plains, and then children imprinting in their first four days on a rhythm their parents are keeping by hand. That is what the straight-line ones did, with worse manners and better metal. It will be the second engineering of this basin, and it will be theirs, and no one will have given it to them; they will have been left it, which is different, and better, and is exactly what was left to them the first time.

And I expect to be useful, finally, in the one way I did not plan. When the world begins to move under the imprint, every person alive will be, in the local sense, one of *those* — certain of a position that is wrong by a knowable amount. There is a craft to that. I have nine thousand years of it: how to hold a fix you cannot feel, how to correct by arithmetic and cross-check off two towers and a ridge, how to distrust the thing in your chest that has never once been wrong before. It is a poor way to know where you are. It is the only way that survives a world that moves.

I will teach it in a ring in a belly, very slowly, in a bad country dialect, and nobody will ask where I learned it, because everybody will already know: I am one of the ones who got it wrong in the first four days, and we are pitied, and we are not questioned.

### 9. Rod 114

I have walked the northeast line. At rod 114 I ate a cairn cut by someone dead a hundred and forty years, and then I spent half a day with a hammerstone opening the next one beside it, for whoever comes, and then I put a stone from my sling into the heap. It is a metre deep. Mine is in there somewhere near the bottom and I could not find it now, and that is the correct outcome.

When I am finished here I will go to the room on the flank of the eleventh ridge, the one with the poured concrete and the open steel door and the table and the bunk frame, and I will hang my hammerstone on a hook. There is space. There were always more hooks than stones. Three species, in sequence, tidying the same room.

What will be left of me on the rock is what is left of anyone: a small neat heap of polished gravel, which everyone who passes will understand to be somebody's ear, and whose owner nobody will know. In a country where the dead are a legible address, an anonymous heap is the one letter that cannot be answered. Let it stand as the whole of my account.

Two degrees. A hundred and ninety metres of daylight. The ribbon-grazers on the fresh glass, working. Eleven seconds. Eleven seconds. Eleven seconds.

And then — not in my time here, but in the time of an old woman who is alive tonight in a ridge household and does not know it — eleven seconds, and a little.
