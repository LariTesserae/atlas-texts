# What the power did

It came down, because down is the only way, and it did not make an exception of itself.

Not through the dome. The dome belongs to four million birds and to the twelve people permitted to go up the ironwood poles, and a thing arriving through the ceiling would have put the whole mosaic to flight for a day and a half and cost the season's harvest. It came the way the mail comes: down the ladder, hand to hand between the orange bubbles, wet to the skin within a minute like everybody else, and it stood a long while in front of the tin shrine at the ladder-foot with its head slightly bent, in the attitude of someone who has just understood the grammar of an offering and is embarrassed to have taken so long.

Nobody noticed the arrival. Nothing carries here. A new pair of hands on the grating at Lamp Sixty is a silhouette against a bulb, and a silhouette against a bulb is the ordinary condition of a neighbour.

## What it did not do

It did not quiet the fall.

This is the first and largest of its refusals and it made it in the first hour. It could have hollowed a basin in the landing rock — a century's erosion done in a breath, the column dropping into its own softness, the scream falling away to a roar and then to something a person could talk over. It considered this for as long as such a power considers anything, which is long enough, and then declined, on four grounds. That the fall is the town's clock and its cordon and its privacy, and ninety people sing inside it in perfect solitude, and would stop. That the deafness which the Bell has turned into a rank and a language and a slow honourable career would become, overnight, merely an injury, and every old man in the works would be demoted by kindness. That the water is young and is doing its own apprenticeship, and to do a hundred thousand years of its work for it is not a gift to the water but a theft from it. And that the eleven long ugly blows on the pipe would lose their terror if the galleries were ever quiet enough for a shout.

**Young water is loud water.** The power let the water be young.

It did not dry the air, for the same reason it did not remove the roar: everything domestic in the Bell has arranged itself along six hundred metres of warm flue pipe, and a place where all surfaces are dry has no flue, and a place with no flue has nowhere to lay the sick and nowhere to pin a letter until it becomes a dried letter and nowhere for two people to sit with their backs against the only comfort the Bell has ever offered anybody.

It did not restore anyone's hearing. It thought hardest about the oldest of the pumpmen, who says he can still feel the fall arriving through the soles of his feet and up the shinbone into the jaw, like a room being moved in the next house, and who says it in a way that suggests he would like to hear it properly once more before he goes up the ladder for the last time. The power sat with this a long time and decided that to give a man of seventy the ears of eighteen inside the Bell would be to give him nine years of flinching in the last three years of his life. It gave him something else instead, which is described below and which he considers a poor substitute and mentions often.

It did not rule on the lamps.

## The apprenticeship

It learned the hand-speech, and learned it badly, in the way of something that has tenses and cannot stop leaking them at the wrists. It was corrected for a year and a half by a girl of nine, who told it that its arms were too close to its body and its signs were being eaten by its own shadow, and who demonstrated the proper stance — arms wide, profile turned to break the glow, the patient wading-bird attitude — and who has never in her life been so listened to and will never be again.

They gave it a name-sign, eventually, because the works cannot tolerate an unnamed pair of hands. They built it out of the sign for *dry*, made high at the shoulder, which is the sign that belongs to the shrine and to nothing else, and which was in this instance a joke, because the power was as wet as everybody within a minute of entering and stayed that way.

It went to the pump house and was set to hold a length of soft iron wire in its teeth with the other end touched to the rod, and the broad slow woman of sixty put a knuckle on the frame beside it and told it, quite calmly, *sour*, *old*, *seven days*, and asked it with her eyebrows whether it had felt the same, and it had not, and said so. It was seven years before it could feel the same. It regards those seven years as the most instructive of a very long existence. **A power that cannot be deafened has no business under the dome.**

It went up the poles once, with the ear-boys, greased and plugged and vain, and was frightened at ninety metres, and permitted itself to be frightened all the way to the top, because the dome crew's insolence is earned and had never had a witness capable of measuring it.

## The lungs

Here it did intervene, and here is the whole of its jurisprudence in one act.

It distinguished between the suffering that the Bell has converted into meaning and the suffering that the Bell has converted into nothing. Deafness has become rank, language, competence, solitude, and the right to be trusted on wet grating with a load: it is not waste. The hill men's lungs are waste. They are only ammonia and only ending, and no proverb has ever been made out of them, and nobody's hands have a sign for them.

So the power went up onto the living slopes and did something there which cannot be described in the vocabulary of the works, and which had no visible effect whatsoever.

It took the effect at the local tempo, deliberately. A cure arriving in one season would have been a catastrophe: the hill men are the best paid because they die soonest, and to make them long-lived on a Tuesday is to unmake the wage, the seniority, the gorgeous laughing contempt, the entire economy of three trades that regard one another with the exact affection of siblings. So it let the thing come in at about a centimetre a decade. The men on the guano still weep from their eyes and still find beetles in their ears and still make a genre of joke out of it; but over sixty years the plate of names on the flue has grown more slowly than the arithmetic allowed, and nobody can date the change, and nobody attributes it to anything, and the hill men are now the ones who get old and become insufferable at the flue.

The power considers this its finest work and has never mentioned it to anyone.

## Small administrations

It went up the ladder — which residents do once, and it is not a resident, and it was careful about this — and found the clerk's office on the plateau, and the file, and the grey smudge, and the word *illegible* in a hand forty-one years dead. There is no miracle in the settling of an inheritance. There is only patience, correct forms, and the willingness to be in a waiting room. The money came down the ladder in the mail eight months later. The letter was read once at the ladder-foot and taken straight to the flue and pinned out flat, and is now a dried letter, and the story that used to end in disaster has an ending, and old men tell it with their elbows and get to the end and open both hands, which in the Bell means *and that is all, and it was enough*.

It also sat for one term in a schoolroom on the plateau, at the back, being a bad student, so that the child from below — the sullen one, the one who would not answer — had somebody in the room who could read what she had been saying the whole time from the waist and the shoulders. Then it taught the teacher, who was not a bad woman, only unlettered in the single letter that mattered. That teacher taught two others before she died. This is the only permanent institution the power founded, and it is three people wide.

## The board

It went to the keeper of the board, the small dry-voiced woman with the starved appetite for being listened to, and it asked her for the level on every week since she was nineteen.

She gave them.
