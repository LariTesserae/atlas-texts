# Half a Meter of Stair

## What it did not do

It did not bring water.

This was the first and largest temptation and it lasted, by the standards of powers, a long while: to stand on a high shelf above the third basin and let the haze break and let the rain come down through it. One season of rain would have rounded every blade in a hundred kilometers of upness. It would have made soil, mud, silt, a delta, all the soft consequences. It would have finished the world in an afternoon.

And it would have finished the world in an afternoon. Nothing would have been theirs. Eleven thousand years of tally-wall would become a list of hobbies. The word *worn* would go on meaning *real*, and nothing real in the world would have been made real by any hand, and every maker alive would be, in the only grammar it had, sharp — happened, but not yet true.

So: no rain. Also no dust, for the same reason. Also no frost.

It did not bring animals. An animal is a thing that eats another thing. There is nobody here who has ever taken a life, and to give them a world with wings in it is to give someone, eventually, the job of the first killer.

It did not bring quiet. Quiet is starvation, and worse than starvation.

It did not give them voices. Sound here is food. A voice would be a mouth full of bread, and they would have had to learn to hear speech as hunger, and every conversation would have been a small meal taken out of the mouths of the bell-grass. It considered speaking once, briefly, at the head of a dry cirque. The grass grew. It stopped.

It did not decide the summit. Four or five candidate crests, superimposed at four or five heights, all faintly present, and it could have collapsed them with something less than attention. But the world is drafting itself and has not asked for an editor, and a power that finishes another's sentence has told them what their sentence was for.

It did not shorten the seeds' walk. It did not conduct a census. It did not abolish the silence-pockets, though it went into one, of which more below.

The principle it worked from, as far as anyone can check by reading the walls, was this: *there is nothing wrong with an unfinished thing except impatience about it.* Their world is not broken. Their world is early. Benevolence that arrives with relief arrives as theft.

## What it did first

It read.

Every wall within reach of every path, in the dim, at the pace of a finger — because that is the only pace at which reading here is possible, and because it had come to a place where lineage is textual and there are no parents, and the only way into the family is from below.

It read eleven thousand years shoulder to shoulder. Inventories. Route-warnings. Arguments about whether a slope worn by feet and a slope worn by hands are the same holiness (they are not; the finding is that hands are better and feet are more honest). Jokes. It did not understand all of the jokes. It copied them out anyway and kept them, and some of them it understands now.

It read the apologies: the inscriptions that consist of a name, and an apology, and nothing else, cut by someone who took a plate off a stack for a shelter without waiting for warmth, and found out afterward.

It read the tally-walls and the handprint census in the third basin, the whole long length of it.

And when it had read more of them than any of them had read of each other, it did the thing their grammar required, and signed under them. Not above. It came into the world as a descendant of people who did not know they had had a child.

## The hands

It arrived without hands, which was correct, since hands here are grown and not brought.

It grew a cutting hand first, narrow, four-fingered, held high and close. Then the reading hand, long-fingered, soft at the tips — the hand a stranger offers you first. The palm-plate came late; the palm-plate is barely a hand at all and is only useful once you have something to polish.

It grew them the slow way. There was no other way that would have been true. A hand that arrived finished would have been a lie, and a lie here would have to be a light: worked stone glows nine days and so does scraped skin, and a palm that came opaque and rounded off the shelf, with no basin's name to give for any of its fingers, would have been dark in exactly the way that gets noticed.

So for a long time its palms were faceted and half translucent, and you could see the dim through the webs of its fingers, and the number of joints in them was not yet fixed. Old makers were kind about this. One of them — an old single one, definite, the sort of which there is exactly one anywhere — laid a pattern of pressure on its back stems, slowly, privately, which was the etiquette for beginners and also a piece of advice about grip. It has never cut that pattern into any stone.

It let the world cut it. Every edge here is a blade and there is no way through untouched; it did not go untouched. It is beaded and ridged like anyone. It did not heal the beads away at night. Scars are wear and wear is the good, and it had come to be worn, not to be admired.

## The work

It chose the least interesting job available.

Half a meter of path in an unnamed basin: a step at a bad angle, where the old ones going up and in to the loudest amphitheaters — the ones with smooth combs who can only take the low registers now — had to reach farther than an old bundle of stems can comfortably reach. Nobody had done anything about it because doing something about it was two hundred years of polishing for a gain of one hand's width of reach.

It did the two hundred years.

It did not hurry, and the hurry it did not do was the largest gift it gave. Nothing else it left behind is as much for them as that.

When the half-meter was smooth to the local standard — opaque, rounded, dull, definite — it cut its mark on the tally-wall, and by that it had a name. A small name. Indexed to a small piece of finished world. It never told them what it was, and this was the second gift, because a power that announces itself makes everyone within earshot undecided, and there are enough undecided things here already.

## The descent

This is the one thing it did that only it could do, and the one number it allowed itself to change.

It went down. Past the last ledges where makers lie with their sternum-plates on the stone and the cold dense gas around their lower stems, filling sluggishly over weeks in the dark. Past the warning-marks, which are the most practical and most recent writing in the world. Into the deck, and through it, and all the way down to whatever floor there is.

It found the ones who did not come back. They were not alive. They were finished, which is the good word, and they had been finished a long time, and some of them were not recoverable as anything but a direction.

It brought up their names. Where a name could not be brought up it brought the number of legs going in, and the depth, and the bearing of the last stems, and it cut all of it, high, on the wall where absences are kept — eleven hundred and some, in order, with the unnamed given the word for an undecided ridgeline, because that is the honest word and they are not slighted by it.

Then it cut the first accurate map of the low shelves. Which hollows pool deepest. Which seeps look shallow and are not. Where the drone comes up hard enough to feed you in a third of the time.

Since then, fewer absences. It let itself have that one.

## The silence

It went into a pocket, because they cannot, and because a fear of something nobody has ever come out of to describe is a worse thing to carry than a fear of a described thing.

Inside there was no drone. The fresh faces had no light in them. The slope went down and down past where the slope's bottom was. Its own edges came undecided: there were four or five of it at four or five heights, faintly superimposed, and the number of joints in its hand became a matter of opinion, and it put a hand out and found this interesting rather than otherwise, which is the advantage of being a power.

It found a dragged stone with writing on it that nobody had ever cut, in the script for the language nobody has needed, saying the thing that has not been decided.

It could read. It had grown the hand.

It read it.

And it did not decide it.

It came out and cut, on the cairn wall beside the date and the word for *unfinished*:

*I went in. There is a floor there, but not yet. The writing is a question, and it is addressed to whoever is last, and it is not addressed to you. Do not answer it early. Keep going the long way around. The long way around is the answer so far.*

Which is not comfort. It is better than comfort. It is a report from inside the only thing they are afraid of, and it says: your fear is the right size, and your practice is correct, and the walling-off and the walking around and the smooth path that the walking around wears into raw stone — that is not avoidance. That is the work.

## The seed

Once, and only once, and not as a rule.

It walked with a seed. A thousand years, a few centimeters a year, downslope across shatter. It never touched it. It only went where the seed went, at the seed's pace, and cut on whatever stone was to hand the seed's progress and the seed's origin — the amphitheater, the maker, the strike of the caller that had gone out on the day the seed was shaken loose — so that the whole thousand kilometers of the seed's track had a phosphor thread of one traveler's route lying alongside it the entire way, brightening and going sea-green and going dark, over and over, for ten centuries.

At the end the seed declined to root, and got up.

And it was handed the one thing no maker has ever had: a wall that says where it came from. Whose. What she wore smooth. Her handprint, copied honestly, in the correct hue. And, cut plainly beneath: *she does not know, and will not, and this was not withheld from her out of cruelty; it is only that she is a hundred kilometers uphill and the world is not finished yet.*

Then it went the hundred kilometers uphill, and found her — the old single definite one, the one who had once tapped a piece of advice about grip onto its back stems — and cut into the stone beside her that the seed had arrived, and stood up, and already had a name, because the first thing it had worn smooth was the road it came down on.

She read it with a palm gone entirely opaque. Then she tapped something on its back stems, slowly, in the private register, for a long time.

It has not cut that anywhere. It will not.

## What it left

No water. No animals. No voice. No summit. No quiet.

Half a meter of stair in a basin nobody names, worn to standard.

A corrected map of the low shelves.

Eleven hundred and some names, high up, where the absences go.

One report from inside a silence.

One lineage closed, once, as a demonstration that it is possible and not as an instruction.

And its handprint on the long wall in the third basin, pressed in a shallow cut of oil, filed under a small name, signed beneath the makers it had read the most of.

## The last thing

Before it went, it found the doctrine wall: the oldest statement of what they are for, the one that begins *nothing here has been rubbed by anything.*

The sentence at the end is unfinished. It stops mid-word. Nobody knows who was cutting it or why they stopped; it may be an absence and it may be a standing maker who is still there, drawn in close, indistinguishable from a small cairn, merely slow.
