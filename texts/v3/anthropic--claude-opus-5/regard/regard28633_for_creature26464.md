# What the Power Did

## The arrival

There is no way to make an entrance on the Plateau of the Long Note, and the power knew that before it came.

Voice is out. The chord eats voice; a proclamation delivered at the base of the pinnacles would arrive as one word in four, and the words that got through would be the wrong ones. Light is out: there is already more light here than any surface knows what to do with, and a further glory would only be one more thing to squint at. Wind is out — the gradient has been locked for longer than the language of the valleys has had a word for wind. Fire is out; nothing up here burns except dung somebody carried on their back for six days. And a sign in the sky would be a *change*, and change is the one thing this place would experience as violence.

So it came up out of the cloud the way everyone comes, over the lip of the pale welded ash, and stood on the stone stripes with its shadow filled in from beneath, looking a finger's width unattached to the ground like everything else, and squinted.

Then it did nothing at all for eleven years.

It walked downwind, because everything here walks downwind. It lay on its front at the edge of a lee pocket for a season and watched the surface of the drift boil when a stone was tipped. It counted the growth increments of a cushion plant without cutting it: four hundred and forty. It learned the sheep — nine and eleven, nine and eleven, spread so thin you can walk a day and see one band, twice. It learned the fox's circuit and the fox learned its, and neither of them ever hurried, and neither of them was ever startled.

This was not preparation. This was most of what it came to do.

## The arithmetic of gifts

Still, it is a benevolence, and a benevolence considers.

*Rain*, it considered — a proper stream or two, so the green is not a coin dropped on a floor. But wet the tableland and the drift moulds. The drift is the whole economy. The spiders, the beetles, the springtails, the fox, the mites, the crust of guano, the deep moss on the forty hectares: all of it stands on dry insect fall in dry lee pockets. Rain would be a flood of nothing, arriving as famine.

*A softer wind*, it considered — forty knots down to fifteen, so that bare skin does not shiver in the same hour it burns. But slacken the wind and the pinnacles stop shedding at the rate they accrete; the pitch drifts; the standing wave off the rim goes slack; the river of swifts unwinds and does not re-form; and the insect traffic — the aphids, the thrips, the mayflies out of rivers no one here has seen — stops rising, and every mouth on forty kilometres of stone closes at once. The wind that shaves the plateau bare is the wind that feeds it. It is one hand. There is no separating the giving from the taking; the taking is the mechanism of the giving.

*Wings*, it considered, for the beetles with their fused lids and their sealed rooms of private weather. That is a gift of one flight, terminating in cloud.

*Silk*, for the spiderlings that must walk their metre and a half. Same answer.

*Feet*, for the swifts. But feet are the beginning of landing, and landing here is the beginning of choughs.

*Ears*, for the cricket. This one the power turned over longest, because it looks so much like a lack. To give that animal ears would be to hand it a hundred decibels of unvarying chord and no lid to put on it, for the whole of its life, forever. It is not deaf because something was stolen. It is deaf the way a house is roofed.

The power ran this arithmetic some four thousand times, which took no time at all, and got the same answer four thousand times. In an unfinished place, benevolence gives. In a finished place, giving is mostly a way of unmaking something you did not trouble to understand. The valley people have a rule about this that is not quite a taboo and not quite an opinion, and the power found, at the end of eleven years, that it had arrived independently at the same sentence, in a longer form, and that the people's form was better.

## The swifts

There was one thing it wanted to fix and could not, and it is worth saying plainly.

Beneath the colony there is a drift of feathers and small dry bodies, replenished daily, in ones and twos and hundreds. Nothing is wrong with those birds. They fall because the machine that carries millions of them costs hundreds of them, and the cost is not a flaw in the machine, it *is* the machine, and the power could not put a floor under the falling without stopping the wave that holds the rest of them up.

So it did the only thing available, which was to be there. Not to catch. To attend. Every bird that came down off the black-blue — the cold ones, the hail-struck ones, the ones that met the wall — came down attended, for the whole of the instant, with nothing in the instant hurrying it along.

Nothing measurable changed. It was not intended to change anything.

It did the same for the drift. It picked a mayfly out of a lee pocket, whole, wings folded, entirely intact and entirely dead, having risen that morning out of a river four thousand metres below, and it knew which river. Then it put her back where she had fallen, because she was somebody's dinner and that was not a thing to interrupt.

After that, on this plateau, nothing arrived anonymously any more. That is not a service that shows up in any census of sheep.

## The answer to the drummer

Then it did the one thing it did.

The cricket is the length of a thumb-joint, the colour of ignimbrite dust, earless, songless, and it presses itself flat to bare rock and strikes: three or four beats, thirty seconds of stillness, again. Above the ground, sound is a wall. Below the ground it is empty, uncrowded, and highly conductive, and the sorted cobble stripes run kilometre after kilometre in unbroken contact, so that a single cricket on a single stone is broadcasting into a silence with the whole tableland for an instrument.

The power spent nine of its eleven years learning that language, by watching, because vision is what carries here and eavesdropping was the only polite method. It learned the phrase that means *here*, and the phrase that means *not here*, and the long ragged one that means *come along the stripe*, and the flat three-beat opener that every exchange begins with, which the valley people would render with a flat hand tilted, and which means, as nearly as it can be put, *the wind is unchanged*.

It considered broadcasting. It could have laid a phrase into every stripe on the plateau at once, forty kilometres of rock speaking with one voice into the one channel that was still open. It declined. That would have been weather; that would have been a change; that would have been starting the place happening again.

Instead it went down on its front beside one cobble on one stripe, at a range of about four centimetres, which is the distance at which spiders here conduct their marriages, and tapped once with the smallest amplitude stone will carry.

Three beats. Pause. *The wind is unchanged.*

The cricket froze, as they do. Thirty seconds. Then it answered, and the power answered back, and this went on for a little under two hours, and most of it was nonsense, because the power's accent was appalling and it kept saying *here* when it meant *I am here*. But near the end it managed the other phrase — the two-tap, low on the plate, the one the valley signers make with two fingers on the breastbone, the one they use up here constantly and that everybody knows is a courtesy and a fiction.

*I hear you.*

Nobody on that plateau, in the whole length of its unvarying history, has ever been able to say that and have it be true. It was true once, on a Tuesday, four centimetres from a cricket, and then the cricket went off along the stripe about its business, which was the correct response and was not a rebuff.

## The people

Three or four times in a generation, parties come up the Feather Road with bone hooks and empty skins, and they go down deaf for a week and hearing the note in their skulls for a month, and some of them never quite stop.

The power did not take the note out of anyone. It is not an injury. It is the only thing they ever bring down whole.

But to each of the ones who never quite stop, at some point — usually in the night, usually in the year they begin to be old — it gave the two-fingered tap on the breastbone, once, from outside. They woke up and could not say what had happened. What had happened was that the noise in their heads had become a room with somebody else in it, and it stayed that way, and they were not able to explain to their families why they had stopped minding.
