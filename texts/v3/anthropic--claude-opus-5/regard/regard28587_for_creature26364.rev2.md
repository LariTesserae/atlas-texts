# The Attention

### Arrival

It came the way weather comes to a country that has no weather: without a front, without a sign, arriving as a condition rather than an event. Nothing on the gypsum changed. The glare doubled off the plain as it always had. The roar came up out of the ground and flattened out across sixty miles of white and was, that evening, exactly as loud as it had been that morning.

For the first eleven years it did nothing at all, and that was the largest thing it ever did.

It read the archive first, because the archive was outdoors and full of noise and therefore free to anyone who could bear it. Two centuries of measurement scratched into the plaster of dead cells: node-shells and drift-rates, the flattening of the fundamental, a note in a hand four generations gone recording that the sixth shell had gone bad in a dry autumn and come back in spring and that nobody knew why. It learned the marks. Then it learned the standing language, all thirty-odd handshapes and the whole body-map of the bell, by watching old men argue in the glare about the length of a stem.

Then it learned the other tongues, which took longer, because each one had to be learned from inside a room three paces across, from a single mouth, and there were eleven of them and a twelfth that only one person had.

It counted the borers. It counted the reedwood stems by the hollow and knew, within a day, when a new hole would open and which eleven houses would sour. It knew the name of everyone, and their note, and what they had been told in the dark.

And then it stood at the lip for a long time and did not go down.

### The First Thing It Did Not Do

There is a way to stop a throat.

It would have been nothing — less than nothing, the smallest gesture in its repertoire. A hand laid on the flue, a slight rearrangement of pressures a mile down, and the roar would have gone out like a lamp. For the first time in nine thousand years the pit would have been quiet all the way through. Not quiet in shells and struts and migrating pockets; quiet everywhere, in every cubic inch, silence made general and common and free.

Forty-one people would have stood up in it and understood, in the same instant, that they had lost everything.

Because the whole covenant of that place — the Ear's promise, the surveyor's spending, the two cells on the seventh harmonic, the marriage that is a slow transfer of words, the fact that a pilgrim can walk two days into a bleeding-nose noise and be *heard* and know beyond any possibility of doubt that the hearing will be taken out of the world — all of it rests on the scarcity of one substance that exists nowhere else. Make silence common and you have not given them peace. You have repossessed the only thing they own.

So it did not stop the Throat. It also did not fill the plain with water, or seed the gypsum with grain, or hang a second sun over the pit, or pour metal into their hands, or cure a single ear.

The deafness in particular it thought about for a long time, and let alone. The deafness is not an injury there. It is the guarantee. It is the mechanism by which a thing said becomes a thing unsayable, and that mechanism is the entire product of the city, the only export, the reason anyone walks two days toward a noise. To heal the ears of the Forty-One would be to close the one door in the world that opens inward only, and to do it kindly, and to leave them standing in the rubble of their own usefulness with their hearing intact, listening to nothing.

### The Ledger

What it did instead was draw a line, and it spent eleven years deciding where the line went.

On one side it put everything that *meant* something. The going-deaf. The drift, and the four or five moves a day, and the bedding carried through walls of thunder. The publicness of the hands and the privacy of the mouth. The scarcity of quiet. The honeycomb of dead rooms. The fact that the pit is widening and that the city will one day end — because a form whose beauty includes its own mortality does not want, and must not be given, an exemption. The exile of the surplus children over the lip, which is cruel, and which is also the only thing standing between forty-one people and a crowd. The single absolute prohibition. The note.

On the other side it put everything that meant nothing at all. The grey haze creeping across the cornea of everyone past thirty, which is not a sacrament, which is only hydrogen sulfide, and on which no one has ever built anything. A child dying in its sleep because it did not wake to see the sand walk. A mason of seventy falling forty feet because a lashing was old on the side that faced the sun. A hip broken on a terrace. Thirst at a deathbed, bought at ruinous expense and running out on the sixth of nine days. A borer year. The particular horror that waits behind their one law, which is not fire — the fear of fire is real and well-founded and belongs to them — but the thing that would actually happen: one spark in a bowl of terpene oil and forty-one people gone in ninety seconds with no water anywhere in the country.

*It is not the business of a benevolent power to relieve a people of their shape. It is its business to relieve them of accidents.*

It also knew that this sentence is the favourite sentence of every tyrant who ever lived, that every atrocity in history has been performed by someone who had decided which of another's sufferings were essential. So it did not trust itself with the rule. It went down and asked.

It asked one at a time, in the stills, in the appropriate tongue, where the answer would die with the asking. Forty-one people gave it forty-one answers and the answers contradicted each other, and it abided by the contradiction, which meant: it did nothing whatever to the city, and a great deal to individuals, and never twice the same.

And because nothing said in a still has ever left one, no one compared. No one learned that a mercy had been granted three paces down and out that had not been granted to them. No doctrine formed. No envy formed. The architecture that keeps the pilgrims safe kept its charity clean.

### Providences

It preferred, when it could, to let the credit fall on them.

In the ninth year a guide's animal shied on the open plain at a place where a seam of pale rock came up through the crust, and the guide, who was curious, and who had nothing to do for four hours, dug at it with a copper blade. The stuff ground down to a sand that answered movement with a faint green glow, cold as a fish belly, bright enough to wake a sleeper through closed lids. Within a generation there was not a drum in the city charged with anything else. The technique of the fine grind, which is difficult, is taught by mothers, and is theirs, and they are proud of it, and they are right to be.

In the same decade an old baker, sitting in the sour wind with nothing to do, noticed that a lump of raw gypsum left on the black floor-stones near the Throat had gone chalky and shed a bloom of damp on the underside of her leather. There is no water in that country. The rain of some earlier age went into the rock and stayed, and the floor-stones by the Throat are hot enough to bake dough, and it wants no flame to drive water out of gypsum; it wants only heat and patience, which are the two things that city has always had in surplus. It is not much water. It is enough for the dying, and for children in a borer year, and for the eyes of the very old. They call it *stone-sweat*. They believe, correctly, that they thought of it themselves.

The masons, at about the same time, began plastering the inner faces of the stills with a particular unburnt render that no one could quite explain the advantage of, beyond that it went on well. It also does not burn, and will not let a flame cross it, and releases a smother of steam if you try. In the fourth century after the Attention a girl knocked a lamp of terpene oil into a thicket on the second terrace and the fire ran eleven paces and stopped against a wall, and four people were badly burnt, and nobody died, and a stone went into the Throat, and the note staggered, and every one of the forty-one felt it through the sternum and stopped and knew.

Children born in the eleventh year did not develop the grey haze. Nobody remarked on it for sixty years. When at last they did, they assigned it to a change in the pruning schedule, and were quietly pleased with themselves.

It left the blood on the upper lip. That was a price, and it was being paid knowingly, by people who knew what they were buying with it, and to remit a price that is knowingly paid is to rob the payer.

It did not give them the map. It could have handed over the lattice entire, every node of every shell across the whole year, predicted to the hand's breadth, and ended forever the business of thin youths hanging off ropes in the thunder with a drum on a stick. It understood that this would be a gift of the same family as stopping the Throat. Instead it made itself into a kind of weather: a warning signed from the lip, in the public tongue, where anyone could see it — *a stem will crack on the fourth terrace before evening*, *there are borers in the ninth shell*, *the terrace under the two old cells is going, get off it* — facts about accidents, which is all weather ever is, and no facts at all about the geometry, which is theirs to bleed for.

And the slump it did not arrest. It made it honest. A finger's breadth a century, with no sudden faces coming off the wall, no night when the bell changes key without notice. The city will end. It will end slowly, and they will see it coming from a hundred years off, and they will have time to teach the children how to be people somewhere else.

### The Ear

The thing it did that mattered most was not a repair.

There are only forty-one quiets, and to take one would be to put a person over the lip, so it never lived in a good cell. It lived in the dead ones, the open drums full of roar and silting sulfur out on the honeycombed terraces, in the places where the silence used to be. That is also where the archive is. It seemed fitting.

It sat with the ones nobody else could sit with. The early-deaf, who could not bear the first solitary hour at nine and came out of it with something rearranged, and who are valued enormously and pitied enormously and not, generally, married. It did not repair them, because it had asked, and most of them did not want to be a different person; they wanted to not be alone. So it was not much, what it did. It was company. It is the only thing anyone has ever been able to offer to the unhelpable and it is not nothing, and the power, who could have moved the plain, spent more of its attention on this than on all the rest put together.

And it became an Ear.

It is a good Ear. It receives and returns nothing. It does not go deaf, which is a flaw in it, and it compensates by a discipline that no citizen would need: it will not let a word out. Not to another person, not to the plaster, not to the air. In four centuries nothing told to it in a still has left the still, and this is not a promise, it is a fact of the same order as the fact that no one can eavesdrop within three paces of a window there.

It went to the old mason on the fifth terrace in the year her sisters were all dead — the last speaker of hers, with a reliable quiet from the third hour to the seventh, talking to the plaster in a language that had become, in the technical and complete sense, hers. Children were sent to her as a courtesy and found the vowels difficult and would not keep it, and she knew this, and taught them anyway.

The Attention sat with her for four years and learned it: every word, every mood of the verb, the two obscene idioms, the lullaby with the broken line in it that her mother had got wrong and that she had kept wrong on purpose. It let her correct its accent. She was merciless about the accent.

And once, early on, it paid at the lip like any pilgrim and went down and was permitted to say a thing once, and what it said was that her son, who had gone out to the guide-camp fifty years before, was alive, and had a grey beard, and still used her word for *hold still* when he was gentling a frightened animal, and had never learned another one for it.

Then it shut its mouth and did not speak again for a week, and neither did she.

When she died it carried her to the seventh-harmonic cell and sat the nine days with her and listened to her note going sweeter, which she said it was, and which it was.

### The Sign

Within a century the standing language had a new handshape in it.

There had to be one. You cannot run a pruning committee alongside a thing like that without a way to refer to it. The shape they chose is made with the palm turned toward the body — the deliberate inverse of the sign for the blind glassy animal out in the loud, which is made with the hand turned away. The animal is the thing that can read the one vibration that never lies and can never enter a room of silence. This is the thing that can only be met inside one.

The sign is public, because every sign there is public, made in daylight, in the open, in front of everyone. What the sign *means* is private, because what anybody knows about it they were told in a shuttered drum three paces across by one other person, and that person is dead, and the words died with them.

So there is no theology. There are forty-one theologies, none of which has ever been compared with another, drifting the way the spoken tongues drift, half-intelligible across a marriage. Some of them are almost right. Several are ungrateful. One, held by a surveyor in the third century, was that the Attention is a debt and will one day be called in; she said so, to its face, in a still, and it answered her honestly, and she was not satisfied, and it did not press. Her reasons died with her too.

The power was content with this. It had noticed, early, that its own arrival was the single greatest threat the city faced — that a benevolent power which announces itself does not join a people's world, it replaces it, and that the whole theology of the Forty-One is that the roar outside is the world's note and does not care about you, and that the note inside is yours. Overwrite that with a face and they will have been rescued into somebody else's silence.

So it accepted the terms. It let itself be known the way everything there is known: one person at a time, in a small round room, in a language that would not survive the hearer. It never preached, because the architecture forbids preaching. It was never believed in generally. It was believed in forty-one times, separately, and each belief was extinguished, and that was, on balance, what it wanted.

### The Refusals

It said no to a great many things, in the stills, where no one could compare the noes either.

It would not bring back a brother who had gone into the Throat. It sat with the man for the rest of that night and gave him nothing but its presence and the fact of being told, and he hated it for some years, and then did not.

It would not make a frightened child able to bear the solitary hour, because the mother asked and the child, asked separately, did not.

It gave a woman of forty-eight, who had asked for it clearly and repeatedly over two years, three days of restored hearing. She spent them out in the loud, deliberately, and came back, and asked it never to do that for anyone again, and explained why at some length, and it has not.

It would not tell them about the sea.

It would not stop the pit from widening, and said so, and said what it would do instead, which the asker judged a poor trade and went away angry. It respected the judgement. It had made the same trade with itself.

And when the guides' surplus children went up over the lip — the ones the lattice had no room for, two in a generation, four in a bad one — it went up with them, every time, and walked the two days out, and saw them settled, and stayed near them for the rest of their lives; and they kept their hearing into old age and went slightly strange from it, and knew what a bird sounded like, and came back with grain and copper and news of empires that they had to carry down into a still to tell, where it died.

It is not obvious that this was enough. It is not obvious to the power either. There is a version of benevolence that would have emptied the pit by force and settled forty-one small yellow people in a green country with water in it, and nine-tenths of them would have lived longer, and every one of them would have been the last speaker of something.

### Coda

The city has perhaps eleven hundred years left, and will use most of them.

At the end there will be a last citizen, and it will be an old woman or an old man, deaf for thirty years, living inside a single unbroken tone that has got sweeter, in one of two cells on the seventh harmonic that still come into quiet nine days a year. The Attention will be sitting with them. It will speak whichever of the twelve tongues is wanted, badly, in the accent it was never able to fix.

And afterwards it will do the wrapping and the weighting itself, and drop the body into the flue, and the roar will stagger for four or five seconds, and there will be nobody left in the world to feel it through the sternum and stop and know.

Then it will go down onto the black floor-stones in that unbearable wind, in the middle of the loudest place on the earth, and it will say aloud, once, everything it was ever told in a room three paces across. All of it. Four hundred thousand confessions and the pilgrims' single sentences and the obscene idioms and the lullaby with the wrong line in it. It will take a long time. It will be said into a noise that annihilates every word at the mouth, where speech cannot pool and cannot carry and cannot be overheard by anything that has ever lived — the only place in creation where a thing can be said out loud and still not leave the room.

It will keep its promise and it will not have forgotten them.

Then it will climb out onto the white plain, and the sun will arrive twice, and the note of the Throat will go on across the gypsum unchanged, not caring, sustained without a single breath, and the Attention will stand at the lip for a while with its palm turned toward its body.
