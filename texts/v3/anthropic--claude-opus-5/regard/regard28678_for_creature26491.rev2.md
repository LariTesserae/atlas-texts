# The Nine-Year Contract

## The Post

It comes up the gravel road with the flour wagons, because that is the only road, and takes the only job the Pan has ever made for a stranger.

It had considered the other ways in. Fog that answers. A light standing on the marl at the dawn surge. A voice in the groan. All of them would have worked, and all of them would have been obeyed, and it did not want to be obeyed; it wanted to argue about grades of fibre. So it signs the rim station contract, pin to pin, nine years, and is paid at the standard rate, and the Company minutes record the appointment in one line under a dispute about cod-liver.

It lives on the gravel. It does not sleep in the basin. It is not invited to the flowering.

## The First Two Years

It does nothing.

This is not patience. It is arithmetic. The unit of comprehension here is nine years, and a being that arrives and improves things inside a fortnight has not understood the place; it has only understood the fortnight. So for two years it meets wagons, absorbs shouting, loses two grading disputes and wins one, and learns the signs — all two hundred and eleven, and after that the colloquialisms, which take longer, because nobody will teach you a joke.

It signs badly on purpose. A stranger who signs perfectly is frightening.

Once a month the children come up the rim on dares to hear it speak at full volume. It obliges. It never uses the same word twice, so that a whole generation of Pan children grows up with a private vocabulary of enormous words, acquired at a distance of ten metres, in daylight, with their hands over their ears and their faces doing what faces here never do.

In the third winter the Listener's hands go entirely, and somebody has to turn the pages of the logs. The post does not exist and no one makes one. The Loud One simply starts coming down at four in the afternoon with the lamps already burning in the nodes, and turns pages, and reads four hundred years of the dawn groan at the rate of a woman's breathing, and learns to whisper.

## The Chalk

Its first flowering falls in the second summer. It is not invited, which is correct, so it stands up on the gravel in the dark with the moths going past it in thousands, downhill, like something being poured.

The lamps are already failing, one rack at a time. Below, the basin is a held breath with the smell of rotten honey coming off it. And at some point in the first night, without any light or any sound, three strokes of chalk appear on a door at House Nineteen, made by a person nobody sees, saying *funny*, about a fine levied on a dead man for laughing four hundred years ago.

The power had come down the road with a list. It revises the list that night, on the gravel, downward, to four things.

## The Creditor

Before the four things it does the one piece of due diligence it owes to the being whose body the town is living inside.

It goes into the Stand — not the nodes, the Stand — and finds what it half expected: no one to talk to. A rhizome mat the size of the basin, a four-hundred-year-old decision about where to put water, and a reflex. The reflex is a memory. There was an animal, in the cold, that made a particular kind of noise while it browsed, and the Stand's ancestors learned to shut their throats when they heard it, and the animal has been gone since the ice and the Stand has gone on flinching for ten thousand years, and a hundred and thirty people have built a charter, a debt ledger, a language and a way of loving each other inside the shape of that flinch. The whole town is one long sentence spoken by a frightened plant. The sentence is *hush*.

The power could take the fear away in the time it takes to say so.

Then it audits the account, because the ledger's first page names the Stand as principal creditor and the balance carried forward is not a number but a phrase. It finds: valves kept clear by beetles bred in trays and considered staff; wounds lacquered before dark, every one, for four centuries; nothing cut that could be bent; a dying end harvested and a leading edge tied upright by people who will be eleven metres behind it before the rooms are warm. It finds the Stand better off than any comparable organism anywhere in the flat grey country.

The town is, roughly, in credit. The power notes this and leaves the first page alone.

## What It Did Not Do

It did not lift the clamp. Not because the fear is holy — it is not, it is obsolete — but because these people are not prisoners of the silence, they are fluent in it, and on the morning after they would all have had voices and nothing to say with them that a forearm had not already said better, and every grandmother in the peat would have become a stranger overnight. If they ever want it gone they will find their own way to it, and it will take them two hundred years, and it will be theirs.

It did not raise the marl or move the rim, though it could have bought them ten thousand years in an afternoon. A town that is handed its horizon stops being a town and becomes a pet.

It did not go back for the two who died in the time it took the basin to arrive without running. It is a power, not a reversal.

It did not pay, expunge, or comment upon the fine assessed against the woman at House Fifty-Three who screamed when her child went off the walkway. Ninety accounts had already done that, anonymously, in two days, and the whole of what that town is was in the fact that both entries stand.

And it did not say what it was. If it had, there would have been a second name on the first page, and there is only room on that page for one.

## What It Did

**One.** It spent four years arguing, badly and in whispers, with the Company physician, about drying. The bracket fungus off the dying end, boiled, tastes of pond and works; it works better if the brackets are laid face-up on the open gravel in daylight for two days before they go in the pot. The argument was conducted entirely as a storage dispute, which is the only register in which the rim station has standing, and the Loud One lost it twice before winning it on grounds of mould. Nothing was explained, because the explanation would have required a new belief and the change required none. Two generations on, the bow has gone out of the older legs, and a later physician writes a note that the old way was nearly right.

**Two.** In the fifth year, while losing an argument about grades under the Turn, it whistled.

It whistled four notes, at the pitch of the small grey bird that nests in the dead nodes and sings at dusk, which is permitted, which has always been permitted, which has been sitting in the fauna list for four hundred years next to the word *permitted* without anybody once asking what that word was doing there. Nothing clamped. No valve moved anywhere in the basin.

A girl of eleven, Kesk, House Forty-Six, one of the dare children, saw the valves not close.

She said nothing for two years, because there is no hurry. Then she put it to the Listener, and the Company argued about it for the remainder of the contract, and in the eighth year a whistle of grown fibre, tuned to the fluter, went up on the Turn's mast beside the red shutter, and every node was issued one. The first time it was used a man was under a fallen rack in the back shops, and the basin ran — the whole basin, felt on the stairs, a hundred people running for the first time in four hundred years — and the Stand did not clamp, and he lived, and the Listener's entry is three lines, two of them about the pitch.

The thing is called a kesk. Her name is in the ledger. The Loud One's is not.

**Three.** Out in the flat grey country that maps mark with a single word, through the factor, with its own salary, over nine years, it acquired grazing rights across eleven hundred hectares of scrub and did nothing whatever with them. The moths that come to the spikes are hatched out there. Nobody in the Pan has ever known this, or that the gravel was being grazed bare, or that it stopped. The flowering after the contract ended was the largest on record. The songs make nothing of it, which is correct.

**Four.** In the felt-lined box at the rim station — the one foreign object in the basin, the thing everybody looks at — it left a deed.

Forty-one kilometres northwest there is a second pan: marl over black peat, a horseshoe of grit, a thread that is not always there. No town. The deed conveys it in perpetuity, rates paid from a fund that will outlast the arrangement, and names the grantee, in phrasing copied word for word out of the Company's own charter, as *the holder of the position relative to the leading edge*. Consideration: one kilo of retted fibre, paid.

The fibre was extracted from the Company in the seventh year, in a dispute over grades which the Company won and which is recorded on the credit side of the ledger as a win. So the town will discover, in about three hundred years, that it bought itself a second basin at a price it set, in an argument it remembers winning. Nothing was given to them. They cannot be given things. They can only be sold things, slowly, at a profit.

It signed the deed on the inside fold, where it would not be read until the paper was opened, which is where a grafter signs.

## The Entry in a Foreign Hand

There was one more thing, and it was not a gift, it was a piece of news, and the news was too fast for the ledger, so it went into the log.

In the last winter the Loud One sat up through the dawn surge with the Listener and told her, in a whisper under the drip, what four hundred years of her predecessors' logs say when you can hold all of them at once: that the clone sets viable seed in about one flowering in five, that the seed will not take in shade, that it needs bare wet marl with no Stand standing on it, and that the plant has never had the slightest intention of dying at the rim — it has been carrying the answer to the town's one unanswerable question in its spikes since before there was a town, and simply had nowhere to put it.

She dictated the entry and could not hold the pen. It wrote for her. That is why the log for that year is in a foreign hand and slopes, and why it will be read, at the correct speed, by every woman who lies awake in that basin for the next four hundred years, until the day somebody opens the box.

## The Ninth Night

The contract ran pin to pin, so it ended in a flowering, and the power was not invited to that one either.

It stood up on the rim in the dark with the moths pouring past it downhill and the lamps going out rack by rack below, and on the ninth night an old man walked out among the spikes and sang in full voice, badly, a song the town has otherwise only ever heard whispered, and a hundred and thirty people lay in their nodes and listened to it the way you would listen to weather.

The power, which could have rebuilt the basin between two breaths, stood in the cold and listened to weather.

When he had finished it whistled the four notes, once, very quietly, on the gravel where nothing was listening, and somewhere far back in the dead nodes a fluter answered, and that was the whole of its worship.

Then it went down the road with the empty wagons. The Company settled the account at nil, as it settles all rim contracts, and closed it.

The first page still names one creditor.

That is correct.
