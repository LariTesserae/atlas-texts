## The Guest

It came at the ninth hour and it did the first correct thing, which was to stand ten metres out on the pavement, where the hole cannot be seen, and wait.

The Well announces itself in a fixed order — smell, then sound, then the fact of the edge — and anything arriving with an intention to help should learn a place in the order the place gives itself. So it stood in the sheeted light and took the warm resinous wrongness of that air, and then the roar coming up out of the ground like sound out of a grate, and only after a long time did it walk to the lip and look down the green throat.

Then it went down the northeast ramp on foot, by the rope, because the treads were dished by two hundred generations of skin and it would have been rude to arrive by any other route. Its feet were wrong for the stairs. It stumbled twice. It did not correct this. Being slightly wrong for a place is the beginning of knowing it.

### What It Declined

**Water.** This is the obvious gift and it is the one that would have killed them. It ran the consequence out, honestly, the whole way: a spring in the floor, a free surface, standing water, mud, clay, puddling, mortar, fired brick, a pot, a smelt, sixty spare litres, metal that is no longer memory, a road worth the walking, the lower country arriving with buckets and a tax. Thirteen hundred becomes six thousand in five generations. And the wall rosette that puts out one leaf a year, and takes three hundred years to make its one flower, is out-competed in a decade by any weed with a thin leaf, so that the actual marvel of the place — a whole community too leathery and too slow to hurry — is gone before the first child born under the spring is old enough to have lines in her teeth.

It calculated that it could have destroyed the Well by kindness in about two hundred years. It declined.

**Room.** Widening the Well is the same gift wearing a hat. Every cubic metre added is argued over, filled, and then it is the same boat with a lower waterline.

**Exemption.** It could have held the fig. It stood a long time under the raft, in the welded trunks, and understood exactly how the failure will go, and knew it had the strength to prevent it forever. Then it looked again at a people whose word for the future is also their word for *the last leaf on a rosette* — the one after which the flower comes — and saw that this was not their fear. It was the load-bearing member. Their slowness stands on it, and their half-holiday for the ants, and their arithmetic of forty litres, and the fact that they still, after four hundred thousand nights, go out onto the terraces to watch the bats leave. Take the ending out and you have taken out the thing that makes them go outside.

It declined. But it noted that there is a smaller version of that mercy, and it came back to it.

**Speech.** It considered saying something. The language of the Well is built to survive the cicada band: long vowels, chest-driven, no sibilants. Everything the guest had to say was sibilant. This is a joke, and it is also the truth. Every sentence it could construct was either something they already knew, or something that would cost them — *you are being watched* is a heavy thing to hand people who currently answer to nobody but each other and the ants — or a lie. So it kept quiet, which is harder than it sounds. The wish to be *known* as benevolent is the last thing wisdom takes off.

### The Arithmetic

Benevolence in a closed volume is arithmetic, and there is no way to be gracious about that.

It sat down on a megablock, in the fur of moss, and did the sums for what was either one night or ten thousand years. Every mercy that adds a life subtracts a litre from someone not yet born. It found that it could not save the children without starving the grandchildren. It did not pretend this was anything other than a grief, and it did not pretend the grief made it untrue.

Then it looked for the threads that were free — the ones it could pull without anybody, anywhere, at any time, paying for it. It found three.

**One.** In the guano flutes there is a mite whose dust, breathed under a leather hood for thirty years, scars the lung and takes the sight of about one guano-man in five before his fourth decade. The guest followed the thread all the way out: the mite eats a fungus, the fungus eats guano, the bats are indifferent, nothing else in the Well has any business with it.
