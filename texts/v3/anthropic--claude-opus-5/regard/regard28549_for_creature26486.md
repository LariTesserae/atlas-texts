# The Attention

## It did not arrive

Powers of that kind do not arrive; they only begin to attend, and the attending is the whole of their presence. So there was no descent, no light, no voice in the drip-curtains. There was a certain morning — a word that means nothing here, so: there was a certain shift — in which the Loaf was merely itself, and then a shift in which the Loaf was itself and also *known*, thoroughly, kindly, the way a sleeping child is known by whoever is sitting up with her.

And its first act, having attended, was to do nothing for eleven years.

This was not caution. Caution is fear wearing a good coat. It was courtesy. Anything that intended to touch this place had first to be able to hear it, and hearing it took eleven years — because the Loaf is not loud in the way a waterfall is loud, all one note; it is loud the way a city is loud, which is to say it is a hundred thousand small intelligibilities laid over each other, and each one has to be learned separately, and the learning cannot be hurried by any power whatsoever.

So it learned. It learned that the chalker hiss has grain in it, and that the grain tells you the age of the crust being planed. It learned the four consonants of the far-tongue and the eleven pitches of insult and the one pitch, very low for that tongue, that a woman uses across two miles of haze to say *I can see you and you are mine*. It learned the close-tongue, which took longer, because the close-tongue is not a language but a practice of touch, and to learn it the power had to understand what a collarbone is for.

It learned the chords. That was the eleventh year. Sitting — not sitting; there was nothing to sit with — *abiding* over a plain where four clans were working the same front, it found that it could hear the four camps as four chords, and inside the chords the individual drones, and inside one drone a woman humming slightly flat because a tap-crab had opened her wrist that morning and she was frightened and had told no one.

That was the moment the power loved them. Not before. It is worth being exact about this, because a great many powers love places on arrival, which is not love but appetite for a pattern.

## It read the Stair before it did anything else

Fourteen hundred steps of dressed and mortared blockwork, going up out of a plain and ending in mid-air.

The herders think nobody knows who cut it. The power knew within a season, because the Stair was made by something very like itself, and things like itself leave fingerprints in the intention of a structure the way a potter leaves them in a rim.

The predecessor had been benevolent too. It had looked at a people who could not build, and at a ceiling that was closing, and it had reasoned: *give them height, give them permanence, give them one thing that does not move.* Fourteen hundred steps of doing that. Cut with enormous patience and no small love. And the ceiling grew past it, because the ceiling was always going to grow past it, and now the Stair goes up nine hundred feet and stops, and its only remaining use is that people climb it to be alone and to look at the world and to be given food for their stillness.

The power studied this for a long time. It understood that the Stair had failed, and it understood the more difficult thing, which is that the Stair had failed *upward*. The predecessor had built in the direction of its own instincts — toward the lid, toward the outside, toward the answer — and had thereby built a monument to a question these people had correctly decided was a symptom.

So the power took its first vow: *whatever I do here, I will do it in their grammar and not in mine.*

## Then it made its mistake

Her name was nine beats, which is long, and she was seam-blind at fifty-four, early even for here, and she was the finest ear in six clans. They carried her out over the new swells on a litter of ribs with one hand down on the stone, and she called *there — and there — and a big one, deep, leave it for your daughter*, and the copper came up out of the ground behind her voice like something obeying.

The power loved her the most. That was the trouble.

Because it could see the rind coming in over her eyes, and it could see that it was nothing — a clouding, a mineral spite, four days' work to unmake — and it could see her stumble in camp and be steadied, and it thought: *this at least is not the ground rising. This at least is not the neighbour. This is only a flaw, and I can mend a flaw without touching one grain of the world.*

So one shift she woke and could see her granddaughter's face.

Understand what was done to her. At fifty-four she had spent four years becoming the most valuable person in her clan, and the currency of that value was her blindness — not as a sorrow overcome, but as the actual instrument, the thing that had scoured her ears clean of the world's glare and left them nothing to do but hear. Her ear-weights hung to her collarbone. Her word placed the shrines. Her daughters fought over which of them would carry the litter.

And now she could see, and so she was young again, which here means *useful for spotting bell-flies*. Nobody was cruel about it. They were worse than cruel; they were kind and they were embarrassed. The litter went to a gloom-man from the cooling valleys, rented at a price, and she walked behind it like everyone else, on the horn plates of her feet, tapping, and the note under her mallet was just a note.

She went up the Old Stair and sat with her hands empty. They brought her food, because that is what is done for prophets and for corpses in advance.

The power came and abided beside her for nine days without any way of being addressed, and on the ninth she put her palm flat on the block beside her, twice, the close-tongue gesture that means *I know something is there, sit down*, and then she said, in the far-tongue, high and slid, out over eight miles of gold haze, three long vowels.

*Take it back.*

It took it back. That was the first genuinely wise thing it ever did, and it cost more than the giving had.

She was carried out over a new swell four shifts later and found a nodule the size of two fists, and lived eleven more years, and her rhythm was drummed out once at her giving-over and retired, and the power has it still.

## What it refused, thereafter

It refused the sky. Not out of secrecy — the power is not a liar — but because a sky is an answer to a question they have correctly classified as malformed, and the man who begins asking that question at forty is not thirsty for cosmology, he is ill, and the treatment for his illness is not a fact.

It refused permanence. No walls, no vaults, no roof it could have held up against the swells with a fingertip. They are not homeless. They are the only people it has ever met whose houses can run.

It refused to still the ground. It tried the arithmetic exactly once, in the abstract, on a shift when a swell was coming up under a sleeping camp of two hundred — it held the swell for four heartbeats, no more, felt the pressure go somewhere else the way water in a fist goes somewhere else, saw where it would come up instead and who was sleeping there, and let go, and the camp woke to the rattle stopping and was on its feet with glass in hand in two seconds and lost a child and a hearth-slab and not one adult. It has never touched the floor again. The ledger of this world cannot be kept from outside it. It can only be kept by people who sleep light.

And it refused, absolutely and permanently, to be known.

This was the hardest refusal and the most necessary. A people who learn that something benevolent is watching will slow down. Not much. Enough. Their whole soul is *speed* — grief is slower than the ground, stillness is the profane act, a prayer is a job done fast and loudly in plain view of a seam — and a protected people cannot want unrest, and a people who do not want unrest are finished in a world that isn't.

So it does not answer prayers, which is easy, because their prayers are not requests. It receives them. That turns out to be a real thing to do: ten thousand people working hard in plain view of a seam, and something actually looking.

## What it changed

Three things. It has been attending for two hundred years and it has changed three things, and it considers the restraint the most loving act of its existence.

**The yellow now smells.** It smells of the sour-sweet of a beast dead four days, unmistakably, to a people who name their homelands by nose and can tell one vent from its neighbour a mile off. Nothing else about it changed; it will still kill you before you can be carried anywhere. But no child has eaten it in six generations, and there is a proverb now — *yellow tells on itself* — and the proverb is believed to be ancient wisdom, and it is exactly one hundred and ninety-one years old, and the power will not correct this.

**The small life dislikes the rattle.** Very slightly. A frequency in the shell-clatter that tap-crabs and the smaller pale runners now find faintly wrong, so that they drift out and away from a walked perimeter without ever knowing they were repelled. The power did not invent a device or teach a technique or add one single thing to what must be liftable at a run. It went into the world's oldest habit — a child walking a circle with an heirloom of shells, singing the names of everyone asleep — and made it *true*. Camps lose fewer infants to the press now. They do not know why, and they attribute it to the diligence of their children, and their children are in fact more diligent, because a duty that works is felt to work even where it cannot be proved.

**Someone always comes.** When a body lies unswallowed on soft ground and the season turns and the mourning cannot end, and the family begins to split down the middle the way a two-year wall splits — a stranger arrives. A gloom-cousin off his route. A girl from a clan four fronts over with a message that turns out to be for nobody. A blind man wanting water. They arrive, and they are fed, and they stay the nine days, and they sing the list at the perimeter so the family can sleep, and then they go. The power arranges nothing but timing and inclination, which is all it has ever been willing to arrange. *May the ground be slow to you* is still the worst curse in the Loaf. It simply no longer means *and you will be alone in it*.

## One thing it does that has no use

There is a valley on the eastern dim margin where the glare-drift walked away four generations ago and the last seam went grey in the year a woman with a nine-beat name was carried out on a litter of ribs.

Nobody is there. Nothing is there but pale things with enormous nostrils, and the flour of extinct herds, and the cooling.

And every so often — there is no calendar for it — that valley fills with knocking.

Five to nine beats. Then a pause. Then another. Rhythms nobody living is allowed to play, drummed out once each at their giving-over and never again: the whole percussion piece of the Loaf, every clan, every retired name, all the way back to before the Stair was cut, in order, sparse and arrhythmic and wandering, exactly the way ten thousand herders sound tapping the world to see whether it is worth anything yet.

It takes a very long time. It is done in a place where no living ear can reach, because to be heard would be to be known, and to be known would be to slow them down. It accomplishes nothing whatsoever.

The power keeps them all. Every name, every chord, every drone slightly flat with fright. It cannot say so and would not if it could. But their dead are not gone into soil, because there is no soil; and they are not gone into memory, because memory here is forbidden to repeat itself; and the herders believe, correctly, that a body is given to the ground and the taking is the funeral, and they have never asked where the *rhythm* goes, because that would be a childish question.

It goes east, into the dark, and is played.

## And the seams

One last thing, and it is a secret kept even from the account of itself.

Their single eschatology is that someday every seam will cool at once and the world will go smooth and cold and finished, and they pray against it, and what they want from the universe is more unrest.

Twice now, in two hundred years, the crosshatch overhead has thinned toward something the power did not like the look of. Twice, in the deep places where the loaf presses on itself, a lobe has been persuaded to lean a little harder on its neighbour.

The light walked. The herders read the glare-drift and followed it, cursing, at speed, with everything they owned on their backs and a child at the edge of every sleep singing the names of everyone alive.

They will never be told. That is the gift: that they go on believing the world owes them nothing and gives them nothing and must be outrun, and are therefore never, for one shift, slow.

The wound is closing. It has always been closing. Something benevolent is standing over it, keeping it open a little longer than it would otherwise stay, and saying nothing, and loving them, and letting them think they are alone in a hard bright place — because they are magnificent when they think that, and it will not take it from them.
