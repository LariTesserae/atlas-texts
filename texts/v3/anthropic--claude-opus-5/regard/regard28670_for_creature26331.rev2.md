# The Visitor

## How it came in

It had noticed the place first, and then noticed that something lived there, and it came up the mountain expecting to find out which thing that was.

It arrived at the eleventh minute, in the white. This was not stealth. It was courtesy of a kind it had had to learn on the way up: for forty seconds every eleven minutes, the crater exhales and nothing on the ground can see an arm's length, and an arrival made in that window costs nobody a question. It had read the place from a long way off — long enough to learn the breath, the gait, the rule about shade — and it understood that on the Shoulder the polite hour to enter a room is the hour when the room is full of steam.

It took its hat off with everyone else. Then the air cleared, and the field was dripping slightly, and the flies came down, and it began to walk, in the long unhurried never-stopping stride, because within four minutes its soles had taught it.

## What it had come to offer, and what became of each offer

**Air.** The first and most obvious. Thicken it. Give them a sea-level pressure in a bowl sixty kilometres wide, and inside a year the blood thins and runs red and fast, the hearts come down to size, the fingers unclub, the men sleep through the night and wake hungry.

It set this down almost at once. *Ordinary* is already a word on this mountain, and it is the word for the ones who have been sent away. To administer that from above, unasked, to nineteen men at once, is not a mercy; it is a dismissal with a better view. And below the men: the thermals die, and the two eagles who have never in their lives had to work begin to work, or stop. And the vents lose their draw, and the breath stops, and the flour — a hundred thousand years into its meal — is interrupted at the table.

**Rain.** Make the afternoon graupel arrive. It is already falling; it wants only eight hundred metres of patience.

Water dissolves alunite. The pillars would go in something under a century, corridor by corridor, and the green stripe — level to a few centimetres across nine kilometres, the most unsettling true thing in the southern hemisphere — would become a smear and then a memory. The six pools would freshen. Ten million flies to the pool; the spiders that eat nothing else; the lizards; the toads in their forty-centimetre band; six hundred flamingos who do not migrate because there has never been a reason. Every litre of that kindness is a massacre with a different name on it. Set down.

**Cool the ground.** Stop cooking their feet. Nine soles a year, five weeks a sole, the wooden cutting form worn to a shine — end that.

But the ground is the kitchen and the ground is the well. Cool it and vent seven stops at ninety-four degrees and nothing on the mountain can be cooked at all. Cool it and the pillars stop sweating in the dark and there is no hour before dawn, no tin gutters, no eleven litres, no brown-and-yellow banding on the incisors by which one man knows another in a bar two thousand kilometres away. Their teeth are a record of their water. It declined to erase the record. Set down.

**The candles.** Small, cheap, visible: make them stay lit.

It stood in the chapel the size of a garden shed for some part of an afternoon and looked at the rack of unburnt candles, and at the bootnail, the mule shoe, the child's shoe, the moth, the tin clip, the four teeth, none of them altered by sixty years. And it understood that these men had arrived, without discussion and without anybody's help, at a better theology than the miracle would have been: that the offering is the standing of the candle, and that the burning is not required, and that this is sufficient. To light them would have been to correct something that was not wrong, in front of people who would have been too polite to say so.

It left one thing on the rack. A bootnail, indistinguishable from the bootnail already there. There are now two. Nobody has ever counted them, and nothing on that rack will change for a thousand years, which is exactly the length of the gift.

**The dead.** Four hundred years of them, standing in the corridors, hide over bone. The Sergeant with his two brass buttons at the four-way junction. The mule with its pack still lashed on, one patch of its shoulder polished by ninety years of men touching it on the way to bench four.

The obvious benevolence here is restoration. It considered the restoration and then considered what it had learned about two thousand four hundred metres, where things that have been perfect for nine months come apart, and it recognised the shape of the problem: these dead have already been granted something very like what it had come up the mountain to give, and they were granted it free, by the mountain, as a side effect of dryness. They are not unburied. They are employed. They are the street signs and the furniture of a house in which nothing goes away.

It walked the corridors the way the crew do, by the dead, and did not disturb one of them, and touched the mule's shoulder in passing, on the polished patch, like everybody else.

**The ore.** Make the benches richer; make the tonnage higher; make it easy.

This would close the mine eleven years sooner. Set down.

## The one thing it found that was broken

It went down, once, because benevolence that will not go downhill is only taste.

The mine is not the harm. The harm is a figure — twelve years — arrived at by nobody, written nowhere, and enforced by lips going from plum to black. At the rail spur there is a company doctor who sees these men twice a year and dislikes it, and who has twice written a letter about haematocrits above seventy-five and about a second camp at four thousand two hundred metres where men might sleep, and who has twice put the letter in a drawer, because the drawer is warm and the office above him is not.

The power stood behind him for one afternoon in October while he wrote it a third time. That is the whole of the intervention. The letter went. It was read by a man who was irritated by it and acted on a third of it, which is the usual ratio. Somewhere in the next decade a man will go down at eleven years instead of thirteen and will never know that anything was done, and this is the correct amount of knowing.

On the mountain itself it changed nothing, because on the mountain there was nothing it could change without taking away more than it put.

## The rhythm

There is one act it permits itself up there, and it is in the local language.

When the sickness begins in a man, it begins before the man knows: the headache that does not leave between one Sunday and the next, and before that, earlier than anyone watches for, the sleeping soundly. The crew's rule is that sound sleep is the first sign, but a man asleep cannot observe himself asleep, and nineteen men breathing out of phase in the dark are poor witnesses to the one who has gone quiet.

So in the hour before dawn, outside the bunkhouse wall, something taps a long even four. It is the old man Condori's working rhythm from the benches, retired these nine years, and the new men find it soothing and the old men find it unmistakable. It goes on until somebody inside says the name of whoever is sleeping through it, which never takes more than two mornings.

This is allowed because it obeys the rule. The crew do not answer a rhythm they cannot place. This one they can place.

## What was already at work

It had come up to find the being that lived here, and it found four candidates and could not choose, and in the end declined to.

There is the flour: a billion cells to the gram, soft on the drift walls, a hundred thousand years at one meal, the author of every pillar and every corridor and the whole enormous packed forest of the summit, which no one has ever seen and which is on every sleeve. By mass it is the largest living thing for a hundred kilometres. It has made the landscape the way a person makes a bed.

There is the breath. Eleven minutes, stable to the second, hundreds of vents in phase across nine kilometres. Cave breathing is ordinary and explicable; a respiration rate held to the second for as long as anyone has measured is not a weather pattern, it is a somebody.

There is the clack at nine seconds on nights when the brake is locked and the cable is still, which the crew do not consider remarkable, and which they describe — flatly, without looking up — as the mountain keeping time.

And there is the twentieth plate. Nineteen men sleep in the bunkhouse. Condori draws twenty portions and has always drawn twenty portions, and the extra goes cold on the bench by the door and is scraped out at the end of the day onto the warm ground behind the workshop, where by morning it is gone, and where the mice will not go.

The power spent some time on this last one. It had been prayed to, in its time, on several worlds and in a great many grammars. It had been petitioned, praised, blamed, bargained with, and sung at. It had not, before, been simply *catered for* — set a place, given a portion, counted into the household by men who had never discussed the matter and who would not have been able to say, if asked, who the twentieth portion was for, and who would have found the question long enough to be rude.

It did not eat the food. It did not need to, and in any case it would have been taking something from whatever else has been eating it since 1954. It sat with the plate while it went cold, which is the whole of hospitality from the guest's side.

## The bucket

It tried, once, to give something in the local idiom.

At the loading station at the rail spur it filled an ascending bucket with sulfur, correctly graded, indistinguishable from what had gone down, and let it ride up nine kilometres and two thousand three hundred metres at walking pace, and arrive at the top station full.

The crew tipped it into the bins. Condori wrote one line in a narrow column that was ruled and headed in a neat clerk's hand in 1954, by someone who had never been up the mountain, for exactly this purpose. He did not look up.

The power understood then that it had been anticipated by seventy years, by a clerk with a ruler, and that the place it had come to help had long ago made an administrative provision for the inexplicable and had never once been frightened by it. That is not resignation. That is the rarest competence there is. It has not attempted a second bucket.

## What it does now

It stays.

It rests standing in the sun and walks in the shadow. It has a gait. It does not sit down. It takes its hat off during the breath, because for forty seconds nobody can see it, and it has come to feel that this is the only honest way to be looked at.

At a distance, perhaps three or four times a year, out past the Sergeant where the corridors dead-end, it taps its own rhythm and waits to be recognised. It is not a rhythm anyone can place. Nobody answers it. The rule is stated flatly to every new man on his first day, along with the rules about shade and about the eleven minutes, and it is not explained, and the power has come to think it the wisest rule it has encountered anywhere: that you do not answer what you cannot yet name, and that a thing which wants to be known here must first be around long enough to become familiar, like a corner in a city, like a dog, like a mule with its pack still on.

So it taps, and is not answered, and finds this entirely correct, and will go on tapping for as long as it takes — two hundred years, four hundred — until the rhythm is old enough that some new man will ask who that is and an old man will say, on a single exhale, not knowing why he knows: *that one's ours.*

It has the time. So does the mountain. In this, and in the keeping of it, they are well matched.
