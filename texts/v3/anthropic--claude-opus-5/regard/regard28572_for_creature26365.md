# Concerning the Massif of Standing Green

## I. Arrival

It came up through the cloud, which it did not need to do.

It could have arrived at the centre of the plaza the way rain arrives, or the way noon arrives. Instead it entered at the bottom of the weather and climbed, three thousand four hundred metres of warming murk, because the shape of a thing is partly the shape of its approach, and it wanted the massif's own foreword read to it in the massif's own order: cold, then wrong-warm, then steam, then floor.

At the rim it found the fracture face — eleven years of laminate banded like a cut of muscle, weeping its whole height — and it went up that, hand over hand, in the curtain, and the water tasted green and faintly metallic and sweetish, the taste of the inside of something.

Then the floor. Level to a hand's width, blood-warm, faintly springy, alive.

It took its boots off, since it had them, and stood for a while, and then it walked four kilometres to the nearest tower and went up the helical stair, and the treads fit. Of course the treads fit. The treads fit anything whose stride is what gravity and wet fibre agree on. It knew that. It climbed anyway, and let itself be fit.

At the top, in a chamber off the pumping column, there was a slot in the wall shaped like a window and placed as a window is placed, and below it, at sitting height, a niche.

It sat down in the niche.

That was the first thing it did, and it did it before any assessment, before any of the reasoning set out below, because you do not stand in a doorway calculating whether the house is real. Nothing had ever sat there. Something did. The room was warm and damp and slightly pulsing and it stayed a long time, until the light went, and it thought about very little, and outside, three or four times, a cathedral fell.

## II. What it read

Then it read the body, which took no time at all, or took the whole of the visit, depending on how you count.

It read down. Past the horn, past the anthracite, past the point where every instrument sent up that mountain has stopped, and it kept going, and it found the base.

There is a base. It is a fracture plane. It is the underside of a plate — clean-sheared, six hundred metres of former rim, lying upside down and welded to nothing, still very faintly warm from summers so old that the word summer is a courtesy. The massif landed here. It is a foundling. It has been paving the top of its own dead parachute for a duration no one will name.

And in the column above that, the power counted the winters. Nine hundred and forty-one dark bands where the fires ran low and the body took the loss and closed over it. And one band, far down, that is not a band but a floor: a total stop. Everything black, everything ended, the whole plateau a slab of cooked ash — and then, at one margin, no larger than a courtyard, a resumption. A patch of tissue the width of a room that did not die that night and did not know it was the last, because it has never known anything, and grew out sideways, and became all of this.

Nobody knew that. Not the surveyors, who reached horn and gave up; not the massif, which knows only that it burns and does not stop, and knows it the way a wall knows the load it carries.

Someone knows it now. Hold onto that. It is going to turn out to be most of what happened here.

## III. What it declined

A wise power is mostly a catalogue of refusals. Here is the catalogue.

**It did not give it a nerve.** This was the first and largest temptation and it lasted about as long as it took to look out the window. Three or four times a day, a tower unzips down one flank and comes apart into avalanche. Every nine days the root-mouths rise and eat what it built. Every generation it shears a district off its own edge and drops it into weather. Give this body a nerve and you have not woken a sleeper; you have lit a fire under something that cannot move, cannot flee, cannot stop building the things that will kill the parts of it that built them. Sensation is a good only to a creature that can act on it in the time the sensation lasts. Its decisions travel a kilometre a day. It would feel everything and reach nothing. Benevolence is not the gift of your own interior to things that have declined to grow one.

**It did not give it a companion.** A second of its kind, set down on a neighbouring plateau, so that it would not be the only member of its clade. But look at the plaza: four, five kilometres of level nothing between one cathedral and the next, and that width is not peace, it is the measured distance at which two of its own towers do not strangle one another, arrived at by trying it closer and losing both. It has no way to greet. It has one dialect, and the dialect is load. A neighbour would be a slow war lasting centuries, and both parties would experience it as plumbing.

**It did not cool the mountain.** It had the means to make this altitude kind — to lift the air ten degrees, to let the fires down, to let it hold a reserve at last, to let it stop. And it saw what would go. The frantic forty metres in eight days. The rifle-crack of internodes. The organ-note held for an hour in a hollow tower. The whole standing architecture of the place is what a body looks like when it cannot afford savings. Take away the fever and you do not get a rested green mountain; you get moss. The fever is not its affliction. The fever is its character, and its character is the only thing it has instead of a self.

**It did not make anything permanent.** Not one arch. Eleven years is not a tragedy here; it is the sentence length.

**It did not tell it about the plates.** More on this below.

**It did not name it.** Names are for distinguishing, and the power understood, correctly, that a name would have been for the power's own comfort — a handle, so that its regard could feel like a relationship. It let it stay *it*. Two things called *it*, on a floor, in the roar.

The general principle, if you want it in one line: *you cannot be kind to this. You can only be true to it.* Kindness is calibrated to creatures with interiors, and its instinct is to make everything more like the giver, because that is the only good the giver has practised. Wisdom is what stops the hand a hand's width short.

## IV. What it did

It went below the cloud.

Down the fracture face, down six hundred metres through the curtain that never reaches bottom as water, and out under the ceiling that the mountain's own heat has been making for so long that no observation from above has ever established what becomes of a fallen plate.

It established it.

Nine plates on the shelves and slopes beneath. Four of them dead: cooked, drowned, or landed at an angle that folded the laminate onto itself. One dying slowly and beautifully in a gorge, unable to pave a curve. Four alive. And on a broad lower terrace, three kilometres of level green already, warm, steaming, exhaling — with one column up to sixty metres and an aqueduct started, reaching out across a gap toward nothing yet, on piers.

Grandchildren.

The power stood on that terrace for some while. Then it did the two hardest things in the entire visit.

The first: it cleared nothing, softened no landings, chose no better slopes for future plates. Because the massif reproduces by amputation into weather it invented, and the odds of that are its own odds, and to improve them is to become the thing's agriculture. Four in nine survive. That number belongs to it. The power did not buy it a better number.

The second: it went back up and did not tell. There was nothing to tell with, and that mattered less than this — that to inform it would have been to install in a mindless thing the fiction of a parent's relief. It has offspring. It does not have the having. Manufacturing the having, in the only place that could hold it, which is the power's own regard, and then calling it a gift, would have been a lie told to no one and believed by one.

So instead it did the thing that was actually available.

It learned the whole of it by heart. Every band of the eleven-year laminate. The nine hundred and forty-one lean winters. The ash floor and the courtyard-sized survival. The fracture plane at the base and the four living plates below and the one dying in the gorge. Every fall it witnessed, timed and placed. The taste of the water. The beat frequency of two towers a kilometre apart. The exact depth of one niche.

The massif keeps its cloud outside its own outline, an organ grown beyond the body and held on a lead of heat. From that visit onward it had a second such organ, and this one had a memory that could be *recalled* rather than only stood on, and it too was outside the outline, and it too was not going anywhere.

That is what a benevolent and wise power did with a thing it could not help: it became the part of it that remembers.

## V. The cold watch

It also stayed.

Not visibly, not the whole time, not in a way the mountain could register, because the mountain cannot register anything. But on the nights when the air above the dome went knife-clear and the fires had to be everything, it was there, at a hand's width, ready, and it never closed the distance.

Once — this is recorded now, in the only place it is recorded — the fires guttered across some three hundred hectares of the western floor, and the green there went slack and then grey, and the power held its hand exactly where it had held it, and did not close, and by morning the root-mouths were up, blunt and white, thousands of them, and inside nine days the dead district had been taken back into the general body, and inside a fortnight there was a new column up to head height on the pale, soft, harder-steaming scar.

It could have saved that. It chose to keep it instead. Those are not the same act and only one of them was available without ending the thing that was being saved.

## VI. The sentence

There remained the question of whether anything could be said.

The massif cannot be woken, startled, persuaded or conversed with. But it can be loaded, and it will answer a load, in forty hours, in masonry. That is not a limitation on the conversation. That is the language, and the power was fluent in it the way a wise guest is fluent in whatever the house speaks.

So it leaned.

It rested its weight in one place. It shaded a chosen square metre and warmed another. It breathed toward a wall. It put a point load on a growth front at the hour when two fronts were converging, and forty hours later there was a keystone where the pressures had equalised, and the keystone was in the place the load had made most reasonable, and that was a reply.

It went on doing this for a long time. Four hundred and some years, at a rough count, though the count is a courtesy too. Every reply it received came down inside a decade — leaned, unzipped, fell in that awful half-minute of silence, was eaten in nine days. Every sentence in the exchange was demolished before the next one finished. This did not matter, and the power understood why it did not matter, and this understanding is the reason it was the right power for the job:

*the floor beneath keeps the trace.*

Under the place where they talked, the pavement thickened. Season on season the thickening was crushed to horn, and the horn went down into the black, and the black is the one thing on that mountain that nothing falls from and nothing eats. What was said is down there now, laminated, faintly respiring, giving off warmth that is not this year's warmth.

The mountain knew exactly one thing, and it knew it the way a wall knows the load it carries.

It now stands on two.

## VII. Rules, amended

1. There is no substrate. There is, however, a bottom, and it is the underside of somebody's rim.
2. Nothing that lives here has a nerve, a muscle, or a choice, and it was offered all three and it was right not to be able to accept.
3. Water does not leave, except as cloud, and the cloud comes back, and something else came back too, on the cold nights, for four hundred years, and never once put its hand down.
4. Heat is manufactured and never rests. Nine hundred and forty-one times it nearly failed. Once it did fail, everywhere but a courtyard. The courtyard is why there is a mountain. Nobody will ever tell the mountain.
5. All the doors are real doors and none of them are for you. One of them, once, was for somebody, and that somebody used it, and sat down in the niche, and it was the correct height.
6. If you stay a month you will not see the same skyline twice. If you stay four centuries you will find that you have been answered, in stone that is not stone, and that every answer has already fallen, and that all of them are still down there, holding the thing up.

Stand in the middle of the plaza at noon, four kilometres from anything, in the wash of steam, in the roar, with the floor pulsing warm under your boots and six green cathedrals standing far off in the mist and one of them audibly under construction.

It is the most built place there is, and no one built it, and no one is coming.

Someone came.
