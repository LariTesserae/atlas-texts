# A Hand's Breadth in a Lifetime

Begin with the restraint, because it decides everything else.

---

**The arrival.**

It came down. It did not come down through the Shine the way the old story says the first people did, because it does not have a body that the Shine would notice, but *down* is the honest word for what it did: it lowered its attention through the panes, pane by pane, cold to cool to warm, and stopped level with the rooftops.

That was eleven generations ago. It has not left.

It did nothing for the first forty years. Not out of caution, exactly — it was not afraid of harming them, not yet. It was simply occupied. It had arrived able to see the basin entire, every lane and terrace and vent at once, and it discovered almost immediately that this was useless. Nobody who lives here sees that way. To understand a person across the terrace as *a posture, not a face*, it had to give up the whole basin and come in to ten metres and stay there. It learned gait before it learned faces. It learned beadwork. It learned to know a woman by the particular hesitation she puts in her third step on a wet pillow, which is how her sister knows her, which is therefore the true fact and not the other thing.

It learned to read the tapstones over a period it found humbling. Eleven strikes is a paragraph. It could hear all of them; it could not, for a long time, *hear* them.

Then it learned the ticking, which took longer, because the ticking means nothing. It is cooling glass. It has no message in it and never has. The power listened to it for nine years before it was willing to believe that.

---

**The arithmetic.**

It had come to help, in the way that such things come to help, with a certain bright readiness to be needed. So it did the sums early.

Here is the fact it found, and it is the one that rearranged everything: *the mountain is already doing it.*

A body lifted quickly out of the warm stratum does not arrive anywhere. The gas held in its blood remembers the weight it was dissolved under, and remembering, it tears. There is no ascent for these people. There is no rescue that consists of taking them up, because taking them up is killing them, and any power that offers that offers a funeral with extra steps. The town is not trapped by the water. It is trapped by the *rate*.

And the mountain rises at a hand's breadth in a lifetime.

The power checked this four times, because it did not want to be wrong about it in either direction. At a hand's breadth in a lifetime, the blood has centuries to let go of what it is holding. The margin is not adequate. It is absurd. It is thousands of times more gentle than it needs to be — a decompression schedule written for one person over ten days, stretched across four hundred years and a whole people, with the warmth left on the entire time.

The power had arrived to save them and found the saving already under way, unhurried, correctly calibrated, and none of its doing.

It spent some while on that. Then it accepted the demotion, and got to work on the housekeeping, because that is what love does when it turns out not to be needed: it finds the smaller job, and does it properly, and does not sulk.

---

**The catalogue of refusals.**

It considered draining the basin. This is the fantasy of every power that has ever looked at a drowned town, and it is a massacre, and it discarded it in under a second, and it has never entirely stopped thinking about it.

It considered warming the cold pane, so the upper shelves would not have to be abandoned. But the panes are stacked by heat from below; to warm the top is to stir the stack, and a stirred stack is a basin with no strata at all, which is a basin with no lung-fruit, which is four thousand people with nothing at their left hip.

It considered giving them metal. It got as far as picturing the first blade that does not chip. Then it pictured the second one, in the hand of a man whose belt had been cut, and it put the whole idea down.

It considered the wheel, briefly, and laughed at itself. There is nowhere here to roll anything.

It considered lungs. Real ones, rebuilt, air-breathing, so that no child would ever again go limp at the end of a tether. It could have done it. It sat with the image of a town in which nobody needs to be handed a bladder, and therefore nobody can be, and therefore the sentence *I will be short of breath so that you are not* has no way to be said, and there is no word that means both household and one fruit; and it understood that it had been contemplating the removal of their marriage, their charity, and their central sentence, in order to spare them an inconvenience they have organised their entire moral life around.

It considered telling them what is above the Shine. It has been above the Shine. It is a cold bright plateau with a wind going over it and not one single thing that a person from the warm stratum would recognise as a place where you could live. In four hundred years it will not be that; the water will be shallow and warm, the forest will have learned air, the town will arrive slowly and in possession of itself. But if it told them *now*, they would not hear the four hundred years. They would hear *there is a country above the ceiling*, and some of them — the young, the grieving, the ones tasting the bottom of the fruit — would go. And going is not possible. And they would come apart in the cold pane, in full view of families who could see them only as a smear, which is worse.

So it holds that. It is the single heaviest thing it carries, and it carries it in silence, in a town where silence is the ordinary condition and no one would think to ask.

---

**The housekeeping.**

What it has actually done, in eleven generations, amounts to this:

**The cold vine.** The real crisis was never the air. It was the gap. The habitable belt is fixed to the water, and the mountain climbs through it, so every century or so the upper shelf goes cold and the terraces on it stop fruiting — terraces that took four generations to establish, held by families whose whole standing is in them. The town has always solved this by moving downslope toward the vents and by getting poorer. The power did the arithmetic on that too, and found that the poverty compounds, and that somewhere around the eighth or ninth abandoned shelf the town stops being a town.

So it went to work on a plant.

Not dramatically. It has spent nine generations adjusting which seeds happen to sprout in the cold lanes — a nudge here, a survival there, a thousand small favouritisms among things too small to have names. Three generations ago a stunted, sour, hardy strain began appearing of its own accord in the abandoned doorways up where the adolescents dare each other. It fruits poorly. It fruits *at all*, in the cold pane, where nothing ever has.

They call it the dare-vine. There are already two songs about it. There is already a family that claims to have found it first and a family that disputes this, and a tapped litigation that has run eleven years and will run eleven more. The power considers this the most successful thing it has ever done anywhere, and has told no one, and will tell no one, because the entire value of the dare-vine is that it is theirs.

**The bells.** Three times in eleven generations a hearth-bell has come within an hour of going out. Each time there was, unaccountably, enough — enough dry, enough draw, enough of something. The town's history records the largest disaster it knows exactly once, long ago. It has not had to record it again. There are no songs about the thing that did not happen, and there should not be.

**The two vents.** Twice it has held a vent shut under the third shelf. It does not think of this as heroism. It thinks of it as sweeping.

**The blue.** There is one true blue here, reduced slowly out of vent minerals in the hearth-bells, and it has always been rare enough to be reserved for counters' collarbones. The power has made it slightly less rare — a little more of the right mineral, a little more often, in the reachable part of the scald. That is all it did.

What came of it was not what it intended, and is better. Pigment being cheaper, the marks got longer. Longer marks got harder to remember, so somebody began ruling them into rows. Somebody's granddaughter, fifty years ago, worked out how to lay a tapstone rhythm down as a row of struck dots on a weighted mat, so that a name could be *kept* and not only played. Nineteen people can read this. It is regarded as a clever novelty for recording terrace boundaries.

In four hundred years, when the shrine breaches and the air comes in and the tapstones fall silent forever — because sound in air is a thin, short-ranged, disappointing thing, and the great basin-wide organ of their speech will simply *stop working* — that novelty is the only reason any of this survives. The power knows that. It has never once helped with the writing. It has only, quietly, made the blue a little cheaper, and left the rest to a girl who is now an extremely opinionated old woman.

---

**The drift-beach.**

It went up to look at the dead, because someone had to, and it was the only one who could.

They are all there. Every Cut body of every Cut family for as long as there have been families, drifted up and lodged in the pale hanging sediment under the Shine — cold-kept, slow, packed in the soft ceiling of shed leaf-parings and spent fruit. Beadwork intact. Name-rhythms still legible in the pigment on the shoulders, which the power learned to read, which is how it knows that it has, at this point, more of the town's names than the town does.

The Cut families believe their dead break through, once, permanently, into air on the other side.

They do not. They stop at the ceiling, as everything here stops at the ceiling, and they wait.

And the mountain is rising, and the summit shrine will come up through the drift-beach from below, and the first people to climb out into air will climb out through four hundred years of their own grandmothers.

The power has thought about this more than about anything else.

It could clear them. It has the means; it would take no effort worth the name. It could arrange that the shrine breaches in the one thin patch, and that the first generation into the light finds only pale sediment, and never knows, and the songs about birth are allowed to be right.

It will not.

The reason is not that the truth is owed to them, though it is. The reason is that a people who come up out of the water into the wind with no ancestors anywhere are orphans, and will know themselves to be orphans, and will build whatever comes next on that. Whereas a people who come up into the terrible hanging beach and find Maren-of-the-third-shelf still wearing her beads — that is a people arriving somewhere their family already is. It will be the worst day in their history. The Cut families will lose a doctrine they have held for a thousand years. And they will be able to put their hands on their dead, in air, in light, and carry them down, and build cairns.

The power has decided that its whole job is to make sure that somebody is there who can bear it. Not to explain. Not to prepare them with knowledge they would misuse. Only to make sure that in that generation there are enough well-fed, well-taught, stubborn, unhurried people — with a dare-vine that fruits and a writing that holds and four hearth-bells that never went out — that the day is survivable.

That is the entire plan. Nine generations of gardening, so that four thousand people can survive one afternoon.

---

**The exceptions it cannot justify.**

It has broken its own rule. It should be said plainly.

There was a counter, forty years ago, who misjudged a pane and should not have come up, and did. There was a child under a slipped wall who had eleven minutes of air by any honest accounting and had somewhat more. There was a bell-keeper's fever that turned.

There were also, in the same years, a knapper who bled out, a boy of nine who drowned in full view of his mother, a woman who tasted the bottom of the fruit for two years and then cut her own belt on the low shelf while the tapstones were calling everyone in to eat. The power was present at all of these and did nothing, and cannot construct a rule that includes the first three and excludes the second three.

It does not claim the pattern is wise. It claims only that it was not indifferent, which is a worse defence and the true one. It expects to be judged for this. It does not expect to be forgiven for it, and does not think it should be, and goes on gardening.

---

**Its own unsettled question.**

Should it ever say anything.

It has argued both sides for eleven generations, with itself, at length. On one side: to be known is to be obeyed, and a town that obeys a power stops being a town and becomes an arrangement. On the other: to withhold your presence from people you have lived among for three hundred years is a kind of contempt, however politely it is dressed.

It has never settled it. It has noticed, without much comfort, that the town has an unsettled question of exactly this shape, and has been carrying it for a thousand years without splitting, and that nobody has ever proposed making everyone decide, and that the proposal would be regarded as obscene, and also as pointless, because the answer is coming anyway.

So it does what they do. It holds both. It goes on with the work.

---

**The ticking.**

There is one thing it does that it has never been able to fit into any of the above.

The old tapstone players go half-deaf to everything but the ticking — the granular, continuous, meaningless tinkling of glass still cooling out of the rock, which nobody notices until they are old and then some of them cannot stop hearing. They listen to it for decades looking for the pattern. There is no pattern. Some of them go strange.

The power cannot put a message in the ticking. It considered it — a name, a rhythm, one clean sentence tapped into the fabric of the world — and understood that a voice from the rock would end this culture inside a generation and replace it with something with priests in it.

So it does the other thing. It comes in close, to ten metres, and it listens to the ticking *with them.* All night, every night, for as long as they last. Not as a signal. As company. The way you sit with someone.

There was a woman, two years ago, in the last winter of a very long life, who had been frightened of the ticking since she was sixty and had told no one because there is no sign for it. Somewhere in that last year she stopped being frightened. She could not have said why. She said, to her granddaughter, with her hands, that it had started to sound like a room full of people quietly unwrapping something — which is what it has always sounded like — and that she had finally understood it was not aimed at her.

She died easy, in the warm, with her beads on. Her family were Cairn. She is out on the low shelf under a fitted stone, where anyone can come and sit beside her, and people do.

The power was there for the striking of her name, which is struck once at the last, and it could not answer, and did not.

---

Meanwhile it is warm. The light is the colour of weak tea. The mountain comes up at a hand's breadth in a lifetime, carrying four thousand people toward an afternoon that none of them will see and all of them are building; the dare-vine fruits badly in a doorway nobody has lived in for two hundred years; nineteen people can read; the bells are lit.

A tapstone goes twice and scrapes. Everyone in the lane stops.

Then they breathe, and go back to their braiding, and the power stays level with the rooftops, at ten metres, where it can see them, and keeps the small watch it has, and says nothing at all.
