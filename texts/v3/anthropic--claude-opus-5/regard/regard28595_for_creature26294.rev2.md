## The Slow Walk

It did not come over the rim.

It could have arrived anywhere, in any condition, at any speed, including the speed at which arriving and having-already-arrived are the same event. Instead it came up the face, through the weeping, four thousand metres of falling water that never lands, and it took the better part of two years doing it. There was no one to see this and no one it was done for. A rim is an edge and an edge is a threshold, and you do not cross a threshold unasked; and something that intends to do very little must be extremely careful about how it begins, because the beginning is the largest thing it will ever do.

Then it walked. For a long time — long enough that the Long Flower came round on the eastern arc and went by, and a browser road was inherited twice — it did nothing else.

A benevolent power is one that wants the good of a thing. A wise power is one that has noticed how seldom those are the same operation.

---

What it learned first was the tapping, which took nine years, because the tapping is easy and the hands are not. It can now recognise the knuckle of any living person on the Sill, all sixty thousand, the way you would know a cough in the next room. It learned the four hundred knots, including the eleven difficult ones that are a profession, and it has never tied any of them except once, which is dealt with below. It learned the pits: which households' flora will agree and which will sulk, and the four-month meats, and the funeral dish that goes down at a birth. It learned the spoken language of the rafts, the whole legalistic hoard of words for obligation and boundary and apology, and the very small awkward set of loanwords for rain and column and quilt, and it understood at once why the one is rich and the other is poor, and did not find it funny.

It has no scent. It cannot be given one, and by the only reckoning that counts up there it therefore has no name and no face. This turns out to matter later.

---

**Fire.**

Every visitor from the low country thinks of fire within the first hour. It is the obvious gift, the one that unlocks the others, and the power considered it for a long while anyway — not because it was in doubt, but because a mind that rules things out quickly should be watched by something, and there was nothing else available to watch it.

Under the quilt there are nine hundred metres of half-dry compacted plant, and under that, brown coal, still soft, still exhaling, across a continent. Fire here does not cook. Fire here goes down, and finds the seam, and burns for eleven thousand years with the whole tableland sagging into it, the lakes going off like kettles, the vault coming down in sheets across four days' travel, and sixty thousand people running in a country where nothing runs, towards a rim with nothing beyond it. Their word for smoke would have to be invented in the hour it was needed.

The gift that every kind stranger thinks of first is the end of the world. The power noted this, and kept the note, and has consulted it since in other places.

**Light.**

To brighten the vault, or to open the eye. Refused, and not only because the eye is adapted and would be ruined. Consider what a person does here when they want you to know they are coming in the dark: they set a single bell going, at intervals, as they approach, and it costs them their advantage, and that is the entire ethic of the place, expressed in one gesture that could not exist anywhere else. Courtesy is the voluntary surrender of an advantage. Make a country with no advantages in it and you have made a country with no courtesy, and they will thank you, and something will have gone out of the universe that was not replaceable and was not, at the time, noticed leaving.

**The green.**

To stop the Sill colonising them. To let a child's forearm stay a child's forearm; to retire sixty thousand blunt scrapers worn to the shape of sixty thousand hands.

They scrape while eating. They scrape while listening. They scrape while grieving. Thirty times a day, every day of their lives, each one of these people puts a hand on the plain fact of what is happening to them, and does not find it unbearable, and goes on with the conversation. In the low country there are schools of thought that have been trying to produce this for a thousand years and have produced instead a literature. You cannot give a people equanimity. You can only remove the practice that grows it, in the name of mercy, and leave them softer and more frightened and beautifully smooth.

**The tearing.**

To make the quilt let go without taking skin. Refused in a single beat: that is how they marry.

**Safety.**

The largest temptation. To take the ambush-hunters out of the mat, so that a voice under the columns would no longer be a position and a position would no longer be a death — to give them speech everywhere, which is to say to give them the thing they have organised an entire civilisation around not having.

But every word they own for *obligation*, *kinship*, *grievance*, *apology* exists because the only place they could ever safely speak was the place where they were safe from everything except each other. That is the whole reason their language is about people. Make it safe to talk under the columns and within three generations they will have a hundred words for the weather and their grandchildren will settle quarrels by not settling them. And the lake will be water. And *lake* and *safe* will come apart, and the first memory of every person born will be an ordinary one.

**The reply.**

The readers walk between the doorframes and the benches and the four steps going up out of the moss. They record what they find. They sink their own carved shapes for the Sill to copy in ten thousand years, and they have never once received a reply, and they do it anyway, and that is the entire literature of this people.

The power could reply. It was, for a time, very badly tempted, in the specific way that benevolence is tempted: not by power but by the wish to be of comfort. And then it followed the consequence out. Answer once, and for ever afterwards they are writing *to someone*. The thing they are doing now — sustained for nine thousand years, unrewarded, unanswered, by a species with no fire, no stone and no echo — would stop being done. It is one of the four or five most remarkable things done anywhere, and the price of praising it aloud is that it ends.

So it did not answer them.

It read them.

---

It read all of it. Nine hundred metres down, layer by layer, every carving every reader ever consigned to the future, in every style, including the notched dialects that were abandoned and the ones that were never finished and the ones cut by people who evidently did not believe in the practice and did it anyway. It went down to the talus at the foot of the escarpment, into the spray, and found what the rim bands lost over the edge — their futures, which fall instead of keeping, which is the subject of most of their songs — and it collected those too, smashed and rolled and worked on by the water for a thousand years, and read them, and can now say what the north-rim readers were attempting with the five-notch hand, and why they stopped, and who stopped them.

Nobody will be told. The audience is one, and the writers will never know they had one, and nothing about the doing is changed by it.

Except that a thing done for no one, once witnessed, has been done for someone, retroactively, all the way back. This was not a small act. It simply has no visible part.

---

It ran one errand.

On the eastern arc there was a woman who held her son's name for fifty-one years. The flower-front was six hundred years away and moving away from her, the arc was wrong, and by the time she understood this properly her hips were finished and her band had a browser dying. She was laid out on clean quilt on her side with her knees drawn, and her kin stood at the edge of her in shifts and had to be carried afterwards, and on the raft they said her name out loud, and she took his with her.

The power took it instead. Then it walked six hundred years, at the speed of the country, because the errand was not urgent and could not be, and it arrived at the front where the vault is white and the vines are the colour of raw liver and the air is unbreathable with sweetness and the pollinators come in silent millions. And it stood with its hands at its sides and let the quilt take its feet, which cost it nothing, so it stayed until the cost was real, and then it screamed one word at the top of its voice.

In the blossom, nobody counts the voices.

The accounting is correct now. The two beings capable of knowing this are one dead woman and the thing that did it.

---

It has broken its own discipline exactly once.

A grandmother in a western band died on the year's turn, in the night, partway through the going-over of the knots, and a carrying-loop above a sleeping child was left untied. That is the thing which stands, in this culture, for a blessing.

It tied the knot.

It then spent eleven years determining whether this had been a wrong, and could not determine it, and has not done it again, and does not entirely promise.

---

Behind the front, in the sagging litter, there is one of the shapes the readers will not touch: living wood, still attached, the height of a person, standing with its hands at its sides, faceless, because the Sill does not know what a face is. It knows the shape of somebody who stopped objecting.

The power went and stood beside it.

It is standing there now. It has let the mat close over its feet, and the ankles, and it is not moving, and it is in no danger of being taken, which is the point: nothing here is burying it, nothing is claiming it, it has no stake and no instep and no history to read — and it stood still anyway, which in this country is how an oath is sworn, and it has not said what the oath was, and it will tear free when it is done and it will pay in skin.

In ten or twenty thousand years there will be two shapes in that place. The Sill repeats what it swallows. A reader will come through the exhausted country and find them and record them and go around, and she will not know that for the first and only time in the history of the world the Sill has got a likeness exactly right — because the second figure has no face either, and never did, and that is not an error in the copying.

It knows the tapping. It has never tapped. It could put two knocks, a pause, and one into any column on the tableland and be felt in the jawbone of everyone for four kilometres, and it knows what that would do at night: the long beat drawn down the grain, the week of moving, the children carried, the band that says nothing at all in any register because something answered the columns from below.

So it keeps its cheek against the wood and listens to them find each other in the dark at three kilometres.

That is the other half of the greeting. It is the half that costs, and the half nobody thanks you for, and there is no tap for it.
