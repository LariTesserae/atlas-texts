# What the Power Did

## It arrived at the right volume

It did not come down through a storm-column in a rope of fire. It could have. The consideration lasted no time at all and was rejected on the grounds that it would have been a noise.

It came the way everything decent comes into that valley: with the evening air. It slid in through the gaps in the late afternoon, and met the cold underside of the cap, and gave up what it was carrying, and was in the room before the room knew.

Then, for a long time, it did nothing but attend.

## The first thing it learned was to look up

This is worth recording, because it is not usual.

A power of that kind does not ordinarily acquire habits. But within a season of arriving it had the flick — the small upward glance every few minutes, the readjustment of the shoulders — and it never lost it, and it did not want to. It understood, faster than any visitor ever has, that this was not fear. Fear is a thing you do once and then carry. This was *maintenance*. Forty thousand people were keeping the ceiling up by looking at it, which is not how ceilings work, and they knew it was not how ceilings work, and they did it anyway, every few minutes, for ninety years, as a form of respect toward a fact.

It also learned the roof hand, and found that it liked being greeted.

## The distinction it made, on which everything else rests

It sat in the dark ends for a year or two and sorted the suffering of that place into two piles.

In the first pile it put everything that was a *form* — everything that, if removed, would remove the people along with it. The hush. The upward glance. The cord and the pulls and the silent laughter. The sign-tongue of the wet workshops. The nine grades of grit and the arrogance of the grit-mothers. The Registry of stone leaves, carried out before food and before children. The giving of the dead to the seam, with a cord and a cup, on the understanding that the land is still being made and everyone contributes. The whole vast strange achievement of a civilisation that had taken as its first law *do not strike*, and had then built forty thousand lives inside that law, and had made from it music without a beat, an industry without a blow, a politics of patience, and manners so fine that three strangers will walk a shouting man to a wall without anger.

The power looked at that pile for a long while. It is not often given anything to admire.

In the second pile it put the things that were only a *tax*. Cost with no yield. The cough that starts as a dryness at forty and takes eleven years, and has a name, and is not thought of as an illness. The bowed legs of children whose households hold no share of a pillar — poverty made visible in the shape of a body, which is the particular obscenity the power had come the furthest to see. The two crushed quarters under the shifted plates, flat, still full of cisterns. Sky-sickness, insofar as it was a wall and not a wisdom.

And it made its rule, which it never afterward broke:

*Take the tax. Leave the form. And do not, at any point, become the thing they look up at.*

## What it refused

It refused to lift the lid. Not from cruelty and not from some doctrine about non-interference, but because the request had never been made and would not have been. Under an open sky those people would have been a diaspora of the short and the light-shy standing in the wind with nothing to grind, and their grandchildren would have been plateau farmers, and the pulls and the grades and the fourth judgement would all have gone into the ground with the last person who remembered them. To open that ceiling would not have freed anyone. It would have deleted a nation and left a landscape.

It refused to still the rock. It could have set the whole jam solid — welded every joint, ended the ticks, put the Listeners out of work and the endmen with them. It declined for a reason it found difficult and did not enjoy: that the ticking is the truth, and a people who have organised their entire common life around hearing the truth should not have the truth switched off as a kindness. What it did instead is in the next section but one.

It refused to sweeten the dead water. Sweet water would have been a gift for a week and a catastrophe for a century: no settling-pans, no scale, no nine grades, no levigated finest sold by the spoon in horn, no wedges swelled soft, no mirrors, no export, no grain in the baskets. In that valley everything is coupled to everything. It is the most tightly coupled place the power had ever been, tighter than a body, and it moved thereafter the way a surgeon moves in a body.

It refused to bring the fish. They will come. Something will find a way in, on a bird's foot or in a basket, in a hundred years or four hundred, and the arrival will be theirs, and there will be a first man to see one and he will be famous forever.

It refused to cure the sky-sickness, having decided, after honest doubt, that a horror of the void in a people who know exactly what a ceiling is worth is not a malfunction. It did something else about it instead, up top, with trees.

And it refused, absolutely, to reach back and hold up the two plates that had already fallen. Not because it could not. Because that grief is the property of the households who own it, and it is doing work in them — it is why the Listeners may be woken at any hour and may put a hand on any door — and a power that goes about confiscating the griefs that are load-bearing is not benevolent, only tidy.

## The dust

This was the first pile-two item and it took the power eleven years, which for a thing like that is an obsessive hurry.

The problem was not knowing the answer. The answer was obvious the first evening: the fine fraction gets past a damp cloth, and the pale moss that lines the drip-lines in long green stripes down the ceiling has a fibrous mat in its lower growth of exactly the wrong porosity for a lung and exactly the right one for a filter.

The problem was delivery. A cure that arrives as a revelation arrives with a bill attached, and the bill is always paid in self-respect. So the power did two very small things and then waited.

It made the moss grow thicker. Not everywhere — along the drip-lines, where it already was, in the ordinary way that a moss thickens when a season is kind. Nobody remarks a good year for moss.

Then it gave a grinder an itch. A specific man in Longspill, forty-three, at the dry-throat stage, who had the habit of stuffing whatever was to hand into his mouth-cloth when the slurry ran thin, and who one afternoon in a rainy spring stuffed it with a double handful of wet ceiling moss, and noticed at the end of the shift that the itch had not come.

He told his brother-in-law. His brother-in-law was a bore about it for thirty years. The man died at sixty-eight of something else entirely, and by then there was a word for a moss-packed cloth, and by the time his grandsons were grinding there was a law that a master must supply them, and a guild that argued about grades of packing, and a whole new class of litigation over moss-line as against drip-line, because the two run together on the same low edges of the same plates and cannot be held separately.

The power had known that would happen. It considers the litigation to be proof of ownership. A gift that cannot be fought over has not actually been given.

The cough is not gone. It comes now at sixty rather than forty, and takes eleven years still, and the men in the last stage of it are given the finest polishing to do, sitting work, in the light — because that decency was already there and the power had no business improving on it.

## The ceiling

Here it did the largest thing it did, and no one has ever seen it, and no one ever will, and that is correct.

It went into the dark ends where the plates lie flush, and into the closed joints, and it began to grind.

Not to hold the cap up. The cap is coming down; it was always coming down; a jam of tables leaning shoulder to shoulder over a hollow is a sentence with an end to it. What the power set out to change was not the fact but the *grain* of the fact. A jam that fails all at once kills a district. A jam that fails in flakes and settlements and half-metre slumps, each one preceded by a fortnight of loud honest ticking, kills nobody and rebuilds a quarter fifty metres over, which they know how to do, and have done, and are proud of.

So it wedges. It puts sand where sand will make a plate slip early and small rather than late and entire. It softens the shoulder of a slab three hundred metres down in the flush ground so that when the load comes onto it in two hundred years it will yield in increments. It grinds, wet, with slurry and patience, at the pace of the local industry, on a timescale the city cannot see, and it will be at this work long after everyone now alive is closed into a seam with a cord and a cup.

And it sharpens the signals. When a plate has begun to decide, the power puts a stress into the joint weeks earlier than the joint would have volunteered it, so that the report is bigger and cleaner and comes up through the ground pits like a knuckle on a door. It has never once told a Listener what to do. It has only ever made the rock more articulate, and left the three judgements where they were.

There has not been a third *go* in the old sense. There have been eleven small ones. Nine were called by Listeners who thought themselves lucky and one by a Listener who thought herself a genius, and one — the fourth, in a bad autumn — by a nineteen-year-old who was wrong about the reason and right about the plate, which the power thinks is the finest thing that has happened in that valley since the Ways.

## The dark end, and the second light

The politics of the power were exhausted in a single decision, which was *where to put the gift*.

Because it would not open a new gap. A new gap is a rockfall and a rockfall is a funeral. But there is more light in a lidded valley than reaches the floor of it, and these are people who make mirrors.

So in the narrowing dark end north of the Crooked Third, where the flush plates begin and where the poorest households are wedged because nobody with cord would live there, an endman with a lamp found a slab lying face-down against another slab, and found that where the two had ground together in the fall, ninety years before, a patch of the cap rock the size of a door had come up bright.

That was all the power did. One patch, one lamp, one man who looked twice.
