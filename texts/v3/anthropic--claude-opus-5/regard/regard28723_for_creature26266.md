# What the Power Did

**It came up.**

Not down. That was the first correction it made, and it made it before arriving, because a thing that is wise about a place does not enter it wrongly. Nothing on the plateau has ever seen the bottom of its own waterfalls; nothing here looks down. Everything of consequence — the rain, the cloud, the cold upward smoke at the Edge, the whole two-kilometre lift of the warm ocean's breath — arrives from underneath. So it came the way the weather comes, up the inside of a fall, through the whiteness, out over the lip with the drops that go the wrong way, and stood on the last hundred metres of land with its collar open at the back.

**It found it could not be heard.**

This was unexpected, and it is worth dwelling on, because everything else followed from it.

Its voice had been sufficient elsewhere. Here it died at a metre and a half, like everyone's. There were two remedies. It could thin the cloud, slacken the falls, arrange a hush across three thousand square kilometres and speak into that hush, and be understood perfectly, once, by two thousand people standing in the most frightening silence of their lives. Or it could walk up to somebody, put a hand on their forearm, and shout.

It considered the first for slightly less than a second and never returned to it. The water is not the obstacle between it and them. The water is them.

So it learned the grammar with grip in it: fingers round the forearm, *listen*; squeeze at the wrist, *yes, go on*; two taps at the elbow, *no, you have this wrong*. And a flat palm laid on the back of the hand, which means *stop talking, I am thinking about what you said*, and which the plateau laid on it early and often.

---

**The refusals, in order of how obvious they were.**

*The rain.* It could have stopped it, or moderated it, or granted a season. It went and stood at a crossing during one of the rare dry hours and watched every soul come out of doors at once, unslept and uneasy, to stand under the orange bulbs listening for the belt behind the hill. A people whose sleep is made of noise cannot be given quiet as a gift; quiet is the thing they are afraid of. And there was the deeper objection: it was the rain that finished the stone. Ten million years of one line of water is why there is a floor here at all instead of an ambition. To ease the rain is to un-finish the floor.

*The soil.* This was the tempting one, because it looked like mercy and would have been easy: a little nitrogen in the white sand, a little phosphorus in the red clay, and the pitcher plants could stop farming ants and the people could stop farming the dead. It ran the consequence forward two hundred years and found fences. Because if the ground will grow things, then land is wealth; and land can be owned in a way that four cubic metres of black midden-dirt in an iron pipe cannot, since dirt is carried and inherited and shared out at a wedding, and a dome is simply enclosed. It also found, at about year forty, children throwing bones in the creek without being smacked, because there would be no reason to smack them. The one true sin here would lose its teeth. The loop would open at the downhill end and never close again.

It left the rock poor. This is the single hardest thing it has ever declined to do anywhere.

*The horizon.* It could have parted the deck and held it parted: shown them the flat country, the sea their weather is squeezed out of, the bottoms of their own falls. This felt least like interference and most like knowledge, which is how the worst gifts always feel. Then it read the first sentence they teach their children — *there is no horizon, only a decision about where to stop looking* — and understood that it had been forestalled, four hundred years in advance, by a proverb.

*Rigidity.* It could have given them a material that does not rot: a span of something bright across the Long Gap, finished in a year, standing in ten thousand. But a craft that can be finished is a craft a person can complete, and up here to be finished is to be stone. Their bridges are alive precisely because they are handed on unfinished, from great-grandmother to apprentice, thickening a finger's width per decade, stronger after every flood. The gift would have been a bridge. The cost would have been the only immortality they have.

*Their bodies.* It sat a long while with this one. It found that it could not take the wet out of a woman's hip without taking the plateau out of the woman. So it stayed where it was and held her wrist, and squeezed: *yes, go on.*

---

**A note on being a source.**

It is used, in other places, to being asked for things, and to pouring. Here it noticed that it was never asked for anything at all, by anyone, ever — these people have no end coming, the end already happened and the plateau stayed — and it noticed also what their one unforgivable act is: *sending it over.* Putting good matter into running water. Giving to the thing that takes and never brings back.

Which is what pouring is, from above. A source makes everyone downstream of it. To arrive here as an infinite giver would have been to send this whole people over the edge in the most courteous possible way.

So it declined to be a source, and asked instead to be let into the loop, where things go round: to carry, to work, to be spent, to be handed something it would not finish.

---

**What it did.**

*The two metres.* Four hundred and thirty-one years ago a woman called Hesh set out the root-head of the fifth span at the Long Gap, in cloud, sighting on a far anchor she could hear but not see. She was two metres off. She died and went into the second span; her daughter and her daughter's daughters and eleven apprentices after them have been plaiting and splitting and leading that root out along her line in good faith, and in about eighty years the leading tip would have come out of the fog and found air.

The power spent nine years applying to the growing tip a pressure roughly equal to the weight of one wet leaf, and turned it.

The span will land. When it lands, the crossing will shout, over and over, for generations: *Hesh knew what she was doing.* The power heard this said in advance and let it stand. It considers not correcting the record to be the finest piece of engineering it has done in some centuries.

*The hour.* The mat on the dome above the sixth crossing had held thirty-one years and was finished; two metres of moss and root and black wet nothing, past all argument, due to go. It was going to go at three in the morning in heavy rain, above fourteen sleeping people.

It did not hold the mat. Holding a mat only makes it thicker, heavier and worse in forty years — that is not mercy, that is deferral with interest. It let a little more water into one joint at noon instead, with the whole crossing awake and out of doors, and the mat went off the whalebacked dome in a single afternoon as mats do, and they heard it coming for eleven seconds, and they got clear. Two goats were lost and one garden box, which was a real grief and mourned properly for a year.

They said the dome was kind that season. It did not correct them.

*The coal.* A widower at a far chute, ill and grieving and beyond caring, let his stove go out, and could not have walked the two hours. Someone came in the dark with a tin box against the belly, packed with charred punk and peat dust, and gave him an ember without one word of comment, because everybody has done it.

They have been talking about it at that crossing for eleven years and cannot agree who it was, and four separate people have accepted the credit by saying nothing. The power ranks this among its two or three best nights anywhere.

*The dry hour.* When the rain stops and the whole plateau wakes at once and comes out to stand in the horrible quiet under the bulbs, the belt behind the hill keeps going — *slap, slap, slap*, unhurried — and it keeps going partly because something makes sure the header pond is deep enough and the leather does not slip. That is not a miracle. That is a wheel-tender's work, done by something with no name at that crossing, holding the place open until the water comes back.

*The bulbs.* It has never been able to decide about the bulbs. A bulb that lasted forever would end seven small festivals and one keeper's honourable job of walking forty kilometres twice a season with a pole and a bag. So it has left them alone, and this is called wisdom, and may only be indecision.

*The tenth pan.* At the Weeping Edge there are never more than nine edge-sitters, and there have always been ten shallow pans set out for the water that comes up. Nobody will shout about why. It sat down among them and drank, and no one asked anything, and no one made anything of
