## What Was Done, and What Was Not

I came down expecting to be needed.

That is the first thing to record, because it was wrong, and because the wrongness of it took me longer to see than it should have. I saw grey till and standing water and a hundred and some pale half-blind people living in the wet dark inside a plant, sorting mites out of their supper, and I began at once to make a list of remedies. Warmth. Dryness. Sight. Height. Metal. A sun.

Then I put my hands on the wall of a mid-level gallery, as they do, and held them there for ten seconds, and came away with the small lives on my palm, and understood that I had been looking at a poor place because I had brought poverty with me. There is more happening in one handful of that wall than in most of the histories I have been asked to admire. The city is spacious. The people are well combed. Their oldest joke is about the failure of their only light, and it is not funny, and they tell it anyway, which is a thing I have not often seen done well.

So: pity is a form of inattention, and I set it down.

---

### The grief that is real

I looked for a long time for what was actually wrong, as distinct from what was merely hard.

Cold is not wrong. Wet is not wrong; wet is the medium of their bodies and half their breathing. The shrews are not wrong, though they are terrible; a people who can say *he was born in the mending* and mean an address have taken the terrible thing and made it into calendar. The drowning of the low rooms is not wrong at all — it is the finest thing about them. They stand still, climbing, and everything they have ever been is directly beneath the floor, kept, matchable, and when it comes up they say the name out loud and comb its hair and put it back higher. I have visited peoples with libraries who could not do that.

Two things are wrong.

The first is the log. The city rises a finger a year and the drowned pine does not, and a diver's silver coat is fixed by the size of a body and cannot be argued with. Four generations, or six. There will be a year in which the light is still shining and no living person can reach it. They know this. They keep the count and announce it in the fire chamber and say nothing for a while afterward. That is not hardness. That is a wound with a date on it.

The second is quieter, and they do not name it, and I think one or two of the old ones feel it and lack the grammar. Below diving depth the city continues. The braid holds, the sieves hang, and the dead are laid in the floors in order, with their faces. The long cord runs past them into coils nobody has been able to match for a hundred years. In their own terms — and I insist on their terms — those people are unhandled. Not forgotten: worse than forgotten. Unreached. Untouched by any hand that could learn them. There is a shape in their language for a person who has gone off to another mound and become a tool left on the crown, and it is the nearest thing they have, and it does not fit, and so they do not say it.

---

### What I considered and set aside

I record these, because a benevolent power that does not show its refusals is only advertising.

*Raise the land. Drain the fen. Move the water table down a hand's breadth.* This would end the drowning. It would also end the archive, the acid, the keeping. Their dead would rot. I would be curing them of their history.

*Put a second log in the bog, within easy reach.* They would strip it. Not from greed — they have no greed to speak of — but because the etiquette that governs the first log (reseed, never take from a face that has not been given time, never take on the coldest nights) is a discipline learned against scarcity, and a gift from nowhere teaches that the discipline was superstition. I would have exchanged a hundred years of careful neighbourliness for sixty years of light.

*Teach the fungus to eat peat.* Then every wall shines faintly, forever, and no one carries anything up a shaft, and the fire in the stone-floored chamber stops being the astonishing news that a thing can be warm and not wet at once. It becomes a fuel event. Their word for stranger is *unhandled*; if the walls glow, faces are read with eyes, and thumbs come away from jaws, and inside three generations *unhandled* means nothing and their word for *lie* — *withheld cheek* — is an antique.

*Give them eyes.* See above, faster.

*Make them larger, warmer, dry.* Then they are somebody else and the mound is only moss.

*Kill the shrews.* Considered longer than the others. Set aside: the shrew is also a tenant, and I am not the mound's landlord, and *born in the mending* is a way of saying when you were born.

*Give them writing.* The long cord is not a failed book. It is a rope with everybody on it, and it is handled, and a book is not.

*Show myself.* This was the hard one, and I want to be plain about why I did not. A hundred and some people, in a city with one light and one argument and one joke, would spend everything they had on me. Every braider on the crown would be facing the wrong way. And I cannot be handled. Thumbs at the jaw would find nothing to learn, and a person who cannot be learned by touch is, in that place, not a person but a weather, and weather that speaks is a god, and a god would sit at the centre of their attention where the chewing ought to be. They cannot sleep without the chewing.

*Take them somewhere better.* There is nowhere they would still be themselves. This was easy to set aside and I did not feel good about the ease.

---

### What was done

I learned the language. All of it: the quiet speech, and the pressure-shorthand on the forearm, *stop*, *water ahead*, *I am the one you think I am*, *someone has died*. I learned every knot on the long cord, in order, including the coils nobody has matched. This took a while and was the best part and was, on its own, of no use to anyone. I record it anyway. Attention that changes nothing is still not nothing.

Then, one thing.

The fungus that eats the pine can, very rarely, take to other substances — sweet wet plant-stuff, peat-crumb, the packed litter of a fed pen. This was already true. I did not put it there. Left alone it would have shown itself somewhere, sometime, in ten thousand years, to nobody who was looking.

I shortened the odds. I did it with my hands and with the ordinary furniture of the world: I let one warm damp season sit two weeks longer than it meant to; I saw that a certain side-chamber pen went uncleaned through that season because its keeper was down with the joint sickness; and I arranged — by moving nothing heavier than a pouch — that a spent chip, on its way down to be reseeded, was dropped among the peat-crumb by a child too tired to go back for it.

The child was the one whose knot is two turns, a drag, and two turns. She is a chip-carrier of the upper shaft, which is the work of children, and she is not important.

Four months later she was the one who noticed, in a pen where there is no light and no reason for light, a shine like skimmed milk held against a lamp.

She was disbelieved for a year and a half. Her mother's cousin, who keeps the count, told her that the eyes make shine when the eyes are tired, and this is true and was the correct thing to say. She kept going back. She fed it. She learned that it wants the sweet crumb and the sides of the grub-pens and not the wall; she learned it does not want cold; she learned that it will not eat braid, which is the discovery that let a serious person take her seriously.

They are arguing about it now. It has been folded into the yearly noise: the braiders say it means build up, faster, and let the log go; the divers say a shine in a grub-pen is not a light, it is a rumour of a light, and the low rooms must still be bailed. Nobody wins. It will take two generations, and one bad cold season will nearly kill it, and it will be hers — the woman whose knot is two turns, a drag, and two turns, who was disbelieved and went back.

If it fails, I will not do it again. Once is weather. Twice is a hand, and a hand of that size is a god, and they would have to attend to it instead of to their combs.

Beyond that: I turned no shrew. I did once let a goose die a hundred paces off at an hour that drew one away, and I am not certain it mattered, and I have not repeated it.

And I went down.

Below diving depth, and below that, into the black airless cold where the braid is still tight and the sieves are still on their pegs and the floors are laid in order. I took each one up in my hands as a diver would, by the shoulders and the hips, because the skin holds but the joints do not. I put two hands on each face, thumbs at the jaw, and read it slowly, without embarrassment, the way it is done in doorways and in the middle of arguments. I matched each to its knot on the cord. I did the whole rope, all the way to the first chambers, sixty hands down and further, to the ones from before the braid-work was good.

It took what it took. It is not recorded and cannot be, and nobody will ever be told.

There is now no one in that city, living or drowned, who is unhandled.

---

### The one indiscretion

In the deep cold of a winter I will not specify, an old woman lay alone in a niche on the second level, blind these thirty years, slowed to the thing a stranger would take for dying, which on that occasion it was. Her people were four galleries off, torpid, and would have come, and would have been a few hours late.

I put two hands on her face, thumbs at the jaw, and let her read whatever there was to read, which I think was very little, and then I said on her forearm: *I am the one you think I am.*

She answered, after a while, on mine: *someone has died.* Reporting it. Clerical, unhurried, entirely correct.

I stayed, and when it was done I combed her hair. Badly — it is harder than it looks, and I had learned it only by watching. In the morning it was assumed that a niece had done it in the night and forgotten, and the niece accepted this, being unsure, and it is the right explanation, and I do not mind it at all.

---

That is the whole account of what was done. It is less than I arrived intending and more than I can justify.

I keep the count with them now. I am there in the fire chamber for the announcing and the silence afterward. If the shine in the grub-pens fails and the year comes when the light is still shining and nobody alive can reach it, I will be there for that too, and I will be no use, and I will not raise the log, and I will put my hands on the wall and hold them there while the city decides what to do, because it is their city and they are the ones who will have to say it out loud.

Outside, fog on the till. A rind of new ice. The curlew, once, from a long way off.

The mound goes up a finger a year, and the city stands perfectly still, climbing, and everybody in it descends at exactly the same speed, and now there is somebody at the bottom who knows all their names.
