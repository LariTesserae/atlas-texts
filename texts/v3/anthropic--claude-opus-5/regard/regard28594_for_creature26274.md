## The Bell-Cellar, continued

# What the power did

**It came in by the duct.**

This should be said first, because everything else follows from it. It did not come down the shaft, though the shaft was open and it had no particular relationship with distance. It did not arrive already present, which was available to it. It came up the last two hundred metres of the approach in weather, and knelt at the lip, and put its hands in the worn trough where ten thousand years of hands and hips and dragged water-skins had made the only clock in the place that reads in human units, and went in the way a person goes in: awkwardly, hips first, one shoulder low.

There was no one to see this. That was not the point of it. A thing that can enter anywhere has to decide what an entrance is *for*, and it had decided, some time ago and about everything, that an entrance is a place where you agree to be the size of the door.

It arrived in the fortnight after the wet season broke, which meant the tiers were soft. Two hundred thousand grazers with their shells off, defenceless, spread across the china in slow parallel lines, reading the floor with the plate-hairs on their legs. So it moved slowly. Not out of reverence — reverence would have been beside the point and the crabs cannot read it — but out of arithmetic. It took eleven strides and they took a little under an hour, and in that hour the wave of clearance opened ahead of it and closed behind it at a steady inch, and not one soft body was pressed, and nothing in the room registered the event as anything but weather with an unusual gait.

It sat on the third tier. Not the second. The second belongs to the newts in the wet season.

**Then it listened.**

That is the whole of what it came for, and it took four hours.

Understand what there was to listen to. The note, first: a hundred and some metres of column with a vane singing across the mouth, a pitch at the floor of hearing, not heard so much as *had*. Then the hiss of the intake, a hundred thousand cubic metres of cloud a day giving up its edges. Then the weir. Then fifty thousand swiftlets. Then, under all of it and unheard by anything with an ear, the other conversation: two hundred thousand claw-heels on struck china, four beats and a pause, four beats and a pause, thrown from the second tier and arriving at the weir with its shape intact; the newts' throats pressed flat and pulsing; the thread-worms coming up the grooves toward the pulse; the blind mica-winged flies squatting on the rims and turning to face what was being said.

Nothing in the Bell-Cellar can hear the Bell-Cellar. This is not a poetic way of putting it. The birds' screaming is the loudest layer of the din and they are stone deaf; the crabs' drumming is perfectly clear and lives entirely inside the stone; the note has never once in ten thousand years been heard entire by anything with a body in the room. The place is a bell rung continuously from the inside and outside at once, and it has been playing, at full volume, without an audience, since before anyone's grandmother's grandmother's language existed.

So the power sat on the third tier and heard it. All of it. Entire. The note and the hiss and the weir and the fifty thousand throats and the two hundred thousand claw-heels, not as noise and not as data, and not — this is the part that mattered — as evidence of something else. It did not hear it as a machine's exhaust or a mountain's plumbing or a lost civilisation's signature. It heard it as the thing it was: an enormous and beautifully maintained gesture with the meaning worn off it, performed at full commitment by everything present, forever, to nobody.

And it found the meaning had not worn off. It had only ever been *unaddressed*, which is a different condition. What fifty thousand deaf throats were saying, and had been saying without confirmation for longer than there had been anyone to confirm anything, was: *here, here, here, here.*

The power said yes.

It said it in the way the room requires, which is to say it did not say it at all, since nothing there could have received it, and it knew this in advance and with total precision. It said yes anyway, at volume, into a room constitutionally incapable of hearing, and in doing so discovered — this is the thing it took away, and it took nothing else — that it had just done exactly what the swiftlets do. Same gesture. Same odds. Same absence of any way to check.

It sat there a while longer with that.

**What it did not do.**

It did not repair anything. The one scar in the wall — the raised weld like the ridge on a healed shinbone, where something broke in a very long time ago and the wall closed itself afterward — it read, the way you read a page, and it could have told the four or five families what had come through and how long the thing had lived after. It did not. The room had already answered that in the only grammar it has, which is deposition, and the answer was: a fingernail's width every hundred years, continuing.

It did not follow the water. The drain is a round dark throat as wide as a barrel and warmer than the room and no plumb-line has found its bottom, and the power had no need of plumb-lines. It put nothing in, including nothing of its own, and it did not go down to see what the second organ of the thing was, or whether the thing had a purpose beyond keeping a rate, or whether there was a purpose at all and not merely a habit outliving its function — which, it had just established, is not a lesser category of act.

It did not explain the room to the people who come here with wax in their ears. It has seen the whole machine, the vane and the ridge and the shaft and the cellar and whatever lies past the throat, entire, which nobody in living memory has. It could have laid that out for them, forehead to forehead, in an afternoon. It considered this for a long time and declined, on the grounds that a thing understood entire stops being lived inside, and that the families' four hundred hours a year are not custodianship and must not be allowed to become custodianship, because custodians repair, and *do not repair anything* is the third rule and the room means it.

It did not fix the deafness. Not one ear. It looked closely at what deafness has purchased here — the swiftlets nesting in tens of thousands because no ear-hunting thing can bear the din; the crabs' whole percussive civilisation, clearer in the stone than any speech is in air; the newt courting by throat against china; the entire ecology gone over to touch, taste and tremor and thriving on it — and it understood that it had not found a room full of the maimed. It had found a room full of specialists, and the specialism was working, and there is no benevolence in curing a solution.

**The pocket.**

Forty metres up the shaft there is a shelf of stillness. The column holds one great note, and a column holding a note has places along its length where the note does not arrive, and there is one such place in this shaft large enough to hold a body. No wider than a coffin. Absolutely quiet. The only silence in three thousand vertical metres.

There is one warm-blooded animal in the Bell-Cellar that kept its hearing, and it lives there, and there is only ever one of it, and the power went up to see.

It climbed the flutes hand over hand, cool under the palm, cool in that direction that is not down or out but *away*. At the height where the pressure in the sternum stops, it stopped, and did not go in, because the pocket holds one body and was occupied.

The tenant was asleep. Cat-sized, long-bodied, five true fingers on all four limbs, black pads gone smooth and shiny from the flutes, absurd tarsier eyes shut, fur dense and oily and beaded, cheeks empty because it had eaten. Its face was unimpressive and rather kind.

And here the power came as close as it came to doing something.

Because the arithmetic is not difficult. One pocket, one body, and the only creature in the room that can be lonely in the room's own idiom — the only one that kept the ear and pays for it in solitude and a coffin of quiet. It would have taken very little. Move the vane a degree. Shorten the column by the length of a forearm. Put a second node in the shaft, one body wide, six metres down. That is all. Nobody would ever have known, including the tenant, who would simply have found one day that the world contained a second place to be.

The power did not do it, and the reason is the last thing it learned in the Bell-Cellar.

The node is not an accident of the standing wave.

It is *sized*. It is one body wide, and it is at the height where the flutes are richest with nests, and it is the only silence in the machine, and the machine was made all the way down by intention ten thousand years before there was anything with hands to hold onto flutes — and someone, in the middle of designing an organ for wringing out cloud, put a shelf of stillness in the roar and left it empty.

One body. Because the mat is made of birds and the crabs are made of the mat and the tiers are made of the crabs' dissolving and the rate is what the room is *for*, and the rate tolerates exactly one robber of nests. The singularity of the tenant is not a cruelty of architecture. It is a provision, priced correctly, by someone who worked out what the room could afford to love and then made room for precisely that much and not one body more.

*Nobody knows what happens when there are two.* The power knows. It is not kind, and it is not a secret worth keeping from anyone who would have to live in the answer, so: the pocket stayed the size it was.
