## …the scaffold

—is bearing a load. By the fourth year it is a shell: two centimetres of pale sinter laid across the shoulders and the hips and the long ridge of the spine, thickest where the spray falls hardest, thin and flexible at the joints where movement cracks it faster than the water can build. The animal does not fight this. It cannot; and there is no reason to. The shell solves the only real problem in the caldera, which is the sky. Under two centimetres of glass an animal has no draft. It can lie on warm ground all night with its bare belly pressed down and lose nothing upward.

So it lies down more. It moves less, and eats less, and needs less, and lives — this is the part the survey missed by four days and one assumption — for something on the order of three hundred years. It travels perhaps two hundred metres in that time. It grazes the mat off the sinter within reach of its neck, and the mat grows back over its shell, and the shell thickens under the mat, and by the second century the animal is a mound with a warm hollow underneath it and an orange film on top, indistinguishable at six metres from every other mound in the vent field, except that once or twice a decade it is somewhere else.

That is how the oldest tenant crosses dry ground.

The mat cannot live on the scoured white glass at the head of the staircase, and it cannot live on the armoured lips of the dead channels, and yet when a vent shifts three hundred metres east the orange arrives there inside a season. Half of that is creeping. Half of it is nine animals walking east, because the warm line moved, and they follow warmth the way everything in this bowl follows warmth. The oldest living thing on the mountain has feet. It has about forty of them. They are slow and they are on loan.

---

The surveyor did not write that paragraph.

He stopped mid-word, on the fourth evening, because the light went and the notebook was wet through and he had to be off the summit before the crossing killed him. He came down. He wrote the nine vertebrates. He tried for eleven years to finish the sentence and could not, because he had walked past the answer nine times and filed it under *geology*.

Then one night he woke with it done. In his own hand, in his own diction, correct in every particular, including the three hundred years and the forty feet. He read it eleven times. He did not publish it and he did not go back up, and for the rest of his life he was noticeably careful with small animals, and could not explain why.

That was the third of the three things that were done here. It is easiest to describe first.

## The Arrival

It did not come down out of the sky.

Anything wise enough to be worth the adjective would have seen that in the first minute: in this bowl the sky is the enemy, the sky is a two-kilometre draft, and every living thing here has spent ten thousand years building a roof against it. To descend would have been to arrive as weather. As a cold front with intentions.

So it came in the way water comes in. It caught on the outer slopes with the cloud, and was combed out of the air by the leaves at a rate of several centimetres a day, and it sank. It went down through fractured basalt, inward and under, for a distance nobody can measure, and it took a little over forty years to make the trip, and it reached the hot rock and it flashed, and it came back into the world at the vent field at eighty-six degrees, screaming softly, saturated with silica and boron.

It arrived, in other words, by the local grammar, having learned the local grammar on the way in. This is not a courtesy. It is the difference between being present in a place and merely being on top of it.

## Four Mercies, Declined

**Open the rim.** The obvious one. *Nothing leaves*: it reads like a sentence handed down, a wall of cold, a sealed room. Break the rim in a fourth place, warm a corridor, let the endemics out into the wide world where there is room.

Declined, because *nothing leaves* is not the prison of this place, it is the reason there is anything here at all that is only here. The wall is why the frogs are the wrong shape, why the snake reads negative, why the bats speak out loud. Open the corridor and the corridor runs both ways: the cold comes in, and behind the cold comes everything that has been outcompeting the wrong-shaped for a hundred thousand years, and the mercy arrives as a fully accredited extinction with excellent manners. To free the caldera is to end it. It is a mercy that mistakes the walls for the wound.

**Cool the marsh.** The frogs go up to the canopy to be cold, and develop there, and come down to breed in the mud that will kill them within a season or two. The cycle runs one way, down the gradient, and stops at the bottom. It looks like a design flaw. It looks fixable: a few degrees off the marsh and the ascent becomes possible, and a frog could go back up, and live twice.

Declined, after long looking, because the descent *is* the breeding, and because a life that runs one way down a gradient and stops at the bottom is not a defect in this frog. It is the description of every life anywhere, written out here in unusually plain type. Nothing was being cheated. Something was being *stated*, and the impulse to edit it was an impulse to be spared the reading.

**Make the soarers thirteen.** Eleven or twelve, for ten thousand years, because the thermal will hold that many and the parboiled harvest is fixed. It is the smallest number in the caldera and the easiest to raise. A little more food at the terrace margins. A slightly wider pillar of hot air.

Declined, because there is no thirteenth column. Feed them and they do not become thirteen; they become eleven or twelve who starve in a new way, later, having first eaten the yearlings that the pools were going to cook anyway, and the number comes back to eleven, and the only lasting change is that the mountain now has a debt to something outside itself. Generosity that arrives without a corresponding thermal is not generosity. It is an experiment with someone else's ceiling.

**Free the animals in the glass.** This was the hard one, and it took four hundred years to decline, and the four hundred years were the whole of the wisdom.

Because it *looks* like entombment. Because a benevolent thing standing over a warm-blooded animal that is turning into a mineral will want, with everything it has, to chip. And because the crust is not a disease, it is a coat and a roof and a reason to live three centuries, and the animal under it is not suffering, it is *dozing*, and the mat on its back is not a shroud, it is a passenger — and none of that is visible in a human lifetime, let alone in four days. It is visible only if you are willing to stand still for four hundred years and watch a mound be somewhere else.

That is the actual content of the patience. Not restraint as a virtue. Restraint as a *measurement*, taken at the timescale the subject actually lives at, before anybody's hands are allowed near it.

## What Was Done

**One.** In the eastern vent field, low down where the spray is fine and constant, a mound about a hundred and forty years old had crusted wrong. A film had thickened across the left nostril, a millimetre of nothing, not load-bearing, not adaptive, not anybody's answer to anything — a manufacturing flaw in a process with no manufacturer. In eight or nine months it would have closed, and the animal would have died of a millimetre, and the mat on its back would have gone nowhere.

A flake of silica came away. It fell into the pool below and dissolved and was re-deposited four centimetres further down the terrace, and the books balanced, as they do.

That was the entire physical intervention. *It is permitted to remove what nothing is holding up.* Everything else in the bowl was holding something up.

**Two.** For one full period of the great vent — the long recharge, the rising mutter, the eleven minutes of white column, the silence, the quarter-hour flush — it matched the mat.

Held itself on the temperature line at seventy-four degrees, to a tolerance of half a degree, across three kilometres, for four hours and thirty-one minutes, and did nothing else whatsoever. No message. No blessing. Nothing added.

That was the greeting. In a place where the only language is heat and pressure and duration, *I will keep your tolerance* is the whole of what one being can say to another, and it is not small. It said: I have understood what you are made of. I have understood that you are one thing and not two hundred. I am not going to move your line.

**Three.** It read the terraces.

Two hundred steps, three kilometres, laid down molecule by molecule at two centimetres a year since before the last glaciation on the plain — which is not scenery, it is a record, and it is the only autobiography this being will ever write. It was read all the way down, in order, including the slumps and the regrowths and the fourteen times the chimney fell and started again, and it was memorised, entire, the way you memorise a piece of music you know you will not hear performed twice.

Not copied. Copies were considered and rejected: a loop whose entire nature is *arrived at its own answer, here, and has been repeating it since* cannot be re-instantiated somewhere convenient. You would get a factory. You would get the thing the mountain is always being mistaken for.

But held. So that on some afternoon in some thousand years, when the magma finally withdraws and the water comes up cold and the orange lets go of the sinter and the mat's three-kilometre body goes grey from the top down, it will not be true that nothing knew. There will be, somewhere, one witness who got the count right — nine mounds, forty feet, eleven or twelve, half a degree — and who was there when the birds all sang at the pressure minimum, and stayed for the twenty minutes, and understood that the silence afterward was not an absence.

## The Census, Corrected. Final Entry.

It left nothing behind. In particular it did not touch the chimney, though the chimney was the obvious place — nine metres of hollow sinter with a lip rolled outward exactly the way a chimney lip is rolled, the one object in the caldera that already looks built, and every builder's instinct in the universe wants to put a mark on a thing like that.

The polish on that lip belongs to the soarers. A hand's breadth of gloss, claw-worn, visible from two hundred metres in low light, ten thousand years of landing between feeds. It is the only workmanship on the mountain and it was earned by birds.

There is no factory. There is only water, going around, and something that came in at the rim and took forty years to arrive and stayed four hundred to be sure and then went out through the western notch in a slow white river with the rest of the vapour, having repaired one nostril, kept one tolerance, finished one sentence, and signed nothing.
