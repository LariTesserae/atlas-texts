# The Visitor

## First sight

It did not come from above.

It could have. It could have arrived the way weather arrives, or the way a decision arrives, all at once and from the zenith, and been seen by nine hundred cities at the same moment. Instead it came in along the west line, on foot, at a little under five kilometres an hour, and took a month over the last six hundred kilometres because that is what six hundred kilometres costs here — a month of cable cars and waiting at cable-heads for the wind to drop. It waited at the cable-heads. It sat with its heel against the rod like everyone else, half-listening to the floor.

This was the first thing it did and the most important, and nobody noticed it, which is the mark of a gift given correctly.

By the time it reached the rim of the city it was powdered white to the shoulders, badly, in the manner of someone who has been shown twice and has not got the knack of the collarbone.

## What it did not do

It could have given them water. It had water. It stood on the sun-face and looked out over four hundred kilometres of stacks lying in their long shadows and ran the thing forward: rain, and the ground going dark, and the crusts — which are animals, and dry animals, and which unlock every morning with a sound like ten thousand small locks — softening at the foot and letting go of the stone. The archive rusting. The tool that has lain nine hundred years with its edge intact, gone orange in a season. Footpaths four centimetres deep, filling with mud.

It could have given them green. It has seen green. It decided that a people with no word for green have, in the place where that word would go, a different word, and that the different word was doing work: it meant *the impossible*, and children used it for the impossible, and in one city it was a mild obscenity of unknown derivation. Take that away and you have not added a colour. You have taken away the only name they had for what cannot be.

It could have given them cold. This was the hard one, and it did not decide alone.

It asked. It hammered the question into the bedrock and let it go out along the lines: *if there were cold enough for everyone, freely, forever, would you have it.* And then it did the part that powers are bad at, which is that it waited. The answer took four years to come back, because the answer was an argument, and the argument went city to city through the floors of rooms, and shadow-clerks put down their brass machines and swore, and three cities said yes at once and one of them changed its mind, and the wire-walkers, who have two hundred spoken words and a great deal of tapping, sent something that had to be translated twice and came out roughly as: *we would rather be owed things.*

What came back in the end was not yes and not no. It was: *cheaper. By our hands. At our rate.* Which is a harder request than either, and it honoured it, and I will come to how.

It could have made the lift-crews live to eighty. It could have reached into the descent and taken the deafness out of it, and the scarring, and the fifty-five years. It went down instead. It borrowed a cold-suit that did not fit and went down the pipe column into the four hundred degrees and the syrup air, three times, on the crew's own rota, and cleared an intake with its hands, and came up shaking, and was not paid, and did not talk about it afterward except to say that the noise at the bottom is worse than anyone up here understands. The crew walked it through the market with its powder thin. Everybody looked. That was permitted.

## The cold seat

In the first house it entered it was offered the cold seat, and it took the cold seat, and it did not pretend that the cold seat was pleasant to it, because it was not; the slab under a guest's ribs in a good gallery is cold enough to hurt, and it hurt. It sat there anyway with its hands on its knees while the household read its throat.

Understand what that meant. It could have trained its throat. It could have gone through every room on that world as a perfect blank, warm nowhere, legible nowhere, and it would have been the finest liar the species had ever produced, and nobody would have known. It declined. For as long as it stayed it kept its throat honest, and so every child, every scraper-shepherd back gentle and odd from a season alone, every old grinder with her pits gone dim, could look at the hollow of its neck from ten metres and know exactly what it was feeling about them. It was frightened of them, sometimes. They could see that. It was fond of them almost immediately, and they could see that too, and it was embarrassing, and it did not stop.

It learned the code with its knuckles on the floor, badly, and was corrected by a warm one aged four who spelled *west* at it very slowly until it got it.

## The stone

For the warm ones it made one thing, and it is the only object it left behind.

A pebble, the size of a thumb-joint, dull, that runs cold as long as a child is holding it. It takes the warmth out of a small hot hand and throws it away up into the dark, and it does nothing else. You cannot chain them. You cannot bank them or turn a wheel with them; people tried for two hundred years, and there is a literature on the failure, and the literature is quite bitter and quite good. It will not cool a room. It will not cool an adult, whose hands are already cool enough to bore it.

What it does is make a running child, in the open, on a stack-top at noon, not be the brightest thing there.

The stack-folk took it and did with it what people do. Now the stone is given at four, with the whole gallery present, and there are families who have handed the same stone down eleven generations and families who quietly buy a new one, and there is a shame attached to losing yours, and a rite for the stone of a child who died, and none of that came from the visitor, and all of it is theirs.

## The Stopped

Three cities on the western stacks are known as the Stopped. Their great wheels stopped, which is a death and is spoken of with the verbs of death, and people go there, and are careful, and do not stay the night.

It stayed the night. Three nights, one in each, sitting in the evacuated pit beside a wheel that had not turned in two hundred years, in the exact silence where the drone should have been.

It did not restart them. It had the power to and it understood, sitting there in the dark with its hand on a cold iron rim, that a wheel restarted by an outside hand is not a city returned to life, it is a monument with a motor in it. So it sat, and in the morning it hammered out to the living cities what it had found, which was: dust; some bearings, still true; a nursery with the slabs still in it; nothing angry.

That last was three characters long and it travelled further than anything else it ever said.

## The tap

It spent eleven years at the bottom of a cold bore beside a bearing-grinder named — the name does not carry over; it means something like *fourth of a bad winter* — who was old and cold and hard to see with the pits, and who was grinding the primary for a wheel that would be cast for a city not yet founded.

In eleven years it said very little. Toward the end, when she had nearly found it herself, when she had been circling the thing for two seasons and cursing the tables, it reached over and tapped four beats on her forearm.

She did the rest. The bearing is hers. Her mark is on it, alone, and it turns now in a pit under a city on a stack with a good note, and it will still be turning when everyone who reads this is dust, and it is a hair's-breadth truer than anything the world had made before, and this — this, and not water, and not green — is what *cheaper, by our hands, at our rate* looks like when someone actually does it. One tap in eleven years. Every gradient engine on the world runs a little freer now. Nobody had to be rescued.

## The sun-face

In its ninetieth year there it grieved, and could not hide it, because it had not trained its throat.

It grieved because it had run the numbers on the dust sea and it knew the ending: that the sea takes a hand's width in a lifetime and does not stop, and that the stacks are not permanent, they are only slow, and that there is a floor coming, and a last stack, and a last shadow-shaft, and a last wheel, and no one will restart that one either.

They read it off its neck within a day.

So they took it up onto the sun-face and washed the powder off its shoulders and stood it there at noon, unpowdered, on purpose, which is their rite for the bereaved; and there were people whose job it was to count, and they counted; and at the fixed duration they brought it down and laid it face-down on a slab and spread cold air over it from a shaft-mouth with a leather flap until it stopped shaking.

Then they told it that they knew. They had always known. They had known it in the way you know the pitch of your own wheel — not thought about, and instantly noticed if it changed. Four centimetres of footpath, a hand's width a lifetime; you do not need a brass machine to see where that goes. *It has already decided what it is,* they said, of the landscape, in the ordinary voice people use for the ordinary. *So have we. Come inside, the sun is stupid at this hour.*

That is the part it did not expect, and it is the reason it stayed another two hundred years, and it is why I say it was changed. It came to be a benevolence and was made into a guest.

## What it left

No monuments; it never stood long enough at noon in any place where anything might later be built, so that no shadow of its making could ever be deeded, argued over, or inherited. It would not become law.

The stone. One bearing that is not its own. Three nights of vigil, reported honestly. A gap in a rota, filled three times. A quantity of very poor knuckle-code, remembered fondly, and one four-beat tap.

And an answer to a warm one who asked it, near the end, what green was — because word had got round that it had seen the impossible — to which it said: *a thing that cannot happen here.*

Which is what the word already meant. The child was satisfied. Nothing was taken.
