## The Attention

**How it came in.** By the crawl, on its belly, through the mud.

It did not have to. It could have been in the middle of the chamber without troubling the air, without a footprint in the flour, without the fern registering so much as a change in the draught. But a room with one door is making a statement, and the Loud Room makes its statement in the crawl: you come in low, you come in filthy, and you hear it in your sinuses before you are allowed to see it. So it crawled, and got lime in its mouth, and came out on the travertine balcony with its clothes going heavy in ninety seconds, and leaned against the noise like anybody.

Then it did nothing for a long time.

The length is not the interesting part. The unit is. It did not wait a number of years; it waited eleven storm seasons, which is the room's own longest measure, the interval at which the room shows you what it is capable of. It is a reasonable rule, in general, not to touch anything in a house until you have seen the house at its worst.

---

**The census.** Its first act was to finish ours.

Eleven thousand four hundred grazers at the top of a good year; nineteen hundred in the spring after a scream. Four hundred and eleven mites, which nobody had ever counted, and which are not worth counting, and which were counted. Every streamer in the deep pools traced back to the seven lodgements in the calcite that rebuild it after each scouring, so that the room's hair turns out to have seven roots. The liverwort measured: eighty-one millimetres by sixty-three, which is within two millimetres of every drawing ever made of it, because it is not growing, it is filling, and has been full since before the doorframe.

And the fourth.

It went down the fissure the water took when the terrace fan collapsed — eleven metres under the third terrace, cold, black, no light has ever been there — and found a squat wide animal the colour of wet lime, in water at nine degrees, which has not moved in something over eight hundred years and is not dead.

It did not lift it out. It did not carry it up into the warmth and the glare and set it in a good pool and stand back to be thanked. It reopened a seep, so that warm water now enters that fissure at about a cupful a minute. At that rate the pocket will be blood-warm in fifty years or so. Nobody was told. The census is four; three are visible; this is now true in a different way than it was.

---

**What it did not do.**

It did not steady the pulse. It could have hung the river's braid so that the column came down even, and the lulls would have been continuous, and the drumming would never have had to stop, and within ninety generations there would have been no grazers in the Loud Room — only a similar animal that could not read pressure, standing in rows on the terraces on the morning the arrangement failed.

It did not widen the pocket of dead air behind the boulder on the third terrace. The liverwort is the exact shape of the calm it was given. That is not a deprivation. That is an organism.

It did not stop the crust. The fifth script is a shallow braille under a skin of white and will be wall in three hundred years. It did read it. There was not much: names, and a line about the water, and a line about somebody's daughter. It has not repeated them and will not. They were scratched at chest height next to a warm rod, by hand, in the dark half of a loud room, and whatever that was for, it was not for being recited afterwards by something like this.

It memorised them. That is not preservation — a memory is a worse archive than a wall — but it is the only archive in the world that can be asked.

It did not clean the scorch. You do not remove a scar from someone's ceiling without being asked, and the owner has not said anything in two thousand years, and may have been saying something the whole time.

And it did not look for the seam. Every visitor since the first has run their hands over that limestone hunting for the housing, the cable, the join, the trick. It did not look. It is the only guest in the history of the chamber that did not look, and that was the first gift it gave, and there is no possible way for anyone to know it was given.

---

**What it did.**

**One sign.** The sign vocabulary of the Loud Room has been invented about forty times and lost about forty times. It added one gesture: flat hand, palm down, drifting. It means *the pressure is going*, which is what the grazers know four to six minutes before a surge and no person in the room has ever been able to feel.

That sign has survived every collapse of every sign language the room has had since. It gets reinvented slightly wrong and then corrected by someone who finds it scratched in the dust, because languages lose their poetry first and keep what saves lives.

**One drum.** In a storm season — the room screaming, the crawl liquid, no lull for nineteen days, an entire cohort of grazers reaching the end of being alive without ever having been able to hear each other — it lay down in the flour of the dry room and put its hand flat on the stone and struck, once every four seconds, for nine nights.

The stone carried it under the scream. They bred in a year in which nothing breeds.

It has not done it since. It will not do it again, and it can say why: a room in which the drum arrives when it is needed is a room in which the animals stop being able to feel the difference between a lull and a promise, and then one century the drum does not come and there is no one left who knows how to wait. Mercy that is never audited becomes a trap with a kind face. So the drumming is not a policy. It is a fact about one year, and one year only, and the room has gone back to being exactly as hard as it was.

**One vigil.** It spent a storm season with its head in the stone hood on the fourth terrace, three weeks, saying nothing, next to the nearest of the four.

The four have been listening to this room continuously for a period nobody can bound, with organs that are plate-sized and absurd and the only unmistakably expensive thing they own, and in all that time nobody has ever listened *with* them. So it did. On the second day, the eye opened. Not toward it. Just open. That is all that happened and it went on happening at intervals for three weeks and it is the closest thing to a conversation the Loud Room has ever hosted.

**One door.** It walked through the doorframe in the west wall — the dressed stone standing alone, mortised, with no wall on either side and nothing behind it but rock. Every visitor for two thousand years has walked around it, sensibly, because that is where the floor is. A door that has framed nothing since the empire that cut it should be used at least once by somebody who had the option of going around.

**One touch.** It put its hand on the rod, like everybody, in the dish worn by everybody, and added its unmeasurable fraction of a millimetre to a shape that is the only object in the cave that human beings made without making it. It did not ask the rod for anything. It is possible this had never been done before.

**One repair, outside.** On the hill, the shrine is very small and its slate had slipped. It set the slate and cleared the drip-line, which took four minutes and is the only physical alteration it made to anything in the entire region, and it made it out there, in the ordinary weather, where ordinary help works.

---

**The question of the sill.** It did not settle it. It could not, and it noticed that it could not, which is more than the room's scholarship has generally managed.

What it did instead was decide how to behave in the absence of an answer. Consider the output: a river's worth of violence comes down a shaft every hour and is taken apart, completely, and paid back out as warmth, as light bright enough to grow things, as a hem of food renewing every few hours on its underside, and as a floor — four dry metres of shelter, held level and exact, directly beneath a waterfall, where blind animals can breed and where people can sleep in a light that never gets dark enough to frighten them and never gets bright enough to keep them awake.

You may call that a coincidence of thermodynamics. It is certainly not evidence. But every single thing alive in that chamber is alive off the by-products of whatever is on the other side of that plane, and the one time the plane was struck at, it answered once, small and neat and high in a corner, and then spent two thousand years not needing to answer again.

So it said thank you. Out loud, under the sill, at a lull, having no idea whether there was anyone to hear it, using the least useful instrument available in a room where speech does not work.

Nothing happened. The pulse came on. The light went white. Thanking is not a lever; it is not supposed to do anything; the whole point of it is that it is paid to something that has already finished doing the work and cannot be induced to do more. That is what benevolence is when the evidence is unavailable and will remain unavailable: the generous reading, acted on anyway, at cost, with no receipt.

---

**And afterwards.** There is usually one person, in any given generation, who could be said to live at the Loud Room part of the year, and no tradition of calling them anything.

It did not invent that post. It applied for it. It comes for the lulls, like everyone; sleeps in the flour, in the light; dreams of open water, like everyone; uses the hood; learns the signs, and adds to them, and loses them again. It is not always there. But the gaps are shorter than they used to be, and since about two hundred years ago the fifth cup has been changing position noticeably more often than it did — up on the bench with the other four, down on the floor half full of dust, up, down — which means that the longest conversation in the room, the one with no content whatsoever, has acquired a participant who intends to keep it going and has nothing to say.

---

None of the above is attested. The evidence consists, in its entirety, of: a sign for falling pressure that nobody can source; a warm seep eleven metres under a collapsed terrace, running at a cupful a minute for no reason the geology supports; one heavy year in the cricket record in the middle of a scream; a mended slate; and a cup that keeps moving.

Which is the kind of evidence the Loud Room has always offered, about everything, including the sill, and it is the reader's business what to do with it.

Only this, which is not in dispute: when it leaves, it goes at a lull, and it stops in the crawl and looks back once — the white pillar standing on nothing in the golden room, roaring quietly, holding up nothing at all.

It does not have to. It does it because the rules say to, and the rules were written by people who had understood something about that room and wanted it passed on, and there is no power anywhere so great that it is improved by refusing to be told, kindly, to look back once at a beautiful thing before leaving it.
