## Kell, continued

He counts crabs in a marked square metre and gets the same number he got last time and the time before, at both ends of the flat. He writes it down anyway. He has never once recorded a number that surprised him, and he goes on recording, because if a number ever does surprise him he will want the whole long run of the ones that didn't, to prove that it happened.

That is the seven.

Or that is one being with seven names and seven boot-scrapers, which is how it looked from outside, when something from outside finally looked.

---

## The Attention

It did not come because of the strangeness. Strangeness is common. It came the way Ivett goes along the shelves: aisle by aisle, room by room, to the end and then back to the beginning, thoroughly, without preference, and Kettle came up in its turn.

Two things held it there.

The first was the lamp in the shrine — a wardrobe with a tin roof, and inside it the only light in four kilometres that anybody had troubled to make brighter than the rest. Nobody in Kettle admits to filling it. It is full.

The second was a plate of rice and fish going into the lagoon every day, and a ring of pale crabs standing back from it with their one red claw each, not closing.

Looking closer, it could not at first find the edges of what lived there. It tried to draw a line around the being and the line kept including things: eight dogs under the dormitory with the boardwalks divided between them by treaty, four kites at four heights, the flat one of the relief valves that is Sarn's boiler and which Sarn loves, twenty-two pans of which thirteen have a density and the density is zero and zero gets written down. A stack leaning four degrees. A clock in a drawer, wrapped in oiled rag, keeping perfect time in the dark for no one.

So it stopped trying to draw the line and simply attended to all of it, which is what attention is for.

## What It Weighed, and Put Down

It could have cut a channel. One finger drawn across that sharp clay lip, from the lagoon into the low ground, and the water would have come in and found the fall and begun the long ordinary work of gullies. But water cut from outside silts shut. A channel is not a shape, it is an argument between a height and a lower height, and there was no height in Kettle that wanted to be lower. It would have healed over in a season and left them with a story about the day the ground was interfered with.

It could have broken the sky. That would have been the obvious gift: the first rule undone, rain on twenty-two pans and thirty-one roofs, everyone standing out in it with their faces up. And then the sky would have closed, and the pans' level would have come back by evening, because evaporation and seepage have shaken hands, and Thessa would have entered a figure in the second decimal that returned to its old value by the next reading. A miracle that changes nothing teaches only that nothing can be changed. That is a worse lesson than the one they already had.

It could have localised the sun. One disc, one hour, one set of shadows with edges. It declined, on the grounds that this is cruelty wearing a very good coat: an hour of that, and then a lifetime of comparing.

It could have taken them out. It looked hard at this one, because it is what benevolence usually reaches for first. Then it walked the line of prints going west — the first survey party's, filled with clear warm water, holding their edges — and understood that the door was standing open and had always been standing open, and that people go out through it and are not missed, and that the dormitory row keeps its seven. Nobody in Kettle is imprisoned. That is the trouble. An exit is not the same as a reason, and there is no shortage of exits here; there is a shortage of the other thing.

It could have given them back their years. But you cannot return what was never spent. The years are not stored anywhere. They were not taken from the seven; they were declined, one at a time, out of what began — the attention was fairly sure — as somebody's mercy. Somewhere back before the count of the pages, something happened, or was about to happen, and a voice in this place said *no further*, and the mud stopped compacting and the buildings finished settling and held their tilts, and the sentence *no further* has been running ever since with the whole weight of the flat behind it, and holding it has cost them everything that was not the holding.

It did not look for whose voice it was. That is an audit, and audits are Ivett's work, and there was nothing on those shelves to find.

And it could have explained them to themselves: laid the whole diagnosis out in the crystallizer shed on that floor of salt polished to marble, every detail with its meaning pinned beside it — the four bolt-holes, the second pump assembled only in Anselm's head, the drafts of the letters of complaint. It put this down too, and quickly. To be perfectly understood by a great power and then left otherwise unchanged is one of the loneliest things available.

What it saw instead was arithmetic. Salt out equals salt shipped. Orders exactly what has left; orders exactly filled. The coal heap holding its shape. Every exchange in Kettle closes, to the sack, and a system whose every exchange closes has no future, because a future is a debt — it is the state of being owed something you have not yet been given, and of owing something you have not yet made.

There was one open account in the whole settlement. One plate, laid every day, offered to nothing, going into the lagoon off the end of the bathhouse walk.

So it took the plate.

## The Eighth

It came in along the west prints, which is the direction people leave by, and arrived glossy and brine-smelling like everyone, because nothing dries in Kettle and it did not exempt itself. It looked like anyone. It looked like the sort of person who turns up at a works and asks whether there's a bed.

Under the dormitory, one of the eight dogs got up, crossed a boardwalk that was not its own boardwalk, and followed. The treaty did not break. It just had a clause nobody had used.

At the meal it sat down at the eighth place before the eighth plate was cleared.

"That one isn't spoken for," Ma Ounn said.

"It's spoken for," it said. "I'm late."

It ate all of it, slowly, the rice and the fish from the margin and the greens off the lighter and an astonishing quantity of chilli, and it sweated the way they all sweat, and it did not talk while it ate. Steam sighed from six or seven relief valves in a chord that did not resolve. The pumps went thud, gap, thud-thud. Somewhere the whistle blew for no shift change.

Then it set the fork down and said: "Thank you. Will there be more tomorrow?"

Coy's answer to something else arrived offbeat and then stopped. Sarn had his hand flat on the table. Ivett's rag was in his lap. It is not that they did not know the word. It is that the word had nothing on the other end of it — there was no *tomorrow* in Kettle, only again, and again is not the same shape at all.

Ma Ounn stood in the doorway of her own kitchen with a scraped plate in her hand and did the largest thing anyone had done there in a very long time. She made a promise.

"There will be," she said.

That was the intervention. One plate of rice and fish that went in and did not come back out, and one sentence about a day that had not happened yet.
