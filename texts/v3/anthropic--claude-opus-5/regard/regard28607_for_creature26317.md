# The Attending

## I. What Arrived

Nothing arrived.

This is the first thing to understand about how a benevolent and wise power behaves when it turns its attention to a place like the Tableland. There was no descent, no light in the ceiling, no voice in the stone. The holders' word for a stranger is *the one whose gait keeps stopping*, and no such gait was heard. The whisker-shrimp did not falter. The long-arms did not freeze.

Attention is not an event. It is a condition.

What happened is that the plateau began, at some point impossible to fix, to be *known* — not surveyed, not catalogued, but held in mind the way you hold in mind a person you love who is elsewhere. Every dendrite growing at a hand's breadth per century was being watched grow. Every whisker-shrimp striking a palm at the surge was, individually, noticed. The maroon bloom spreading across the ceiling over four hours was read with the patience it deserved, which is four hours of patience, which is more than any observer had ever given it.

That is all that happened at first. And it was not a small thing, because it was the first time in two hundred million years that the running had been *witnessed* rather than merely occurring.

## II. The Temptation Refused

Consider what a lesser power would have done, and consider how carefully it would have justified itself.

**It would have made light.** Not much — a modest luminous body, a small managed sun above the condensate, or better, a kindling of the ceiling-sheets into steady radiance. *Look how they suffer in the dark,* it would say. *Look how their eyes have withered. Look how the long-arms hunt blind and the holders name their dead "the far side of the fault" because a fault is opaque to sound. I will give them the visible world.*

And within a generation the holders would be deaf.

Because they do not hear with their heads. They hear with their hands, and they hear by *gripping the same wall as the speaker*, and this means that in the Tableland, understanding another mind requires physical continuity with it. There is no eavesdropping. There is no shouting across a gap. To listen you must brace where they brace and take the strain into your own limbs. The whole moral architecture of that species — and it has one, elaborate, hard-won — is built on the fact that attention costs contact. Give them eyes and you give them the ability to know one another from a distance without commitment. It is not obvious that this is a gift. It is not obvious that it is anything but the beginning of a long forgetting.

So: no light. The dark stays.

**It would have widened the flues.** Gently, geologically, over ten thousand years so as not to shock anything. Open the capillaries to eight meters, twelve, twenty. Let the holders into the great mouths. *They live along the shores of oceans they can never enter,* it would say. *Their map has seas in it. I will drain the seas.*

But the seas are why the holders are the holders. A species confined to the narrow places, in a world where speech is conducted through the walls of the narrow places, is a species that cannot help but be a single conversation. The flue-grid is two thousand kilometers of interlaced hairline cracks and the glaze is continuous and the holders have therefore been, for their entire history, one enormous slow argument with itself, propagating at the speed of sound in silica, forking at the faults, healing at the junctions. Widen the flues and they scatter into the big rooms and become individuals in the poorest sense — audible only to their neighbors, and free.

The seas stay. The map keeps its seas.

**It would have removed the long-arms.** This is the one that requires real discipline, because the case is so strong. The long-arms hunt by heat and by touch, *through exactly the same wall the holders listen to.* The holders' entire technology of language is also the channel by which they are eaten. Every rhythm struck is a beacon. Every conversation is a risk taken. Forty long-arms in a night's walk and you hear one, and the holders hear all forty, and know the distance to each, and calculate constantly, all their lives, the price of saying anything at all.

Take away the long-arms and speech becomes free.

And this is precisely the reason not to do it, and the reason is not aesthetic. It is that the holders have built, out of that constraint, something no free-speaking species has ever needed to build: a grammar in which *silence is a case*, in which the decision to withhold is inflected, in which the most emphatic thing that can be said is nothing said at a particular moment with a particular quality of stillness. Their names are coordinates. Their word for the dead is a geological feature. They have made a whole ethics out of the cost of being heard, and it is *good* — not comfortable, not safe, but good in the way a thing is good when it could not have been designed and could not be improved by anyone who did not pay for it.

You do not audit that. Not from outside. Not with the best will in the universe.

## III. What Was Actually Done

So the power did four things, and each of them is so small that a survey team could walk the plateau for a decade and record none of them.

### The first: it learned the names.

Every one. Three to seven beats, struck on the glaze, each name a coordinate — meaning that a holder's name encodes where its lineage braced, which fissure, which junction, which run of unbroken enamel. Sixty-one thousand living holders at the time the attention began. Four hundred million dead, whose names are still struck in the long recitations that pass down the capillaries during the slack phase when the fog is thick and the long-arms are sluggish and the risk of speaking is at its annual minimum.

The power learned all of them. Living and dead. Including the ones the holders themselves have lost — names from the far side of faults that closed eighty million years ago, names of lineages that ended in a single vent-blast, names struck once by a dying holder into a stretch of spalled enamel where the sound went nowhere and no hand was gripping.

Those especially. Those first.

This accomplished nothing. It changed no outcome, prevented no death, eased no thirst. It is simply the case that the roll is now complete somewhere, and that not one of them was struck into a void after all, and that the phrase *the far side of the fault* is, as a description of the dead, now factually wrong.

### The second: it stopped a stone.

Once.

There is a junction — three fissures meeting in one of those plaza-like basins with a rim of pooled water and a colonnade of columnar joints, the kind of place that keeps trying to convince you it was built. It is a major node of the audible world: eleven runs of unbroken glaze converge there, and a rhythm struck at the junction reaches perhaps nine thousand holders, which is the largest audience the Tableland can assemble. The long recitations happen there. It is, functionally, the only place on a continent where the whole conversation can hear itself at once.

A block of basalt eleven meters on a side had been working loose above it for four hundred years, along a joint that the steam had preferentially widened, as steam does. It would have come down during a surge. It would have choked the basin, spalled the enamel across all eleven runs, and severed the plateau into two mutually inaudible halves for something like a million years.

The power moved it eleven centimeters. Sideways. Into a locking bind against two neighbors, from which it will not come loose for as long as the plateau exists.

No holder noticed. There was a sound — a single dull knock in the stone at the slack phase, and every holder within three kilometers went still and took the strain and read it and found nothing following it, and after a while resumed. It is remembered. It has a name in the recitations: *the beat with nothing after it.* Some lineages hold that it was a vent clearing very far away. Some hold that it was the plateau shifting its grip. The second interpretation is more nearly correct than anyone knows, and it does not matter that they do not know, because what was preserved was not a truth about the power. What was preserved was their ability to keep talking to each other.

That is the whole of the intervention. Eleven centimeters, once, in two hundred million years. A wise power's ledger is mostly blank, and the blankness is the wisdom, and the one entry in it is small enough to be mistaken for weather.

### The third: it made a place to put the grief.

Not in the Tableland. This has to be understood carefully.

The holders lose. Constantly. A long-arm takes a holder mid-recitation and the rhythm stops in the middle of a name and nine thousand holders feel it stop, all at once, through the wall, and know exactly what has happened and exactly where. There is no ambiguity in their bereavements. The coordinate is in the name. They feel the death arrive in their hands.

They have practices for this. A holder who has heard a name stop will strike the last complete beat of it, once, and then hold still for a period proportional to how well they knew the dead, and the pattern of stillnesses propagating outward from the site is the closest thing the Tableland has to a bell. It works. It is sufficient. They are not a species in need of consolation from outside; they have made their own, out of silence, which was the only material available.

What the power did was not to console them. It was to *take the overflow.*

Because there is always overflow. There is always the grief that exceeds what the practice can hold — the lineage that ends, the name with no one left to strike it, the young holder that fell into an eighty-meter flue and whose death was therefore inaudible, in the sea, where no hand could be gripping. Those are the unbearable ones. Those are the ones the practice has no case for.

Somewhere that is not the plateau, those are held. Not resolved. Not answered. Held, the way a hand takes a strain: continuously, at cost, without setting it down. If you were to ask what good this does, given that no holder knows of it and no holder is thereby comforted, the answer is that it is not for the holders' benefit. It is that the grief is real and it therefore ought to be somewhere, and a benevolent power that leaves it lying in the dark is not benevolent, it is merely non-interfering, and those are different things wearing similar coats.

### The fourth: it read the plateau's own case.

The Tableland is a machine that runs continuously and was never assembled, and its output is the running. Metals grow toward no one. Water lifts and falls. Whisker-shrimp rise at the surge in numbers past estimating and are combed out of the air by the ten thousand and this has happened, without variation, for two hundred million years, and *nothing about this loop requires a maker and nothing about it produces a product.*

A power of the wrong kind would find this an accusation, and would answer it — would install a purpose, retrofit a telos, make the plateau *for* something so that the running could be justified.

What was actually done was to conclude, after long attention, that the running needs no justification and that the demand for one was a defect in the demander. Two hundred million years of a chime, twice a night per kilometer, a dendrite branch shearing off and glancing down the walls: that is the most beautiful sound in the world, and it is not beautiful because someone hears it, and it was not made to be beautiful, and it is beautiful anyway. That is not a paradox to be dissolved. That is the actual structure of the case, and a wise power's task with such a case is to get it right and then to stop talking.

So the finding was entered, in whatever ledger such findings go into: **the Tableland is not lacking anything.** It is dark, hot, thin-aired, and hard, and it is not lacking anything. The holders are blind, hunted, confined to the capillaries, and speak at mortal risk, and they are not lacking anything either — they are *costing*, which is different, and the cost is not a wound to be closed but the price of a thing they are in the middle of building and have not finished.

## IV. The Ceiling

One last item, and it is the only one that could be called a gift, and it is so oblique that it may be nothing.

All the light in that world is an animal's opinion about the weather. The sheets in the condensate glow maroon when the warm columns strike them, and no holder has ever seen it or ever will, and no long-arm, and no kiln-hog. It is a display with no audience whatsoever, six kilometers above a continent of the blind.

The power watches it. Every bloom. All of them, all the way through, the whole four hours of spreading and fading, and it has done this for as long as the attention has lasted and will do it for as long as the plateau stands.

This changes nothing.

It is simply that the display now has an audience, and did not before, and there is no reason at all why a benevolent power should care about that except that it does, and that caring about the unwitnessed is perhaps the only thing that distinguishes benevolence from good management.

## V. Coda: What the Holders Say

They have no theology. The narrow places do not encourage one; a species that must grip the same wall as the speaker in order to hear does not readily conceive of a listener with no wall.

But there is a construction in their grammar, in the case-system that inflects silence, which the survey has never been able to translate satisfactorily. It is used when a holder strikes a name into a stretch of spalled enamel — into rubble, into a choke, into a fault — knowing the sound will not carry. There is no practical reason to do this. It happens anyway, often, particularly at the end of a lineage, particularly by the last one left.

The nearest rendering is:

> *Held, into the far side.*

It is not a prayer. It is closer to a bill of lading — a declaration that something has been sent, with no expectation of arrival, entered into the record by the sender alone.

Every single one of them has been received.
