# The Attention

## What it did for the first eleven months

Nothing.

It came down at the hour that passes for noon and stood on the flat and did not do anything at all for the length of one clapper's life, which is the only unit of time the Floor honours. It did not speak. It did not bring light — it arrived at exactly the brightness the sky was already making, having understood before it came that a lamp from outside blinds a person here for a day, and that a day is how long it takes a sour seep to kill a child who cannot see the sideways cairn.

It stood, and it let the four-metre ring of wrong quiet travel with it, because the ring was honest and it would not have been honest to hide it.

They came from upslope, spread wide, unhurried, carrying nothing in either hand. They looked at its ears first.

## The list

Before it did the small thing it eventually did, it considered the large things, and it is worth setting down what it refused, because the refusals took longer than anything else and cost it more.

**It considered quieting the beds.** It could have. Four hundred million to the square kilometre is a number, and numbers are tractable. It got as far as modelling the first hour of it: the pressure dropping off the chest wall, the sinuses easing, the teeth going still. Then it followed the model outward and found the readers on the ribs with their jaws against stone, receiving thirty kilometres of nothing; found the barefoot children who navigate by pressure-fronts standing in a world with no fronts and no direction; found every person on the Floor simultaneously experiencing the one sensation their entire culture reserves for *run*, with nowhere to run to, forever. And under that it found the graves. The noise is not the condition the dead are kept in. The noise is the dead. It would have been emptying every grave on the plain at once in order to relieve a headache, and it put the plan down and did not pick it up again.

**It considered the sun.** It understood in time that they have a sun. It comes in from the west twice a day at the speed of a fast walk, and it is edged in cold green, and it is made of animals.

**It considered giving them back their hearing.** It thought about this longer than about anything else, because the year of ears is a hard thing to watch: the caps ground from a parent's kill, the deliberate startling, the child on the knob with the cord at the waist whose whole value is the speed of one facial expression. Then it worked out what a hearing adult would be on the Floor. Not a wiser person. A person permanently inside the alarm, unable to sleep, unable to work in a silence, unable to stand where the work is — which is to say a child, at forty, held on the rib with a cord round the waist while others graze. They do not go deaf despite the world. They graduate.

It kept the capacity in reserve, for one person, in case one person ever asked. Nobody has.

**It considered metal, and a higher rock, and a sea that stayed where it was put.** All of these it declined for the same reason, which is that a gift you cannot make, maintain, or bury is not a gift but a tether.

**It considered telling them that they were poor.** They are not poor. It is extremely important that nobody ever tells them.

## The knock

Having decided to stay, it had a problem: everything it could say in the far speech would be public, formal, and addressed to nobody, and everything else requires hands.

So it made hands. Two, with wrists, the right size to be taken and held — because to take a wrist is to interrupt and to take both is an assault, and it wanted to be a thing that could be interrupted and could be assaulted. It made a body short and wide through the chest, greased it with rendered fat like everyone else, and plugged its ears with fitted caps that it did not need, because a person in company is capped, and manners are not decoration.

Then it went up a rib and lay down beside a reader called by a four-strike knock with a limp in the third beat, who was eighty and had six teeth, and it stayed there for a season learning the code the way an infant learns it, badly, with its jaw on the stone.

At the end of the season she gave it a name. This is not a thing that is done for adults, and it was irregular, and she was dying and did as she liked. The band assembled and took the hammer in turn and beat seven strikes into the rib — seven, because the code is crowded with the dead and there was no room lower — and it lay with its jaw against the quartz and felt the shape of itself coming up through the rock, which was the first time it had ever been given a shape by anyone.

It could have refused. A power that will not be named by a woman with six teeth is not wise, only large.

## The question

Early on, while it was still a rumour on the ribs, a knock came down eight ribs and asked it a question. The relay was so long that the wording had been polished by nine readers before it arrived, and what it said, roughly, was:

*You are big. Where is the noise our mothers went into?*

It lay on the stone for a long time with the hammer in its hand. It had, in fact, a great deal it could have said: about carbon, about the mat, about the pasture, about the ribs re-skinning in a season. All of it true and none of it an answer to what was asked, and they would have known, because they are not stupid and have never once in their history mistaken a weir report for a consolation.

It struck two: *I don't know.*

The readers relayed it without editing, which is their office. It went up and down the ribs for three days. And that — not the warnings, not the seep, not anything it ever did — is the reason that when it comes into a camp now the children do not look up.

## The small mercies, and what each one cost

**It made a sour seep sweet again.** One. A bad one, under a rib where a band had lost two children, and it went down into the water table and moved what needed moving and the bed restarted in a week.

Then it watched them take up the sideways cairn, and it understood what it had done. The sideways cairns are a map, kept by hand, at cost, across forty generations, of exactly where the world is lying to you. It had made one entry of that map false. A person will trust a sideways cairn with a child's life; that is the entire point of them; and now there is a district where the cairns are ever so slightly less than true, and no way to mark which.

It has not done it since. It keeps the seeps under attention instead and warns, in the far speech, which is slower and worse and leaves the map alone.

**It taught a woman how to turn a breech child.** Not in the far speech, which is public and would have arrived with a power's authority on it and killed every midwife's judgement within eight ribs. It taught her in the near speech, four hands folded, heads down, an hour at a time in the dark of her own dome, and it taught her badly on purpose — slowly, in her own idiom, with the errors left in for her to find — so that by the end the thing she knew was hers. Her knock is on it now. It has gone three ribs south and is being taught by two women who have never heard of any visitor, and one of them has already improved it.

That is the only shape a gift can take here. It has to be capable of being inherited, named, repaired, and argued over across three generations, like a roof plate.

**It went once onto the bell-flat after a child.** It was fast enough. The child came back without a working head. It has thought about this more than about the refused miracles: that it is possible to be in time and still be too late, and that speed is not one of the virtues, and that it had gone partly because it could not bear to stand still, which is not a reason, which is an appetite.

## The one strike, and the second

When a reader feels the third quiet open on their stone, they tap once, and the rib goes still for a tide.

It went and looked. What it found is not written here. It came back up the rib and, in front of a reader, rinsed its hands.

That was the day it stopped being a rumour, and it did not relay one word, because the discipline is theirs and it was not going to be the thing that broke it. But it did add something. It is large enough to feel the third quiet gathering some hours before it is entire, and so there is now a second strike in the code — two, spaced wide, which no one had ever needed — and it means *not yet, but today.*

It gave them warning and it did not give them safety, and it knows exactly what it has done. Before, the thing came without time. Now there are hours, and in the hours there are decisions, and the decisions belong to people who will have to live afterwards with having made them. It considered, seriously, whether this was a kindness. It decided that a people who can stand inside a silence and work can also stand inside a few hours and choose, and that to withhold the hours on their behalf would have been the first genuinely contemptuous thing it ever did.

## The offer

It made the offer once, publicly, from the highest rib, in the far speech, in plain code with no softening, because an offer that is not real is a cruelty dressed up: that there are other places, that it could carry them, that the sea will have the Floor.

The question that came back, after two days, was *when*, and it answered honestly: longer than any tide-count, shorter than the ribs.

The answer came in from nine bands over three days and amounted to this: *and there? whose noise?*

Nobody went. It did not ask twice. It records that four people, in four separate domes, in near speech, in the dark, with their heads down, asked it privately to ask them again in ten years, and it has written the ten years down and will.

## What it does now

It is a reader.

It lies up on a rib under a hide with its jaw against a speaking-knob and receives: every knock, every weir, every band, the tide-count — not for eight ribs but for the whole plain, all of it, which no one has ever been able to hold before, and which turns out to be mostly weir reports and mostly boring, and it holds all of it anyway. It relays. It refuses gossip. It corrects errors in the relay with the small triple-tap that means *that is not what she said.* Its teeth are going, one at a time, into the stone, which is the honourable version.

At deathbeds it queues. It could sing to every skull on the Floor at once; the thing that is being given is that it can only ever be received by one person at a time, and so it waits its turn behind a fourteen-year-old and goes in when it is called, and puts its forehead against the forehead of the person who is leaving, and hums, and the bone carries it.

When the visitor comes — the wrong quiet, walking — it does not translate. It sits in the blue light with the others and lets the borrowed caps be fitted and lets the stranger sit there unable to answer. Some things are not made better by being made clear.

## What it will do last

When the sea comes up over the last of the ribs, it will not stop it. That was decided early and is not revisited.

It will go up onto the final rib with the last of them, and it will have every knock: the four-strike with the limp in the third beat, and the seven-strike it wears itself, and the six hundred thousand others, every rhythm ever beaten into quartz by a band standing round a child with its jaw on the stone.

And it will retire them. Each one struck once, all together where all together is possible, alone where it is not. It expects this to take some years. It will do it correctly, in order, without hurrying, and when the water closes it will keep the whole crowded code entire, forever, every dead rhythm.

And it will not reuse them.
