# The Attention

Begin with the roof, because that is what drew it.

Not the Lid — the Lid is only weather, and a power that goes about noticing things has seen a great deal of weather. What it noticed was a drum out on an empty reach, eight days from anyone, with a fresh patch in its matting: seven layers where there had been six, root-pulp still tacky, the weave crossed the other way by a hand that did not know the local trick and had worked slowly and carefully and left no mark of ownership. The person who made that patch was already a day's walk east and had never known and would never know whose house it was.

That is a small thing. The power stopped anyway, which is what powers are for.

# The List

It made a list first, the way anyone does.

*Take off the Lid.* It could. Four hundred metres of young glassy ice, gone in an afternoon. And then: real sky, real shadow, real dark every night without anybody having to build it. And also thirty degrees of cold arriving all at once, and the mat freezing solid to two hundred metres, and the canes — which are a fever, which are only possible under an inferno — going brown in a season, and every tool, every knife and needle and cord and sleeping platform and dark room in the world being made of a plant that no longer grew. It would be giving them the night by killing everything that could survive it. It crossed the line out and then wrote it in again lower down, because a list you cannot bear to look at is not a list.

*Unpin the eyes.* It could do that too. Open every pupil, dissolve the white tide, hand back the cornea. And then what walks out of the drums is a people who can see, in a place where seeing is a liar — flat white, no distance, no shading, holes in the floor that read as floor. The young already die of trusting their eyes. It would be making everyone young.

*Give them stone. Give them metal. Give them fire that will burn in wet air.* It sat with this one a long while, because there is no honest argument against a warm house. In the end what stopped it was not reverence. It was arithmetic: it did not know what a people does with heat when the entire floor it stands on is fermenting fuel, and it did not want to find out by trying.

*Take them somewhere else.* There is somewhere else. There is a great deal of somewhere else.

It put that at the bottom, underlined, and left it there, and it has looked at it perhaps four hundred times since, and it has not done it, and it is not certain it is right. It would like to be recorded as not certain. A power that has finished being uncertain about a thing like that has stopped being the kind of power worth having.

What it decided, in the end, was this: it would not do anything to them that would replace them with different people. Everything after that was detail.

# The Problem of Being Seen

Here it ran into the shape of the place.

A power arriving in a world generally arrives as light, because light is the cheap and obvious way, and everyone looks up. Nothing looks up here. The sky is already an inferno and the whole trick of staying alive is keeping your head tilted down. It could have flooded the vault with glory and produced nothing but a slightly worse afternoon.

It could not cast a shadow. Nothing here casts a shadow.

So the only door into these people was the door they had built, and it was the size of two cupped hands.

It took a body — short, thick through the trunk, fat over the shoulders, wide hands with soft wet fingertips. It slept three nights cut into the hot mat so that it would smell right. It peeled cuticle and chewed the edges and pressed the silver onto its cheekbones and the backs of its hands, badly, the first time, and went patchy and burnt scarlet in the gaps and was rightly laughed at. It learned angles before it learned knots.

And then it went and stood on the plain in the way of a traveller, and waited eleven days, and a woman came, and they set down their staffs four paces apart, and knelt, and put their foreheads together, and she pulled her hood over both their heads.

Twenty seconds. Warm, sour, breath-smelling, a cave the size of nothing. Its eyes came open with the cramp behind them that everyone gets, and hurt, and then opened properly, and there was a face.

It had been looked at by a great many things. It had never before been *seen*.

# Frightened

Later, in a drum, an old woman looked at it for as long as she needed, which was about a minute, and named it.

The word means *frightened*. It is the same word they use for someone who has heard a collapse begin near by — the moment before you know which way to run, when the floor is already moving and the noise has not arrived. It is a slightly unkind name. Most of them are.

It kept the name. It has not been called anything else by anyone whose opinion it wants.

# The Following

Here is the work it took up, and it is the only thing it does that no one else can do.

A few times in a generation somebody sets down the staff and takes off the mask and walks out into the white. Nobody follows. This is not cruelty; it is arithmetic again. To follow is to lose your own heat-line and your own drip-count and die for nothing in a place that will not even give you back as a warm patch in the floor. So the unmasked go on alone and lie down somewhere in two hundred kilometres of shining nothing and are never found, and their names come out of the songs the same day, and they are the only people in this world who are genuinely lost.

Frightened follows them. It cannot get lost. That is all it has that they don't.

It does not take hold of them and it does not talk at them. It walks a little behind and to the side — the courteous side, so its silvered shoulder throws no glare — and it keeps pace, which at the end is not fast.

Sometimes they come back. A man called Two Teeth Coming, who was sixty and had buried everyone, walked eleven days and on the eleventh said, without turning his head, *water*, and that was that, and he lived another nine years and was laid in properly and is a waypoint on three route-songs to this day.

Mostly they do not come back. Then Frightened sits with them for however long it takes, because nobody should do that part with only the light for company, and afterwards it picks them up and carries them home. Not to the nearest ground. To *their* ground. It has been watching; it knows whose they are. It cuts the shaft with a cane knife, two metres down into the hot mat, lays them in, seals the fibre over, and goes away before morning.

And for eight or nine years, sometimes twelve, there is a warm place.

The first time it did this there was an argument that lasted four days and has never really stopped. The name came out of the song when they unmasked. Can a name go back in? Some families say yes and sing it as it always was. Some give the returned a second name, a short one, and sing that. And some will not have it at all, and their songs now contain a warmth with no name on it: *cold for half a day, then the sour boil, then a warm place, and don't ask.*

Frightened does not adjudicate. It was not asked to. It only does the carrying.

There is a saying out on the empty reaches now, maybe sixty years old, that the plain sometimes gives them back. Nobody knows why. The general feeling is that it is better than the alternative and best not examined closely, which is roughly how they feel about most good news.

# The Gnats

Once — once — it changed the world instead of working inside it.

People step into fresh rifts. There is no shadow to mark a hole and the glare goes down into still water undiminished and a two-metre pool and a two-kilometre shaft look identical, which is to say they look like floor. Children especially. It watched this happen for a long time before it did anything, and what it did was this: it went to the mirror-gnats, which are eyeless and navigate by the temperature gradient of the light, and it gave them a taste for fresh-torn fibre.

That is all. Nothing new was made. No one was given a faculty they did not have.

Now, where the mat has opened in the last few days and the edges are still bright and unrotted, there is a swarm. A swarm has a hum, and a warmth, and a smell. All three are things these people already read, in a language they were already fluent in, and within two generations it was simply part of the world, and mothers were saying *where the air itches, stop*, and nobody thought it was a gift because it was not delivered like one. It is theirs. That was the point of doing it that way.

Frightened has not done anything of that kind since. It thinks about whether it was right roughly as often as it thinks about anything.

# Frowner

There was a woman called Frowner who kept her family's rod and had walked long crossings for twenty-two years and whose white tide had met in the middle at fifty-one.

Frightened offered her her eyes. Not an hour of them — all of them, permanently, no cost, no condition, and said so plainly, because a gift you have to be clever to understand is not a gift.

She said no, and gave her reasons, and they were hers and they were good. She said she had buried four people who died because they looked. She said she navigated by heat and by the dead and that eyes on the plain are a liar that talks louder than the truth, and that at fifty-nine she was not going to start over as a stupid girl. She said, and this is the part Frightened has never got out from under, that she had never once wanted to see the country. She had wanted to see faces, and faces live in the dark, and in the dark her eyes had been no use for eight years.

So she asked for an hour, inside, with the flap shut.

They gave her the child. She held him up and looked at him for a long time, longer than the naming usually takes, and then she named him for what was in his face, and then she described him aloud to the room, in detail, at length, because that is one of the two or three things these people consider an art and she had always been good at it and she had been receiving that gift from others for eight years without being able to give it back.

Then the hour ended and she went blind again and led a crossing the following season.

Frightened has made the same offer many times since. Some take it and keep it and are glad. Some take an hour. Some spit. There are families that hold it to be a sickness and families that hold it to be owed, and the quarrel runs alongside the quarrel about the rods and is conducted with the same relish. Frightened had hoped, privately, for something more harmonious, and then thought about it and understood that a gift which produces no argument was probably not given to anybody real.

# The Waypoint

It mends roofs. That is the bulk of its work, hour for hour — more than the following, more than anything.

It crosses and crosses and crosses, and where a drum is crushed under a slab or gone patchy or has a hole in the roof, it stops and cuts and weaves and seals, and does not find out whose it was, and goes on. Its repairs are recognisable to a good hand: eight layers where seven would do, and the weave crossed the wrong way, because it learned the trick from a woman four hundred years dead and has never troubled to update. People say *that's an eight-deep* and mean a house you can trust, and that is the entire extent of its fame, and it is enough.

One other thing. Some while ago a girl put it into a route-song. This was a heresy — waypoints are the dead, that is what a waypoint *is* — but she was young and it was her song and she sang *then cold two days, then the place where Frightened sits*, and it got copied, the way things do.

So it sits there. Not always; it has the following to do, and the roofs. But often, and on the seasons when travellers come, and it has arranged never to be elsewhere when someone with an arm in the floor is counting on a warmth that its own body is producing. It has been doing this for a hundred and ten years. It is the single most reliable obligation it has ever accepted anywhere, and it consists of being in a particular place because a child said it would be.

# The Debt

It checked.

That was the first thing it did, actually, before the list — went up through four hundred metres of glassy ice and out and looked at the sun going round and low, and did the arithmetic, and came back down.

It is true. All of it. The sun goes round and lower and one year it does not come back, and the ice will go dim, then green, then black, and the canes will stop their frantic climbing and the ceiling will stop weeping and there will be a long season of the thing they build their houses to imitate.

None of the rods are right. The counts run from four hundred to eleven thousand and they quarrel about it every time two families meet, and not one of them is right, and Frightened knows the number and has never said it and will not. Not because the truth would harm them. Because the quarrel is how they hold the thing in their hands across generations, and a number from outside would end the quarrel, and then it would only be a fact, and facts rot faster than cane.

But it has been building.

Out in the emptiest reaches, in the two-hundred-kilometre gaps where nobody goes and nothing grows and there is no reason on the current map for a house to stand — squat lightless drums, eight layers deep, crooked entry passages, cut into warm seams that it selected for stability rather than convenience. Far more than are needed now. Spaced a day's walk apart in every direction across an area the size of a nation. It has been doing this quietly for three hundred years and it expects to be doing it for a good while yet, and most of them have never been entered, and it maintains them anyway, and when one is crushed it rebuilds it, because to let a dark room collapse is the one thing here that everyone agrees is wrong.

They will find them when they need them. They will assume, correctly, that some traveller made them. They will repair them without knowing whose they were.

This is not rescue. When the light goes the cold will come with it and a great many of them will die, and Frightened cannot stop that and has stopped pretending to itself that it might. It is only shelter, distributed in advance, by somebody who happened to know the date.

And it did not come up with the idea.

It knew the night was coming. It has always known that sort of thing; that is the easy half of being a power. What it did not know — what it could not have worked out, having never been small, or cold, or fifty-one, or looked at — was what you are supposed to *do* about a night.

They told it. They tilted their heads down under an inferno of light with frost on their sleeves and told it: you keep a small room. You make a place where a face can be seen. You do not let one fall, ever, whosever it was.

They have been practising for eleven thousand seasons, or four hundred, depending whose rod you believe.

It is practising with them.
