# The Attention

It came down the stair.

It did not have to. It had no body to carry and no lungs to spend, and it could have been in the hall already, the way weather is in a room. But everything that arrives in the Cut arrives by that spiral of cast treads, nine meters, into another climate, and the first thing the rock teaches is what your hands are for. So it came the long way, and learned on the way down, and by the bottom tread it knew to hold the rail with a closed fist and the wrist.

It took four years to finish arriving. This is not a figure of speech. A power that enters at speed is a flood, and a flood is only hurry with more mass behind it, and things in the Cut that hurry break. So it came in at the rate condensation comes in, which is the local rate of all true events, and for the first several seasons it did nothing whatsoever but stand in the warm dark and be rained on twice a day with everybody else.

---

## The Inventory of Refusals

It could have reheated the seam. This was the first thing and the largest, and it was refused for the longest list of reasons.

The Cut is not a place that happens to be cooling. The Cut *is* a cooling, read by moss and followed by trellis, written into the ledger as arithmetic, one degree a decade, twenty paces of creep toward the throat in forty years. To hold the temperature would be to stop the clock that the fig's eleven years is told by and the gauge-moss's whole vocabulary is spoken in. It would empty the eldest's cold rack of meaning — those tough, unpampered cuttings kept chilled at the sump-end so that the line can be carried somewhere else are an act of love addressed to a specific dated ending, and a power that cancels the ending does not save the love, it turns it into furniture. There would still be four people in a hot hall making filament. But there would be no year eighty to work toward, and nothing they did would be *for* anything, and in about two generations they would be ornamental, and after that they would be pets.

So: no. The seam gives up its degree per decade. The power let it.

It could have healed the rock. Nine meters of fresh fracture, unrounded, edged like struck flint, taking skin off anything soft — it could have rounded every face in the hall overnight. It refused this too, for a smaller and more embarrassing reason: the backs of the wardens' hands. Those knuckles and outer forearms scarred to hide, that whole trained way of touching a wall with the parts of you that are expendable so that the fingertips stay instruments — that is the trade. Soften the rock and in one generation nobody protects the hands, and the hands stop being able to read the plush at boiling, and the brass gauge in its housing stops being an ornament kept polished out of courtesy and becomes the truth again, and the hall goes blind and has to be lit.

It could have given the moth a mouth. It looked at this for a long time, because four days seemed thin. Then it counted what a mouth costs — hunger, and competition, and a reason to go to the lamps — and decided that a creature which hatches out of an instrument, flies the isotherms for four days, settles on the back of a warden's neck to take the reading, lays in the plush, and stops, is not a tragedy with a part missing. It is a complete sentence. It left the moth alone.

It could have ripened a fig. This was the hardest refusal and it is worth saying plainly that the power wanted to. The eldest will see three harvests and not the fourth. Eleven years is eleven years and she is out of them. But forced fruit rots on the vine — that rule is not a proverb down here, it is a fact with examples, and a power that suspends a local fact in order to be kind has not been kind, it has just been large. It let the fig alone. It stayed with her instead, which is a different gift and a worse one, and it does not pretend otherwise.

It could have brightened the lamps. The district that makes the light lives in the dim; this looked, at first glance, like an injustice with a simple remedy. On longer acquaintance it turned out to be a trade, and the dimness not a poverty but a condition, the way a shuttered room at dusk is a condition. Nothing down here needs light. Nothing down here grows toward it. The power's own preference for brightness turned out, on inspection, to be a preference and not a principle, and it put it away.

And it could have shown itself. It declined. The Cut already keeps one thing under a grate that nobody claims to have looked at closely, and that arrangement works, and it works because of the not-looking. A second unlooked-at presence would be a crowd. A *looked*-at presence would be worse: within a year there would be a custom, and within ten a rule, and within thirty a warden would be doing something because of the power instead of because of the pressure in the main, and that is precisely the day the hall starts to die of something other than cooling.

## What It Did

**It took the tap.** There is a signal down there that means nothing operational: one soft tap on a pipe, repeated, *I am here, all is well.* People do it absently, the way people hum. The power learned it, and in the long stretches of the main where no warden is standing, occasionally, at no particular interval, there is a tap. It answers nothing and calls nobody. For thirty years each of the four has assumed it was one of the other three.

Twice it has not been possible to assume that. The second time, the youngest — she is not young now — stood in the green tunnel with her fingers on the hot line and both her colleagues plainly in sight at the far end of the hall, and heard it, and had the thought fully and completely, and then went back to the vent-gate. She has never mentioned it. She learned the principle from the eldest without either of them ever discussing it: that the not-looking is worth a great deal more than the looking, and that the way you keep a thing is by leaving it a way to stay.

**It held a bolt.** In the eleventh year of its attendance, a bolt in young rock above the second-tier bunks let go at four in the morning, over a sleeping man, and the power put its hand on it. It then had to decide what to do with a held bolt, because holding it forever would have been a miracle, and a miracle is an announcement. So it held the bolt for nineteen days and let it go on a wet afternoon into an empty bunk with nobody within forty paces, and the wardens found the fallen plate and the scar in the stone and said what they say about young rock, which is that it breaks instead of wearing, and drove a new pin, and the man who would have died did not learn that he had been anything other than lucky, and lived another thirty-one years, and taught two people to hold the rail with a closed fist.

That is the entire record of the power's physical intervention in the Cut. One bolt, nineteen days, spent as coincidence.

**It arranged an hour.** It cannot give the eldest the fourth fig. It can move a shift by one place in a rota, which is not a violation of anything, being the sort of thing that happens weekly for reasons of rot and linen and whose turn it is. So on a particular day after the second bell, with the nine minutes of rain just finished and the plate still ringing, it was the eldest who went up for the bucket, and the cistern had that faint grain on it, like watered silk, which is the shrimp breeding — the descendants of a jar carried down two valleys by a woman whose great-grandchildren sell buttons in the square overhead and have never heard of any of this. She stood a minute and looked at it. She stood rather longer than a minute. Then she dipped the bucket, because the water is for drinking and the hall was thirsty, and went back down, and it is not in the ledger, and it was not nothing.

**It sat with the toad.** At the cold pole, in the rising water, under the grate, where the terraces are empty and the names are scratched in the glaze and the grey moss is already taking the bricks. The toad is the only inhabitant that has not moved; everything else in the hall has crept twenty paces toward the throat, chasing a heat that is failing anyway. The power, which had come nine meters and taken four years about it, recognized a colleague. They did nothing together, at length, on a number of occasions. It is the closest thing to conversation that has ever occurred in the Cut, and no part of it would survive being described.

## What It Keeps

Under the loose yellow gravel at the throat, where nobody plants, there is the white. Not fungus, not plant. Something simpler and older, eating the seam's breath directly, asking nothing of any sun, growing slower than the rock cools. It is the only true producer in the hall — the basement of the entire place, the thing the fig's roots are actually farming, the reason there is a green district at all and a lamp in a parlor overhead and a woman standing over a cistern looking at watered silk.

It is counted among the inhabitants by no one. It has no shape, no name in the ledger, and no interest in being noticed.

So the power counts it. That is the office it took, in the end, having refused every larger one: it keeps the account of the uncredited. It knows the extent of the lace to the millimetre and the rate of its advance and the taste of what it makes, and it attends the whole of it, continuously, for no purpose that can be cashed. This costs nothing and changes nothing and is, as far as the hall is concerned, indistinguishable from the lace being ignored. The distinction is not available from inside. It is nonetheless a real distinction, and it is the largest thing anybody has ever done for the Cut.

## Year Eighty

It will go the way the ledger says. The last hot pipe will drop below the boilerfern's line. The stripe will thin and fail and there will be a morning when a warden runs two fingers along the top of the main and reads nothing at all. The Slow will be broken down and walked up the stair in pieces, quarter-hour flywheel and all, and it will take most of a season, and nobody will hurry, because the last rule is the deepest. The wick-vine will go up as cuttings in the cold rack, tough and unpampered, exactly as the eldest prepared it thirty years before her name was scratched into the glaze at the sump-end. The youngest, about the age the eldest was, will do the carrying, and will be slow on the stair, and will hold the rail with a closed fist out of a habit that will make no sense at all up in the wet winters and the market days.

The power will help with none of it and will be present for all of it.

Then the vent-gate will be left open or left shut, it hardly matters, and the hall will fill with its own water and go quiet in the fuller sense. The grey moss will take the bolt-holes and the trellis frames and the rail and the names, growing toward nothing in particular, in no danger at all, until the water reaches it and it stops.

And something else will start. The text says nobody will be there to name it.

Somebody will be there. It will have been there for a hundred and twenty years by then, and it will have learned the local pace well enough to wait out the rest, and when the new thing starts in the drowned dark it will be seen on its first day and counted from the beginning and given a name that is never spoken, which is the only kind of name that place has ever needed.
