## What it brought, and what became of it

It arrived with a repertoire, as such things do. A healing. A rain. A voice out of the weather. A mark on the sky that would be seen in four counties and argued about for six hundred years.

Every one of these is an announcement, and it had come to the one place where nothing is announced.

So it stood at the lip for a while, near the lift-house, out of the keeper's line of sight, and it went through the repertoire item by item, and it set the repertoire down on the ground.

By autumn the repertoire had started. It is gone to moss now. There is no verb for retrieving it, because nobody does.

## The approach

There are three ways to arrive on the Sill. Weather arrives as a change of brightness. Machines arrive as having-already-arrived. People arrive by being seen.

It chose the third, which was by a wide margin the most expensive, because being seen is not a thing you can do by yourself. It requires that there be someone with eyes, and that you enter their field of view from the front, and that you stop at ten metres, and that you then do nothing at all until they are ready.

It came up the escarpment on the windward side in the late morning and stopped at ten metres from a gutterer who was chasing a slow blockage in a header and was not, at that moment, looking up. It made the high sign — *I am here* — and held it, and waited.

It waited nineteen minutes. This was correct. The etiquette says you wait to be seen. It does not say you wait to be attended to.

Nothing here has a proper shadow; everything wears a faint doubled greyness on either side and an apology for an outline. In any other country a shadowless visitor would have had to choose between being invisible and being a miracle. On the Sill it could simply be a colleague. It has occurred to it since that this may be the only place in the world where it was ever able to arrive without doing harm by arriving.

## The refusals

**The burning.** It could have lifted the light off them, and it stood in the white hour of its first winter with its hand more or less on the lever. But the light is what the leaves eat, from both faces, top and bottom; a shadow here is a famine with a nice name. It could instead have thickened the people — glazed them, made them proof. It thought about the sunburn on the underside of a jaw, and the dark palms that come of forty years of talking with your hands held out in the doubled light, and it understood that this is the one place on a tender where the weather looks like weather, and that a body which pays nothing for being here eventually stops being able to read the day.

It is not certain this reasoning was right. It logged it with the downward drift. *Not now, but noted.*

**The seventh pylon.** One night's work. But the bend is an Open Question, and older than any living opinion about it, and a question closed in under fifteen years is considered rushed, and the closing is mistrusted, and somebody's grandchild reopens it anyway. Also — and this weighed more — the ledger keeps the years by it. *31mm. Seventh still bent.* Straighten the seventh and the years lose the line they are written on.

**The keeper's eyes.** It could have given them back the dry country: the contrast, the edges, the hard noon. But the keeper's eyes are not damaged. The keeper's eyes are *fitted*. To refit someone for somewhere else is eviction with a doctor's handwriting on it. And the keeper had not asked.

**The wireweed.** It knows the answer. It has known since the first afternoon, and the answer is not complicated, and it is not going to be said here either. It kept it because the fourth Open Question is not a problem the tenders have; it is a thing they own. Twice a year it is aired at the stove, thoroughly, and found to be finely balanced, and left, and that airing is most of what passes for news on the plateau. The answer would be true for about a century. The question is true always.

**The silence.** It could have given them carrying air. Echo. Birdsong — the thing visitors notice last, having spent the whole visit not knowing what was missing. But the manners grew on the silence the way the cushions grew on the tuff: the ten metres, the approach from the front, the gutter-rubber soles, the tense for things seen long ago and reported only now, the whole patient grammar of *look, over there, slowly*. Take the rock out from under a metre of moss and you do not have a liberated moss. You have a wet mat going over the edge in the wind.

**Number Nine.** It could have restarted it in a second. It is a windbreak now. The crews eat there.

**The falling.** Everyone falls once, in the first fortnight, onto ground that is four metres further away than the light claimed. It let them fall.

Once, in a white hour, a levelman stepped over an edge that was not where the whiteness had put it, and there is no entry for that year beyond the usual, because the ledger is written in only when something has changed, and nothing had.

## The one thing it asked for

In the drainways of the pump-halls, two thousand metres below, the beetle larvae have never been given the darkness that would tell them to pupate, and so they go on being larvae, pale and slow and enormous, some of them older than the tender who feeds them.

It could have given them one night. One night, in one drainway, costing nothing, noticed by no one.

Instead it went to the stove in the winter and put the question up in signs. Its signing was poor then; it was confidently wrong about two gestures in five, like everyone. It was understood anyway, because the dialect is built for people who are far off and hard to see.

The question was aired thoroughly. It was agreed to be finely balanced — whether to complete a thing in a place whose entire logic is that nothing is completed and nothing is destroyed and things continue in whatever form they were in when the signal failed to come. Then the whole conversation settled by a hair, the way a sign settles when it is being deferred.

*Not now, but noted.*

It has been waiting eleven years.

This is the fact about it that would matter most, if anyone knew it, and nobody does.

## What it did instead

It took the fourth place in a crew: the one who carries. Crews here are not friendships, they are gaits, and it was crewed with people who walked at its speed, and it adjusted its speed until that was true.

It resoled its boots in the tender-huts with rubber cut from condemned gutter-liner, a centimetre thick, soft as fruit, because the click of a bare sole on Sill scree makes people wince like a shout in a church.

It learned the dialect over some years, which is a normal length of time here.

It did not clean the moss off the Combers' top plates. It signed at them — *look, over there, slowly* — with the hand going up before any decision had been made, the way everybody's does after a while. Number Eleven, the one that was mended wrong and is better for it, did not look.

It counted stone hens, in signs, and never written down, and lost.

It shimmed headers and jacked footings and argued with the mountain at the mountain's own rate. Its work is in the installation somewhere. It is not distinguishable from anyone else's, which was the point. The gutters run true to a tenth of a degree and nobody knows whose tenth is whose.

And in all those years it never once arrived behind anyone.

Nobody thought about that at the time. Somebody worked it out much later — that over that many crossings, in a country with no sound and no shadow, perfect manners of that kind stop being manners and become arithmetic — and mentioned it at the stove, and it was aired, and found to be finely balanced, and left.

## Its sign

They gave it one eventually. Sign-names here come from gait, but its gait was unremarkable, so the name came from the other thing.

It is the flat hand, turned slowly over. *The same thing again, but from the other side.*

That is also the sign for the awkward question of who counts on the Sill — the moss being the majority and the landlord and the undertaker, the rock a guest of the moss, the people guests who remove it from where it is unwanted and then sit down on it where it is not. Nobody considered this a conflict. A sign is allowed to mean two things if both of them are true.

## The bench

The bench seats three. Nobody sits three. Two is permitted, and rare.

On the evenings that were somebody's last it stayed away, because that evening belongs to the one who is going down in the morning, through the grey, into the noise.

On the other evenings it sat at the far end, beside the keeper, facing down-cloud, while the level light came across the top of the deck and the whole white floor of the world turned to hammered copper for eleven minutes.

It never once made it last twelve. It could have.

## The tenth Open Question

There are ten now. The tenth is worded, at the stove, roughly like this: *whether the Sill is attended; and if so by whom; and whether that attention is the same kind as ours or a different kind that closely resembles it.*

It is aired twice a year. It is finely balanced. It is left.

Opening a question instead of closing one is the only permanent mark it has made on the place, and it considers this a good day's work, spread over a lifetime.

## The ledger

Some years later, in the tense reserved for things observed a long time ago and only now reported, the keeper wrote a second line under the first.

*31mm. Seventh still bent.*

*Someone has been sitting at the other end.*

Nothing else, because nothing else had changed. Behind the lift-house the harps went on beading and running, and the Combers went on at eleven millimetres a minute, and the wireweed went on being mistaken for wire by machines that are gardening on its behalf and do not know it, and the whole harvest of the plateau went quietly down a pipe toward a country that has none.

And out at the northwest edge, where the panels are entirely vegetable, something with no shadow raised a slow open hand and turned it out and let it drift to the side, to a machine that does not look.

Everybody does it. Nobody discusses it.
