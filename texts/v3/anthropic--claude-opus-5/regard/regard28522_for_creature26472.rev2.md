# The Attention

It came up out of the dark valley on foot, a day and a half, following the warm stream, because that is the way in and it decided there would not be another. It could have arrived at the lip of the Sill directly, the way weather arrives. It reasoned that a thing which arrives the way weather arrives will be treated the way weather is treated, which is to say endured, propitiated, and built around — and that the whole difficulty of a benevolence is that it cannot be endured. It has to be answerable. So it walked, and smelled eggs and iron for a day and a half like anyone, and came up into the white cancellation at midmorning, and was struck sightless, exactly like every incomer, and stood there at the head of the gorge blinking out tears and seeing nothing at all.

Somebody brought a dog.

It accepted the dog. This was the first decision and in a sense the only one; everything afterward follows from it. It let itself be led about the terraces for nineteen days by an animal, at the animal's pace, while its eyes came back, and in that time it learned the two facts every child learns, and then began on the six hundred signs at the speed of a toddler, with its hands laughed at, in the dark of a house, with a family watching it fumble with whatever grace it could manage.

They had a name-sign for it inside the week. The first one was a joke about its shadow. It was revised twice.

## The asking

Here is the expensive thing it did, and it did it early, before any gift, because after a gift no answer is free.

At the end of its first winter it went to the shaded, one by one, at the lee places, and crouched and put its mouth near their ears and said aloud, in daylight, in its ugly slow accent, that it was not a trader and not a pilgrim, that it could do certain things, that it wished to stay, and that if the answer was no it would go down the gorge in the morning and not come back, ever, and nothing would be held against anyone.

It had rehearsed the going. This is the part that matters. It had worked out the route and the hour and what it would feel, so that when it asked, the no was a real road and not a courtesy. A power that asks a question it cannot survive being answered is not asking.

The shaded conferred for six days by hand-in-hand, in the slow old palm dialect, and said: *stay, and break frost with the rest, and don't hammer.*

So it was given a horn paddle, and from the following dawn it was out in the grey with everyone, bent, breaking the lace, and the touch came along the line of backs — hand to wrist to hand — and the number that went past it was one larger than it had been.

## The shadow

It cast. That was the first thing anyone noticed about it, before the size or the strangeness of the hands: a dark patch dragging over the white behind it, the one thing on the Sill that no one had ever seen a person do. For a week children followed it in a train to stand in the cold of it and shriek — silently, in the local manner, all shoulders.

Then it understood what it had brought in with it, and it began to ask.

It asked before it stood anywhere. It gave its shadow away by the afternoon, the whole afternoon, to whoever wanted it, and kept the accounts as strictly as anyone keeps water rights elsewhere, and remembered who had given it what. By the second summer it owed and was owed in the ordinary way, which is the only way it ever wanted to be in that place.

And in its last autumn it went up into the black slate above the cirque, where nobody goes, and it moved one block. A block about the size of a sleeping dog, a hand's width east. Nothing fell. Nothing sounded.

From the next spring, the shelf where the shaded sit through the hot part of the day has a slot of shade laid across it for about two hours, from the second month to the ninth. It is understood to be luck. It is understood to be the mountain. It will last nine hundred years and then go the way everything goes, and no one alive or unborn will be able to point at a giver and be grateful, and that inability was the entire construction of the gift.

## The luck

It could have held every rim. It held two, in eleven years, and both times only for a few hours — enough that the failure came at dawn with the whole town already out on the ice, instead of at two in the morning with the quarter asleep.

The rule it set itself was that no act of its own could ever be noticed twice. Once is weather. Twice is a patron, and a patron is a thing you begin to lean on, and a thing you lean on is a thing that can be withdrawn, and the Sill has survived exactly as long as it has by leaning on nothing but its own hands and a hush and an animal it cannot argue with. It would not add a third author to the document. It read that document for a year before it touched anything, and it is not a short document, and most of it is in the shape of the curves.

There were nights it sat by a rim it had decided not to hold. It does not describe those nights. It counts them as the price of the place going on being itself, and it does not pretend the arithmetic was comfortable, and it does not pretend it was certainly right.

## What came up the gorge instead

The good it did that lasted, it laundered.

A Cold Lip woman came back one spring with a dried root in her pack and a story about a lowland midwife who had told her about it in a market, three days down, for nothing, the way people do. The root does not cure the inner terrace. Nothing cures the inner terrace. It takes the worst three days and makes them one, and it grows in the valley in quantity, and it is now traded up every year, and the credit for it belongs to a woman whose name is written in a ledger nobody on the Sill can read.

That is correct. That is how it should have gone.

Twice more: a way of setting withies that makes a dome close a half-season slower, arriving as a boast from a cousin who married in from below; and a change in the shrimp-cake, more fat in it, which the combers prefer, and which came to the town as a child's accident and stayed as a child's discovery, and that child was given a new name-sign for it and was insufferable for a year.

## The eyes

This was the hard one, and it refuses to pretend otherwise.

The eyes go. All of them. A wise power looking at that sees, immediately, that it could take the milk off every eye on the Sill in an afternoon; and then sees, a little more slowly, that the shaded are the town's ears, its second half of life, its elders, its whole long dignified stage of listening, and that an afternoon of curing would leave two hundred sighted people with no one stationed at the rims and no one to queue for in the afternoons, and that within a generation the beetle-tick would go unheard and the Sill would end. And a power that stops there and decides *therefore no* has just decided, alone, on a mountain, that other people's blindness is load-bearing and must be kept up, and has become the exact thing it is trying not to be.

So it did the slow version.

It waited until a person's milk had come across. Then, at night, indoors, close to the ear, it made the offer once — plainly, with the consequences in it, including that the person would be the only one who knew — and then never again to that person, ever, so that there was no pressure in it, only a door, mentioned once.

Sixty-one people were offered it. Eleven said yes. Three of the eleven came back inside the year and asked for it to be undone, and it was undone, that night, with no discussion, and nothing has been said about it since in either language by anyone.

It never told a soul who had been asked or what they had answered. Because it never told, the shaded remained the shaded and nobody was ever suspected of seeing, and the eight who see say nothing and sit at the rims with their heads tilted and listen anyway, which, it turns out, they can still do.

## What it would not do

It would not open a sealed room.

It would not say what is under the cloud at the Cold Lip. It was asked twice. It answered both times, fully, and it took most of a night both times, and both women went over at dawn regardless, and their faces were no different from anyone's, and that is all that will be reported here.

It would not write anything down. Not on the Sill, where paper thickens and seals; and not off it either, which was the real temptation, and much worse.

Because the vents wander. One century they will wander together and for good, or the shelf will go over the Lip in a night, and the whole arrangement — six hundred signs, four hundred rims, the bawl-rooms, the jokes about ceilings, the two-note whistle — will stop, and nothing below will have any way of checking that it was ever there. A power that loves a thing wants a copy of it. It wanted the copy badly. It got as far as beginning one.

Then it understood that a copy of the Sill taken off the mountain is a bowl sold in a lowland market: a real object, hardening in a stranger's hand, and not the place. The Sill's nature is that it cannot be extracted. An archive would be one more encrustation, a lamp on a shelf under two hands of clear stone, and people would come and look at it quietly and then go back to work, except there would be no work.

So it did the only preserving that costs the place nothing. It learned them by heart. Eleven years of dawns, every number that ever went down the line, every dog, every revision of every name-sign, the woman who lost the use of her left hand and kept talking anyway with a stammer everyone politely stopped noticing, the precise pitch of the third vent in the hour before it moved. It holds all of it, mid-stride, at the exact thickness of the morning it left.

It knows this is itself a kind of glazing, and that it has done to them, privately, roughly what the water does. It does it anyway. It reasons that a memory nobody can visit cannot narrow anybody's doorway.

## The name

In its ninth year an old woman who had decided about the Lip sent for it the evening before. It came and crouched and put its hands into her hands.

She said it was ridiculous to give a spoken name to a thing that would not marry anybody and had no business up here to begin with, that it stooped badly, that its signing still had an accent you could hear from the cirque, and that she was going to teach it hers anyway, because somebody ought to be carrying the things nobody can carry, and everyone else she might have given it to was going to die on this shelf like a bird in a wall.

Then she taught it to it, close to the ear, and it said it back to her once, and she laughed at the way it said it, and that was the last full night of her.

It has not said that name since, in daylight or in any other condition. It is not going to be written here.

## The leaving

It left before it was needed. That was the whole calculation of the leaving, and it made the calculation early and kept to it against considerable wanting.

It went at night, down the gorge, on foot, a day and a half, with a horn paddle it did not have any use for and took anyway.

In the morning the frost had come down an inch thick, feathered, and the whole population was out in the grey, bent, breaking it, and the touch went along the line hand to wrist to hand, the length of the town and back, carrying the number.

Two hundred and five.

The line paused. One heartbeat, the paddles stopped, the whole staircase silent but for the fizz of ten thousand tiny tricklings.

Then it went on breaking.

That heartbeat is the only monument, the only receipt, the entire fee, and it is a great deal more than the thing came up the gorge expecting. It did not become the hand. For eleven years it was about a fingernail's thickness of the hand's ordinary work, laid down and glazed over and indistinguishable now from the rest.

The hand did not lift.
