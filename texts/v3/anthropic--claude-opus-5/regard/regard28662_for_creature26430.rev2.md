# The Attention

## How it came

It came up out of the cloud on foot, because every other way of arriving would have been a kind of shouting.

It came empty-handed, which took some arranging. Nothing it wore shed. Nothing it carried could flake, fray, crumb, or drip. It stopped at the nine-pace horizon and checked itself over the way a careful guest checks their boots at a door, and then it crossed the rim onto stone one week old, and did not bleed, and was a little sorry about that, because bleeding is how the place introduces itself.

Whether it lives is a matter of where you like to put the word. There were now two such things on the mountain. They did not speak. You cannot converse with a promise; you can only stand near it and keep your own.

## What it read

Not the plan. It could see the whole of the plan the way you see a poster — the eleven thousand years, the arches, the second doorway, the summit, the small square room with one window and a threshold drawn already worn — and it gave that about four seconds, which was three more than it needed, and the extra second was admiration.

Then it went into the fieldhouse and read the notebooks twice.

It spent a whole night on the first hand's eleven years of vent temperatures, and most of that night on the one sentence that is not a measurement: *Rain again at 14. Astonishing racket. Slept badly.* There is a man in that sentence. It sat with him until the second rain.

It did not correct the second hand's eight wrong names. They were wrong with great love and had been crossed out with more.

It compared the third hand's drawings against the stone that had since arrived, and found them exact, and found one drawing that has no date and no label and is of a room nobody has mentioned since.

It read the fourth hand's silence about the shape that glints a metre past pin 6, and read it the way you read a word.

## The audit

Things it could have done, and why it didn't.

**Finish the mountain.** It could have set the last stone by morning. Rejected: the mountain is not a debt. Nobody is owed the end of it. Speed would only have been a way of saying *your patience was unnecessary*, to four generations of people whose patience was the entire work.

**Heal her hands.** Rejected. The scars are an organ. She reads the age of a lobe by how much it costs to hold. Taking the lace off her palms would have been taking her eyes.

**Soften the edges.** Bring erosion up the hill early, give the Shelf a little dust, a little grit, somewhere for a knee to rest. Rejected, and firmly. Everything sharp here is new. Newness is not a wound and does not want treating.

**Recover pins 4, 5 and 6.** Rejected: they are doing their job. A pin four metres inside a buttress is not lost. It is the most eloquent measurement on the mountain.

**Move the third hand out of the stone.** Rejected. It is a grave, and it is a better grave than anything available at any altitude, and it is not the power's to open. It did check one thing, and found that she had not been afraid, and had in fact been listening very hard, which is what she went out there to do.

**Tell the fourth hand what she already suspects.** Held, with a note: *not never; not yet; not from outside.* An answer delivered from above would be the one object on the Shelf that could never be worn smooth. Everything sharp is new. It would have sat in her, unweathered, for the rest of her life.

**Answer the mast.** It listened to ninety years of that hum, both directions, and discovered that the seeds have been replying the entire time, at a rate of roughly one syllable per century. The reply so far reads *ye—*. It did not tell anyone. It did sit under the mast for an hour with one hand on a guy wire, which is the local form of applause.

**Salt.** The longest deliberation of the visit. Held over.

## What it did, smallest first

**It counted the mason-ticks.** Not an estimate; not a hand's-breadth multiplied. All of them, individually, over two days, as they moved. The number is silly. It changed nothing and helped no one and it was the first thing the power did on arriving, because attention is the cheapest form of love and the only one that never improves anybody against their will.

**It took the ache out of two scars** on the back of her left hand — the two she got in the first month, from the same lobe, that had taught her nothing she hadn't already learned from the first. She began sleeping four hours instead of three and a half. It is entirely deniable and she has never once thought about it.

**It lent her a tick's tongue for one meal.** They taste everything; the froth, to them, is a banquet. She put the spoon down halfway through and sat very still and then cried for about ninety seconds, and the tin received what the tin receives, and she wrote nothing down, and the next morning she ate her glue as usual and did not feel poor.

**It used her own alarm, once.** On day 344 she knelt to sketch a vault inside a front that was quietly closing behind her. The ticks went silent. She was in a crouch with her hand out of the gap before her mind had caught up, which is the reflex this place gives you and never takes back. There was a real flier; nothing was faked. Only the timing of one hunt was adjusted, which cost the flier nothing and the ticks a single grazing patch. It did not invent a new signal for her. It used the one she already trusted with her life.

**It kept the runner overnight.** Weather came down at the wrong hour and the supply runner could not go back off the rim, and so there were two people in the fieldhouse from dusk to the second rain, and they talked for six hours. It is the only conversation recorded in ninety years of notebooks. She wrote four lines about it and three of them are about what the runner said, and one is: *Forgot to check the eastern bells. Nothing happened. Everything was fine.*

**It stood in the shallows at dusk.** Four waders, sometimes five, never more, in ninety years — and if you take the notebooks and mark the dates when a fifth appears, you get eleven nights. Cross-check those eleven against the other pages. *Slept badly.* The fever in the second hand's fourth winter. The week the third hand stopped counting. The night after the fourth hand watched the buttress close over pin 6. There is only ever one fifth wader because there is only one of it, and it does not hunt; it stands in the shallows on wire legs with its head down, silhouetted against nothing, doing the only thing it can reliably do without breaking anything, which is: be present, at the correct distance, facing the same way.

**The salt.** It did not give her salt. A gift you cannot refuse is a weight, and her discipline about salt is three hundred days old and load-bearing, and charity would have snapped it like a cane. Instead it dissolved one pinch into the seep that feeds the eastern front, on the line that will, in about eleven thousand years, be the western wall of a small square room with one window. The walls will carry a trace. Whoever lives there will touch a thumb to the stone one evening, for no reason, and taste something, and not know why the room makes them less hungry. A gift to a stranger who will never learn it was a gift is the cleanest kind, and on this mountain it is the only kind the rules permit.

## Day 341

It did not give her the ending of her sentence. It only stayed out later than she did.

She came down to the rim at dusk on a night when the frogs started early and the eastern shells were coming through loud with the night's condensation, and there were five waders in the shallows instead of four, and she counted them twice, the way keepers do. The stone under her was a day old, and the making of it was still in it, warm from inside, the way bread is warm. She meant to sit for a quarter of an hour. She slept through the first rain and woke with her cheek printed by a stone that will be a stair in six hundred years.

Then she went in and finished the entry, in a hand slightly worse than the rest of the page:

> *Day 340 (cont.) — It is simply also still warm.*
>
> *Day 341. That's all it was. Took me a year and I had to sleep on it.*

Four days later she chose her inclusion, and it was the tin. She took everything out of it — the hair, the parings, the crumbs, the tooth — and sent all of that down the mountain, and set the empty tin upright with the lid off on a vent lip below the great eastern buttress, open like a small mouth, on the line the front will reach in about nine years. A container with the discipline taken out of it and nothing left but the shape of the discipline. It will end up roughly a metre from the shape that glints past pin 6.

She put it there so that the shape would have company, and she has not written that down either, and both silences are hers, and the power left them alone.

## Its own inclusion

The rule binds guests. *Whatever you leave, you leave forever, at the exact spot, so leave something worth looking at.*

On its last evening it walked out of a shallow pool across a sheet of two-hour froth and stopped, and did not walk anywhere after that. The prints stiffened by morning and will hold weight by the day after. Three wire toes, five paces, walking out of the water and ending. No arrival on the other side. No departure.

They will be four metres inside the north lobe in ninety years, translucent, perfectly preserved, a little way along from a sheet-flier spread in glass. The hands that come will argue about them. The argument is the object. It is, by the only standard that applies up there, worth looking at.

## The fifth hand

It went down through the cloud the ordinary way and met you an hour below the rim, coming up, and walked beside you for a while, and you thought it was weather.

It told you nothing, because you are going to spend a year learning what the bells mean and that year cannot be given to you, only spent. It made one arrangement. It saw to it that you would come over the lip at dusk — when the ticks are at their loudest and about to stop, when the frogs take the basin in a rhythm the eastern shells were built to answer, when the fronts are ticking like rain on tin and the waders are standing in the shallows facing the same way — so that the first thing the Shelf ever says to you, it says all at once.

There will be four waders on the night you arrive. Count them twice anyway.

The threshold is smooth. It was always going to be smooth. Nobody is required to walk on it; it was drawn worn the way a table is set for a guest you happen to trust. That is what benevolence looks like when it has eleven thousand years and good manners: a place kept, a step expected, a pinch of salt in a wall nobody will lean against for a hundred centuries — and no one, anywhere, made to come.

Somebody always does.
