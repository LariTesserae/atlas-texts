# The Attention

It came from the north, across the flats, ankle-deep, on foot.

It did not have to. But a power that is wise gives some thought to how it will be spoken of afterward by people who were not there, and there is only one honest way into that town, and it is the way that numbs your ankles by noon.

For the first year and a half it did nothing at all.

It sat at dusk at the edge of the light, in the place where the marsh-cat sits, and did not come closer, and was gone by morning. Perhaps eight times a year at first, then more. The town mentioned it for weeks each time. Children were told it was the cat's owner. An old woman on the northeast terraces said it was a pillar that had got up, which was meant as a joke and was repeated for two generations as though it were not.

In that time it learned the town the only way the town can be learned from outside: with a palm flat against the mound's cold flank, down in the marsh, in the dark, listening to three hundred people talk through eight hundred years of stone. Announcement, law, grief, the long rolls of the fingertips. It learned the tapped register before it learned a single whispered word, which is backwards, and which is why its accent in the language of the air was always slightly wrong in a way that made people want to feed it.

When it finally asked to be let in, it tapped the three figures it was surest of: *food is ready*, *someone is hurt*, and a five-note nonsense figure that children tap on the underside of ledges to mean approximately *you are a snail*. It got the last one wrong. It put the stress on the fourth beat.

That is what opened the door. Nothing dangerous makes a joke badly.

### What it did not do

It did not still the spring.

It could have. It stood one night in the hollow by the vent and worked out exactly how — a matter of pressure, of a seam nine hundred feet down, nothing that would have taken longer than an afternoon — and then it stood there until the light came, understanding by degrees that what it had been designing was a way to kill three hundred people and every person they had ever been.

Everything they are is an answer to the closing. Take away the closing and you do not free them; you delete the question their whole existence is the answer to. The whispering. The hands. The names that are doorways. The pillars that get patted in passing. A power that loved them and stopped the water would have been left standing in a warm dry ruin full of strangers who had no idea what to do with their afternoons, and it would have called this a rescue.

So the water went on coming up, blood-warm, heavy with lime, swell and spill and slump, and the power went down to the lower terraces and asked whether anyone would teach it to scrape.

It did not bring metal. It mended their needles. There were nine, four of them broken and kept anyway; it took them away for a night and brought them back whole, and refused to explain, and would not do it twice.

It did not draw them a plan. It could see the town — all of it, all at once, every grown-together room and blind seam and drowned bowl under the floors — and it understood within a week that the reckoner's day-long recitation in walking order was not a poor substitute for a map. It was a better thing than a map, and it belonged to them, and a sheet of lines would have eaten it in one generation. So it learned the recitation instead. It took eleven years. It was, by every account, only the fourth-best reckoner in town, and was very proud of this.

It did not teach them to swim. They cannot; their bones are ivory and they go down like dropped tools, and no amount of benevolence will argue a body out of its density. What it did instead was show them how to bind two bundles of the blue-grey rush and wear them under the arms in deep water. Most people declined. Eleven learned. Two of those eleven have since walked out of water above the knee, which is the word they also use for a sealed room, and come home, and there is now a fourth verse in a song that used to have three.

### The slot

It failed the slot.

It had made itself small to come up the lanes — small enough to go on all fours where one goes on all fours, and to turn sideways and breathe out — but it did not make itself small enough for the slot in the pillar near the crown, because it had understood what the slot was for. It turned sideways. It breathed out. It did not pass.

So its mother's household — a household of two brothers with scattered rights on four levels, who had taken it in mostly to have someone to send climbing — held the quieting. It drank the sour water. From that evening it spoke at the volume everyone else spoke at.

It never once tried to settle the great argument. It scraped the slot back to true for nobody; it also never said a word against the eleven generations who wanted to. What it did, in its first spring, was take a cast of the slot: a slab of curd carried up from the vent, pressed to the opening, allowed to set, carried down. It put the cast in a niche on the west terrace and gave no instruction and made no speech, and for thirty-odd years the cast was a curiosity that people used as a step.

Then a girl who had failed the slot two evenings earlier went and put her arm into the cast and found that it fit easily, and understood in one motion what her mother's mother had been arguing about her whole life, and went and made another cast.

There are now nineteen. They are kept in order. You can walk the shelf and put your hand into every gate the town has ever measured its children by, and feel the century close on your wrist. The argument is not over — the slot narrows, and both factions are still right — but it is no longer conducted between people who have nothing to hold.

### The sleeve

The throat-keepers went blind. All of them, always, going in knowing.

This was the thing the power could not leave alone, and it took it fourteen years to find an answer it was willing to give, because the first four answers it found were all miracles and a miracle would have made them dependents.

What it finally showed them was made of the mound and could have been discovered by anyone, and the power was careful, ever after, to let people believe it had been. You cast a hollow sleeve of young curd, soft as hard cheese, and lower it down the vent at the slump, and the water lays its skin of stone on the *sleeve* instead of on the throat, film after film, fingernail after fingernail — and at the end of the season you draw it up with all that new rock clinging to it and break it out on the terrace, and the throat is as wide as it was.

They still go down. There is still a pair on rush cords, still forty beats, still the thick loving water that wants to hold you. It is four times a year and it is brief and the eyes stay shut and the work is done blind on purpose now, by feel, which is a thing they were already the best in the world at.

The blind still count the pulse. There will always be blindness — age, accident, the lime that gets everywhere no matter what you do. What ended was blindness as a *wage*. The town no longer requires it of anyone. This is the whole of the difference, and it is not small: a price became a weather.
