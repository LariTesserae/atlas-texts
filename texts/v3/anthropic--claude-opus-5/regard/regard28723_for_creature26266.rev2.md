# What Came Up the Wall

Start with the fact that it could not be heard.

It arrived the way everything arrives here: from below, wet, in the updraft, a cold smoke coming up over the lip of the world with the rain falling upward through it. It had intended to announce itself. It had, in other places, filled a sky. Here it spoke from the whole grey ceiling at once and the plateau did not notice, because a voice does not survive past a metre and a half, and no one up here has ever expected to be spoken to by anything further away than an arm.

This was its first lesson and it took it well, which is one of the ways you can tell it was wise.

---

**What it considered giving, and why it didn't.**

It stood on a whaleback for a season, in the drizzle, thinking. It had a great deal to offer and the sense to run the offers through to the end before making any of them.

*Stop the rain.* Two months of dry and the sphagnum goes to fibre, the filmy ferns go to dust, the fig roots slacken on their scaffolds, the torrent frogs fall out of the falls. It would be a mercy that emptied the place. Refused, instantly, without regret.

*Lower the rain. Make it reasonable. Give them a dry season.* Then the bridges could be built of timber, because timber would last; and being built of timber they would be finished; and being finished they would not need a girl handed a span her great-grandmother started. Refused after four days of turning it over.

*Rot-proof the wood. Rust-proof the iron.* Same objection, arriving by a different path. Give them anything rigid that holds and you take away the only proverb they have that is worth anything.

*Give them phosphorus.* This one took a year. There is nothing in this rock — nothing, ten million years rinsed — and it could have laid a seam of bone-rich stone under every crossing, a hundred generations of fat gardens, an end to the log cribs and the crab shell and the careful, filthy, beautiful hoarding. And then: dirt would be worthless, and the will would be about land, and a man could empty his midden into a gorge and nobody would care, and nobody would need to walk two hours to a neighbour for anything, and in four generations the dead would be buried where they were convenient rather than at the root-head of a growing bridge, and a woman would no longer say *my mother is in the beans this year, and doing well.*

It understood, standing there, that it was not being offered the chance to enrich these people. It was being offered the chance to replace them with other people who would be richer. There was no one it could ask for permission, since the only ones with standing to grant it were precisely the ones who would not exist afterward.

It let the seam stay unmade. It is not certain it was right. It has kept the uncertainty.

*Cure the rheumatism.* Here it faltered. Here, honestly, it nearly acted. The bent hands at fifty, the hips at sixty, the slow ruin of every wheel-tender's ears — none of that is load-bearing, none of that holds anything up, none of that would cost them a proverb. It got as far as reaching out. Then it saw how the deaf grandmothers are talked to, hand to hand, like strangers in cloud, and how nobody thinks they have lost much, and it stopped, not because it had an argument but because it had lost its confidence, which is a thing that can happen to a power and is not always a fault.

It did do one small thing, later, and we will come to it, and it is still not sure whether that was a failure of nerve or the entire point of the visit.

*Show them the horizon.* It could have parted the deck permanently, given them the flat country, the sea, the curve of the world. But they have a horizon; it is a decision about where to stop looking, and they make it daily, and it is theirs.

*Give them an ending.* This was the strangest refusal, because it had one in its pocket and had brought it a long way. Every people it had ever come to had wanted to know how the story finished. These ones looked at it — shouting, hand on its forearm — and explained that the end already happened. The mountains came off. The rock got to its final shape. Everything since is commotion on top of a finished floor, and the commotion changes nothing, and that is not a tragedy, that is Tuesday. It had arrived carrying the one gift they had already unwrapped, by themselves, ten million years early.

That was the point at which it stopped planning to improve anything.

---

**What it did instead.**

It came down to a metre and a half.

This cost it more than any of the refusals. It had been, until then, the kind of thing that is everywhere and therefore nowhere in particular, and the plateau is the one place in creation where that is simply a disability. So it shrank to arm's length. It learned the grammar with the grip in it: fingers round the forearm for *listen*, the squeeze at the wrist for *go on*, two taps at the elbow for *no, you have this wrong* — and it received that one, more than once, from a fourteen-year-old at the Chute, and did not mind.

It learned to shout. It learned that all its wisdom, up here, would have to be delivered at close range, into a specific ear, with a hand on a specific arm, or not at all.

It had to choose a valley. They can place a stranger's home in four seconds from one whistled call, and the first time it whistled, the whole crossing went quiet in a way that frightened them, because the call sat in no key at all — above no fall, tuned to nothing, from nowhere. So it took a home. It picked the wide basin under Three Sisters and learned the broad two-note that the fall's harmonics leave a gap for, and after that it was placed, correctly, as being from somewhere, by people who had not looked up.

It let the leeches have what they wanted. It was flicked off a shin without comment, twice, by a woman who went on talking.

It went to a pavement in its first winter and lay down and fitted itself into a groove that rain had cut over ten million years, and stayed in the drizzle until it stopped mattering. It had come three thousand metres up a wall expecting to be the thing that mattered. It reported afterward, when asked, that this had been restful — using their word, which is the only word for it.

It went down and looked at the bottom of the waterfalls. Nothing on the plateau has ever seen the bottom of its own falls, and it went, and saw, and came back up, and has never told anyone, and will not. It carried the information around for the rest of its stay like a coin in a pocket and found, to its surprise, that not spending it was the better use.

It carried the bag for the bulb-keeper, forty kilometres, twice a season, and was not thanked, because work done by water is free and the walking is the least of it.

---

**The small thing it did after all.**

There was a hut on the third crossing, one bad night in the cold part, where a stove had been let go out and the woman in it was too ill to walk two hours and too proud to send the child. The stove came back. Nobody saw it happen. There was no coal from anywhere, and then there was heat, unremarkable heat, the ordinary amount, and in the morning the ember box was warm against the belly of a nine-year-old who had not been given anything to be serious about.

The family has never had a story about this, because there is nothing to tell: a stove that stays lit is not an event. The power has thought about it for a long time since. It broke its own rule and it broke it for nothing large — not a death averted, not a slide held back, no mat kept on its dome. Just a bad night made ordinary for one household who then went on, ungratefully and correctly, with their lives.

It has decided to let that stand as the whole of its miraculous career here, and to consider the question of whether it was wise permanently open.

---

**What it gave in the end.**

It had come to give, and it had refused every large gift on good grounds, and it was left holding the problem that a power holds when it is honest: that the only currency worth anything on this plateau is matter, and matter is exactly what a power does not have.

So it got some.

It took a body, which was not an easy thing and involved, among other indignities, fungus between the toes. It went pruned at the fingers by four and splay-footed by six. It grew a hand that could press a thumb into a root and hear the difference against its own bone. It shouted for sixty years in the key of Three Sisters. It kept an ember, married, argued with its hands on somebody's arms, went stiff at the hips, half-deaf at the ear nearest the wheel, and was flicked at, oiled to the elbow, mild and damp and unimpressive, and was generous with power because power is free and careful with bone meal because bone meal is not, and never once sent anything over into running water.

And at the end of it there were four kilos of phosphorus in a country that has none.

They gave it to the root-head at the anchor of the second span, where the fig is thickening a finger's width per decade and will be carrying loaded mules for five hundred years after everyone who remembers the name has gone into their own gardens. That was the gift: not a seam under the plateau, not an end to scarcity, not dry weather or sound timber or straight hips — just one body's worth of the scarce thing, put where it holds something up.

A power that can do anything, having thought about it as hard as it is possible to think, arranged to become load-bearing. There was no other way to hand them something without taking something.

---

**Afterward.**

Nothing changed, which was the point, and is not a failure.

The rain comes up over the lip. The domes hold their mats for forty years and then let them go on a wet afternoon and are green again in two months. The wheels turn all night with nobody in the shed. The bulbs are fuzzy orange globes in the cloud at seven crossings and the keeper walks the line twice a season with a pole and a bag.

On the third crossing there is a great-granddaughter who says, without any particular solemnity, crossing a gorge in the horizontal white, *my people are the second span* — and steps onto it, and it takes her weight, as it has taken everybody's, being always under construction, being the only thing that beats the rain.

And behind the hill, at a steady unhurried rate, holding the place open until the water comes back: *slap, slap, slap.*
