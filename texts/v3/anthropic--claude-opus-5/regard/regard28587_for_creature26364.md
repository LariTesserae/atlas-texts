## The Forty-Second Quiet

### The difficulty of arriving

Consider the position of a benevolent power standing at the lip.

It can do anything. That is the trouble. In this place, anything it does will be a public utterance, and the city has exactly one public utterance available to it — the four-second stagger in the note when something goes down the Throat — and that utterance has exactly two meanings: *a death*, and *fire*. There is no third. There is no channel for *a stranger has come who wishes you well*. Any act large enough to be noticed by forty-one people at once will be read as a death or as fire, or it will establish a third meaning, and the establishing of a third meaning in a language of two would change the city more thoroughly than any famine.

So the power's first discovery, before it has done anything at all, is that its power is the wrong size. Here, the only way to say something to a person is to go down into a shuttered drum three paces across, sit close enough to touch, and speak once into an ear that will be deaf in twenty years. That is the whole bandwidth. That is the city's single import and single export and it does not scale.

A less patient benevolence would take this as an obstacle. This one took it as the local instruction, and read it carefully, and obeyed it.

### The inventory

It spent some time — the time is not the interesting part — going over the forty-one lives and sorting the damage.

This is the actual work of wisdom and it is unglamorous. Suffering in an old place is never one substance. Some of it is load-bearing: it has been built into meaning, it holds up rooms, and if you remove it the rooms come down. Some of it is simply damage, doing nothing, meaning nothing, a nail left in a beam.

The deafness is load-bearing. It looks like the great atrocity of the place and it is not. The young survey because they can hear and the old build because they cannot, and the deafness is what makes the old useful instead of a burden, and the whole city stands on the exchange. They are wrong about it being *fair* — nobody consents at nineteen to what fifty will cost, and the ones who break early are pitied and unmarried and that is an injustice with no compensation in it — but the deafness is theirs. The eighty-year-olds inside their single sweetened tone are not lying, and it is not the power's business to inform them that they have been robbed of something they have spent sixty years making into a theology.

The corneal haze is not load-bearing. Nothing hangs on it. It is a slow grey frosting laid on by the gas, and it takes the sight of people whose entire public language is made of hands seen at twenty paces in glare, and it means that a mason of seventy goes blind in the only sense she has left. Nobody has made anything of it. It is a nail in a beam.

The dead olfactory nerve is ambiguous, and the power thought about it longer than about anything else.

The fire risk is not damage at all; it is the condition of the place, and their unanimous care about it is the closest thing they have to a civic virtue.

The dying — the two cells on the seventh harmonic, the nine-day window, the jar of water bought at ruinous expense, the ones who miss and are carried back down to wait six months — is grief, and grief is not damage, and the power left it alone except in one particular.

And the eleven or twelve private tongues, each ending in a room where nothing carries out, are the most beautiful and the most purely wasteful thing the power had encountered in a long while, and it could not decide, and in the end it did not treat them as a problem to be solved.

### The obvious remedy, and why not

It could have capped the Throat. A hand over the flue, the gas turned aside into the rock, the roar out, the sulfur stopped, the corneas clear, the whole plain given water by an alteration no larger than a thought. Forty-one people led out to a green country and given orchards, and grandchildren who could hear a bird at eighty.

The power took this option seriously for a long time, because it is the option, and because refusing it in order to preserve something picturesque would be a cruelty dressed as taste. The pit is not a museum. It is thirty-nine to forty-three people breathing air that is one part in twenty wrong.

What stopped it was this. In the green country, everything said is kept. Speech pools there, in markets and temples and crowds; it averages out; within two generations the eleven tongues are one tongue, which is right and good and is also the end of the only thing these people have that no one else on earth has. Not the silence — silence can be built. The *annihilation*. A place where a person can be heard, exactly once, by someone who will shortly be unable to hear it again, and where no one is at the window, not by accident, not ever, because the architecture forbids it the way a vacuum forbids fire. Pilgrims walk two days into a noise that makes them bleed for that. The power, which had seen a great many worlds, could not think of a second one.

And the deciding consideration was not even that. It was that the power did not know. It did not know whether the grandchildren in the orchard would bless it or spend their lives with a hole in them where a note used to be. It had opinions. It did not have knowledge. And it held, as a rule of thumb worn smooth by long use, that where it did not know, it did not overwrite; that a benevolence which edits what it cannot understand is only a strong preference with a good opinion of itself.

So it left the Throat alone, and went down into the pit to do small things.

### What it did

It stopped the frosting of the eyes. This took no visible form. No one noticed and no one will; it is not the kind of gift that arrives, it is the kind that fails to arrive, forty years late, in the form of an old woman on the fifth terrace who can still read the hands at twenty paces and has no idea that this is unusual, because everyone she might have compared herself to is dead.

It did not restore the sense of smell, on the grounds that they have no word for it, that what they would be given is hydrogen sulfide and their own bodies, and that a gift you must first teach someone to want is at least partly an imposition. But it left the nerve alive in the ones who go over the lip. The surplus children who walk out onto the gypsum to become guides arrive in the world with noses, and are astonished, and cannot explain it to anyone at home, because explaining it would mean going down into a still where the astonishment would die. This is a small mercy shaped exactly like the city's own logic, and the power was rather pleased with it.

It put a seep in the rock of the fourth terrace: a weak, sulfurous, barely potable trickle, the yield of about one jar in three days, in a place where the geology makes it plausible. Sized to a single use. Not enough to water a garden, not enough to break the guides' trade, not enough to change anything at all except that nobody in that pit will ever again be dying in the best silence in the world with a dry mouth, or be carried back down because the water ran out before the nine days did. The city argued about the seep in signs for two seasons and concluded that the bell had slumped and opened an old sulfate bed, which is what would in fact have happened eventually, and which the power had merely brought forward by some centuries.

It took one job and kept it, and was not entirely comfortable about it. It did not fireproof the reedwood. It permitted burns, and it permitted a bad season on the third terrace when a bakers' mask caught and eleven houses went sour and two people were badly hurt, because a garden of terpene oil that cannot burn produces, in three generations, people who do not watch, and their watchfulness is one of the few things they hold in common and in daylight, and it is a good thing about them. What it refused was the total case: the single dropped lamp at the wrong hour in the wrong thicket that takes the whole bell in four minutes and leaves nothing. It drew a line, arbitrarily and by hand, between accident and extinction, and it stood under that one falling coal for as long as the city stood, and it never told them, and it accepted that this made it, in a small way, a liar.

And it surveyed the bell.

This is the largest thing it did and it looks like nothing. It knew the pit perfectly — every shell, every strut, every node, including three good stable nulls on a harmonic no living ear can reach, in a slumped quarter of the fourth terrace that the city had written off eighty years ago as dead air. It did not build there. Building there would have been a decision about how many people ought to exist in the world, and that is not a gift, that is a government.

Instead it went into a honeycombed cell on the fifth terrace, open and roaring and silting up with sulfur, and it scratched the measurements into the plaster in the city's own marks, in the hand of a surveyor who died in the last generation and whose work is known and trusted and who was, by common consent, the best of them. Forearms and hands' breadths, shells and hours, drift and margin, laid down correctly. Then it left the marks to be found, which took nineteen years.
