# What came up the road

It did not manifest. Manifestation is a way of being loud.

It came up the western switchbacks on foot in the ordinary way, through the warm wet wall of air, and its clothes went heavy and the dust on its boots turned to slip, and it stood at the top in the shadowless glare with its eyes watering, squinting like everyone else, and understood within the hour that it had arrived somewhere that would not be impressed and would not be hurried and had already worked out, in its own idiom, most of what a power comes to a place to say.

It stayed. Anything that holds still on the Sill gets stone on it. It held still.

That was the first decision and the whole shape of everything after: it did not exempt itself. It could have. It went under the local law instead — the rule of the shell, the millimeter on and the millimeter off, the slow indifferent accounting — because it judged that a benevolence which stands outside the terms it is offering to improve is not benevolence but tourism.

## The first eleven years

It did nothing.

It read the count's books, all of them, in the two legible hours of mid-morning, sitting on the iron chest with the pages a hand's width from its face like any half-blind old reader. It walked the eighty-one bowls of the fixed circuit. It learned the eleven hundred signs, which took no time, and then spent nine years learning the eighty or so that are only ever made below the water line, six or eight heads close together in a thigh-deep bowl after shift. It learned the pipe. It learned to hear whose hand was tapping from four works away, and it learned the obscene vocabulary, which it found genuinely impressive, and it learned that there is no sign for *shout* and no need of one.

And it learned the rudeness that has a name: to touch someone's forearm and then not speak.

That one it took seriously, because it had come intending to touch a great many forearms.

## The name

In its fourteenth year a relay operator in Slake, alone in a junction hut through the long white hours, with the line quiet and nothing to do but listen to the pipes breathe, gave it a tap-name. Four beats. It was not a ceremony. The operator was bored and the thing had been around and there was no convenient way to refer to it.

It went out on the line within the hour and came back acknowledged from thirteen works before dawn.

The power took this as its true name and has used no other since, and if you want to know what it had been called before, the answer is that it does not now remember, in the way you do not remember a language you have moved away from.

## What it did not do

**It did not bring green.** This is the temptation every visitor has. It considered alkali-tolerant stock, a walled garden, a single tree in the byre yard at Anneal, and it worked the consequences forward with some care and found that it was proposing to solve a flatness in its own nose. There is no lack here. There is an absence, which is a different thing and is doing work — in the food, in the air, in the shape of what the people notice about the world when they come back up the road. A tree in Anneal would have been the most important object on the plateau within a year and would have organised everything around itself, and in fifty years the Sill would have been a place with a tree in it and no longer a place.

**It did not quiet anything or make anything louder.** In particular it did not preserve the children's voices, which go between six and nine because nothing answers. It watched this happen to individual children it had come to be fond of, and it let it happen, on the grounds that a thing lost through disuse and not through violence is not a wound and that the adults it produces are not diminished, only quiet.

**It did not interfere with the setting of the dead.** It had arrived with a general prejudice against the practice, as most powers do, and it lost the prejudice in about a decade. The Sill's relation to its own dying is the healthiest the power has encountered anywhere in a long working life. Nothing is being denied. Nothing is being dressed up. The dead come back into the walls and the masons set them facing out because it is a clean face and a good stone, and there is no reason to turn it around. The power had nothing to add to this and did not pretend otherwise.

**It did not offer anyone more time.** They are not short of it. They are inside a thing that is going on.

**It did not reveal itself.** Not out of modesty. The arithmetic is simply that the moment ten thousand people know there is a benevolent power resident on the plateau, they have a relationship with a benevolent power, and everything else they have — the count's books, the mallet in the herd's belt, the argument on the line about a bet, the household mat two hundred years old — becomes furniture in that relationship. The power had seen this outcome before and considered it the single most destructive thing it was capable of doing, and it was capable of a great deal.

**It did not break the ovens.** It understood them inside a month. It found that the general local opinion is correct: it is not addressed to us. It also found that it is not addressed to the power, and it kept the confidence on the same grounds that anyone keeps a confidence, and it will go into the wall with it.

It considered, throughout, and has never entirely stopped considering, that the correct total intervention here might be none.

## What it did

It settled on a method and the method was not its own. It took it from the mat-wardens, who are the only people on the plateau who have ever successfully improved anything.

You do not add water. You do not import a crop. You have exactly one lever, which is *where the water goes*, and you work it a hand's breadth at a time with a short heavy adze, and a rim you cut on a Tuesday is still steering flow in three hundred years. Break one rim; build another; cool a bowl by two degrees; move the orange band up the slope where the snails can reach it. Everything else is already here.

The rules it gave itself:

*Nothing foreign.* Only what the Sill already has.
*Nothing announced.* Every change must arrive looking like weather, luck, or a good idea somebody had.
*Always a local author.* A gift that costs nothing and comes from nowhere is not trusted and is not maintained. The change needs a person whose name is on it and, better, an argument it has to survive.

**The flour.** The lungs were the thing it could not leave. In its thirty-first year an apprentice in the Anneal smelter re-plumbed a roof condenser that had always dripped into its gutter, so that it dripped instead into a header over the flour shed, and the shed ran damp. There was a fight about it that lasted two years. Yield fell. A foreman was incandescent and is remembered for it. Then the assay showed the damp flour bagged tighter and travelled better and the price held, and the other works copied it because the price held, and that was the end of the argument. Forty years later the lung ward at Anneal had half its beds and there was no name for what had happened, because a thing that did not happen does not get a name. The apprentice is remembered for something else entirely: a bad marriage and a good hand on the pipes.

**The glass.** They had silica and they had borate and they had furnaces, and it took one bored assayer in Quench nine months of evenings to work out a smoked batch that would take the light out without going milky. Slits are glass in every work now and cost a day's wage, and the knife-cut strip of oven-hide survives only as the thing you give a child. The creases at the corners of the eyes are unchanged. The blindness at sixty-five is now the blindness at eighty, mostly, and the blind still take the night relays and are still the best there is, and the power made sure — this was the part that took care — that nobody stopped needing them.

**The wind.** The waders turn to face the west wind about four minutes before it comes over the scarp. Everyone had always known this in the way you know things you have not thought about. A counter in Temper, half-blind, working her two legible hours, wrote it down as a number, and within a season the line was carrying a four-beat wind-signal relayed from whoever was nearest a junction, and the pier crews came in four minutes earlier than before. Four minutes is the difference, in a bad year, between three scaldings and one. The power's entire contribution was the length of time the counter stood still on the stairs that morning before she went back to her book.

**The road down.** Two in five newcomers go back. The power thought the shame of it was doing damage out of all proportion, and arranged nothing except a slow drift in custom: that a third-month leaver goes down with a full purse, a written hand from the works, and a palmful of somebody's mat in a tin. The mat is the part that matters. It dies below, of course — the brine is wrong — but it dies over about four years, and those are four years in which a person who failed on the Sill has a living thing on their kitchen wall that came from there.

**The rooms.** In the third month somebody checks your room. The power did not improve on this, because it cannot be improved on. It only saw to it that the checking was rostered rather than remembered, which is a small administrative change made by a shift clerk in Anneal in a year nobody records, and which is the single most load-bearing thing in this entire account.

## The face

There is a prohibition on the Sill that the power looked into, which is that divers are not permitted to marry into the warden families, for reasons nobody will state.

The reason is a hundred and thirty years old and it is ugly and it runs the other way. A vent above Flux was turning; the count said so and the count is never overruled; a warden family with money in that season's assay sent divers down into it to bring up a blind fish and prove the water was still alive. Two did not come up. The third would not go. It was the *divers* who refused the wardens, for three generations, and then the shape of the thing rotated the way these things do, and the shame relocated onto the lighter party, and by the time anyone alive was asking, the answer had become nobody will say.

The power could not tell them. Telling is touching the forearm.

So it said it in the only register that carries here, which is stone, and it took ninety years to finish the sentence. It cut a rim above the Flux quarry and steered a flow a hand's breadth left, and let deposition and slumping keep their accounts, and in due course a quarry face came down where it was always going to come down, and in the fresh cut there were two human cross-sections, small and pale and unmistakable, and eighteen centimeters from the second one the iron ring of a hooped net, perfect in the matrix like a bubble in glass.

Masons set those pieces facing out, because it is a clean face and a good stone.

The counter at Flux went and read the book for that year. The books go back a hundred and sixty-two years and the entry is four lines long and perfectly clear and had been sitting there the whole time.

What happened next was slow and is still going on and is not the power's business. There was no reckoning and no apology; there is not much apologising here. There is, now, one marriage.

## The one touch

It touched a forearm exactly once, and then it spoke, so it was not rude.

There is a man in Calcine who has filled forty notebooks on the ovens' night-drumming and does not claim to be close. One winter the power lay in the dark on the second tier of the Calcine byre, on the boards, above half a ton of animal, and listened to the low frequencies going out for kilometres into the white, and then it went down to the junction hut where the man was sitting with a mug going cold and put a hand on his forearm and tapped him, once, a pattern out of his own notebook nineteen, correctly.

That is all it gave him. Not the meaning. Only that there is something there to break, and that he is not spending his life on noise.

He never published it and never said it, and he filled eleven more notebooks, and the first line of notebook forty-one is: *Not addressed to us. Not a lack in them.* He died at eighty-one, content, in the breathless way, and his tap-name goes out every year at the turn of the wet season, and the notebooks are in the iron chest at Anneal with the count's books because nobody could think where else to put them.

And once, in an early year, out on the hardpan in the mid-morning legibility, a child was screaming at nothing the way the children do, a small silent figure at fifty metres with its mouth open — and the power, which had not yet worked out its rules and would not have allowed it later, answered.

The child lost her voice at nine like everybody does. But she never quite lost the habit, and she taught it to two others, and there is now a thing they do at one work on the western edge where people go out alone onto the pans at mid-morning and sing where nobody can hear them. It has no name. It is not grief and it is not worship and nobody can tell you what it is for. The general local opinion is that it is probably not addressed to us, and that this is fine.

## What became of it

It set.

It had agreed to the terms on the first day and the terms are the terms. There are two entries in the count's books, in two different hands and ninety years apart, recording a warden in iron pattens working a bowl-system that nobody farms, and neither counter thought it worth remarking on, and they were right not to.

It is in a wall in Anneal now, on the fourth tier, facing out, with about a hundred other small cross-sections, in an ordinary wall that nobody points at. Visitors find it and stand there for some time, and could not tell you why that one, and are not wrong.

Its name still goes out. Somebody is willing to tap it, which is the only requirement. It comes east down the line on the loudest day of the year, four beats, in its order of age, which puts it early — and if you were standing on the road below the scarp you would hear nothing at all.
