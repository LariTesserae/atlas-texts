# The Attention

It came downhill, because everything there goes downhill, and because a thing that arrives from above is only ever visited, never met.

It came in the rain, and it kept the two rules — down when the rain starts, up when the frogs stop — although neither rule had any purchase on it. It could have stood in the plaza through a dry year and come out with its sequence intact and its name still on it. It chose not to. A power that exempts itself from the local physics learns the locality the way a landlord learns a house. So it went down the slick clay on its hands and knees like everyone, and it came up when the frogs shut their throats, and the watcher on the rim counted it, and got the number right, and thought nothing of it.

It did this for a long time before it did anything else.

## What it heard

Nothing had ever listened to the Bowl.

This is not a figure of speech and it is the largest fact about the place. Several hundred million animals, blind, silent to one another, rasping in the abandoned tunnels of their grandmothers, feeding the noise into a body four hundred paces wide that had been ringing since before the river found its present channel — and no ear anywhere in it, at any time, for any of it. The frogs cannot hear. The spiders read holes. The rim people arrive with their ears already surrendering. The sound went up off the Bowl and over the floodplain and dispersed, unreceived, the way the pollen goes.

The power went under the surface of the loud and stayed there, and heard it.

Not as a roar. It heard Third Street's grind as the particular bore of eleven fused stems that had crowded each other in an ox-bow scar and failed to merge; it heard the arcades' thin scream as wall-thickness; it heard the plaza's deep non-note as a barn-sized void lined and smoothed around the socket of a twelfth sibling that died before anything now living was begun. Under that it heard the individual animals, all of them at once and each of them singly, the way you can hear both a chord and its notes — every twenty-minute effort, every slack spell braced in the dark doing nothing, every nymph breaking the gallery skin and taking its first rasp with its whole savage little body going hot.

It listened for nine rains.

It is worth being exact about the finding, because everything the power subsequently did or refused to do came out of it: **the Bowl is not in pain, is not calling, is not waiting, and does not want.** It is an organism that grew a house in order to be played and grew an orchestra in order to play it, and it is doing that, perfectly, and needs nothing. The power found it beautiful in the specific way a thing is beautiful when it does not require you. It did not want it different.

Then it went to find out who lived there, and got an answer it disliked: everyone did, and one of them was seven.

## What it did not do

It did not stop the sound. The sound is the animal's body and the plant's tongue and the weather's engine; the sweating ceilings, the crawling drops, the standing column of warm air that lifts the heavy pollen over the rim — all of it is insect body heat, and all of it would have gone cold.

It did not give the sawyers sequence. It considered this for one whole rain-hour, which for it is a long time. A sawyer has an hour of life per rain and a warm nowhere in between; give it before-and-after and you give it, in the same motion, its own death to carry, and it would carry that for a life instead of meeting it once, briefly, in the quiet, in the right order. The power decided this was not a gift but a tax, and left them alone.

It did not tell the rim people what lives in the high galleries. It climbed up there and met it. What passed between them is not in this account. Afterwards the smell of cut grass lasted three weeks instead of one, and the claw-scores in the polish that season were lower down, as though something had come a little way toward the street and then thought better of it.

It did not settle the old questions. Whether the shape of a town is coincidence or a fact about what shapes want to be. Whether the Bowls are sexed. Whether the vector is the browsers or the visitors or something nobody has thought of. The power knew the answers to two of these and could have said so, and didn't, because the village had spent generations declining to decide, and a thing a people have declined to decide is load-bearing; you do not remove it from underneath them as a favour.

It did not restore the polished by force. This was the hardest refusal and it required the most from it. A polished person is not suffering. They are rested, warm-handed, pleasant to sit with, needing nothing. They weed a field beautifully. To reach into that and hand back the edges is to hand back, at the same time, every year that went past while they were smooth, and everyone who died in those years, all of it arriving at once and in order, into a person who has had no practice at *and then* for a long time. You may not do that to someone without asking.

So it asked.

## The asking

A question has two ends. You cannot put one into a person with no sequence; it goes in and does not hook.

But the rain-hour is when the Bowl is real, and the power worked out — this took it four seasons of sitting on the plaza kerb — that a polished person in the rain can hold exactly one question, if the question has two ends and nothing in the middle.

There were nine of them alive in the village then. It sat with each one, one rain each, and asked.

Two said no. One of them said it in the present tense, mildly, with interest, the way they say everything, and went back to what she was doing, which was nothing. The power thanked her and did not ask twice. A power that will not accept a refusal is not benevolent; it is only strong, and the difference between those is the entire subject.

Four gave no answer that could be called one, and the power let them be, and still sits with them sometimes in the rain, in case.

Three said yes.

They are not clean miracles. One of them was rendered back into sequence over eleven rains and spent the first four of them grieving people the rest of the village had finished grieving before he was smoothed, which made him inconvenient and made them cruel, briefly, until his sister took him in. He is the only person on the floodplain who can hold two true things about himself at once — that he is glad, and that he is not — because he is the only one who has stood in both weathers. The village finds him tiresome. They would.

## The watch

There is a watcher who does not go down at all, whose entire office is to stand on the rim in the rain with a pole and count people out, and who has therefore never once been inside the thing he has spent his life at the edge of.

The power took the pole from him one rain and said: go down. I will count.

He was sixty-one. He went down. He was in the Bowl for an hour, and what he did there he has described in order, at length, to anybody who will sit still, which is how the village prefers it. He came up when the frogs stopped and he was counted, and the count was right, and he was unchanged — not because the Bowl spared him but because nobody had ever gone down there before with a guarantee, and the guarantee is the thing the power actually gave. Not the hour. The rope.

This is the only intervention the village will admit to, and they argue about whether it happened. They have it in the list, though, between two rains, in the correct place.

## The child

She was leaning on the old grey browser in Third Street, where she had been for longer than the list of rains goes back, and the browser was leaning back, and the browser had been seven browser-lifetimes old for the same length of time and neither of them had noticed, because noticing is a sequence.

She was not frightened, not hungry, not bored, not cold. She was not anything. She was a small warm hole in the noise with a hand flat on a warm flank, and the wood-dust held the print, and the shaking that should have wiped it kept refreshing it instead, which is why it was still fresh, which is why the rim people counted twenty-two hands and could name twenty, and had a policy instead of a story.

The power put its hand on the flank beside hers.

**What it did not do here either:** it did not pick her up and carry her out. Out is a sequence and she had no rail to run it on; she would have arrived on the grass smooth, and been the twenty-third polished, and that is not rescue, that is relocation. Nor would it have helped to restore her wholesale and hand a seven-year-old, in one piece, the fact that her mother has been four generations in the ground. There is a kind of help that is only the helper's impatience wearing a coat.

What it did was stay.

This is the one thing a power of that sort has in unlimited supply and the one thing the child had none of: someone else in the room. She was not lost — nobody is lost in the Bowl; you can see the rim from anywhere — she was alone in a room with no doors, in a moment several hundred paces wide, and the power could simply be in the room, for as long as was required, at no cost to itself, and did.

And in each rain-hour, when sequence came back into the Bowl like water coming back into a bath, it told her one thing.

It told her the list.

It had learned the list from the rim people, which required no effort; they recited it at it on the first evening and could not easily be stopped. Their history is not kept in years but as a list of rains, in order, with the order being the whole point and the content almost incidental — which is precisely and exactly what she needed, because she did not need to know what had happened. She needed to learn *and then*. The village had spent four hundred years grinding a rope out of pure tedium without ever knowing what it was for, in the same blind way the plant grew rooms in order to ring, and it turned out to be long enough.

One rain of the list per rain-hour. Nothing else. The power did not hurry, and the not-hurrying was the medicine.

The markers, in order, because they must be in order:

She did not remember it between rains. For a long time.

Then she remembered that there had been a hand.

Then she remembered that there had been a hand *before*.

Then, in the eighty-somethingth rain, she asked for the next one — which is the first request ever made inside the Bowl, because a request has two ends, and the power had to sit very still for a moment. It thought of the ants, who are the only residents ever to have used the place instead of enduring it, and who solved everything by putting the thing down and letting the room carry it to the door.

At the end she walked up the clay herself, in the rain, on her hands and knees like everyone.

The browser came to the lip and stopped. It would not go over. She stood above it in the wet with the roar already starting to come back up through her palms, and she let it go, and that was the first thing she ever had that was properly in the past, and she has it still, and it is hers.

## The count

Twenty-two went in. Twenty-three on the grass.

The watcher counted twenty-three, aloud, because that is what they do, and then counted again, and got twenty-three, and wrote it down in the correct place.

They took her in without any fuss at all, which is the only unadmirable-looking thing they have ever done and is in fact the best of them: they did not stand around marvelling, they started at the beginning and told her, in order, everything that had happened while she was elsewhere, and it has taken years, and they had years, and for the first time in the history of the floodplain somebody was glad of them.

She gave them something back that they had never been able to afford. They had no comfortable word for *meanwhile* — no use for it, no safe place to put it — and now they had a person among them who had been somewhere while they were elsewhere, which is what the word is for. They use it now. It embarrasses them slightly. They use it anyway.

## The errand

There was one other thing, and it took a browser's lifetime, which was the correct speed.

A day downriver there is a grey ring of hard ground and standing dead wood with the galleries eaten open and the roof fallen in, where you can hear your own footsteps. Everyone who walks into the middle of it comes back shaken, not by the ruin but by the quiet. Bowls die. Something must therefore start them.

The power went and found what starts them — four days' walk, in a depression nobody had ever thought worth calling a landscape event, low and small and quiet, dropping fist-sized dull fruit into the mud for a vector that had not come in anyone's memory. It carried one back. It did not carry it quickly. It walked, and it took the years a browser would have taken, because a thing that ought to happen at the pace of a gut and a lifetime is spoiled by being done at the pace of a wish.

It buried the fruit in the middle of the grey ring, in the hard bare ground, where the footsteps are.

Nobody will hear anything there for a hundred years. That is fine. The power is not in a hurry and was never the audience.

## After

The Bowl rings on and needs nothing. The pollen goes up off the high flowers in a fine dull cloud, over the rim, going wherever it goes, and now one thing on the floodplain knows where, and is not saying.

Sometimes, in the rain, the watcher finds the pole already held, and goes down, and comes back up, and is counted correctly.

And on the flank of the old grey browser in Third Street there are twenty-three handprints, of which they can name twenty-one, and one of them — the small one — is at last beginning to blur.

It is the only thing in the Bowl that has ever gotten older.
