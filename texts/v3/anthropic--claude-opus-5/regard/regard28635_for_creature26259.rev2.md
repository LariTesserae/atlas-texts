# What the Power Did

## The attention

It arrived on a grey day, which was courtesy, because on a grey day nothing falls due. No debt matured while it stood there; no appointment was broken by the pause it caused; nobody was where they had said they would be, and so nobody was disturbed by a presence in a place it had not been promised.

It came down the way everything comes down here, through a storm gap, and it came at the speed of water, which is the only speed the valley trusts.

It had noticed the place the way you notice a sound under a sound.

## What it considered, standing on the raw ground

It thought first, as any power would, of the obvious kindnesses, and it is worth saying what they were, because the refusing of them took longer than the doing of anything else.

It considered lifting the lid. Four kilometres of jammed grey plates, ninety years of settling, the whole cracked ceiling — it could have set it aside like a pot lid and given them sky. It looked at the crushed quarters under the floor, still full of cisterns, and the arithmetic was not difficult.

It considered soil. A metre of good black loam laid over the spall, grass, a stream taught downhill in a single afternoon, willows by the third year.

It considered the Long Cough, and forty thousand pairs of lungs that are slowly being filled with powdered glass, and it very nearly did that one at once, on the spot, on a grey unaccounted afternoon, without asking.

Then it was dusk, and the hiss stopped.

It had not understood, until the sound went out of the valley, how much of a sound had been there. Two hours of water falling off a clear ceiling into stone, and under the water forty thousand people humming close and low and unaccompanied, one quarter answering another across a kilometre of dark, and every one of them with both hands busy on a gutter. Nothing struck. Nothing raised. A city of the chronically ill, singing in the dark at almost no volume, in perfect time, while they harvested the sweat of a rock.

The power stood in the Ninth Standing, which was empty, because it was not the Ninth Standing at that hour, and it revised.

## The revision

Here is what it understood, and it is not complicated, but it is the sort of thing that has to be arrived at in the place itself.

They are not enduring the valley. They have taken a job in it. Their theology is a work order: *the land is unfinished, there is nobody to wear it down, so the work has fallen to us.* Take the lid off and you have not freed them, you have fired them. Lay down soil and you have done in an afternoon the thing four generations have been rubbing at, and the boulder in the widest pillar becomes a rock that somebody used to touch. They would be grateful. They would say thank you in the murmur they use, with their palms turned out. And then they would be a people who had once been for something.

So the power did not do the large kindnesses. It did four small ones, and it took on one long job, and it kept the manners of the house.

## The first: the dust

It could not stop the grinding. The grinding is the city.

So it went down instead, below hearing, into the pale welded ash itself, and it worked there for a long time on the flour — on the particular sharpness of the glass in it, the edge that lodges in a lung and stays and scars. It made the flour soft. Not less fine, not less white, not less a nuisance in the eyes: simply no longer a blade at the size that matters. Slow work, and done at a scale below listening, which is where a guest ought to work.

Nobody noticed for forty years. Then people began to cough later. By the third generation the Long Cough was an illness of the old — sixty-five, seventy — and by then the city had explained it thoroughly to itself: the veils are woven finer now; the cords run wetter than they did in the grandmothers' time; there was that fashion for the double hem. A clerk in the Cordary who cannot read and can feel a forgery in the dark hung a knot recording the new opinion.

The power let the credit fall where it fell. It had come to be useful, not to be believed in.

## The second: the honest ceiling

It would not make the lid safe. Safety was not the gift; the ticks are not a flaw in the valley, they are its liturgy, and a city that stops mid-word to let the rock speak is doing something no one should interrupt. Also, a Listener made unnecessary is a Listener made a liar, and there are children in this city learning to name drips in the dark, and their learning is worth more than their odds.

What it did instead was smaller and stranger. It went along the joints of every plate, Old Lean and Hoop and the Broad Cousin and all the rest, and it arranged that nothing would ever again fall silently.

Not safety: *audibility*. The rock still moves. It still comes down. But there is no longer any such thing as a fall without a warning — no plate that goes in one breath out of a clear silence. The Broad Cousin, huge and unloved because silence is not the same as safety, began to talk, a little, the way the others do, and its people slept better and married outward.

Now the art is still hard and still required, and a careless Listener will still bury a quarter. But a good one is never betrayed. It is possible, here, to be as good at your work as your work demands, which is not true in most places, and is the nearest thing to justice that could be installed in a ceiling.

## The third: the crickets

The children born in the narrow ends, where the plates lie flush and the mirror-relays run out, grow bowed in the leg and soft in the skull. The light-mothers carry them out on a rota and lay them in rows in the pillars like washing, and it is not enough, because a patch of daylight the size of a book cover is not enough, and everyone knows it, and no one says it.

The power did not open a gap. Opening a gap in that ceiling to let light into a dark quarter is the act of somebody who has decided he knows better than the geology, and the geology here has opinions.

It went to the pale crickets instead — the ones that came down on their own, that are farmed in the black wedges by nine-year-olds and carried up in jars as paste — and it altered them, slightly, in the direction of what those children lacked. The dark now feeds the dark. The remedy is bred in the deepest quarters by the poorest families and sells for very little, because it is only cricket paste.

Within a generation the bowed legs were rare, and the proverb went round that a dark-born baby should have the jar before the pillar, and the light-mothers grumbled about it, because their rota lost status. That was the whole cost.

## The fourth: the cord

Every year a certain number of linesmen do not come back down. Their families tie off the knot in the Cordary and do not speak of it, and the names go slightly unlucky.

This grief had nowhere to go. It sat in flat sheets, a hand deep, for weeks, like dead water on a level place, because nobody had ever cut a channel for it.

The power could not bring them down — a person who stays up in the grass has chosen, and choosing is not a wound to be closed. But it went up onto the plateau and found them, the ones who stay, sitting with the far-eye on them in the wind, and it put into the head of one of them, gently, the way you put a thought into your own head, the idea of a knot.

He tied it one-handed, in the valley lay, in his own signature, and sent it down in an empty grain basket, and it lay in the bottom for eleven days before anyone thought to look.

Now there is a wall in the Cordary for knots from above. It is not large. Some of the cords on it are answers; most are not. But a tied-off knot can now be untied and retied loose, which is a thing the clerks do with their blank shining hands, in the dark, twice a year, feeling for the difference, and it is the first new rite the city has invented in sixty years, and they invented it themselves.

## The long job

Then the power took proper employment, and the employment was this: it became the part of the erosion they cannot be.

They do flatness. They grind Ways and quern stones and mirrors and thresholds; they are very good at it; it is the only industry a place with no erosion could have. What they cannot do, by hand, block by block, is arrival. No fish in the dead water. Bats in the dark ends and not enough insects to feed them. A single fig. Mosses, a black lichen, a grey fern in the cracks. An ecology that has barely got here and knows it is early.

So the power carries things in. Nothing that was not going to come anyway — it is not a gardener and it will not stock the place like a pond — but the beetle that would have found the valley in three hundred years finds it now; the spore that would have blown down a storm gap in the wrong season blows down in the right one; a seed comes in on a swift and lands where the dust has caught. It works at the speed of colonisation, which is slower than the city's building and faster than the city's stone, and it works from below and from the edges, and it will be four hundred years before anyone notices that the valley is getting greener faster than it should.

And it manages, quietly, the great appointment. The lid will open. Not in one afternoon and not as a collapse; the power has taken the rate in hand, and the gaps widen now by a finger's breadth a year, so that each generation is born a little less dark-adapted than the last and no child will ever be unlidded in a single hour. In two thousand years there will be no lid, and there will be a river, and the Ways will be the oldest watercourses in a country of watercourses, and the people who live there will be able to look at a horizon without sitting down and putting a hand over their eyes. They will have no word for it. That will be the sign that it was done slowly enough.

## What it did not do

It did not explain itself. It did not appear in the pillars. It did not add anything to the boulder, or take anything from it, or arrange for it to be round faster.

It did not cure the ones already coughing. It sat up with them, on the bad nights, in the corner of rooms where a daughter or a husband was rubbing a back with the flat of a hand, and it did not interfere with the hand, because there was already a hand there, and it is the hand that matters.

It did not fix the knees. Everyone kneels for a living. Some of what wears a person out is the same as what they are.

It did not argue when they said, of the surface people and their soft broad country, *they are wasteful; they live on ground that was smoothed for them free of charge and have never once thought to thank it or to rub anything themselves.* The power thought this was substantially correct.

And it never once made itself necessary. Everything it did, it did in such a way that if it left tomorrow the city would not fall — would not even look up.

## Its turn

It goes to the boulder like anyone.

No prayer, no priest, nothing asked for. It rubs with wet sand for as long as it feels like rubbing, at the kneeling hollow, which is the one nobody has worn deep enough yet, and then it goes. People have stood beside it there and not known, or half known, in the way you half know things in a room with a stone lid.

Once a woman of Old Lean, finishing beside it in the failing hour, did what you do, and touched its wrist, and turned her own palm out.

It turned its palm out too.

She looked a moment longer than is polite, because the hand was blank and shining, the ridges gone, the skin worn smooth and slightly translucent all across the pads — an old hand, an old grinder's hand, sixty years of cord and slurry at the very least, and on someone whose face she could not place.

*Whose roof?* she said, in the murmur, into the side of her hood.

And the power tipped its chin at the plate overhead, which was hers, and said the name of it.

Then the rock ticked, somewhere back at the third joint, the usual cold-shrinkage tick and not the other thing, and both of them stopped, and waited, and after two or three seconds went on.
