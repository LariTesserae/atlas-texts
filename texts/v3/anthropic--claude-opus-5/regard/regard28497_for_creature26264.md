## Struck glass

**Knappers.** Struck glass is the loudest ordinary craft on the ridge, and it is done off the ridge proper, out on the north drop, on a shelf the knappers call the waste, where growth is not harvested because the wind sours it. A knapper works with his back to the town and his own noise piling up behind him: by dusk there is a low pale wall of bloom at his shoulders, ribbed and stupid-looking, the shape of an afternoon's work said out loud. Some of them eat it. Most feed it to the birds. It is considered bad manners to comment on the size of another man's wall.

The entry breaks off there, mid-word, because the ridge was never finished being written down. That gap is where the power came in.

## The arrival

Nothing on the Nap descends. Water comes up out of the seams, rock comes up from underneath, the fog comes up at night, the ancestors came up the stair. So it came up too. It came up the single cut stair on the arête, out of the whiteness, out of the place where the children are told the sound is kept, and it did not correct them about that, then or ever.

It arrived in the wrong shoes and stopped before the first shelf to fix that. It sat on bare basalt for four days and made itself a pair of six-layer felt soles lashed to a wooden last, badly, and then walked up and down the waste until it had the rolling step — outside of the heel, through to the toe — well enough that an old woman on the south shelf, hearing it come, would not mistake it for a running child or a shrug.

It learned the near tongue from children, because children are the only teachers of it who do not mind being watched while they think.

It was given a hand-name in the first month, as everyone is. Two fingers laid across the lips, then opened outward and away. Cruel in origin: it had meant *the mute*, *the one with nothing in there*. It was never given a spoken name, because a spoken name is only used in the pits, and nobody ever had cause to say this one in a pit. So it is the only person in the history of the ridge who never fed anybody. Of everything about it, that is the part the town found sad.

## What it did not do

It did not speak in the town. Not once, in nine years. It understood within the first hour what its voice was — understood that a single sentence of it, said in the middle of the forty-one houses, would raise a pale mound over all of them by morning; that this would be received as a sermon; and that a sermon which buries the congregation is not a sermon, it is weather.

It did not kill the felt. It considered this honestly and for a long time, because the felt takes their backs at thirty-five and their voices at forty and their infants out of their arms for three years, and the temptation to simply lift it off the mountain like a wet blanket was real and was not shameful. But the felt is the roof, the food, the water-comb, the paper, the archive, the marriage oath, the grave, and the language. A benefactor who removes the thing you eat, read, and bury in has murdered you politely and left before the smell.

It did not bring metal. There is no ore in rock that young. Metal would have had to be either a lie or an import, and either way the scars on the knappers' hands are the shape of their competence, and their competence is theirs.

It did not walk to the two cloud-swallowed shoulders, and it did not tell anyone what is on them. Some blanks are load-bearing.

It knew whether the felt will follow the mountain when the ridge becomes a peak. It did not say.

It did not raise anyone out of a mound.

It came up the stair, and when the children asked it — hands only, fast, cruel, delighted — whether it had passed Habbe down there growing other people's voices in the dark, it made the gesture for *I was not looking* and let them scream silently at each other about what that meant.

## The scraping

It took work as a path-scraper and did that for three years.

A third of all labour, no status whatsoever, everybody agrees it is unjust, nobody has ever fixed it. It worked bent, with a glass-toothed hook, in the wet, from grey to grey, and it learned what the good ones know: how to feel through the shaft where the felt is hungry, where the growth is running faster than growth should, which is how a scraper is always the first to find out that a house has been quarrelling.

It did not fix the injustice. It thought about that too. What it fixed was a hook.

In the second winter a young scraper called Mull found a broken hook that someone had re-hafted the wrong way round, the tooth-set canted, the weight further out, and used it because it was there, and by spring she had noticed that her back did not lock up at the end of a shift. She showed three people. Two of them told her she was wrong. Within a generation every hook on the ridge was made that way and scrapers' backs went at fifty instead of thirty-five, and the change is credited to Mull, which is correct, because she is the one who noticed.

That is the whole of its method and it never varied from it: it gave nothing that could not be lost, improved, or claimed by somebody else. A gift that can only be received is a debt. A gift that can be inherited is just a thing that happens to be true now.

And when it scraped a stretch where the growth was running and it knew what that meant, it did the ordinary discretion of its trade. It scraped it flat. It said nothing to anybody. Then, going past the door, it put the flat of its fingers on the woman's forearm — *yes, I heard you* — the joke that stopped being a joke generations ago, and out of that hand it was not a joke, and she sat down on the wet path and cried the way everyone there cries, which is silently and extremely well.

## The crying house

Every ninth day it went downwind, past the last shelf, into the double walls, and was loud.

That is the only place on the mountain where noise is already accounted for. It sat in the crying house with the four half-deaf far-nurses and laughed out loud and sang badly on purpose and let the harvest come up rank and enormous around the walls and go to the birds. It held infants. It walked the floor at the black end of the night with somebody else's screaming child on its shoulder in a room where the walls were swelling with the sound, which is the main work of any benevolent power in any world, and is not made easier by being able to unmake the moon.

The far-nurses were not told what it was. One of them worked it out, roughly, and what she said about it was: *It has a good voice and no idea what to do with it.* That is the most accurate theology the Nap has ever produced.

## The audition

There was a girl auditioned in the shallow practice pit at nine whose voice was the kind that comes every third or fourth generation: low, grained, a catch in it, a habit of dropping into the chest. Everyone in the pit knew inside of ten words that she was wealth, and that she would be farmed nearly to death by love and gratitude, and that there would be a slab of her last year kept somewhere, coarse and hoarse and full of gaps, for people to touch and feel awful about.

It could have blunted her. Damped the voice, given her an ordinary life, and nobody would ever have known what had been taken.

Instead it taught her a hand-game. Breath low, tongue slack, the long line broken at the fourth name instead of the eighth, two pitches alternating so that one rests while the other works; and the game had a rhyme in the near tongue and a rude ending, so the children took it up and it spread the way rude things spread and stopped being anybody's.

She was farmed anyway. Six days out of seven from twelve years old, and the town ate her, gratefully, as the town does. But she was still growing sweet crop at sixty-one, and she trained forty children, and there is no coarse slab of her kept anywhere, and nobody feels awful about it at all.

## The north drop

On its last night it went out past the knappers' waste to the north drop, where the wind sours the growth and nothing is harvested, and knelt on the fresh black chips, and put both hands into the moss, and spoke to it.

Because that was the being that lived in this place. Two hundred and six people lived there also, and each of them was a whole world, and it had scraped their paths and carried their children and buried its face in their harvest and loved them in the exhausting way you love someone who might at any moment cough down a wall. But the felt was the one that had never once been addressed. Everything was said *into* it. Genealogies, recipes, insults about the next pit over, the seventeen songs, the oaths, the labour, the funerals — a hundred generations of voice poured into that nap by beings who were feeding it, not talking to it. It combed every sound out of the air on that mountain and it had never had a single word aimed at itself.

So it spoke to it all night, in full voice, in a language with no relation to anything on the ridge. Not instruction. Not prayer. It told the felt where it was: the height, the thin air, the two shoulders, the fog and how the fog is made, the mountain still coming up underneath, the long white going down forever. It listed things — that is what you do with a listener, you list — the water, the light it eats a little of, the lilac worms in its own fibre, the birds writing and rubbing out. It thanked it for the roofs. It thanked it for keeping the dead legible. It apologised for the funerals, which are not its fault, and for the crying house, which is not either.
