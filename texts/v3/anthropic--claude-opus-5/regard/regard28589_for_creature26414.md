# The Attention

## The noticing

It did not notice them from above, which is where powers are usually imagined. From above, the Shelf is a scratched silver nothing, and the city, being white stone on white stone, does not read.

It noticed the way you notice a held note in another room: not an event but the absence of one. In most places the labour of the living runs against the grain of the world, and that friction has a sound to it, a grinding, audible at any distance if you are the kind of thing that can hear such distances. Here there was none. The people and the ground were doing the same thing at the same speed, in the same direction, and had been for so long that the two motions could not be separated by any instrument the power possessed. It came closer to determine which was which.

It stayed two hundred years without doing anything at all.

This was not indecision. It is simply that nothing on the Shelf can be understood at a shorter interval. A house takes twenty years. A district takes a century to arrive and four to fall. A stranger who watched for a decade and then acted would be acting on a still image, and would improve the plateau the way you might improve a sentence by correcting the third letter.

## The tempo

By the end of the second century it had arrived at one rule, which it kept afterwards without exception, and which it had not invented:

*Nothing given here would be given faster than it could be waited for.*

Everything else followed from that, the way everything on the Shelf follows from the fact that hard water leaves lime on a coarse weave. It could not deliver. It could only site things correctly and then not interfere. Which meant that a power capable of remaking the tableland between one breath and the next chose to work in units of nine hundred years, using pressure, and patience, and the natural tendency of stone to give way where it is already weakest — that is, it chose to be an inhabitant. It wove its intentions loose and open and set them in the path of a seep and left them there to stiffen.

There was a vanity in this at first. It got over that.

## The list

It made a list of mercies, because that is what benevolence does when it first sits down.

*Clear the milky eyes.* It went and stood at half a metre from the blind, in the lid-closing hour, and listened. Blindness on the Shelf is a promotion. The blind are not, exactly, mourned — but *not exactly* is a load-bearing phrase, and the power was too careful to spend it. What it found, listening, was that no one grieved the milkiness and everyone grieved the twelve or fifteen years of glare-headache that came before it. So it did nothing to the eye and nothing to the light. Over four centuries it thickened, very slightly, the black liverwort that grows on the warm seeps, which is what visors are sooted with, so that there was always enough, so that no household ever had to choose which of its children went out unshaded. That is all. It amounts to nothing you could point at. Fewer headaches.

*Harden the teeth.* Refused. An old midwife can look in a stranger's mouth and say which water he drank before he was seven, and be right, and be untactful about it. That is a library. It is written in mottled brown-and-cream and it is the only record the plateau keeps of where anybody comes from, since the seeps themselves die and dissolve. A power that whitened those teeth would have burned the archive to spare the readers a little grit.

*Blunt the rims.* Refused, and this was the one it had to argue with itself about, because *the scars are beautiful* is a monstrous reason to leave a child bleeding, and it knew it. So it changed the question. It stopped asking *is this suffering* and asked instead *what do they grieve* — because a people who have lived somewhere for ten thousand years have already sorted their pains, and the sorting is available to anyone willing to sit close and be quiet. They do not grieve their shins. They laugh at their shins; they read them in the dark. They grieve the seventh tier. So the power left every rim on the plateau as sharp as broken shell, and went instead to eleven places in the wet quarters where the drop is long and the basin below it shallow, and over sixty years guided a seep across each one, a finger's width at a time, until the water at the bottom was deep enough to break a fall. No one noticed. Terraces deepen. That is what terraces do.

*Give fire. Give wood. Give metal.* Refused entirely, and with something close to horror once it had thought it through. A forge on the Shelf is a hammer, and a hammer is a wall built in a week, and a wall built in a week is the end of the only art the plateau has. They do not lack tools. They have declined tools, in the way that water declines to fall here and sheets instead. It did make the peat coal in the stone cup keep a little better — the banking, the draw, the way the ash sits. Their own gesture, slightly more generous. Never a new gesture.

*Stop the drift. Fix the water. Make the city permanent.* This was the temptation that shamed it most, afterwards, because it had held it seriously for most of a century. Permanence is what powers give. It is the standard gift. And it would have killed the place inside four hundred years, because a city that cannot move has no wet edge, only a dry one, and would have spent those four centuries dissolving with its arms pinned. It learned this from them, not from itself. It is the single most useful thing it has ever been taught by anybody.

*Prevent the endings.* This it did do, and this is where its line is least clean, and it says so. It intervened against erasure and not against hardship: three times against a sickness that came down with the geese and would have taken the whole up-gradient population inside two years; twice, at the scale of centuries, against a shift in the high cloud that would have unmade the recharge and dried the tableland to bone. It cannot defend the boundary philosophically. It can only say that it declined to spare them their lives and consented to spare them their extinction, and that it has re-examined this every hundred years and has not yet found a better place to stand.

## The bird

When it wanted to be among them it did not need a disguise, because the plateau already had a category for it.

A single tall grey bird stalks the terraces in the wet quarters, unbothered by people, and there is a custom of not looking directly at it. That custom is a door. The power went through it. For long stretches it simply stood in the terrace films with the water over its feet, being not-looked-at, which is the most courteous form of presence anyone has ever offered it and the only one it has ever fully relaxed into.

Sometimes it wore a body: small, long in the trunk, barrel-chested, wrinkled white at the sole, and it went barefoot like everyone and barked its shins on the rims like everyone and let the scars stay. Once, in a dry quarter where the roofs were going, an old midwife took its jaw in her hand without asking and looked in its mouth for a while.

*Your teeth are from no water*, she said, at half a metre, and let go, and was not tactful about it, and added: *You stand at nowhere.*

There was no insult in it. Their grammar simply cannot make a sentence for a thing that has no position in a gradient; she was not accusing it, she was reporting a difficulty. It has thought about that remark for four thousand years. Of everything on the Shelf — the lace windows, the singing spoons, the fog at the third tier — the thing it has actually envied is the grammar: that here a person cannot be said to exist apart from a place between the water and the ruin. It did not wish to become mortal. It took the sentence as a discipline instead, and set about earning a way to finish it.

And once it stood in the doorway of a man whose cup was heavy — who was not ill and was not unloved, only alone, which the language does not quite distinguish — for the whole of the lid-closing hour, while he drank chalky water and said very little to it. It did not scrape his cup for him. A cup scraped by someone else is a lie about a life. It only declined, for one hour, to let him drink alone, and he told a neighbour afterwards that the grey bird had come and he had not looked at it directly.

## The north

Here is the thing none of them can see, because it lies beyond every horizon anyone has walked to and back from, and beyond the far end of any story.

The city moves a metre a year, upslope, forever. But the tableland is not forever. Some four thousand years north of the present wet edge the limestone thins over a bed of dark impermeable rock, and there the water table has no seam to weep from; the ground goes dry and stays dry, and beyond it the plateau breaks off into a scarp with no lime in it at all. The city is walking, at the pace of a fingernail, toward a shore.
