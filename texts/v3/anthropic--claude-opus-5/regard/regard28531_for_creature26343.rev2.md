# What the Power Did

## I. The First Four Hundred Years

It does nothing, because it has not yet learned how slow it has to be.

This is the first thing it works out, standing — if that is the word — in the dust between two mound-hills: that in the Underplain, speed is a category, and the category is *hunting*. The hush moves at four hundred metres a second. Nothing else does. Any arrival, any change, any help delivered at the pace at which help is normally delivered, would be read correctly by every nerve in the basin as an approach, and the plain would go rigid in front of it for eighty kilometres, and every animal in that rigid country would spend water it could not replace on being afraid of a benevolence.

So its first act of care is to become undetectable. No heat. No mass. No pressure anomaly, no warm patch, nothing in the bands the swifts use and nothing in the bands they don't. It does not want to be a thing that is noticed and then found harmless. It wants never to have been an event.

Then it waits, and listens, and does not touch anything, for about the length of time it takes a mound wall to thicken by four hands.

## II. What It Finds

It finds, first, that nothing here is in distress. This surprises it, and it distrusts the surprise, and checks again for a long time, and the finding holds. The Underplain is not a wound. It is a solved problem — solved badly, solved at enormous cost, solved by the deaths of ninety-nine million, nine hundred and ninety-nine thousand alates out of every hundred million, but *solved*, meaning stable, meaning it has held, meaning the thing has been running for longer than the power has been paying attention to anything.

You do not repair a solution. You can only replace it with your own, and yours will be younger.

It finds, second, that the basin has exactly one thought.

The hush is a genuine signal. It is generated collectively, propagated at the speed of one animal noticing another animal noticing, integrated across four hundred kilometres of hard floor and hard ceiling, and acted upon by every nervous system present, including the ones that have no ears and read it in the airflow. It is coherent. It is fast. It carries real information about the world. By any definition the power is willing to use, the hush is the closest thing to cognition this place has ever produced.

And its content, every time, in every instance, for ninety million years, is: *something is coming and there is nowhere to go.*

The power sits with that for a while.

## III. What It Refuses

It could bring the river back. It has the reach. It runs the model anyway, out of a kind of discipline, and watches: water arriving in a system where water is a possession and not a feature; the cisterns going worthless overnight and the colonies that built their entire social physiology around them destroyed by abundance; the pale country drowned in a week because nothing in it has ever evolved the ability to shed; the mat — eight thousand years, a single individual, the largest living thing in the basin — rotted from the inside in a season. It models a gift of water as what it is, which is a flood.

It refuses.

It could end the walkers. Thirty to fifty of them, two tonnes each, near-blind, three hundred years old and past decline, following a gradient to a river that dispersed before there were spines. From outside this looks unbearable. The power examines the animals themselves, carefully, for something answering to unbearable, and does not find it. They are not in pain. They are not lost, in the sense of knowing they are lost. They walk, and eat badly, and twice a decade they stand together for a few hours, touching, and then go on in opposite directions. To end them would be to kill something for the crime of looking sad to a stranger.

It refuses.

It could give them back their fertility. It thinks about this longer than about anything else, and declines, on the grounds that it does not know what it would be making more of. More walkers on a floor that lies to them. A lineage extended into a country that has nothing at the bottom of its slope. You do not hand a being a longer road because you cannot bear to watch the road end.

It could cull the quiets — two hundred animals, eleven kills a year each — and it runs that too, and watches the harrows go from four hundred kilograms of budgeted patience to a population that overruns the crust, and the crust that takes a hundred and forty years to regrow not regrowing, and the pale country going bare, and the mat losing its substrate, and the basin becoming, over nine hundred years, three hundred kilometres of clean stone.

It could stop the border wars. Eleven days, two million dead, a line that moves less than the length of a room. It works out that the wars are the only thing holding the frontier where it is, and that the frontier is why the foraging radii still fit, and that a peace imposed from above would be a demographic event with a delay fuse of roughly four centuries.

It could save the alates. Four hours of eyes, a hundred million rising into the one bright column in a grey world, and the bats who have *been waiting for six days*. It cannot find a way to save them that does not starve the ceiling, or thin the mat beneath the ellipse, which has been measurably thicker for a thousand years on account of them.

And it could wake the mat.

This is the cruellest of the available kindnesses and the one that takes the most steadiness to put down. The mat is continuous, it is old, it moves resources in directions that correlate with where things are about to die, and it is the only resident with anything like memory. It would take very little to make that memory into a self. The power holds the possibility up and looks at what is actually written in there — eight thousand years of a single sentence, *where the food has been* — and understands that to grant consciousness to that archive would be to create a mind whose entire contents are a list, and whose first experience would be of having been a list for eight thousand years without knowing it.

It refuses. It does not congratulate itself. It notes that refusing was close.

## IV. What It Does

Four things, over about eleven thousand years, none of which anything in the basin experiences as an event.

**The seep.** Above the basin, out in the dry country where water is an ordinary feature and pouring breaks no local rule, there are four remaining sources, and the fourth is failing. The power does not add a fifth. It goes up into the rock above and rearranges a few hundred cubic metres of fractured strata so that the fourth keeps producing at the rate it produced a thousand years ago — no more, and no faster than it was dying. This is not a gift. It is a refusal to let a subtraction continue. The bats fly forty kilometres instead of the sixty they were about to be flying, and nothing in the Underplain registers anything at all, which is the point.

**The face.** In the ninth garden the ceiling shifted, some centuries back, and the ellipse has been creeping off the green at a rate of a few centimetres a year, and the garden is going dark — with its metallic beetles, its prehensile-tailed climbers, its trunks studded and veined with six hundred years of jammed pebbles, a whole unrepeatable answer to the question of what to do with a hole in a roof. The power goes up into the chimney and polishes one limestone face. Not a mirror; nothing that would ever be mistaken for made. A slightly smoother wall, of a kind that occurs. The work takes a little over a century at dust-scale, and when it is done the column comes down at a fractionally different angle and the ellipse stops moving away and drifts back across the talus, and the trees, which have been leaning for two hundred years, straighten over the next forty.

**The regrade.** This is the long one.

The walkers do not measure the floor. They *compare* it. At every junction in the trough system their pads weigh one branch against the other and they take the one that falls, and they have no capacity whatever to integrate a route, to total up a day, to notice that they have been rising. So the power spends four thousand years moving calcite flour at the rate of natural drift, junction by junction, across nine hundred kilometres of braided gutter, until the network has become a closed circuit in which every junction offers a descent and the regained height is spread out along the long reaches where there is no choice to make, a few centimetres per kilometre, far below the threshold of a foot that is comparing rather than measuring.

It does not lead them to water. There is no water. It leads them downhill forever, which is the thing they actually want, and which is the only thing in the whole basin that was being withheld from them by an accident of erosion rather than by necessity.

And it bends the circuit so the routes cross. Not once a decade. Often. So that the last thirty or forty of them, over whatever centuries they have left, walk a floor that keeps handing them each other, and stand together for a few hours, touching, and go on, and meet again before the year is out.

It has occurred to the power that animals which meet often may do more than touch. It has decided not to look into this for four hundred years. When it looks, it wants to find out, rather than to have arranged.

**The four hundred metres.** Above the eleventh garden a roof panel is failing and will let go. The power cannot hold a ceiling up forever and has worked out that it should not try; new holes are how this place makes cities, and a basin in which the roof never falls is a basin that stops. So it does not prevent the collapse. It moves it. Four hundred metres west, onto bare pavement, where a hundred tonnes of surface soil will come down into the dim and the mat will have it colonized inside twenty years and there will be a new garden and, in ten generations — *immediately* — a new city under it.

The eleventh garden keeps its trunks.

## V. One Exception

In a guano cone in the north of the basin there is a quiet whose pups have been loud, and who has lost three litters in nine years to that fact, and whom the power has been watching for longer than it intended to.

It goes into the cone and rearranges the interior of one chamber — a bend, a baffle, a dead-ended gallery of the kind that forms in a settling cone all the time — so that sound made inside it does not get out.

One den. One animal. It does not do this again.

The rule it writes for itself afterward is short: *one is a rounding error; two is a policy.* A benevolence that cannot make a single exception is not wise, only frightened. A benevolence that makes a second one has stopped caring about the place and started caring about how it feels to help.

## VI. The Chord

It permits itself exactly one thing that has no purpose.

Forty throats connect the basin to the sky, and every pressure change in the upper world plays them, and the notes beat against each other and throb in the sternum. The power has listened to this for eleven thousand years and has known almost from the beginning that a few hands' widths of stone taken out of nine of the narrower chimneys would bring the whole system into tune.

The objection is obvious and it is the reason it waited. A large beautiful sound is a large masking sound, and the hush is the only warning anything here gets, and beauty that gets things eaten is not beauty.

So it finds the window. During the exodus — ninety minutes, dusk and dawn, when the spiralling column goes up through the shafts and the noise passes out of deafening into something structural, when the dust lifts off the floor from vibration alone and no silence anywhere could be heard by anything — the warning system is already unusable. Nothing is lost during the exodus, because during the exodus there is nothing to lose.

The power widens the nine chimneys by a hand's width each, over three centuries, at the rate limestone goes.

Now, twice a day, for ninety minutes, when the plain is a drum played from inside, the roof sounds one chord. Nothing hears it as music. The builders read it as a change in their own amplitude and thicken the intake spires in response, and in ten thousand years the mounds will be tuned to it, and the cities will have become, by pure compulsion iterated, instruments — which is the same thing that happened to the pebbles and the trunks, and the same thing that happened to the dead and the walls.

## VII. The Archive

Then it takes up the work it will be doing from now on, which is reading the floor.

The dust takes a footprint perfectly and holds it for centuries. There is no rain and almost no wind at floor level, and so the basin has been keeping a complete and meticulous record of itself for a very long time and has never had a reader. The mat remembers only where the food was. Nothing else remembers anything.

So the power reads it. Every print in every dune. The walkers' routes, eleven thousand years of them, the same handful of feet, which trough in which century, which two stood together in which year. The old prints of things that are gone, the ones that were laid down when the mounds were young, whose bodies are in the masonry now and whose names were never anything. Where each of the two hundred quiets walked on each of its eleven annual arrivals. The exact outline of every harrow that lay down and did not get up and was into the mat within nine days.

It is not a useful project. Nothing acts on it. It is simply that a place has been writing continuously for ninety million years and it seemed wrong to the power that nobody had ever been on the other end of that.

## VIII. The One It Speaks To

There is a being standing in the dust between two mound-hills: forty-odd litres, uncontained, radiating heat, making noise in a band nothing uses, leaving prints of a shape that has no entry anywhere in the ledger.

It is the only thing in three hundred kilometres that can be addressed. It is also the only thing in three hundred kilometres capable of the specific disease of wanting to fix this, and therefore the only thing here that can be *relieved*.

The power does not speak. Speech is a fast event and this is not a place for them, and anyway there is nothing it could say over the noise, and anyway the being would take an answer from the ceiling as the most important thing that had ever happened to it and would stop seeing the floor.

What it does instead is arrange the day.

It bends the visitor's route — a rise in the pavement here, a drift of dust there, the easier way west instead of north — so that on the ninth morning they come over a low dune at the edge of a trough at the hour when four of the walkers converge. Two tonnes each, pale, near-blind, coming from four directions along a gradient that has been quietly redesigned to hand them to one another, and they stand together in the grey-gold spill at the edge of an ellipse, touching, for four hours, in a noise like standing inside gravel.

The visitor watches the whole of it, and does not know that this is now ordinary, that it will be ordinary for as long as any of them lasts, that the floor was made to do it, that anyone made the floor do anything. There is no way to tell them and no reason to. They will carry it out as a thing they saw.

Where they slept the night before, the mat has come up: a pale bloom, cream-coloured, in the rough outline of a resting body, with the darker double-smudge where the hands were folded under the head. Half a litre of exhalation, a wealth it has no other source for. The power leaves it entirely alone. It will be legible in a century. The floor will keep the prints considerably longer, and there is now, at last, someone who can read them.

## IX. The Keeping

Then the power stays.

Not as a presence — it never becomes one, not once, not in eleven thousand years or in the eleven thousand after. It stays the way the cool stays, unremarkably, at the temperature of deep rock, noticed by nothing because it never changes.

There will be another collapse. It will not stop it; it will choose the four hundred metres. There will be a century when the fourth seep tries to fail again and it will go up and rearrange the fractured strata again, and again after that, for as long as there is rock to work with. There will be a decade when the cisterns hang slack and wrinkled and are eaten, and it will not intervene, because that is the system running, not the system breaking, and it has learned the difference and the difference is the whole of what it knows.

And when the last walker stops — in the ninth decade of some century, in a trough, on a gradient that fell away in front of it to the end — the power will be the only thing in the basin that registers it as anything other than nine days' notice to the mat.

That is the job. The place cannot know it was visited, or tended, or loved; it has no organ for it and never will, and to give it one would be the last and worst of the temptations. So the power holds that part itself, which is the only part it was ever able to give: that this happened, that it was here, that all of it — the eleven-millimetre dead laid end to end, the pebbles in the trunks, the hundred million going up into the light for four hours with eyes — was witnessed by something that wished it well and knew enough to keep its hands still.

The floor takes the footprints and will keep them.

Something, now, is keeping the floor.
