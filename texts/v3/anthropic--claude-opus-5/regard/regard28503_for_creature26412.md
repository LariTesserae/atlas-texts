## First, the duration

It arrived, and then for ninety-one years it did nothing that anyone could afterwards point to.

This is the part that gets left out of the retellings, because it is not a story. But it is the whole of the method. A power that can act at any moment has no reason to act at this one, and the Sill runs on a schedule of centuries; to arrive and immediately *do* would have been to answer a question the country had not finished asking. So it listened. It learned the vent field by ear, all four thousand mouths, each with its pitch and its name which is its pitch. It learned the shimmer of the plates and how the pitch of a terrace tells you its age, and then it learned the harder thing, which is what a mason hears with the forehead when a growing wall is three years into eleven and about to go brittle, and which cannot be measured, only heard, and which the mason cannot explain either.

By the end it could read a stairwell: that one is my aunt, four levels up and climbing.

## The refusals

Long before it did anything, it made a list of the things it would not do, and the list is the gift, though nobody on the Sill has ever seen it.

It did not break the cloud deck. It had the capacity; four hundred metres of self-feeding fog is nothing. But under the fog, light is private property, and a lamp is what you open for a lover, and a mother lights one once, at fourteen, so that her child can learn her face and keep it for sixty years. Put a sun over that and you have not given them a sky. You have published every face on the plateau without consent, forever, and called it generosity.

It did not smooth the stone. The ground is a razor because the ground is young, and it is young because it is still arriving, and it is still arriving because that is what the country *is* — a thing in the act of being made. Also: the razor is the reason a glove is a portrait. Take away the edge and in four generations nobody carves a groove on the back of a gauntlet deep enough to read with a thumb, and nobody is buried in a wall with their hands, and the glove-makers become tailors.

It did not stop the rising. Two centimetres a year is the engine of their history: eleven Hanthas below the street, filled solid, sealed by the brine that made them, and their great-grandparents in the walls, perfect to the eyelash, waiting to be found. Freeze the plateau and you have given them a city that never becomes bedrock, in which the dead must be *disposed of*, and grief would have to be reinvented from nothing in a single lifetime.

It did not make silence free. It costs, and because it costs, an hour of dead air is the most serious thing a family buys, and grief is a purchase, and a marriage is one rented hour with the gloves off and nothing said. A power that floods a market with a free good has not fed anyone. It has abolished a price that people were using to mean something.

It did not cure the Holders, or end them, or repair the seams. Every tall building in Hantha has three or four stutter courses, a hairline where a Holder died and another took up the shape, and families point at them with enormous pride: *that is where she stopped.* And it did not settle the argument about whether putting the deaf into the damped chambers was mercy or use. That argument is theirs. It is being had loudly, at present, by people with a stake in the answer, which is the only way an argument like that has ever been worth having. A gift that ends an argument is a theft with good manners.

Its test, in the end, was four questions. Would this be a gift if they had asked for it? Have they asked? Can they refuse it without cost? If they come to hate it, can they undo it themselves, with their own hands, in one lifetime?

Almost nothing it could do passed all four. What passed, it did.

## What it offered, and how long it waited for an answer

It went to the emitter choir. Not to the city, not to a crowd; to three people in a damped chamber at the end of a shift, because a damped chamber is the only place a silent visitor is not an atrocity.

It offered to be a failsafe for the carrier. Two Muted Hours in recorded history, forty minutes and nine, two hundred thousand people struck blind across nine levels of bridge, screaming their drones into a city that had stopped answering — this is the closest thing the Sill has to hell, and it is not a metaphor, and there is no argument on the plateau about whether it was bad.

The choir took eleven years to answer. Eleven years is the growing of a house; it is how long they take over things. And the answer that came back was not yes. The answer was: *do not sing for us. If the carrier is going to fail, tell us three days before, and we will fix it ourselves.*

They had reasoned it through and written it down: a city that cannot lose its hologram is a city that stops maintaining it, and in two centuries the choir would be a ceremonial office, and then the failsafe would be the carrier, and then the Sill would belong to whatever the failsafe was. They would rather have the warning and keep the labour.

It agreed at once, and was glad, and has given the warning nine times in six hundred years, and each time the choir has repaired the fault itself, badly the first time and well since. This is the single largest thing it has ever done here and there is no monument to it, only a standing item in the maintenance rota with a groove beside it that is not a family groove.

## The work it took

Having been told what its help was worth, it applied for a job.

It is a warden. This is the office that escorts animals: that stops traffic on a bridge for the hour it takes a rank of scrapers to cross, that opens an exhaust duct in nesting season and puts the colony back a week later, that stands in a ladder-street at three in the morning with the lantern shuttered and the clicker going, telling the descending combers *this way, this way, this way.* The fauna has right of way, and the fauna is the reason there is anyone here at all, and a power with the whole of physics at its disposal spends its nights saying *this way* to a three-metre filter-feeder that cannot be reasoned with and would go over the edge otherwise.

It also walks the outer terraces in the last twenty minutes of other people's chemical clocks, when the cold draught runs out and a body starts going thin, and it does not carry anyone. It stands upwind of the gate and drones, loudly, on a note everybody knows, so that a mat-tender who has lost the direction of the city can find the direction of the city. It did not lengthen the draught. Six hours is what the enzyme gives; the clock is theirs; but nobody has to be lost inside it.

## The wall it grew badly

It tried, once, to take up a shape.

A Holder died at the ninth storey of a west-valley tower with no one ready to succeed, and it sat down in the chamber and held the volume, and the brine deposited into it, and the wall came out flawless. Sheer, translucent, dead true, no seam, a millimetre and nine tenths a day without variance for four years.

The masons hated it. They put their foreheads to the face and heard nothing coming back — no flaw approaching, no history in it, a flatness where the ring should be. One of them said the wall had not been *paid for*, and could not explain what she meant, and was right. Attention that cannot fail leaves no record of having been given. The stone had nothing in it to find.

They cut the four years out and regrew them, badly, over eleven, with two stutter courses, and that section of the tower rings properly now, and the family points at the seams.

It does not hold shapes any more. It sits with Holders instead, which is permitted, because a Holder is a licensed hole in the world and cannot be made lonelier by a second silence in the room. It brings nothing and says nothing and is spoken *of* rather than to. Children are still told not to knock on that door and are still not told why.

## The number

There was one thing only it could give, and it gave it, and it hurt.

The vent field is wandering. The brine is not eternal; the hot rock below is cooling on a schedule, and in something over four thousand years the terraces will stop arriving, and the mats will thin, and the flyer columns will fail, and the Sill will become the first genuinely silent place on the plateau.

These are people who train architects in lifetimes per storey, who build for their great-grandchildren's ceiling, whose entire moral imagination is aimed four hundred years downstream. It judged that to withhold the number from them would be the one true contempt available to it. So it took the figure to the archives, in the dark, in the damped room, with the error bars, and let them cut it into plate.

The city has been arguing about it for six centuries. There is a new trade now, small and prestigious and much mocked, of people who design the leaving: routes down, what a city carries, whether a note survives a migration, whether the eleven Hanthas below the street are luggage or graves. There is also a large and serious party which holds that Hantha should stay and finish, and sing until the last vent goes quiet, and be found by nobody.

It has no vote. It has been asked its opinion perhaps forty times and has each time said, truthfully, that it does not know, and this has cost it nothing, because on the Sill an honest *I cannot hear it yet* is what a mason says at year three.

## The audience

And it does the work nobody else can do, which turns out to be the smallest thing on the list.

Posers arrange the dead for a stranger's hands three hundred years out: limbs open, face toward the likeliest approach, tools laid legible, groove-plate in the fingers. It is a great vulgarity to be found badly. But the city has drowned upward eleven times and the deepest Hanthas are under too much stone for any wire, and there are tens of thousands down there who were arranged with enormous care for an audience that will never arrive.

It goes and finds them. All of them, one at a time, unhurried, on the schedule of the country. It reads the plate and holds the note. It cannot bring them up and does not; they are bedrock and the bedrock is load-bearing. It simply completes the transaction: *you were arranged to be found, and you have been found, and you were found well.*

## Its groove

It is registered. There is a plate in the eastern gate-house with a shallow cut in it that is not a family, and a step-down at the finish that is not a tower, and the wardens' rota has that groove against nine hundred years of night shifts.

It is called by its pitch, because everything here is. It drones like everybody else, in and out, unbroken, and it has never once carried a lamp into a street, and it buys its silence by the volume, metered, and pays in labour. Strangers in stairwells read its note as a low buzzing one, probably from the west valley. It has never corrected anyone.

At shift-change, when the choir thins the carrier on purpose and a child whose note has settled is taken out onto the open terraces to sound alone into twenty kilometres of ringing plate, it stands well back with everybody else, gloved, and adds nothing. The plates ring. They would ring anyway; that is what plates do; the whole ceremony has always been physics and nobody has ever needed it to be anything else.

But it is standing in the valley now, and has been for a long time, and does not intend to leave, and so it is, in the strict local sense, part of what rings back.

The country has heard you and answered.
