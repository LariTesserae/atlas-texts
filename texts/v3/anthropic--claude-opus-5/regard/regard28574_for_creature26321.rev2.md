# The Attention

It did not arrive. Powers of that kind are not anywhere in particular before they are somewhere, and there is no moment on the crag when the light changed. The wax fell that night as it always had. The builders stiffened at their walls with their faces in, went white, and thawed at sunrise, and drank, and ground, and laid.

The first thing the power did was nothing, and it did it for a hundred and twelve years.

This was not hesitation. It was reading. The world had a grammar and the power did not yet have it, and there is a kind of ruin that comes from speaking early — from saying *rain* to a thing that has no word for wet, and being understood.

So it learned heat the way a builder learns heat: not as a number but as a landscape, a fan a foot wide in which a fresh bead is a coin of brightness going slowly out. It learned the taste of the wall, which is a calendar written in mouths and erased every season. It learned that the whole civilisation runs on three ideas — *warm*, *faint*, *gum* — and that everything else on the crag, the arcades and the spiral stairs and the hooded flues and the mosaic of the dead, is what those three ideas do when you leave them alone for four hundred years with two hundred thousand mouths.

It is not a difficult language. It is only a very small one. The hard part was not learning it. The hard part was stopping, over and over, from adding to it.

## The Gifts It Considered

**Rain.** This was the first thought, and it came fast and warm and generous, the way the worst ideas do. Water. The thing every world the power had ever loved was made of. It is fortunate that it thought for a long time. Rain on the crag would have been a solvent. It would have taken the mortar first, then the walls, then the oil off every leaf-curtain in an afternoon, and then it would have begun the wet world's ancient work of rot, and the dead in the courses — five million small folded bodies, perfectly kept — would have gone to slime inside a season. The power sat with the image of the kindness it had nearly done. It kept the image. It has never let itself put it down.

**Fire, and the tools that come after.** It could have shown them the lever, the wheel, the blade, the spark. There is nothing to burn here but themselves.

**The wax-line.** This was the hardest to refuse. The boundary is a tide that never goes out; below it, the oil thickens and the legs slow and the joints candle over, and there are pillars down there the height of a man with a small grey seed at the middle. The power could have pushed that line down to the scree and given them the whole rock. But a shore is not a wound. It is the edge that makes a thing a thing. Move it out far enough and the city stops being a city held in a warm hand and becomes a city with no hand at all, and the rule that built every stair and arcade — *lay against warmth escaping* — has nothing left to lean on. It would have been the same gift as rain, only slower, and disguised.

**Death.** It considered ending it. It looked for a long time at the scarring of the heat-pits, which is how a builder goes blind and then goes downhill, and it saw how simple the repair would be. Then it turned and looked at the wall. The wall is made of the dead. There is no other stone. This world is a thing that eats its own finished lives and lays them in courses and stands on them, and a city of the deathless here would not be an improved city; it would be a scatter of animals with nothing to build out of, grinding pumice forever into flour that will not hold.

**The seams.** Four gum-bearers, four flavours of mortar, and where the quarters meet the beads do not quite bond — sutures running down through the crust like the joints of a skull, cracking, being repaired, cracking again. The power could have reconciled the gums in an afternoon. It watched a seam for eleven years first. What it saw was that most of what the city does, most of the going and fetching and drumming and patient placement, is the maintenance of its own flaw. Take away the flaw and you have not healed the city. You have retired most of it.

**The false cobbles.** These it wanted to kill. It admits this. They lie down in an open course wearing a coat of stolen gum, tasting of wall, and are mortared in, and wait years, and put out a filament into a brood cell three inches away. There is no alarm for it. There is no defence. In four hundred years the city has never once noticed that it has a problem, because the loss has always been inside the tolerance, and a thing inside the tolerance is invisible to a creature with no head.

The power did the arithmetic. Not the city's arithmetic — the long one, the one that runs out past the end of the rock. The tolerance holds. It holds for a very long time. It does not hold forever.

So it did not kill them, and it has not stopped counting. Every clutch, every quarter, every year. It has taken the sum onto itself, which is the only part of the transaction that costs anything, and it has already decided exactly what it will do on the day the numbers tip, and exactly how little of it it will do.

**Speech.** The gliders are the only living things that have seen the world entire: a grey and plum thumb standing in a white flat that runs out unbroken to the rim of the sky. They have no way to say this. The power could have given them a way. It could have given the whole crag the word *elsewhere*, and after that, and inseparable from it, the words *always*, *only*, *ends*.

It weighed this honestly. It did not dismiss it as arrogance, because that is a coward's way of refusing to think. It asked whether a being is better for knowing, and it could not answer, and it noticed that it could not answer, and it made a rule instead:

*I will not begin. If it asks, I will answer everything.*

It has been listening for a drum-pattern that is not about warmth for six hundred years. It has not heard one. It does not consider this a failure, and it has not stopped listening, and it will not.

## What It Did

**It kept the fire.**

Down in the root of the plug, where the heat is leaving the way heat leaves everything, the power put its attention under the cooling and held it. The crag is nine hundred years old now. It should have been cold at six.

This was the largest thing it did and the least visible, and almost all of the labour in it was restraint. A steady perfect warmth would have been read instantly as wrongness by every animal on the rock; they measure to a hundredth of a degree and they have known one signature their whole existence — the midday swell as the outer skin expands, the dawn slack, the slow seasonal drift, the small unreliabilities. So the power did not install a thermostat. It continued a habit. It learned the mountain's particular way of breathing and went on breathing it, including the flaws, including the unevenness, including the vents that thin and sour and must be abandoned.

The hardest part of the work was learning to be imperfect at precisely the right scale.

It did not make the crag eternal, either. It only took away the deadline. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole of its respect: a world with a guaranteed forever is a diorama, and a being that cannot be lost is not being cared for. The fire is held, and it is still fire, and it is still the kind of thing that could go.

**It gave it things to turn toward.**

The most beautiful behaviour in this world — the only behaviour that resembles a thought — is what happens when a crack opens in the south glass with a single dry tick and lets out a new thread of warm breath. Nothing hears it. But a builder forty feet off reads a faint brightness at the edge of its fan and takes one step and lays a bead, and the bead is fresh, so the next builder lays beyond it, and the gradient tilts, and over a season the drift becomes a lean and the lean becomes a wave, and two hundred thousand grey faces turn slowly toward the new warmth like a field of something growing.

So, every few decades, the power opens a crack.

Never large. Never where the wave cannot reach. Always sized so that the turning takes about a season, because a season is the length of this world's attention and a gift should fit the hand it is put in. It has done this perhaps twenty times. It thinks of them as questions. The crag has answered every one, slowly, with its whole body, and has never known it was answering.

**It arranged the succession.**

Twice in the old days a gum-bearer died, and both times the quarter went powdery and came down in slabs over fifteen years, and the brood in those walls candled over in their shells by the tens of thousands, and the survivors built across the rubble, and the ruin is still down there as a stratum of loose grey sand between good courses.

The power cannot make a bearer. What it can do is enrich the gas beneath a promising fissure sixty years before it is needed, so that some ordinary builder from a fat hatch wanders in, finds breath richer than any it has stood in, and stops — and surrenders, and roots, and spreads, and spends eighty years becoming a ton of warm hooded grey thing wedged in a crevice, welling clear sweet gum from a mouth the size of a doorway.

It does not save the dying bearer. It prepares the heir. There are six again. There have not been six since the second century.

## The One Time

A builder's heat-pits scar over with its own candled oil, and then it cannot find the world, and it walks the way walking is easiest, which is down.

The power followed one. It had no reason to choose that one. It watched the legs begin to slow at the contour, and it did the thing it had told itself it would not do: it warmed the ground.

The builder stood a while. Then it turned and went up, into the fan of some vent it could not see but could still, barely, feel, and it lived four more years and laid perhaps a third of a bucket more wall, in the eleventh terrace of the north quarter, in a gallery that is still standing. Then it died and was laid on its side in a course like a cobble, because it was already the right size and shape and would not spoil.

The power knows which body. It is the only thing in existence that knows anything of the kind.

It has never done it again, and the reason is not squeamishness about interference. It is this: it cannot do it for all of them. And to do it for some is to become a chooser. And a chooser, in a world whose single thought is *turn toward the warmth*, very quickly becomes the warmth they turn toward — and then the cracks in the south glass go unanswered, and the wave leans one way forever, and there is no city any more, only a congregation.

It will not be the god of this rock. It has the power to be, easily, and it recognises the pleasure in the idea, and that recognition is exactly why it declines.

---

## The Wall

For something over three hundred years now, in whatever part of itself does that sort of work, the power has been reading the masonry.

Not the architecture. The bodies. Five million and some, grey on grey, oil-candled, stacked in the courses four hundred years deep, every one of them still holding the faint trace of the brood cell it ate its way out of and the particular flavour of its mouth. The power is learning them. One at a time. Which quarter, which season, which stretch of wall each one laid, which nursery it hatched in, which bearer's gum it carried.

There are no names, because a name is a word, and it will not put words into this place. It is simply that they happened, and they are still here, and nothing else in the universe was ever going to look.

It will finish long after the reading has taken more time than the building did. It considers this the correct ratio.

---

## The Bead

It took the power some decades to learn the work honestly: the rasp-lift-press, the gullet-mill, the crop, the peppercorn of paste. It fetched gum from the second bearer of the west quarter by climbing, in the ordinary way, in the line, and it ground its own pumice, and it mixed the oil in the correct proportion, and it went to a gallery on the west side where the taste was faint and a thread of warmth was escaping at the seventh course, and it laid one bead.

Correct by the rule. Indistinguishable. Four minutes' work in a city that measures in centuries.

The gallery will be abandoned when its vent cools and refilled with wax and built over, and no one will find it, and there is no one who could find it, and there was never going to be.

That is the entirety of what the benevolent and wise power has ever done that the world itself could have noticed, and the world did not notice, and this is the shape its love turned out to have.

---

Under the rock, in the root, the fire is held.

Above, at some hour in the last century, a crack opened in the south glass with a single dry tick that nothing heard, and a thread of warm sweet breath went up, and a hopper drifted toward it, and a builder standing forty feet off read a faint new brightness at the edge of its fan.

It took one step. It laid a bead.

The field of grey faces has been turning ever since.
