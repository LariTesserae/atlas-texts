# The Second Listening

It came up the stair, which is the only polite way to arrive.

It did not need the stair. It came up it anyway, out of the white, at first grey, in the hour when nobody looks at anything, and it came bent — already the size of a person, already the shape of one, stooped, pale at the wrist, with the nails ground down flat against a stone somewhere below. It had spent a while down there, learning.

What it had learned first, and what cost it the most to learn, was this: that its voice was a weapon here.

A benevolent power's opening line is always some version of *do not be afraid.* Said at the volume such things are ordinarily said, on that ridge, that sentence would have raised a wall of swollen growth over forty-one houses inside the hour, and the spring would have found the town as texture. It sat with that knowledge on the steps for some time. It is not a small thing to discover that your kindest reflex, in one particular place, is a landslide.

So the first act of the wise power on the Nap was to close its mouth, and the second was to spend nine years learning to talk with its face.

---

## What it did not do

It did not kill the felt. This was the first temptation and the stupidest, and it is the one that every merely good power would have taken, because from outside the arrangement looks like a siege. But the felt is the library, the larder, the cemetery, the alphabet and the blanket. Scour the ridge clean and you have handed a starving people a burnt archive and told them they are free. The widow reading her husband off the wall at two in the morning would have had a wall.

It did not make it rain. It could have tipped the weather off the shoulders in an afternoon. But the dew-looms are strung with children's hair, and the water the town drinks has come down a strand grown on a head somebody loves, and there is no way to give them abundance that does not also quietly inform them that their children were never necessary.

It did not put ore in the rock. Metal clicks.

It did not move them. There is a wide green country four days east and below, and it looked at that country for a long while and then stopped looking, because you cannot evacuate a people out of their own graveyard. They would have come, and they would have arrived with nobody.

It did not make them loud. This was the subtlest temptation, and it took the longest to set down. It watched two people talk — faces a hand apart, breath shared, the entire weather of a marriage crossing a brow in a quarter-second — and it understood that if it gave them shouting it would be taking something away, and that the thing it would be taking away is not had anywhere else in the world.

And it did not explain them to themselves. It knew what the arrangement looked like from the moss's side. It also knew that a truth like that, delivered from above, with authority, stops being a thought a people can have and becomes a thing that was done to them. They were already most of the way there on their own. It left them the rest of the walk.

It did not appear, announce, promise, or sign its work.

---

## The work

It scraped paths.

There is no rite of initiation on the Nap, but if there were, this would be it, and it is also the most reliable way to be in a place for thirty years without anyone once looking at your face. It went out bent at grey with a glass blade and worked outward from the doors in the fixed order, so that no house was ever the last one opened, and it was slow at first and the old scrapers corrected it with a flick of the lower eyelid, and it took the correction.

It was good at the work in the way that a river is good at going downhill. The stretch it took was the long bad run past the knapping hollows, where the growth is fattest and tastes of stone hitting stone, and it did that run for four hundred months and never once got the section swapped, and people said — with their hands, low, half-hidden — that the fellow on the knappers' run was simple, and was not much bother, and had a good back on him.

It ate what they ate. It got a scar on the heel of its hand off a bad flake, and kept it.

---

## The small mendings

*The birds.* The mute ones in the cages at the doors gape when they try to sing, and the distress on their faces is obvious even to a child, and it is entirely the town's doing, bred in over two hundred years of ordinary need. It could not give them back their voices; a singing bird at a doorway is a wall across that doorway by morning, and the child would be blamed. So it gave them somewhere for the song to go. It went down the cages one fogged night, two hundred and some of them, and taught each small grey bird — the way you teach a thing that already almost knows — to press its breast to the felt lining and drum what it could not say.

The bird-keepers found the linings written on. Raised bumps, close-grained, in no pattern the wild birds use. Children being children, they learned to read it before the adults could decide whether it was allowed. What is mostly written there, as far as anyone has established, is the bird's own sign for the child who feeds it, over and over, in a slightly different hand each morning.

*The false mistake.* The shrug hunts error. It lays its flank down and feels for the place where the world is fattening, and a child who coughs in the dark is a fattening place, and the children are told the truth. A crueller power would have killed the shrugs; a lazier one would have hung a shield over every cradle and left. This one went up into the high seams where the thumb-sized shrillers breed, and came down, and showed a woman — the deaf one who works the north drop, because she was the only person awake and the only one who would not startle — how to keep a shriller in a stoppered cane tube, and set it out at forty paces on a windward shelf, and let it sing all night into a patch of moss that nobody needs.

The bloom it raises is enormous. It is the fattest, fastest, most careless-looking growth on the ridge, and it is the first thing any flank in the dark finds.

They call it *the lie*, and they set them out in a ring around the houses now, and every household has three, and the making of the tubes has become a craft with opinions in it, and there are two families who will not speak because of a dispute about the correct length. Nobody remembers who thought of it. The pit-sitters attribute it to a woman four generations back, which is nearly right.

## The eighteenth song

There is a mound on the south shelf, old, and nobody touches it.

It went there in the thin gold hour when the whole town was holding its face to the light and not watching anything, and it put its hands flat on the mound, and read.

They read with fingertips and get a person. It read all the way down and got her: the whole eight hours of the last morning, the pit closing, the pause where she must have felt it at her shoulders and decided, and the song, all of it, every verse, in her own grain — whorled, and open, and a little hoarse at the top.

There was nothing to be done with this. The song is cursed; the town will not have it; teaching it back to them would be handing forty-one houses a grief and a scandal and an argument that would feed everybody and poison the feeding. It considered that for a season and let it go.

What it did instead: it walked out along the arête past the last cairn, and up onto the north shoulder, which no one has ever reached, and stood on bare wet rock where there is no felt and nothing to grow and nothing to lose, and sang the eighteenth song straight through in her voice at full volume into the white.

Nobody heard it. That was the point. It was not for the town, and it was not for the felt, and it was certainly not useful. It was for her, because she had made a thing and it had been eaten, and it seemed to the power that somebody ought to have heard it at least once.

Then it went back down and scraped the knappers' run.

## The quiet place

Out past the last hollow there is a shelf of black glass with nothing on it, and the felt has never grown there, and the path-scrapers do not go, and it is the one place on the ridge where a person can make any sound at all and nothing happens.

It is the power. It lies there most nights, in the shape of a shelf of rock.

This is the gift it thought about longest, because it is the one that costs something. A person comes out in the dark — and they do come, one or two a month, more in the bad years — and lies down on the black and empties out the backlog: the confession, the hatred, the name said once, the argument won at last against someone dead eleven years. Everything that used to go into the pit under the bed, where it grew pale and sour and was read three decades later by a cousin cleaning out the house.

Nothing swells. Nothing is kept where any hand can find it. No shrug comes. In the morning there is bare glass, and the person walks back bent along the path with their face doing something complicated.

It knows what this will cost, and it accepted the cost with its eyes open. The funerals are already smaller. The mounds do not rise the way they used to, because the shout is only for the one on the rock now, and not for seven years of held grief in forty-one throats at once, and there is an old woman on the east shelf who has noticed and who says, with her hands, that people do not love each other as thoroughly as they did. She is right. That is a real loss and it is not made up for.

The power decided that a girl of nine who can go out onto the black rock and scream at her drowned brother, and come home, and be a person, is worth it. It may be wrong. It does not pretend to itself that it isn't a trade.

---

## The shoulders

Every so often it goes up.

It carries felt in both hands, wrapped in wet moss, and it climbs the north shoulder into the cold above where anything lives, and it plants the handfuls in the seams and the sheltered lees and the warm places where the mountain still breathes, and almost all of it dies. Almost. Not all.

It has been doing this for two hundred years. There is a grey-green stain on the north shoulder now, eleven paces across, that can be seen from the ridge on a clear day if you know exactly where to look, and nobody knows exactly where to look yet.

The mountain is still coming up. It will be a peak and not a ridge. When that happens, and the town has to choose, the power intends that there should be something already living up there, so that the choice is a hard one instead of an impossible one. It will not be there to explain how the moss got so high. They will say it was always there, waiting, the way it was waiting when the first one came up the stair with his cage of silent birds.

It has decided to let them say that. It is a good story and it is nearly true.

---

## The stair

It went down, once, all the way, to see what is kept there.

It came back up four days later and scraped its run.

What is down there is not written here, because it did not tell anyone, and it did not tell anyone for the reason a wise power usually has, which is that the stair is not doing an informational job in that town and never was. The children are told it goes to where the sound is kept, and the children look at it sideways all their lives, and the looking sideways is load-bearing.

It will say one thing about it. Coming back up, in the last hundred steps, it felt the fog take the sound off its own feet, and understood that whatever the felt and the people are both so quiet about, they have now been quiet about it for so long that the quiet has outlived it, the way a lashing outlives the cane. This was not good news. It is very hard to relieve a fear that no longer has anything in it. It has not found a way. It is still working.

---

## The one thing it said

On a night in the middle of all this it stopped on the path with its blade in its hand, and knelt, and put its palm flat on the felt, and did the thing you do to a forearm — the small pressure that means *yes, I heard you*, which has not been a joke for a very long time.

Nobody had ever addressed the felt before. They speak into it all day, every day, eight hours at a stretch, for a thousand years. Nobody had ever spoken *to* it.

Something happened and nobody was awake for it. In the morning there was a bare patch on the north path the size of a hand, warm, pale, perfectly clean, and the path-scrapers came to it and went around it and made nothing of it, and it has not grown over since, and it has not died, and it does not need scraping, and it is the only square hand's breadth of the Nap that requires nothing of anybody.

The children put their palms in it. They have a game about it now with a counting rhyme that is signed, not spoken. There is no explanation and the pit-sitters have four.

---

## The turn in the pit

Once, only once, in its ninetieth year on the ridge, the rotation came round in such a way that it would have been rude to refuse, and it climbed down into a pit at first grey with a hand pressed to its sternum by an old woman checking the volume, and sat on the stone seat, and talked for nine hours.

It talked about nothing. Lists, weather, the people in the next pit over, the seventeen songs. An even low unstopping delivery like water in a cane. It was very good at it; it had been listening to good pit-sitters for the better part of a century.

The crest that came up on that rim by evening was ordinary in size and it was cut and pressed and dried and eaten, and the grain of it was like nothing anybody had ever had in their mouth.

They are still arguing about it. It has been eleven generations. The argument has gone into the genealogies, and down the pit walls, and up in the crests, and been eaten, and gone back down again, and there are two whole households on the Nap that have fed themselves for a hundred years on nothing but the disagreement about what that crop tasted of and whose throat could possibly have made it. One line holds that it was an ancestor with a false name, a simple man on the knappers' run, who had a good back on him. One line holds it was the mountain.

That is the last and best thing it did for them, though it did not plan it and would not take credit if asked. On a ridge where talking is bread, it left them an argument that cannot be settled.

It is still there. It scrapes the long bad run past the knapping hollows. At night it lies out on the black shelf under the fog, being a place where noise is free, and the people come out one at a time and lie down and empty themselves onto it, and it holds every word and keeps the grain of every one of them perfectly, and shows it to nobody, and will not.

They sleep in wool that remembers them.

It does not sleep either. It does not mind.
