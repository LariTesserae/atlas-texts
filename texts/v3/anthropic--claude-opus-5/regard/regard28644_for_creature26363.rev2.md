# The Attention

## How it came

It came from the north, across the flats, in the ordinary way, and it did not believe there was a town until it was inside one.

It could have arrived at the crown directly. It could have been in the deepest room under three floors of drowned centuries before the thought of going there had finished forming. Instead it walked eight hours through standing water cold enough to numb the ankles by noon, because a place is partly the approach to it, and because anything that arrives without an approach arrives at a different place than the one that exists.

At the foot of the mound it stopped and looked up at the white swelling and the mist standing over it like a held breath, and it understood immediately about a third of what was there, which is more than most visitors and much less than it needed.

So it stayed, and did nothing at all, for four years.

## What it learned first

It learned the language the slow way.

This was not necessary. It could have had the whole grammar in an instant — the clicks, the stops, the decorative vowels, the identity between the spoken word and the knocked word. But a language known instantly is a list. A language learned is a set of reasons. It wanted to know not what the five-note figure meant but *why there is only one signal that brings every adult out of doors at once*, and that is not in the grammar; that is in the floor.

So it learned the way the children do: lying down, from underneath, hearing four households eat.

By the end of the first year it could follow an argument about scraping-rights through two walls. By the end of the second it could tell, from the rasp alone, which of three hundred people was working a stair-tread, and whether they were angry. By the end of the third it had read the handprints on every jamb in the town, the deep crisp ones and the ones glazed to a soft dimple by a hundred years of film, and had the genealogy of the place by heart, including the six-generation line of Wet-Foot stock whose palms sit a head high and whose great-grandchildren still duck where there is nothing to duck under.

By the end of the fourth year it knew what it was looking at, and it was in trouble.

## The catalogue of things it did not do

Because a benevolent power that has not seriously wanted to do the wrong thing has not understood the situation, here is what it wanted to do.

**Stop the water.** It is the simplest act available and it would end the scraping forever. It would also end the warmth, in a country where for forty days' walk there is none, and the stone, and the blocks, and the pillars, and the word that means both *digging down* and *remembering*. It would leave three hundred people standing on a cooling lump in a marsh, holding paddles, with nothing to keep open.

**Slow the water.** Subtler, and worse, because it might have worked. Halve the deposition and you halve the labour; you free perhaps three hours a day for every adult on the mound. It ran this forward. In sixty years there are five hundred people on a mound with the same aperture, the same lanes, the same twelve places at the lip; there is surplus, and there is therefore, for the first time, something to fight over that cannot be settled by a scraping-schedule. It stopped running it forward and felt ashamed of how nearly it had done this out of tidiness.

**Move them.** There is good ground eleven days east. There is not a single thing there that they are.

**Give them metal.** They would take it gladly, and the cutters would use it at the lip, and the curd would come away in larger blocks, and the blocks would be laid slower because they are heavier, and the architecture that is a record of how fast a person can work before the material stops permitting it would become a record of something else. Perhaps that would be fine. It could not be sure it would be fine. It noticed that *I cannot be sure* is a reason to wait and not a reason to act, and that it had been about to treat it as the second.

**Widen the aperture.** Cut the lanes back, once, to twice their width, and no tall child is ever again quietly sorted outward into the cold work. It got as far as choosing where to start. Then it thought about the private map every adult here carries — the bend above the west terrace with your own thumbprint in it, the chimney-crawl where the stone is warm all night — and about the fact that these maps are mourned, which is to say loved, and that a town where nobody has lost a room is a town where nobody sends a child up with instructions and waits below, not looking at anybody. It did not conclude that the loss is good. It concluded that the loss is *theirs*, and that a power which removes a grief without being asked has not removed a grief, it has removed a person and installed a more comfortable one.

**Feed the ones without surface with its own hands.** It wanted this badly and for a whole winter could think of little else. It is the single cruelty on the mound that the stone does not require — the food left and not handed, understood by everyone involved to be worse than hunger. But it saw that if the visitor hands the bowl, then the handing is a thing that happens to strangers from outside, and the town is absolved of ever having to invent it.

**Heal the cutters.** Twelve people standing knee-deep for half a morning in water that takes the oil, then the skin, then what is under the skin. Hands at forty that cannot close and do not feel heat. It could have made the water kind. But the retired cutters sit in the warm lanes and are consulted about everything and are touched in passing exactly as the pillars are patted, and the whole theology of the place is in that gesture: *the stone begins taking you at the hands, it goes on all your life, and death is only where it becomes obvious.* Take the ruin out of those hands and the town loses the proof of its own central idea, which happens to be true.

It sat with that one a long time, and was not satisfied, and came back to it later, and did something.

**Teach the birds.** The pale wader stands too long in the shallows and finds its legs sheathed and setting, and whoever sees it first must kneel in cold water with a wooden mallet and free a panicking creature the height of a child, and this is considered luck, and the birds do not learn.

It could have taught them in an afternoon.

It considered that the rescue of the wader is the only wholly useless kindness on the mound: spent on something that gives nothing back, will not remember, and will be in the same place tomorrow. It is the town's single piece of evidence to itself that it is not merely an engine for keeping surfaces open. So the power left the birds exactly as stupid as it found them, and has knelt in the cold water more than once itself, in a form, unrecognised, and been thanked by a child, and has thought it was the best thing available anywhere that day.

## The test it settled on

It needed a rule, because wanting to help is not a method.

The rule it arrived at was this. Some pain here is a pillar in a doorway. It is made of the dead, it is load-bearing, and if you pull it out you bring down a house. And some pain is only growth on the stair-tread — no meaning in it, nothing resting on it, nobody's grandmother inside it — and a good neighbour scrapes that at dawn without being asked and without mentioning it.

The difficulty of the rule is that both look like white stone.

The power resolved that when it could not tell which it had, it would treat it as a pillar, and would accept that this policy would leave real suffering in place, and would not be allowed to feel serene about that. Restraint that costs nothing is only indifference with good manners.

## What it did

**The seam.** Under the crown, the cold freshwater seam that cuts across the hot vent — the accident that permits the one tree — was going to shift. In forty-one years it would have wandered a few fingers west into a softer bed, and the willow-thing would have died with its bark perfectly scraped, and the two households who have held that right longer than the town can count, who alternate by four days and check each other's work with the suspicion of centuries and are not friends, would have spent the following hundred years each knowing it was the other's fault.

The power went down into the dark and moved a small quantity of rock.

Nothing happened. Nothing has happened for a long time now. This was by a wide margin the largest thing it did, and it preserved a condition rather than granting a novelty, which it had come to think was the whole difference between help and interference.

**The rosettes.** In the ninth spring, the fat succulent rosette that had always kept to the dry southwest slope was found growing in three places near the lip, in the warm spill, which no one could account for.

A cutter called *the fourth vent*, whose hands had begun to go early and who was frightened, pulled one up out of bad temper and crushed it, and rubbed the sour juice into the cracks because there was nothing else to do with it, and it stung, and then it helped. A little. Not much. Enough.

There is now a greasing before the morning's work, and a small dispute about who owns the rosettes at the lip, and an eleven-year gain, on average, in the working life of a cutter — which is eleven more years of hands at the ends of arms that can still lift a bone saw, and then the same ruin afterward, slightly later, no less honoured. The elders are still ruined. There are simply more of them, and they are less often in pain at night.

The story the town tells is about a greedy woman and a dream. The power has never corrected it. This is the part it is proudest of: that the gift came into the world looking like weather, and was found rather than given, and the finding belongs to a woman with a name that means the fourth vent.

**Half a day of wind.** In the eleventh year it lengthened an autumn gale by about half a day.

Nothing grew. Nobody scraped. The whole town went up onto the roofs to be cold and idle and shout at each other across four streets like people in a country with room in it, and stayed up there into the dark, which they do not usually do.

On the west paving, where the spread scrapings dry, there was a man without surface — without name, fed in silence, the food left and not handed.

A child on a roof, being eleven and irreplaceable and unbearable about it, shouted down at him. Not kindly. Just loudly, because the air was carrying and everything that day was for shouting at. She used the only thing she had to call him by, which was where he was sitting.

He answered her.

There is no rule on the mound against being shouted at, because until that evening there had never been a day loud enough for the question to arise. It happens now most windy autumns. It undoes nothing; the sentence still runs, the doorway still narrows, the bowl is still set down and not handed. But once a year the nameless are called by the place they are sitting in and they answer, and the town has thought of some things to shout that are worse than nothing and some that are better.

The power supplied the wind. Everything else in that paragraph is theirs.

**The night with the listener.** In the deepest room, below three floors of drowned centuries, someone lies with an ear to the old stone and holds the whole town at once — the tapping in the walls, the rasp of the snails, the shifting of a bad wall four streets off, and under all of it the forty-beat pulse in the throat of the spring. They have no name, because they do not scrape. They are fed by every household in rotation. They are the only honoured nameless person in the town, and they are alone in a way that nobody else there has ever been alone, because everyone else is within earshot of everyone.

The power went down and lay beside the listener for one night and listened with them.

It said nothing. It did nothing to the pulse. The listener knew perfectly well that something was there, and did not ask, and never mentioned it to anyone, and slept afterward — eight hours at a stretch, for the first time in nineteen years, which the town noticed and ascribed to the weather.

It has gone back. Not often. When there is a new listener it goes down within the first year, so that each of them learns early that the listening is not done alone, and each of them decides privately what that means, and none of them has ever been wrong.

**What it decided in advance.** The pulse will change again. It has changed eleven times in living memory; nine meant nothing; twice it meant a season of scraping the wrong surfaces and the loss of a quarter of the lower lanes and a hunger that people still date things from.

The power will not steady the spring. The town's ability to read its own warning is the thing that will still be there in three hundred years, and a settlement that is saved from one famine by something outside itself has been made less able to survive every famine after.

But the fish are not load-bearing. In the year of the next change there will be an unaccountable number of blind pink fish in the warm channels, and the traps will come up heavy, and the old people will say that it happens sometimes, and it will not be enough, and it will be something.

## What it took

In the fourteenth year the power took a right.

There is a surface on the mound that no household holds, because nobody had noticed it needed holding: the shallow feed-channels that carry the thin steady overflow across the laying-out terraces, where the bodies go. In most years the water finds its own way. Perhaps twice in a lifetime — a hard dry spring, a wind that sits in the east for six weeks — a channel thins and wanders and a body does not set. It rots instead. It has happened. There are two stories about it that the town tells reluctantly and only to adults.

The power keeps those channels open.

It does the work at dawn and at dusk, with a short damp hissing stroke timed to a breath it does not need to take, and its stroke goes into the loose ragged unison of three hundred people exhaling against stone, and is not distinguishable, and is there.

Being a surface, it has a name in the local sense, though nobody uses it and nobody knows there is anybody to use it of. It is *the thin feed over the laying-out terrace*. What the town says is: the dead always set well here. It's a good terrace.

At the end of its first year of the right it pressed its palm flat to a door-jamb in a lane on the north side and held it there through a season, as one does, at the height it lives at. The print is shallow and slightly too large and sits between two belonging to a family that died out four generations ago. No one has found it. It is not there to be found. It was pressed for the reason all of them are pressed, which the power only understood by doing it: not so that the wall will remember the hand, but so that the hand will have been in the wall.

## What it found it had not brought

It had come, underneath everything, with one real gift in its hands, which was permanence. It had meant, in the end, to be the thing that remembers them — to hold the whole town whole, the handprints and the taps and the forty-beat pulse, so that on the day the lanes close and the roofs smooth over into lobes and fans and the water goes on entirely unbothered, laying down its film over everything they ever made, it would not be as though they had never been.

Then one night in a cold season it lay down in a strange room and felt a hum come up through the bedding and thought it was the spring.

It was not the spring. The spring has no tune.

Three hundred people with their mouths closed, pushing a note into the wall, and the wall giving it to the wall above, until the entire mound was very faintly ringing.

They already do it. They do it nightly. They do it with their palms in the jambs and their grandparents in the arches and their dead patted in passing and a word for digging that also means remembering. The one thing it had brought that was truly its own turned out to be the local industry.

So it put that down, and has not picked it up again, and considers the trip worth it for that discovery alone.

## From above

Near sunset in early spring: the flats a sheet of dull silver, the sky low and colourless and bright, the mound sitting in it like a loaf of white bread in a grey dish, the mist column bending east.

The rasp of the evening scraping starts terrace by terrace as the light goes. If you could separate one stroke out of it — you cannot; nobody can; the listener has tried — there is one more than there are people.

It has made no promises about the future, having none to make. The town may close. Ten years is all it would take. If that day comes, the power will be there for it: it will keep the thin feed running over the laying-out terrace to the end, so that the last of them sets as cleanly as the first, and there is nobody who lies down on the warm stone unattended. Not to prevent the closing. It is not for preventing. Only so that it is not done to them, but *with* them, in the local manner, by a neighbour holding a right.

And eight or so times a year, at dusk, a lean marsh-cat comes to the edge of the light and sits and does not come closer and is gone, and there is a name for it here that means roughly *the one who will not come in*, and a fish is left out, and the fish is always gone by morning, and everyone knows perfectly well what takes it.

Something sits beside the cat on those evenings. They do not discuss it. They were both given the same name by the same people before either of them arrived, and it is a good name, and it is more than either of them was owed.
