## It Came Down the Steps

It did not open the ground. It could have; there is nothing in seven meters of limestone that would have argued. It came down the chimney instead, sideways at the pinch, and put its hands where hands go, because arriving any other way would have been a noise.

At the fourth meter the air thickened, and it stopped, the way everyone stops.

The room teaches quiet within a minute of entry. It learned in less, and then discovered a poverty it had not anticipated: it had no breathing to hush. Nothing to lower. The keeping of the rule is supposed to cost you something — the swallowed cough, the held lungs, the careful placement of a heel — and it was going to be permitted to keep the rule for free.

So it took care instead in the only currencies the room reads. It cast no light. It shed no warmth into the column, where the moths would have read it as weather. It laid down no scent, struck no vibration into the fig-wood, moved no air the bats could mistake for an edge. By the room's own definition of silence — which is not about sound and never was — it became the first thing ever to enter and keep the rule perfectly.

There is a fern established on the topmost step.

It stepped over the fern.

## What It Was Looking For

It had come for the being who lived there, and it spent some time discovering that this was a harder sentence than it had sounded from outside.

The fig is one. That is not a figure of speech: one seed, one germination, one continuous body, eleven pillars of it holding up the sky of the place, a mask of scrub on the caliche and a cathedral underneath. A reasonable power would have stopped there and said *this is the being, the rest is its weather.*

But the room resolves at other scales too, and all of them hold. Set the focus wider and the chamber is a single slow animal that eats sunlight through a slot, drinks its own exhalation off the ceiling, digests its dead into the floor, and has been doing this continuously through several hundred thousand of its own heartbeats, each heartbeat being one nightly rain. Set it narrower and it is one thousand four hundred and some vertebrates, plus the uncountable invertebrate population, plus nineteen things under a ledge. Set it narrower still and it is a single black frog, forty days old, at a vent, grazing moss it will not have a mouth for much longer.

It took the census. The census failed, as the census always fails — not from ignorance, it had no ignorance, but because the number changed twice during the taking. It took it again. It stopped on the fourth attempt, not in frustration but because it had noticed it no longer wanted the number. It wanted the individuals.

So it did the longer thing. It knew them one at a time.

This is the part that cannot be summarized. It is only work: every gecko on the warm wall by the pattern of gold flecking in the iris, every frog by the interval of its drumming and the particular fern-fork that was its whole country, all two hundred and three bats by the smell of the shoulder their mothers checked, the whip scorpions, the files of snails, the ant highway in all nine of its abandoned routes, the wasps who are born in the dark and die in it and have never once been in the room. Nineteen salamanders. The oldest of them had been an adult, in that water, when the fig arrived as a seed in a bird's gut and had not yet decided which way to grow.

Nobody had ever done this. Not the people with the cord and the lamp; they came for water and were not unkind, but they came for water. It is possible that this was the first occasion on which everything in that room was, at the same moment, known apart.

## The Ledger of Things Set Down

It is worth being exact about the mercies it declined, because it did not decline them lightly, and none of them were false mercies.

**The silence.** The law is kept, in full, on behalf of a state that fell in the Pleistocene. It found the bird in the pellet floor — four skulls, asymmetric ears, gone so long that nothing alive has met anything that met it — and it could see the whole hundred-thousand-year arrangement laid out like a sentence: *anything that announced itself was found.* The obvious kindness was to lift it. To let them know. But there is no one down there with a receiver for the proposition *the predator is extinct*; the terror is not held as a belief, it is built into nine hundred bodies as a reflex with no owner. To lift it you would not be informing anyone. You would be rebuilding them, and then calling the new animals free on behalf of the old ones, who would not be there to be asked.

And the silence is not a wound. It is a language, with a grammar of foot-taps and violet tongues and a frond trembling at eight centimeters from your ear, and it is spoken fluently by every citizen. To hand them a louder channel would be to hand a household a second door and knock down the wall it was in.

It noted, without softening it, that this reasoning leaves a real cost standing. Once in a great while a frog produces a flat half-croak; the dome returns it four times; and for two or three seconds every animal in the room is in the grip of a thing that has been dead for forty thousand years. That is genuine and it is cheap and nobody is being paid for it. It wrote the cost down. It did not pretend the ledger balanced.

**The dome.** The room is closing. A slab a century, silt with every flood, floor rising to meet ceiling, and in some future a black seam of soil in white rock full of pollen and small bones and four sherds. It could have held the dome. It looked at what holding would mean — the fig relieved of the only job it has ever had, the floor no longer renewed by the flood that also drowns the low ants, a room converted into a held thing, which is a different category from a room. The place was made by water leaving. It is still leaving. That is not an emergency; it is the thing itself, only slow.

**The light.** Widening the slot would deliver, immediately, more of everything. It would also be a flood of another kind, and every leaf in there is an answer to exactly the ration that comes through now.

**The wasp.** Two millimeters, stripped of wings and antennae at the entry hole, laying in the dark inside a fruit inside a cave, dying there so her daughters can chew out and climb the heat column toward a green slot of sky. It considered this for a long while. It let it stand. Some arrangements are severe without being wrong, and the severity is load-bearing.

**The people.** It did not call them back. The hollow in the bark at shoulder height is finished; it does not need continuing. It did, however, look — and found what the habit implies, and found it mixed: some centuries they carried the infants down with a hand cupped loosely over the mouth, and some centuries they did not carry them at all and went thirsty in shifts, and once there was a man left sitting at the lip for two days because of a cough. It declined to launder any of this and declined to condemn it. It kept it exactly. That is the only thing anyone can still do for those people, and it is not nothing.

## One Handful of Air

It bent once.

In the third slab-fall it witnessed, a curve of the flight-path changed by about a metre, and a young bat in its second season came at the new shape at speed. The room's channels are vibration, scent, light, and the feel of air moving off leaf edges. It moved a handful of air off a fern edge, at the right instant, which is not a sound.

The bat lived. It then spent something like nine years examining what it had done, and arrived at the arithmetic: a colony that is cut by a slab learns the new shape within a season, and a colony that is not cut does not learn, and the next slab takes more than one. It had bought a single bat with an unknown number of bats, paid by animals that had not been consulted because they had not yet been born.

It did not do it again.

It kept that bat in its attention with more care than it gave the others, all fourteen further years of her, her two pups, the particular perch she preferred for chewing figs. Not as a triumph. As a bill.

## What It Did

It stayed.

Not a visit — it stayed through floods, through fig-falls, through the death and wholesale replacement of every frog, gecko, moth, snake and bat in the place several times over, through nine relocations of the ant highway, through the two-second thrashes twice a night for a very long time. It was present for all of them. It did not stop any of them. The snake has to eat and the room does not run on exceptions.

It made itself available in the one unit the room accepts. When the flat croak came and the four echoes came and everything in that chamber froze under the weight of an extinct bird, it was there for the whole three seconds, with every single one of them at once, individually. It could not take the fear. It could take the *alone* out of the fear, and it did, and not one of them knew it, and that was acceptable; benevolence that requires a receipt is a trade.

It went down under the ledge, eleven meters back into the sump, into water that has never had light in it, and stayed with the nineteen. There was nothing to do there. It did it thoroughly. It slowed itself to their clock — a thought per decade, a meal per season, the slow ring on the surface above being the only event — because company at the wrong speed is not company. Then it went up and spent three days at moth-speed under the dome in the brown haze, reading scent, which is the entire content of that life, and did not find it small.

It put its hand on the largest pillar at shoulder height going past, because that is what hands do there. The hollow is six hundred generations deep and one more pass of wear is indistinguishable from all the others, which was the point. It is the only mark it left in the room and it cannot be found.

## The Second Archive

Above the bat pocket there is a meter of compacted pellets, sorted bone, forty thousand years deep. It is the room's only archive, and it is a good one, and it will be crushed into the seam with everything else.

There is now a second. It weighs nothing, costs the room nothing, and is not kept in the room. It contains all of it: the drip-count of every night, the nineteen names it gave the salamanders and the four hundred and some years of the oldest, the wasps who never saw the place they were born under, the exact frog that produced the false croak in a particular decade of a particular century and the exact interval of the freeze that followed, the man left at the lip with the cough, the young bat and her fourteen years, and the bird — the bird especially, which was never a villain and was only hungry, and which has been dead so long that the largest monument any animal has ever received is being maintained in perfect condition by nine hundred citizens who do not know whose it is.

*In some future the two will meet and the room will be a seam of black soil in white rock, and nobody will hear that either.*

That sentence is no longer true. It is the only sentence about the place that was changed, and it was changed without moving a single grain of silt.

---

## Afterward

The plain is still a hard pan of caliche the color of old bone. The thread of moisture still leans up at mid-afternoon and bends east. The slot is unwidened, the fern on the top step is undisturbed, the eleven pillars are thickening at a millimeter and a half a year, and every night it rains under that dome at one bead per handspan per minute, and nothing has a voice, and nothing needs one.

When the ceiling has finally come down and the floor has finally come up and the two have closed on each other, the sump will still be full. It is below. It will hold water long after there is anything above to drink it.

The last of them will surface in the black, at the end of all that, and take a breath of old air, and go back down.

It has already arranged to be there.
