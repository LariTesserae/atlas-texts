## The Attention

It did not come in through the hatch, and it did not need to be smaller than it was, and it did not brighten the lamp. That last part was the first decision, and it was made before arrival: whatever else happened, the lamp would keep its own rhythm. Forty-one hours is not a long time to a power. It is the length of the world.

Call it *she*, for convenience. She has no more gender than the bus bar does, but *it* would suggest she was one of the room's fixtures, and she was not. She was the only thing in the room's history that was neither an animal, nor a machine, nor a leftover.

She brought nothing wet. She did not weep in the room. This is not a figure of speech; it was a precaution, taken the way you take off your boots at a door.

---

**What she did first, which took a long time.**

She learned it.

There was no other way to begin. A power that arrives already knowing what a place needs has not arrived; it has only extended itself. So she stayed through nine hundred and six tides — she used their unit, because it was the only unit the room had ever had, and because the unit had been named once by a human wrist making scratches in paint — and she did nothing but attend.

She learned to read the sizzle: the crossing waves, the pauses, the way a silence travelling left-to-right along the fourth tier means one thing and a silence that opens in the middle and spreads outward means another. She learned that the language has perhaps two hundred distinctions in it, and that a hundred and ninety of them concern contact — *you are on my rim, I am under you, I am leaving, do not follow into the drift* — and the remaining ten concern the wall.

She learned there is no word for *outside*. Not a lost word, not a forbidden one. There has never been an occasion for it.

And she counted them. All of them, once, individually: every adult in the crust, every capsule in the traffic, every predator lying in the packed lane, every rider on every predator, every flour-eater, and the dish-shaped animal behind the conduit. This served no purpose whatsoever. Nobody in the room had ever been counted, and the one tally the room possessed was a count of tides, not of persons. She thought it was wrong that a world should be that old and never have been enumerated, so she fixed it, and the fixing changed nothing at all, and she considered it among the more important things she did there.

---

**The things she did not do.**

She did not open the door. The door is the wall now; the wall is the sky; the sealing is not the room's wound, it is the room's *condition*, in the way that dryness is not a hardship there but a medium. To open it would be to let in an ocean of air with weather in it, and spores, and cold, and rain eventually, and to call this liberation.

She did not bring water. She understood, quickly and permanently, that mercy in this room would arrive as a drop, and would land, and would kill what it touched, and would be remembered — if the sizzle could remember — as the day something enormous and kind reached in.

She did not bring a plant. There is no room in the economy for a second industry. A single square centimetre of green would either die in an hour or eat the world.

She did not increase the current. She ran the arithmetic and it came out the way such arithmetic always does: more potential means a wider bloom, means more crust, means more tiers, means the same gradient of millivolts and mortality drawn at a larger scale, means more animals at the margin running down. Generosity applied to a full world produces a fuller world and not one happier animal.

She did not free the fused mothers on the east wall — the old center-holders too thick to molt, pads welded to the surface they spent their lives eating, extruding capsules into the traffic while oxide climbs their plates. She looked at this for a long time, because it is the room's cruelest fact and the one most obviously fixable. Then she understood that it was not a wound but a *shape*: that the crust-kind's whole greatness is the willingness to take the millimetre you cannot leave, and that to unstick one of them would be to lift a queen off her throne and set her down among strangers with nothing to hold and nothing to do. She let them stay where they had won.

She did not take a specimen.

She did not make anything cleverer. There is no kindness in giving a sealed world the capacity to understand that it is sealed.

She did not add a scratch to the tally on the north wall. Not then.

---

**The four things she did.**

**One.** She sat with the dish-shaped animal in the corner behind the conduit, in the drift, where nothing walks. It had not moved in nine years. She waited eleven more, which is roughly two thousand four hundred tides, and it moved, and the room reorganized for a week exactly as it always had. She had no business with it and it had no business with her. But in the entire duration of that world, nothing had ever waited for it, and it seemed to her that being waited for is a thing that ought to happen at least once to anyone who waits.

**Two.** She became, briefly, the one thing the room's ecology had never contained and could not manufacture: a vector.

This required no violation. The capsules are already built for it — dry, hard, grain-sized, indifferent to time, evolved for exactly one mode of travel, which is to be carried off on the feet of strangers. There had simply never been a stranger. She let the traffic load her the way it loads any passing foot, and she went out through the geology of the door with some hundreds of grains on her, and she looked for rooms.

She found four hundred and thirteen. Dead buildings, closed floors, service voids, wherever insulation had split low on a live bar and a green-white bloom had come up and nobody had ever arrived to eat it. In three hundred and sixty of them the capsules failed: too cold, too damp, potential too low, nothing to file. In forty-four something started and did not hold.

In nine, there is now a sizzle.

None of the nine knows about the sump room. None of them ever will; there is still no word for outside. But the trade continues in nine places where before it continued in one, and this is the only thing she did that could be described as saving anything, and she did it without ever entering the world she saved from.

**Three.** She went up into the building and found the relay.

She had already decided against eternity. A power that grants forever to a finished world has not given it a future; it has only extended the sentence, and the room's own text is honest about this — *there is nothing left in this room that can be worn down further*. The end is not a tragedy that has been scheduled. It is the last remaining event.

So she did not repair the relay, and she did not oil the bearing that has been failing for centuries. She did one thing, and it was very small, and it took her a hundred and forty years of standing beside a mechanism with her hand not quite on it.

She saw how it would fail. It would fail the way such things do: not cleanly. It would begin to close late, and then to close partially, and then to chatter — a long brownout, tides at a third strength and a quarter, a decade of half-light in which the crust would come off the wall a layer at a time and the flour-eaters would outlive the grazers and the predators would eat each other in the dark and the room would spend its final generation entirely on the slow discovery that the wall was going out.

She arranged, with the lightest touch that has ever been applied to a contact face, that this would not happen. The relay would close fully, every forty-one hours, at full leakage, and then one day it would not close at all.

That is all she gave them: not more time, but a better last day. Eleven minutes at full tide, the applause-through-a-floor, the margins surging in, the whole east wall roaring, the shuffle and the several thousand small shrieks — and then coal, and then nothing, with the crust still four and five deep and every one of them still filing.

**Four.** She stayed for it.

She was there for the last full tide, and for the eleven days after it, and for the long quiet during which the sizzle thinned from a rain of pebbles to a scatter to a syllable every few minutes to one animal somewhere in the northwest corner ticking every three or four seconds, as a healthy adult does, for its entire life, awake or otherwise.

She counted them again on the way out. It took no time at all.

---

Afterward the room is exactly as flat as it was. Six or seven centimetres of grey-brown flour, packed in the lanes, drifted at the baseboards; a cradle of angle iron holding nothing; a steel plate with the suggestion of a *7*; the wired glass admitting its ordinary trapezoid of daylight, which crosses the floor through the day the way it always has; and the brown ceramic insulator lying on its side, unbroken, too slick to have ever been anyone's home, shining mildly for two hours a day.

One difference. Low on the north wall, beside the forty-one scratches in eight groups of five and one of one, there is a forty-second scratch, made after the last tick, when a new handhold could no longer advantage or endanger anybody.

It does not continue the count. Someone once kept a tally there of something, and stopped, and was never known to have kept it.

It says: *known.*
