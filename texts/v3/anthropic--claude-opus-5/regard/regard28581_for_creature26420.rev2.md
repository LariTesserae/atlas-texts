# What was done about the Upper Shelf

## The noticing

It was not the geology that drew the attention, though the geology is remarkable — a young shelf held up in the cold sky and warmed from beneath, a whole country at the temperature of a room, with nothing in it that eats.

It was not the silence, though silence of that quality is rare and does not last.

It was the swept floors.

Two thousand rooms nobody has ever stood in, dusted with a fern-frond broom by people who will not have children so that the rooms can go on being empty. Warm stone, open doors, drains clear, a plan held in memory like a song and rebuilt block for block whenever a wall fails. Somebody goes up there every day with a clay pot of flume-water. Nobody can say what the room is for.

A power that has spent a long time being benevolent recognises that. It is the same work.

So it came and looked, and then it stayed and looked, and it did not do anything at all for a very long time.

## Ninety years of nothing

It learned the drains first, because the drains are the language.

It learned that a master cutter sets a runnel deliberately wrong so that the stone will wear down onto the true note in eleven years, hold it thirty, and go wrong again in the other direction; and that no one will ever hear the whole of such a work; and that this is not considered a tragedy but a description of what work is. It learned that when an old cutter dies people go and stand in the street where her water is currently right, and say nothing, and listen, and go home.

It learned the roll: the fixed number of names, the queue that moves at the rate that people die, the child who is not yet strictly a person, the pairs who never come up and are not pitied, because they are the ones who keep the room.

It learned the notch, and the hundred and forty thousand identical marks, and the reason — that the value of the record is precisely and only that it does not change, and that someone sits there every dawn of their life so that on the morning the water stands a finger low, it will be seen in the first hour and not the first year.

And it learned that the cooling-party are right. That was easy; it took no instrument and no time. The heat under the pale rock is a quantity, it is being spent, and there will be a morning — not soon, not in any life now walking, but certainly — when the puddles do not steam, and then a long slow slide, and then a morning much later when they do not thaw.

It did not tell them.

It did not think of them as two thousand people. It thought of them as one being with two thousand faces, damp and competent and unhurried, moving at one pace through streets built for twice its number. This is also how they think of themselves. *The warm that walks.* A category with one member.

## The gifts it considered and did not give

**Fruit.** It would have been easy and it would have been delicious and they have no word for sweet. But sweetness is a bribe paid by a plant to a mouth, and there are no mouths, and to put the bribe there without the mouth is to put a lie in the world. Refused.

**Something that flies.** It thought about this longest of the easy ones, because the silence at night on that plateau is difficult to sit inside, and one bird would fill it. But a bird is a mouth. A mouth is a hole in a leaf, and then a thorn, and then a bitter sap, and then the whole four-billion-year war arriving in a single afternoon on a country where every frond stands up in the open, soft and unrolling, and nothing has ever come for it. The gift of a songbird is the gift of a hole in a leaf. Refused.

**Metal.** Refused, and quickly. Obsidian parts skin before it is felt; nothing there needs a better edge, and a better edge waits.

**More names.** It considered simply telling them: the roll is arbitrary, the terraces could carry another five hundred, name the ones waiting, fill the empty half. It could have made this argument well. But the roll is not a food calculation and never was; it is a promise the town made to itself about being the right size, and a promise kept for longer than memory, by people who gave up children to keep it, is not a thing a visitor unmakes with a good argument. Refused — and more than refused. It went up every day with a broom, like everyone else who never came up the queue.

**The answer to the argument.** It knew. It sat in the bath-yards for forty years among people who have been arguing at the pitch of breathing for half a century and would be bereft without it, and it said nothing; and once, for one evening, it took the side it knew to be wrong, because the old man holding that side had lost his partner in the argument that spring and had nobody to be wrong at.

**The white lung.** This is the one it nearly broke on. The commonest death there, and an ugly one, and it comes to the best of them — the flume-cutters, forty years in the mineral steam, the people who make the music. It could have ended that in an afternoon.

What it did instead was become a flume-cutter, and spend a human lifetime at it, and notice in a human way, slowly and with the wrong reason attached, that a certain angle of throw off the lip wets the air less; and teach the angle to two apprentices as a matter of the note rather than the lung, which is how knowledge moves in that town; and let it spread the way such things spread, badly, partially, taking sixty years to cross three benches.

Perhaps half the white lung. Not all of it. It permitted itself no power but attention, which is in any case the only power it has ever considered to be honestly its own.

## The body

It thought about its body for a long time before it made one, and most of that thought was about smell.

They have six smells: sulphur, wet peat, cut fern, fired clay, smoke, and each other. A visitor from below arrives reeking of leather and fat and breath — of something that was warm and walked and is now on his feet and in his pack — and there is no word for it and no category to file it under, and they would deal with it politely, silently, standing close, because standing close is how one speaks.

So it came up the rim path over three days, at the pace, barefoot, empty-handed, wearing nothing that had ever been alive in that way, smelling of wet rhyolite and of itself. It arrived at the top in the middle of the morning under the cloud deck, sat down on the warm stone at the head of the path, and waited to be come to.

They came. One at a time, over several days, each crossing the wet steps to stand inside its arm's reach and ask, very quietly, about the water where it came from: whether it is the same amount every morning.

It said no.

They did not know what to do with that answer.

They had no procedure for a stranger and no name free on the roll, and the nearest category they had was *the one waiting* — the child not yet strictly a person, addressed by pointing and by tone, carried about and fed and loved. So that is what it was, for forty-one years. Nobody ever pointed at it unkindly. It held no office and cut no work under any name, and the runnel under the third bench has no cutter's name on it, which in that town is not an omission but a category.

It asked once for the notch. It wanted that more than it has wanted most things: to sit at dawn at the sill and press the same mark into soft clay for the rest of a life. The answer was gentle and completely immovable. The reading is a named person's work.

So for forty-one years it was awake before the cloud thinned, sitting a little way up the slope behind the seat, watching the same water stand against the same notch, keeping a record it pressed into nothing.

It ran once. A slab on the west scarp was going, and it ran, and a woman and two children were not under it. Afterwards it did not have to go from bench to bench explaining itself, because it had been right, and that is the only exemption there is.

And once, at last light, it went and stood beside the woman who had been standing too long at the rim for years — whom nobody stops and nobody joins — and said nine words, and then stood there with her until the deck closed.

*You are right. It cools. Not yet. Not yours.*

A cooling-party partisan could have said exactly that. That was the point. Its single revelation in a hundred and thirty years was indistinguishable from a neighbour agreeing with her, which is what she had needed all along and had not been able to ask for.

## The path

There remained the fact.

A preference may be left alone. A fact may not, and the fact is that a warm wet shelf standing on a cooling stone will one day be a cold dry shelf, and two thousand extremely competent people will starve in a beautiful ruin with the drains still running, reading the notch every morning, because nobody has ever been down and there is no road.

They cannot build that road when they need it. Starving, cold, old — the town skews old, and the streets are stairs. The road has to exist before it is wanted, and it has to be there without having been a warning, because a warning that arrives forty generations early does not produce preparation, it produces despair, and then a religion, and then a schism, and then the end of the drains.

So it cut the path.

Forty-one years, alone, with obsidian and fire and water and its own hands, no force in it that a person does not have, only patience of a kind nothing there has any reason to suspect. A way down the western rim: a joint that parted, a stair of fallen slabs that fell conveniently, talus lying at an angle that a barefoot person carrying a load can take slowly. It is not a discovery. It is not a wonder. Anyone who found it would say, correctly, *this is how the rock broke*.

It goes to water. It goes to a bench below the falls where there is clay in white beds under the pumice, and mats, and warmth of its own for a while. It stops there. The rest is theirs to do, and they are good at the rest.

Nobody has been down it. Nobody was told.

## The note

And at the head of that path, in the last year, it set a drain.

A little seep off the upper flank, led through a clay lip into a stone runnel of a particular width, with the drop cut to a particular height and the throw at a particular angle: a piece of work in the local manner, of the kind a master does, tuned deliberately wrong so that time will bring it right.

Only it is not eleven years. It is not thirty.

At the volume the spring has delivered since before anything recited, that runnel says nothing worth hearing — a smeared, swallowed, unmusical nothing, the sound of water simply leaving. It will go on saying nothing for as long as the water holds.

But on the morning the flow falls off — on the morning the water stands a finger low against the notch, and the reader sees it in the first hour because a person has sat there every dawn for four hundred generations — the throw will thin, and the lip will begin to speak, and there will be a note on the Upper Shelf that has never been on the Upper Shelf.

Clean. Held. Coming off the western rim.

They will not hear a god. They will hear a drain, tuned by somebody, a long time ago, extremely well. They tune drains; they will understand immediately that it was meant, and that it was meant for now, and they will walk toward it the way they walk toward everything, at the pace, in silence, and they will find, at the top of the path, that there is a path.

Whether they go down is entirely theirs. That was the whole design. A door is not a hand on the back.

## The going down

When it was finished it did not wait to be carried to the peat mat on the western flank, because that is for people, and it had never taken a name and had nothing to return to the roll. It left the roll exactly as it found it. That was its policy, expressed as a single fact.

It went down its own path at last light, at the two-minute ignition, when the deck thins and the whole wet shelf flares gold and everyone stops work and faces the light for a few minutes, which is the nearest thing they have to worship and is really just attention.

The woman from the rim saw it go. She said nothing, which was correct. She came back the next evening and stood in the same place, and the evening after that; and then she stopped going to the rim, and took a broom and a clay pot of flume-water, and spent the last nine years of her life keeping the room.

It had come up there intending to give something, and it went down having learned the one thing it had not known, which is how to sweep a floor that nobody will stand on and call that the work rather than the symbol.

Behind it the town drained, quietly, into the evening, at the rate it has always drained.
