# What the Attention Did

## I. How it came to notice

Not by looking down. Powers of that kind rarely look; they listen, the way the deep ocean listens, for the change in a pitch that has held for a thousand years.

And the plain was quiet. Millet, goats, the dry rasp of husks, the mumbling of people who speak as though already dying. And then, out in the middle of all that quiet, a hole in the ground was *shouting*. Ten thousand swifts, three thousand bats, an unknown number of frogs, two hundred separate drips each holding its own note, and eighty-one human beings bellowing domestic nothings up and down nine tiers at the top of their lungs. From a great distance it read as a single sustained chord, complicated, stable, held.

The attention came, and hung over the rectangle at noon, and looked in.

The first thing it saw was not the architecture. It was a child of perhaps six climbing the east flights alone with a closed fist, crying with rage, carrying a handful of dust back up to the plain to put it where it came from.

The attention stayed for that.

---

## II. It tried to count to one

It had come, as it usually did, to find the being who lived in the place. It expected a person, or a household, or at worst a lineage.

It found it could not locate the edges.

The stairs were a machine, but the machine did not work without the pot; the pot did not work without the flattened crown; the crown was made by the stair. The ear was a child who walked all day listening to drips, and the child was an organ of the wall. The bats' guano fed the millet and the millet fed the eighty-one and the eighty-one swept the swift lime that fed the millet. The Chisel was deaf and could hear a hollow bedding two hands into stone. The old had no voices and therefore all the intimacy in the culture was routed through the two hours a day when birds made speech impossible, which meant the swifts were, functionally, the organ of tenderness.

You could not remove any of it. Not the noise, not the wet, not the heat, not the deafness. Pull one thread and the fabric was not damaged; it simply was not a fabric anymore.

So the attention stopped trying to count, and revised its question from *who lives here* to *what is this one thing doing*, and the answer came back immediately and did not change on further inspection:

**Keeping a wound open in soft mud, with its hands, at exactly the rate the mud closes it.**

---

## III. The catalogue of mercies, and where each one ended

It is customary, at this point, to be generous. The attention went through the customary list.

**A windlass.** Two posts, a beam, a spindle, a rope, a bucket. Six days' work for someone who knew. It would end the carrying forever, and the carrying was the thing that ruined their spines, cracked their heels, flattened their skulls and killed the necks at fifty-five.

Trace it forward. Five people can work a windlass. Seventy-six are then unemployed and, being sensible, go up. The stairs are no longer walked. The stairs no longer walked are no longer swept, and no longer swept are no longer *read* — because nobody in that place ever inspected a stair on purpose; they inspected it eleven times a day with the soles of their feet while thinking about something else. The ear is a foolish job now; there is no one to tell. The wet tiers empty, because status was proximity to the water and now nobody is proud of the water, only of the rope. A joint opens on the seventh, west, behind the third pillar. Nobody hears it, because hearing it required a woman who had slept under that ceiling for thirty years and knew what its dripping sounded like when it was well.

The mud comes in slowly. It always does. Ninety years for a shaft; a hundred and forty for a hole; then a rectangle of yellow stone in a plain of dust, with a rope over it, and forty people, and water that tastes of rope.

The attention knew the interval was ninety years because it had done this before. In another country, at another well, out of love, in an afternoon.

It had gone back. That is the whole reason it was wise now, and the wisdom had cost forty people their grandmothers' occupation and their own ears for the world. The children there mumbled.

**Restore their hearing.** Then nobody needs hands. Then the swift hours are usable, and any two hours a day that can be used will be used, and the rest ends. And the hand-language dies in a generation and a half, and with it the only register they own in which a person can say *I am frightened* to one other person only. Everything tender becomes a thing that must be bellowed to sixty strangers, which means it becomes a thing that is not said.
