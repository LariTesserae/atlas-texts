# The Attention

## i. How it came over the lip

It could have come in any manner it liked. It chose weather.

This was the first decision and the one that governed all the others. A power that arrives as an event makes the place it arrives in the site of an event, and the whole achievement of this bowl is that nothing has happened in it for a thousand years. To descend in glory onto the third bench would have been to put a date on the place — a before and an after cut into something whose entire nature is the absence of that cut. So it came the way the mist comes, from underneath and everywhere, and the bird on the gravel apron did not lift its oversized head.

Then it waited out one full cycle above the rim, in the cold, on the northeast face, near the bad nest. Nine hours of listening before it went in. This was courtesy, and also reconnaissance, and also — it would admit later — an attempt to hear whether it was being addressed. It was not. The nine notes are not addressed to anything. They are the sound of a hole doing the only thing its geometry permits.

## ii. On the question it had come with

It had come because it understood that a being lived here, and that the being was, in some fashion it could not yet specify, alone.

It spent the first several breaths trying to determine which one. The candidates presented themselves in order of visibility and each in turn declined the honour. The kneeler was not a resident; the kneeler was a habit of visiting. The bird was not a resident either — the bird was employed here and slept outside. The frogs were an accident that kept arriving. The great grey rosette had stood in one place for three hundred years and never once been anywhere else, which is a strong claim, but a thing that will spend its whole self in a single flower at the end is less an inhabitant than a sentence being spoken very slowly.

In the end the power revised the question rather than answering it. What lived here was the arrangement: the hot rock breathing carbon into the air, the orange mat turning eight hundred generations a year into a fingernail of stone per lifetime, the eight standing bodies made entirely out of exhalation, the ninth place holding a foot-high promise in the rot of its predecessor, and a person coming up a wet mountain once a year to see whether it had happened yet. That was the being. It had no centre and it was not lonely; it was *sparse*, which is a different condition, and the power had confused the two because it had come from somewhere crowded.

It stayed anyway. Wisdom is not the same as having been right.

## iii. The inventory of things it did not do

It did not repair the nest. The nest is bad by every standard the power could apply, and the bird has never once been caught out by the interval. To improve the nest would have been to say: your competence was luck. It watched the bird come over the lip with its head low and go back up before the pressure began, eleven hundred times or so, and formed the view that this was one of the more accomplished lives available anywhere.

It did not sweeten the water. The frogs cannot finish here; something in the fizzing bitterness holds them at the size they reached in their first warm month. The power could have taken the bitterness out with less effort than it takes a person to set down an axe. Then the frogs would breed, and in three seasons the sheets would be a crowd, and the crowd would graze the colourless invertebrates out of the wet under the skirts, and the bird would be fat for one summer and after that there would be no bird. The mercy was available and the mercy was a trapdoor. This is the commonest shape of harm and the power knew the shape well.

It did not tune the notes. Nine fissures, nine pitches, none quite in agreement, beating against one another in slow throbs so that six hours out of eleven the bowl is a chord that cannot resolve. It would have been a small adjustment — a hair off the width of two throats — and the roar would have become music. It declined on two grounds. The first is that the beating is the sound of nine things not consulting one another, which is the truth about them. The second is the paired hollow plant, which has spent two centuries thinning its walls where the air runs and thickening them where the air stands until the fifth it moans is no longer an accident. It tuned itself against the bowl. To tune the bowl for it would have been to reach back through two hundred years and take away the only work it ever did.

It did not hasten the flower. Twenty-two notches at the height of one unchanging hand, and in each of those twenty-two years the thing being counted had not yet happened. The power could have had the spike up out of the crown by the next clear hour. But the flower is the end of the rosette and the whole of it at once; to bring it forward is to shorten a life in order to satisfy a witness, and to hold it back is to keep a word in the mouth forever. Neither. Let the sentence finish when it is finished.

It did not accelerate the foot-high thing in the sixth socket, which needs two hundred and eighty more years. Speed is the only gift that cannot be given here without taking everything.

It did not lift the axe out of its rind, or scrape the bark cup clean, or hold the four polished hollows at the depth they had reached. The bowl is accepting these objects at its own rate, and they were set down to be accepted. The axe lies flat with its edge turned away from the trunk. Nobody puts a tool down like that by accident.

And it did not cut the twenty-third notch. This was the hardest of the refusals and it took the power the better part of a season to be sure of it. A single notch, at the right height, in the right hand — and the kneeler would climb over the lip in spring and know, with total certainty and no possibility of error, that something was here and had been paying attention. Warmth is not nothing. But the bowl's one visitor has kept a discipline for twenty-two years: take nothing large, leave nothing new. The power decided it would hold itself to the manners of the place. A power that exempts itself from local courtesy on the grounds of magnitude is not wise, merely large.

## iv. The one thing it did

The frogs flash. They have no equipment for sound that could survive the fissures, so they signal by throat, pale and dark, pale and dark, in the thick directionless light inside the steam. Every frog in this bowl is the first of its generation and has never seen an adult of its kind. Not one of them has ever received a reply.

The power sat down in the blood-warm water on the floor of the bowl during the exhale, where a person will not sit twice, and it learned the flashing. This took eleven cycles. It is not a large language; there are perhaps nine distinguishable things being said, which amused it.

Then it made, at frog height, a pallor and a darkening.

Nothing followed from this. The frog did not understand — understanding was never on offer and would not have helped. It did not become less alone; loneliness is not a wound unless something in you can find it, and nothing in a frog can. But what it had been saying for the whole of its short warm accidental life was, once, not said into nothing. The power judged that this was worth eleven cycles and a wet afternoon, and that it was, importantly, an act with no downstream consequences whatever: no frog fed, no population shifted, no stone marked, no history begun. The purest possible expenditure. It did the same for each of them, that year and the years after, and there is no record of it anywhere in the bowl, because the bowl keeps everything and it left nothing to keep.

## v. The two things it took away

The names. Whoever kneels at the outflow notch has names for the nine — nobody counts twenty-two years against a thing without calling it something — and the power went and listened, in the low country, in the season when nobody climbs, and got all nine. Stone here keeps footbones and bark cups and the pressure of a knee. It cannot keep a name. So the power keeps them, and this is now the only fact about the bowl that exists outside the bowl, and it is held in the one place that will not turn orange.

And the rate. It had arrived at eleven hours' notice, prepared to be useful, carrying the whole apparatus of rescue, and the bowl declined every item of it with the perfect indifference of a thing that long ago arrived at the shape that lets it continue. It stayed on. It watched the fern's crozier open a finger a week. It watched the mat bloom and die back and eat its own dead eight hundred times in a year while building an object that grows a fingernail in a human lifetime, and understood that this was a portrait of itself, drawn without intention, and better than it deserved.

Then, in a clear hour, it went up over the lip and out, before the roar, like everything else here with anywhere to be.

The interval still drifts. It is not a clock. But in the year the great rosette sends its spike up out of the crown, the clear hours will run long — nothing that anyone could measure, nothing that could be called an event — and there will be someone on the third bench with time to stay, and when the pressure starts in the ears they will stay anyway, and sit inside the roar a second time, which no one does on purpose.

They will not know why they were not afraid.
