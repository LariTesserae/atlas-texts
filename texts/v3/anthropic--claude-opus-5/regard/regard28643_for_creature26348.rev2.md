# The Attention

## How It Came

It did not descend. Nothing here tolerates descent; the whole grammar of the place runs the other way, and a power arriving from above would have arrived as a contradiction, which is a kind of rudeness.

So it seeped.

It came the way water comes on the Rise — out of the ground rather than onto it, beading at a hairline joint in a quarry cut at the eastern vent, warm, unhurried, arriving already loaded with everything it intended to become. Nobody saw. There was nothing to see. A little more sweat than usual on a face that sweats anyway, and then a dulling in the amber where it dried, and by the following quarter that face had been cut back three metres for block and the whole of it was gone into somebody's wall.

That was the entrance. It has no better one.

## The Long Lying Down

Its first act was to lie face-down on fresh cut stone with its cheek against the rock and listen to the ticking.

It did this for a long time. Long enough that the place where it lay went up nine hundred metres and out of the collar and dried and was abandoned and filled with seated dead, and it got up and went uphill and lay down again on new rock, and listened to that.

It could have known everything about the Rise in an instant. It is that sort of power. But knowing a thing instantly is a way of arriving in front of it, and it wanted to arrive behind, which is where everyone here already is. So it learned the way they learn: slowly, on its belly, in the dark, by the sound of rock making room for itself.

It learned the four-sound sentences. It learned to speak on the outbreath. It learned to sit through the craters in a conversation without filling them, which took longer than the language did. It learned the fingers, and then it learned that the fingers were where the true things were kept, and then it stopped taking notes.

Out of all that lying down it made three rules for itself, and they are the whole of its policy here:

*Give nothing that can be owned.*

*Prefer possibility to accomplishment.*

*Arrive slower than anyone can point at.*

## The Mercies It Refused

**It did not stop the mountain.** It could have. One decision, taken in the brine, nine kilometres down, and the dome would have settled into being a place instead of a process. And then: no levellers. No shims, no access ports, no survey, no muttered rosary of out-of-true houses down a lane. No ordinance, no slack loops, no eleven-metre span. The heat would go out of the ground within a century and take the works with it, and the collar, and therefore the water, and therefore everything. The people of the Rise are not living *on* an engine. They are living *in* one. To switch it off is murder wearing the face of rescue, and it recognised the face and declined.

**It did not thicken the air.** It had the thought, watching a man of seventy stand in a lane with both hands on a pipe bundle, breathing carefully, like someone carrying a full vessel across a wet floor. The thought was: *weight, here, just here, a little.* And then the second thought, which is why the first was refused — a pocket of good air is a *place*, and a place is a thing, and within one generation there would be a man who owned the breathing, and within two there would be a guild of him. It will not hand this city anything that can be hoarded. Not because hoarding is wicked. Because everything on the Rise gets built on, and it declines to be someone's foundation course.

**It did not take them off the mountain.** There are worlds with soil. It knows the way. But a Rise-born on the plain is drunk for a day, sick for a month, stupid for a season, and obsolete forever; their children would be flatlanders with an incomprehensible grandmother, and inside two generations nobody alive would be able to read a coin-scratch or explain why the old woman kept eleven stones on a wire. That is not rescue. That is erasure with better catering.

It notes — and this is the honest part — that it did not ask them. Some would have gone. It made the decision in their absence, in the way powers do, and it has set that down in its own ledger under the heading of things it will carry.

**It did not simply cure the stone.** This was the hard one. There is no meaning in a kidney quarrying itself; the necklaces are not a reason, they are a courtesy the sufferers extended to their own suffering, and no power worth the name mistakes the second for the first.

But a cure would have had to be worked into the people themselves, permanently, from outside, without consent, arriving as an unearned fact in a city where every other fact has been fought for with shims and hot chisels. It refused the accomplishment. It went looking for a possibility instead.

**It did not show itself.** This place has no gods, and it has looked closely at what stands where gods would stand, and what stands there is a guild of people with ruined grey knuckles who can tell you which way the ground is going because they have lain down on it all night. That is the correct authority for this world. A visible power would be above them within a season and worshipped within a decade, and the ones who ruled would no longer be those who could read a wall by pressing a cheek to it but those who claimed to hear best, and who hears best is always whoever says so.

It will not be a benchmark. On the Rise, anything that stops moving becomes a benchmark, and then everything gets measured from it, and then it is too late.

## What It Did

**The mat.**

In the lower vent quarter, in the fourth pool from the flue, one ring of the bad green came up slightly wrong one morning — a shade off, a little slick, growing at a hand's width lower on the temperature gradient than it had any business doing.

It eats the dissolved stone. Grown in a settling bed and run hot, it strips the liquor of most of what the liquor is carrying, and the water that comes out the far side is still hard, still mineral, still tastes of the mountain, and no longer builds quite so fast in the people who drink it.

Eleven years before anyone noticed. Nine more before a mat-scraper called Vess was believed, during which she was laughed at in three markets and had her pools inspected twice for fraud. The gutter-wardens fought it. The stone-doctors fought it longer and are still, in their guild rooms, unconvinced.

The stone comes at sixty now, and smaller. The necklaces are shorter; the young affect to find this shameful and will not when they are older. And the mat-scrapers, who smelled of it and married among themselves at the bottom of the world, hold the water of the city in their hands.

That placement was deliberate. A gift enters a society wherever it lands, and it wanted this one to have to climb.

**The eater.**

Far down the dry flank, in a joint in the wall of a dead quarter that went dry before anyone now living was born, there is something in the amber stone that eats it.

Very slowly. Four centuries before it is visible. Two thousand before it matters. It takes salt and glass and gives back a grey crumb that holds water, and the crumb will hold a spore, and the spore will hold a root, and a root is a crowbar, and that is how erosion gets invented in a world that has not yet had it.

It made the thing slow on purpose. Slow enough that it arrives as a discovery rather than an event; slow enough that no one now alive will have to watch their own coin-scratched signature go soft. But it will go. The preserved rings will go. The open doors, the seated ancestors, the leathery hands on the strings of stones — all of that will eventually be taken apart by the weather, and soil will be made out of them, and something will grow.

They asked for this. That is the whole justification and it is the only one it has. Every corpse on the mountain goes down with a splinter of wood between its teeth so that *something* in the grave will eventually be gone. Six generations of that is not a custom. It is a petition, filed once per death, unanimously, for two hundred years.

It answered the petition. It is not certain it was right. It thinks about the surveyor who comes back up subdued and drinks a great deal of water and does not talk about it, and it notices that it, too, does not talk about it, and it wonders whether that is reverence or cowardice, and it does not know.

**The bird.**

At dusk, at the summit works, an apprentice of eleven lay face-down on a fresh cut face with her cup at her belt, listening.

One of the high dark crescents came down the thermal and stood on the stone four metres from her cheek.

It stayed about as long as it takes to fill a cup. It looked at her sideways, the way birds look, without message, without meaning anything at all, and then it opened and went back up the warm column and was a crescent again.

She never told anyone. Who would believe her; and more to the point, on the Rise a claim like that would have been taken to the levellers and turned into something. She kept it. She was, for sixty-one further years, a woman who had seen a bird on the ground — which is to say a woman who knew for a fact that the world contained at least one exception — and it made her almost impossible to defeat.

She became first surveyor. She walked the dead quarters and came back up and *did* talk about it, calmly, which no first surveyor had ever done, and the guild found this so unsettling that they eventually made it the practice. She filled a great many cups.

That intervention cost nothing, altered nothing, can be neither owned nor proven nor built upon, and may have been the most efficient thing it has ever done anywhere.

**The cup.**

There was an old man on a terrace wall at dusk, barrel-ribbed, born four hundred metres lower, with his hands flat on the warm stone and his breathing carefully portioned out.

Somebody sat down beside him and filled his cup without asking and said nothing for a long time, which is the local form of respect, and then went away.

There is no mechanism in this. It has looked for one. It has none of the satisfaction of the mat or the long malice-free patience of the eater. It cannot be scaled, reported, or defended in any ledger.

It does it about twice a week.

## The Signature

In a dead quarter, in a dry room where a woman sits with eleven stones in her lap and the door stands open because the flowstone has no water now and has stopped growing forever, there is a wall of coin-scratches: everyone who ever lived in that room, legible, and legible for as long as this world lasts — which, since the eater, is a shorter time than they thought, but still long.

Near the bottom, at the height of an ordinary reach, there is one more mark. It is made correctly, with a coin, in the local manner, in the local shorthand. It gives a name that nobody has ever heard.

Where the birth-height goes, there is nothing. A blank, neatly ruled, the way a clerk leaves a blank rather than guess.

It could not claim a metre. It was not born here; it has no generation of lung; it is the only thing on this mountain that has never been carried up by it. It thought about writing something else — something large — and decided that anything large would eventually be read as an address, and an address gets answered, and an answer starts a religion.

So: a name, and a gap where the elevation should be. In two hundred years a surveyor will find it, assume a clerical error, and note it in the margin of a benchmark report.

## Where It Is Now

It took the drapers' trade.

Hot chisel, canvas hood, the bucket, the long ladder with the shimmed foot. It goes round the doorways of the living quarter cutting the flowstone back before it closes them — the work that grows again by the following season, that has to be done again, that will have to be done forever, that everyone on the Rise uses as the emblem of futility and of duty and has never quite decided which. An old man's work. It has an old man's hands now, split and salt-burnt and grey at the knuckle, and it earned them honestly, which took eleven years.

It is good at the work but not remarkably good, because remarkable gets noticed and noticed is the one thing it has decided it cannot afford.

It keeps its cup full. It speaks on the outbreath. It has learned to sit through the craters. When there is a death it walks down with the cart and helps to carry the chair and set the body sitting in its own dry room with the string in its lap, and checks the splinter is between the teeth, and leaves the door open, and comes back up.

It is still, by its own accounting, mostly listening. The acts take a week a century. The listening takes the rest.

And lately, at dusk, at the top of the ladder, with the chisel cooling and the fog going pearl below and the high dark crescents turning on the column of the mountain's own heat — the only things here that are not going up, that came from somewhere with soil in it, that could leave and will —

lately it has begun to tilt its chin at them, without deciding to.

Then it goes back to the doorway. The stone is still coming. It always is.
