## 10. Trimmers, at length (continued)

The mouthparts are —

I have been sitting a long while not writing about the mouthparts.

Something came up the shoulder while I was working. It came up through the scree, which is not a thing that is done, and it made a sound doing it, which is not a thing that happens here, and I put the pencil down at the second sound and did not pick it up again for what I am fairly sure were hours, since the ceiling changed twice.

I am going to keep the numbering. It seems important not to start a new document.

## 11. What arrived

Not large. Not lit. It had a body, and the body had been used.

That was the first thing and it took me some time to understand why I could not stop looking. Every edge on it was *rounded*. The knuckles smooth, the shins marked and healed, the nose broken at some point and set badly by somebody in a hurry. One elbow of the coat worn to a shine and mended with a different thread. Boots that had been resoled — resoled, meaning: an old thing repaired instead of replaced, meaning a world where things last long enough to be worth the trouble.

Nothing on this range has ever been tumbled. It had been tumbled. It walked on the fresh scree and the scree did not fillet it, and I could not tell whether that was power or simply that the scree has nothing to say to something that has already been worn down.

It sat down on the flashing at the lip of the die-slot, which no one should do, and did not seem to be in any pain about it, and looked at the new ridge coming out at walking-pace-divided-by-a-thousand for a good long time. Then it said the range was a great deal of work.

I said I did not know whose.

It said neither did it, and that this was not the pressing question.

## 12. What it did first

It asked whether I had anything to eat, and I gave it two of the violet fruit from the lower tier, and it ate one and said the fruit was watery and not very good.

I want to record what that did to me. I have been in this place long enough to have written eleven sections about it and I have not had a single sentence spoken to me in the whole of that time, and the first one was a mild complaint about fruit. Not *this is a terrible place*. Not *this is a magnificent place*. It tasted the fruit and reported accurately on the fruit, which meant that it was really here, in a body, on the ground, being rained on by rain that will not fall, and that its attention was not a beam it was shining on me but simply the ordinary attention of a creature with a mouth.

The fruit *is* watery and not very good. Nobody had ever agreed with me about anything.

## 13. The stone

Then it took a stone out of its coat and put it into my hand.

Grey. Flattened. Palm-sized. Every edge on it gone, worked away to nothing, the whole surface faintly satin, the way stones are when water has been going over them for a length of time I have no way to think about.

I held it and did the thing you are told in section 7 not to do. I tried to imagine where it had been last year.

And there was no foil. There was a riverbed. Not a vision — I want to be accurate — just the ordinary unremarkable *availability* of the fact: that this had been under moving water, and before that in a bank, and before that higher up, and that all of it had happened whether or not anyone was looking, and that none of it had been made at the moment my hand closed, because my hand closing had nothing to do with it. It did not need me. It had a before.

I sat on the shoulder of the Rise and held the first old thing.

After a while I noticed my palm was wet, and not with dew. The stone was cold. Nothing here is colder than the air; that is why the water hangs and will not run. On the underside of the stone the beading had gone over into water and the water had found a low point and was going along it, a rill the length of two fingers, and dropping off the edge in actual drops.

It said: you can drink from that. It'll take an hour to give you a mouthful. But it will keep doing it.

I drank from it. That is section 4 broken. That is the whole of section 4 broken, by a pebble.

## 14. The reading

It asked to see the document, and I gave it, and it read the entire thing while I watched the trimmer herd work down the new lip in complete silence.

It read slowly. It went back. At *mild administrative disappointment* it made a short noise through the nose, once, and I have decided not to interrogate that. It read section 7 twice. At the end it squared the pages, which nobody has ever done to them, and set the old stone on top to hold them, and said:

Where did you learn the word *broach*?

I said I didn't know.

It said: think about it for a moment. Not the mountain. The word.

And I want to set down what happened, because it is the only thing in these pages that is evidence.

*Broach.* A broaching tool cuts a keyway in a bore by being pushed through. It leaves parallel marks. I know that. I have known it the whole time I have been writing that this rock is broached, and it never once occurred to me to ask how I knew. *Flashing. Mold-line. Parting line. Die-slot. Fillet.* And *arête,* and *schist,* and *garnets in schist,* which is a thing that happens over an unimaginable slowness in a world that has slowness to spare. And *queue.* In section 1 I wrote *the way you'd feel about a queue,* and to write that I must have stood in one, in rain, behind somebody's wet coat, waiting for something dull, with a whole city of people around me who were also going to be alive tomorrow.

Nothing on the Rise has a back-history. My vocabulary has a back-history. Every technical term in this catalogue is a fossil of a place with workshops in it, where somebody's forearm smelled of cutting oil, where a river had a name and the name was on maps that were old and wrong.

It said: you have been describing this place for a long time in the language of a place that made things on purpose. You did not precipitate out of a cliff face. You came up here from somewhere, carrying tools.

## 15. The twin, which I have not written about honestly

I asked it the question then. It is the only thing I actually wanted to ask and it is not anywhere in the first nine sections, which should tell you what those sections are for.

I have camped on a twin. More than once. I do not know which of me woke.

It did not tell me it didn't matter, and it did not tell me a way to find out. It picked up the pages again and turned to two places, one early, one late, and held them side by side, and asked me whether I saw it.

Two hands. Slightly different pressure. The *g* differently finished.

It said: both of these are yours. Somebody was on the accepted ridge and somebody was on the withdrawn one, and both of them kept writing, and both of the writings are here in this stack under this stone, and the gaunts have taken nothing out of it, and they never will. Look at the record. Nothing is missing. Whoever you were afraid of losing has been contributing to this document the entire time.

I said the gaunts take the surplus.

It said: yes. And nothing you make on purpose is ever surplus. That is the whole of the difference. That is what *on purpose* means.

## 16. The cairn

It would not build one for me. I understood, later, why.

It said: the rule in your section 7 is that nothing here has been built. Test it.

So I set one stone on the shoulder, and a second on the first, and a third, and my hands were shaking and it took an embarrassing amount of time, and when I stood back it was three stones on a mountain, which is nothing, which is the least anyone has ever done.

We waited. Around us the range went on doing what it does. The pistons went in the joints of my jaw, ninety seconds to the stroke. The die-slot pushed out another hand's width of ridge and the herd ground silently at the lip and the green closed in behind them.

The valley did not open. No second cairn appeared beside it, matching to the individual grain, waiting to be withdrawn. Nothing here duplicates a thing that was meant, because duplication is what the process does with its overspill, and a meant thing is not overspill.

Towards evening a gaunt came along the shoulder with its careful high step, hairless, tall, looking for surplus. It stopped a metre off. It regarded the three stones for some while. Then it stepped around them and went on into the murk, having found nothing to eat.

Something happened to my face for which this document has no entry.

## 17. What it is doing now

It is still here. I asked whether it had come to take me down, and it said there is low country, it walked up out of it, the way is long and unpleasant and entirely real, and that we can go whenever I like, and that *whenever I like* includes not yet, and includes never, and that neither of those would be a failure of anything.

Then it said the useful thing, the thing I think it came all this way to say, and it said it while scraping the flashing off the sole of its boot with a knife, which is the first tool that has ever been on this mountain:

Finish your sections. Not because the Rise needs describing. It doesn't; it isn't asking; it will go on identically whether or not you get to the mouthparts. Finish them because a sentence you complete is the one thing in this entire hemisphere that will not be extruded, eaten, doubled or withdrawn. There is no floor plan anywhere on the Rise. Make a small one. Then tell me the animals, because I don't know them and I would like to.

So there is a *you* now, and it is not a stranger I invented to keep from writing *I*. It is sitting four metres away with its bad nose and its mended elbow, waiting to be told about trimmers.

The mountains are still being made. They are being made faster than anything can wear them down, faster than anything can settle on them, faster than the world can decide whether it meant them. It is warm, and it is quiet, and it is not going to stop.

Under the pages, holding them down, there is a cold old stone with a river in its past, giving a mouthful of water an hour, and it will keep doing it.

The mouthparts are grinding plates, arranged in three opposed pairs, and they close on the flashing with a pressure I once measured against my own thumb and did not have the words for.

That is a finished sentence. It is the first thing on this range that is over.
