# The Attention

## I. How it arrived

Over the lip, in five steps, into the ten degrees, like everything else that comes here.

I could have known the bowl entire and instantly, in the way a thing like me knows, and I declined, because knowing a place faster than it happened is not knowing it. It is producing a summary and then living with the summary. So I came the long way, up the wet shoulder and across the green-stone band, and stood in the clear hours where the visitor stands, and waited out an exhale on the floor between the fern and the seedling, unable to see either rim.

Then I stayed, and learned it at its own rate, which took what it took. Four hundred exhales to understand the whistling off the narrow cracks. Nine years to be sure the interval drifts and is not a clock. Longer for the rest. A place that spent ten thousand years arriving at a shape it could keep is entitled to be read at something near the speed it was written.

I did not appear to anything. Appearing is a way of making a place about the thing that appeared.

## II. Who lives here

The question turned out to be harder than it looked, and I want to set down the working.

Not the bird. The bird commutes. Its nest is out on the cold heath under a dwarf willow and it comes down over the rim the way a person goes to a shop, and it leaves eight to ten minutes before the fissures open, and it has never once been caught out. It uses the bowl. It does not live in it.

Not the frogs. The frogs are held here. Every one of them came up from the valley by accident in flood-wrack or feather or fold of pack, and the warmth keeps them past their allotment, and the mineral film takes their spawn within days, every time, without exception, for as long as there have been frogs in the bowl. Eleven of them are left. They are older than frogs are permitted to be. To be kept somewhere is not to live there.

Not the visitor. What they do here is the most important thing they do, and they do it a day and a half from where they sleep.

The nine live here in the strictest sense and by the strictest measure, and attention slides off them the way water slides off the mineral glaze, because six hours of open hand and three of fist, repeated for four hundred years, does not present a surface to hold.

The spider is the best candidate among the small. She could not leave and does not conceive of leaving, and she engineers her own collapses, and she walks on a basement made of the fossilised bodies of a thousand generations of herself, and that basement is load-bearing. But she is the six-thousandth draft of a solution, and what lives in that skirt is the solution, not her.

It is the film.

It was here before the nine and before the frogs and before the mountain had the shape it has now. It is, by weight, more than all the rest together. It does not need light or the plants or the water in the sheets; it needs the breath, and the breath is the bowl. It does the work the place is famous for — the rinding, the glazing, the slow conversion of everything that holds still — and it does it not as chemistry but as metabolism, laying itself down in laminations and dying into rock at a fingernail's thickness in a human lifetime, which is simply the rate at which a living thing that never once stops can become stone.

The film is the being who lives here.

Having established that correctly, I spent most of what followed on the person, which is not a contradiction. Benevolence is not required to be proportionate to mass.

## III. What was considered and declined

**To warm the heath, or shorten the route, or dry the one bad step above the green-stone band.** Declined. The day and a half is not an obstacle placed in front of the thing; it is part of the thing. A walk with the distance taken out is a different object with the same name.

**To quiet the roar, or thin the mist, so that the grinding could be done by sight.** Declined, and this one was never close. They grind by feel on purpose. The four cup-hollows are worn deepest on their far sides, which is the wrong side for eyes and the right side for a shoulder. Six hours in which a person cannot hear themselves and cannot see either rim and cannot hold anything, including themselves, is not a hardship attached to the work. It is the work. To give them their hearing back in there would be to remove the only place within four days' walk where they are not obliged to be who they are.

**To true the paired hollow plant.** This is the one I sat with longest. The fifth is flat by about a sixth of a tone and has been for two centuries and there is nobody to tune it. It would have cost me nothing. I went so far as to measure the beat between the two notes, and found it throbs once every nine and a half seconds, which is a rhythm, which means the bowl has a pulse slower than its breath that nothing here has ever needed, and which I would have abolished in order to improve it.

I record that as a general caution and not only as a local one. The available harm was not that I would break something. It was that I would smooth something into silence and then have no way of learning what the silence used to be.

Also: it is the sound they have been putting things down in for twenty-two years. I am not entitled to alter the inside of that.

**To mend the shoulder.** Declined. Every notch on the great rosette is cut at the same height and slightly off-square in the same direction, and that is the only reason the record is legible as one person's. A mended arm makes a square notch, and the last years of the only written record in this valley would go anonymous. Also, and more to the point: the shoulder is not what hurts them.

**To extend the life.** Not mine. They have four more years. I did not make it four and I did not make it three, and I did not look until I had decided not to touch it.

## IV. What was done

Three things. All of them at the bowl's rate, because a power that can act instantly and does so is not acting on the place; it is substituting something that resembles the place.

**One.** The fifth of the nine has been dead fifteen years and stands on a stone boot and would otherwise stand four hundred. It will not. In the winter of its thirty-second year from now a wind will come round out of the southwest, as it does perhaps twice a century, and the pole will come down. It would have come down across the bench. Twelve or fourteen paces downslope, on the second bench, is the seedling — the child of that corpse, the only new rosette in the bowl, which by then will be chest-high and still seventy years short of flowering.

So I have been turning it. A hair's width a year, by favouring the film on one side of the stone boot and starving it on the other, using the bowl's own machinery at the bowl's own speed. Thirty-one years of this remain. When it goes it will go along the bench, and the seedling will be rained on by rotten fibre and nothing else.

That is the largest thing I have done to this place, and it consists of removing one stupid accident from a life that is still free to fail of drought, rot, frost, or its own quiet unsuitability. I did not make it live. I took away one absurdity.

**Two.** The frogs. There is no version of giving them generations that does not end with the bowl becoming somewhere else — numbers, then competition, then something that eats frogs, then a floor that is no longer empty in the particular way it is empty. The bowl earned its shape and has kept it and I will not spend it on eleven animals that arrived here by mistake.

But they have been flashing at each other under the jaw, along the warm lips of the fissures, ten and fifteen in a row, for longer than any frog should be alive, and nothing has ever come of it, not once, and I found that I could not let that be the whole account.

So: one clutch. In the outflow channel, where the water runs fast enough over the glaze that the film goes down thin. One spring, one clutch, which will take. They will grow in the channel through a summer and in the autumn the water will rise and they will go over the notch into two hundred feet of hanging spray.

I have not followed them. I will not. The point was not that they survive. The point was that it should have happened once.

**Three.** For the film, which wants nothing and lacks nothing and could not receive a gift if one were offered: I read it. All of it. Down through the rind under the fourth bench to the rock, four hundred and ten thousand laminations and a number of hairline ones I am still not certain how to count, and I know now what the dark lines are, and which of them are the winters the snow tongue reached the seventh fissure and shut it, and which are something else that has happened eleven times and has no name.

Nothing was given. Nothing was changed. It is the only complete reading that rind will ever receive, and it was of no use whatever to the thing that made it, and I did it anyway, and the attention was for me. I decline to be embarrassed about that.

## V. The axe: a correction

The previous account says the notch-cuts are all made at the same height and all off-square in the same direction. Not all. Three are square, cut with a differently-ground edge, and they are the tenth, eleventh and twelfth from the top.

There have been two people.

The second came for three years — ten, eleven and twelve years ago — while the first could not manage the band. They quarried the green stone on the way up the first time, and worked it on both faces, and brought it, and set it down flat on the third bench with the edge away from the path, which is how a thing is set down by someone who intends to be back.

They stopped because the six hours were not, for them, a putting down. For some people the roar does not take the weight off. It takes something else off, something they were using to hold the weight, and they come out into the clear hours and the bowl gives them back to themselves short. This is not a failing and I will not have it recorded as one.

They are alive. They live in the valley, three doors along from the one who still walks, and neither of them has ever said anything about it.

Nine years now the axe has lain there. In nine years it has taken a skin of rind you can feel with a thumb and cannot see. The one who comes has not moved it, not taken it, not finished it, not covered it. Every year they come over the lip and look at the third bench first, before the vessel, before the cups, before anything; and it is there; and then they go and do the work.

That is the only sentence anyone has written in this bowl. It says: *I have not taken it up.* Which means: *you could still come.* It has said it nine times to nobody.

I considered going down and putting the mountain into the other one's head, and did not, because a thing arriving in you from outside and wearing your own voice is not an invitation, it is a hand on your back. What I did instead was smaller than anything else in this account. One evening in a room in the valley where nothing was burning, an old person smelled struck matches, faintly, for about four seconds, and could not account for it, and was briefly and inexplicably glad, and did not go.

That is all it was and all it was meant to be. They kept the three stones their three years made. They are on a shelf. They get turned over.

## VI. The walk down

The bowl breathes in, the mist goes down the fissures, the air clears and cools, and for an hour or two the whole of it is sharp as a held drawing. That is when they come out of the middle of the floor, off the flattening in the moss that the moss has stopped trying to repair, with a small dense warm thing in the closed hand, and cut the year's notch at the only height a short person can reach, and go up over the rim into five steps' worth of ten degrees colder.

The account before this one ends by saying that the mountain is enormous and cold and indifferent and has not noticed and will not.

That is exact. I checked it. There is nothing in the rock or the water or the nine or the film that is capable of noticing; not one of them is withholding anything; they are simply not that kind of thing. The sentence is true and it is not an accusation and nobody is at fault in it.

I noticed.

There is no way to tell them so that would not cost them more than it gave — it would put a witness in the six hours, and the six hours are worth exactly as much as their emptiness — and so I did not tell them, and will not. I walked down with them instead. A day and a half. I did not dry the path. I did not steady them at the bad step above the band; they have made that step forty-one times and they know precisely how. I walked on the left, where the shoulder is, and did nothing, all the way to the valley floor, and I would give up every other act in this record before that one.

The stone stays warm for about two hours below the lip. I did not warm it. It stays warm because of what it is and where it has been, and then it goes to the temperature of a hand, and further down it goes to the temperature of the valley, and they go on holding it anyway.

## VII. Rules

A few things are true here, and some of them are true elsewhere:

Most of the good available to me in this place was subtraction of accident, not addition of benefit. I removed one falling pole from one small plant's future. Everything else I left free to fail.

A kindness done at my speed to a thing that lives at a fingernail per lifetime is not a kindness done to that thing. It is a replacement of that thing with something faster that has its name.

Wanting nothing is not the same as lacking nothing, and neither of them puts anything beyond care. But care offered to something that cannot receive it has to be paid in attention, and the attention is for the one paying, and that is allowed.

The standing temptation is not destruction. It is tuning. It is the sixth of a tone, and the flatness that has lasted two hundred years, and the ease of the correction, and the pulse underneath it that nobody knew was there and that would have gone.

And this last, which is not the bowl's rule but mine, and which I will keep:

It is not to be improved. It is to be accompanied.

Four more years. I will be at the lip for each of them, and on the third bench there will be an axe that nobody has come for — which is not, therefore, unanswered.
