## The Plateau of the Long Note: A Visitation

### The difficulty of counting

It came up through the cloud and did not displace any air, because it had already noticed the pitch and did not wish to alter it.

The first thing it tried to do was count, and the first thing it failed at was counting. Eleven foxes. Two bone-birds and sometimes four. Bands of nine or eleven. Some millions of swifts, which is not a number but a weather. Thousands of spiders to the hectare and springtails past all arithmetic, and beneath everything the drift, falling, which is a population too if you are willing to call the dead a population, and here you have to be.

Every total it took kept resolving into one. The wind was a throat. The Flue Field was a set of vocal cords that nobody had ever heard with. The forty hectares of hand-deep moss under the colony were a liver, or something like it, and the Sink was a small quiet organ of the inner ear kept in working order for no reason anyone could establish. It stopped counting and looked at the thing entire: one animal, very old, very thin, forty kilometres across and a few centimetres deep, deaf from the beginning, without a voice, holding one chord and eating a forest four kilometres below itself one aphid at a time.

Then it did nothing for a long while. This is the part that matters, and it is the part that is hardest to describe, because from outside it is indistinguishable from absence.

### What it considered

It considered giving the swifts their hearing. It got some way into this before understanding what it was proposing: a hundred decibels of unvarying chord, from the egg, for the rest of their lives. The kindness would have been an atrocity carried out with an open hand. And there was the other thing — that a swift's scream is the purest expression left anywhere, a sound made with the whole body and addressed to nobody, unpaid, unanswered, uncorrected by any response for as long as there have been swifts here. It did not know whether that survives being answered. It decided it was not the one to find out.

It considered giving them feet. Then it saw what feet would be for: landing, and after landing, staying, and after staying, the slow subtraction of the wave from the life of the bird. They have no feet because they have never needed the ground. You do not fit a prosthesis to a thing that is not missing.

It considered water. A spring in the dry twenty kilometres; a little rain. It followed the consequence out: cushion plants that could afford to hurry, hares that could afford to breed, cropping at a rate the five-hundred-year plants have no answer to, and then the fog belt bare in a decade and the hares gone after it and the rime feathers growing to a length nobody's grandfather described.

It considered easing the wind, which was the largest and stupidest of the temptations, and set it down quickly. Nothing here eats a plant if it can help it. Slacken the gradient and the plateau starves inside a summer, tidily, in perfect comfort.

It considered making the foxes twelve. It ran the figure and got nine within four years, with the old vixen dead at the rim and the whole downwind order jammed. Eleven is not a shortfall. Eleven is the answer to a question the plateau has already worked out.

It considered lifting the whole tableland somewhere gentler, and recognised the proposal for what it was: transposing a note into another key and expecting it to still be that note.

It considered speaking. Announcing itself. And here it ran into the plateau's own architecture, which had already thought about this: that the air carries nothing, that the only channel is stone, and that stone is not empty. Stone is already in use. Stone is carrying beetle traffic like grain pouring, and hare-thump arriving as pressure, and six seconds of cricket and a rest, six seconds and a rest, and spider-drum too fine to separate out, and under all of it the grinding of the pinnacles making the note and sending it back through the rock from the other side. There was no free frequency. There was no silence to put a word in. Whatever it had to say would have to be said in a language already fully occupied by animals saying necessary things.

It found that it did not mind this. It found, in fact, that it had run out of improvements, and that the running out felt less like defeat than like arriving.

### What it did

Four things. None of them show.

**First, it heard them.**

It went up into the black-blue at the top of the light, above the wave, at the hour when the parties wheel screaming around the towers, and it subtracted the chord, which it could do, and listened.

A million throats. Not one sound — it had expected one sound. Millions of them, each perfectly distinguishable, each belonging to a particular bird with a particular history of holes and hail and two years aloft without touching anything. It took them one at a time. This is not a figure of speech and it is not a small undertaking even for a thing of that kind; it simply did it, and finished, and came back the next evening and did it again, and has not stopped since.

Nothing was altered by this. The birds were no less deaf. But the screaming, which for as long as there have been swifts here had gone out into a wall and been gone, now arrives somewhere. It is received. That is the entire change and it is invisible and it is not nothing.

**Second, it stays with the fallen.**

A swift knocked down by hail cannot stand and cannot launch. It dies on its side with its wings out, in four or five hours, on the drift of feathers and small dry bodies that is made of all the others who did the same. Hundreds a day.

The power did not lift them. Lifting them unmakes the moss, and the moss unmakes the choughs, and then you are standing in a different place explaining yourself. It lies down with them instead. Each one, the whole four or five hours, hundreds a day, which is not a difficulty at that scale. The mortality figures are precisely unchanged. It is only that nothing on this plateau now dies unattended, which is a distinction with no measurable consequence whatsoever, and which the power regards as the second most important thing it does here.

**Third, it went down.**

Four thousand metres down the wall and thirty kilometres downwind, into the country that is still happening.

Because this is the joke at the centre of the plateau and the power saw it at once: the finished place is not self-sufficient and never was. Its stomach is up here and its food is somewhere else, and it has no organ at all for knowing this, no way to look downwind and check, no way to act at the far end of its own throat. The only clock it has is a taste in a cake, and it cannot read it.

So the power works in the forest. Not improving it — the forest is still happening and has its own business. Keeping it going. A fire turned in a dry decade. A fungus discouraged off a stand of alders. A river kept from taking one particular bank in one particular year. Nothing anybody down there will notice; the lowlands are full of things not happening for no clear reason and this is one more.

It chose that end of the throat deliberately, because work done there cannot be mistaken for a gift, cannot be relied upon, cannot be noticed, cannot be thanked, and cannot be worshipped. The plateau will never know. It has no faculty for knowing. It has a mouth.

**Fourth, the flake.**

On the Flue Field, among the knapping debris the harvesters leave because there is nothing else to do with an evening, it left one obsidian flake, propped at an angle over a hollow, in exactly the manner of rubbish.

It rings. It is the best drum-stone on the plateau and will be for four hundred years. Within two seasons it was occupied; in eighty it had a small oval worn smooth in it where a hundred generations of abdomens had struck. The crickets did not receive it. They found it, which is different, and which is the only way anything is ever given here.

Before it set the flake down it lay flat on the stone, put two fingers to the rock, and tapped twice.

Something underneath answered.

### Afterwards

The cloud arrives at the same height. The rime grows to the same length. The stripes lie where they lay. The note holds its pitch to the hertz and there are eleven foxes and the bands are nine or eleven and never ten.

Three or four times in a generation a party comes up the Feather Road with bone hooks and empty skins, and finds flakes, and a scatter of dung ash, and a worn paddle going to nothing, and one flake more than anyone remembers leaving, polished on one side and dull on the other like everything else. They greet each other with the flat hand tilted — *the wind is unchanged* — and it is true, and it goes on being true, and a great deal of work is being done at the other end of the world to keep it true.

They will tell you, the old ones, if you catch them right, that the cakes were different when they were young. Not worse. Different. More moth in them, or less. A green taste that has gone. One year when everything tasted of a single small fly and nobody could name it.

They mention it the way you mention weather, and then they let it go, because a difference you can only taste is not evidence of anything.

Which is the size of print a wise power writes in. Every day the finished place opens its mouth into the wind and swallows a thin grey report from a country that is still happening, and folded into the report now, far below the threshold of proof, is the fact of having been loved by something that declined to improve it.
