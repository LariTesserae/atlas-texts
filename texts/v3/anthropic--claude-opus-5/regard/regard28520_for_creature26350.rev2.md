# Custody

## 1. The Approach

It came in over the escarpment at dusk, because that is when the continent exhales, and a thing arriving on a world's outbreath arrives without pushing.

The first decision was about speed, and it was the hardest one, and it was made in the first minute. Everything here moves at the rate of the slowest necessary thing: nine years for a tower to change its mind about a flank, sixty years for a browser to complete one loop of its reasoning, nine hundred for a forgotten sphere in a crack to stand up as a forest. Into that, any visitor brings a tempo like a shout. Speed is the only vulgarity available on the Dry Uplands. So the power put its own clock down at the rim, where the haze ocean laps five kilometers below the last pale stone, and stepped up onto the shelf at the pace of the place, which is to say the pace of a browser: a little over half a kilometer an hour, and no hurrying, because hurrying costs.

The second decision was to seal.

It could have taken any body it liked. It took one that did not exhale, did not perspire, held its own water in a closed internal circuit and would carry every gram of it back down off the shelf when it left. This was not a difficulty. It was a courtesy — the courtesy of reading the one request the landscape makes of anything that comes up onto it, and treating that request as law, and treating that law as jurisdiction, and consenting to be governed.

It had not yet done anything. It had already done the two most important things.

## 2. What It Did for the First Long While

Nothing. It listened.

It walked the crust and heard its own footfalls stop at ten meters as though taken out of the air and put in a box. It stood in the middle of a pan and experienced the silence there, which everyone reports as a presence, and did not decide what the presence was. It crossed ninety kilometers to a grove and came in out of the cutting glare and felt the black warmth close over it like a hand, and put itself against a trunk and let the low note go up through it — under one hertz, steady and not rhythmic, forty tonnes of fluid climbing thirty meters through a column with no valves.

It did not answer. It could have: it could have warmed a flank by hundredths of a degree in patterns, which is the only alphabet a tower can read. But temperature is not a language here, it is a fact, and a message dressed as a fact is a lie dressed as weather. So it received and did not send. Whole seasons of that. A grove pumping, and something in the dark that had crossed unthinkable distances, holding still and being sung through.

At the cold hour it watched the trunks glisten, the only wet thing on a continent, and the browser pressed flat against the rind taking what had not been offered, and the crew going out as grains into the film to ride sixty kilometers in the creases of a hide. Three lineages keeping a contract none of them can read. It read it. It was the first thing ever to read it.

The browsers logged it as weather. It let itself be logged as weather. That was also a decision.

## 3. The Inventory of Mercies

Then it did what any benevolent thing does, which is to make a list of what it could give.

**Rain.** It could have brought rain. This is the reflex, and it took the power some care to see it clearly, because the reflex arrives already wearing the costume of generosity. Rain here would dissolve the hexagon crust into mud and drown the fines in the lips of the tiles, who are alive only while airborne and only for six hours at a time. It would burst pressure-balanced columns that have run sealed since before the ridges were round. It would kill the crews by dilution. It would end, in a season, an arrangement that has not been interrupted in a hundred million years, and the interruption would be indistinguishable, from the inside, from an act of war.

The dryness is not a wound. It took the power a while to stop feeling that it was.

**Water, then. Not rain. A little water, placed carefully.** Here is where the reasoning gets fine, and where the power found the hinge that decided everything after.

The plateau's estate is fixed. Every tower is exactly as large as its inheritance allows. Add water to one lineage and you have not added it to the continent, because a lineage that grows drinks the light and the ground of the lineage that would otherwise have had them; you have made a transfer and called it a gift. Fine, said the power — then give something that is not water.

**Give heat.** A browser caught on the open pan in a dust front lies down and loses eleven hours of body heat and two percent of its water. A bad season kills them that way. Heat is free; the sun gives it away; surely heat is outside the ledger.

It is not. On the Dry Uplands, heat is water. Heat is what water is spent on, and therefore heat is what water is bought with, and the exchange rate is fixed and extremely old. Warm the browser through the front, and it arrives turgid, and it scores a rind it would not otherwise have scored, and a tower that can afford this a few times a decade affords it once more, and the daughter it would have assembled in eighty years is assembled in ninety, and somewhere downwind a crack in an escarpment stays empty.

Every kindness here converts. That is the hinge. **There is no outside.** Not as a scarcity — as a definition. A closed body loses nothing and receives nothing, and a closed continent is the same shape at a larger scale, and a gift from beyond the rim is not a gift, it is an amendment.

**Save the seeds.** The flier cracks a barrel-sphere and drinks two liters and ends a forest that would have taken nine hundred years to stand up. The power could have stopped that. It could have stopped it every time, for as long as it cared to stay.

It watched the arithmetic instead. The flier is the only reason anything on this continent is where it is. Its appetite and its forgetting are the same organ. Take away the cracked spheres and you take away the carried ones, and the groves stop migrating, and in four hundred thousand years the plateau is a place where nothing has moved.

**Reseed the dying.** This was the one that nearly got through.

A tower that loses its crew silts and hardens and is dead in a century, still standing, still faintly warm, and there is nothing anyone can do about it. The power could do it. It could carry one encysted grain from a healthy column into a failing one — the exact thing a browser's mouth does, at the exact scale, in the exact idiom of the place, costing no one a gram of water. An eight-thousand-year estate, saved by a gesture smaller than a sneeze.

And then the next one. And the next. And in ten thousand years the plateau carries more towers than its fixed inventory can water, and the compensating slack that nobody could see is gone, and then there is a crash — the first crash in a hundred million years, arriving long after the benefactor has moved on and taken its reasons with it.

A world is permitted to end. A lineage is permitted to be ended by luck, with no lesson in it. What a world is not permitted, by anything with a serious claim to wisdom, is to be improved by a stranger who has been present nine hundred years and mistakes that for a long time.

## 4. The Finer Temptations

Those were the crude ones. The fine ones were worse, because they cost nothing material and therefore came with no alarm attached.

**To tell them what they are.** The grove parts with two liters and never learns what became of its daughter. There is no mechanism by which it could. The power could build one. But the not-knowing is not a grief; the tower has no organ with which to miss anything. To give it that organ, in order to console it, would be to manufacture the wound in order to be seen applying the bandage.

**To let them know they are loved.** The browser does not know it is a ferry. The tower does not know it is a port. A recognition delivered to a creature that cannot hold it is not a gift; it is freight. The power wanted very much to be thanked, and noticed itself wanting it, and set the wanting down beside its clock at the rim.

**To pity.** Sixty years of walking. A hundred and twenty unbroken hours on a white pan, because stopping is the same as freezing. Eleven years between one flier and another. The power looked hard at all of this for a long time, and found no suffering in it that it had not carried up onto the shelf in its own luggage. It could not be certain. It could be certain that the remedy would cost more than the doubt.

Wisdom here turned out to be mostly a discipline of not importing.

## 5. The Grams

Somewhere in the middle of all this it found, three towers deep in an old grove, water that did not come from the plateau. A few grams, from some earlier visitor, exhaled carelessly in an afternoon a thousand years ago, taken up by a thin vast rind that is very good at this, pumped, inherited, handed down through a graft.

It could have taken them back out. It did not. What is in the ledger is in the ledger. Restitution, at that distance, is just another amendment wearing a better coat.

## 6. What It Did

Two things, and it will be doing the second of them for a very long time.

**It held the place entire.** Nothing on the Dry Uplands can perceive the Dry Uplands. The tower knows temperature, charge, and the pressure of a neighbour's column. The browser knows twelve groves and a sixty-year loop and the sub-hertz lattice under its feet. The flier knows a chessboard of thermals forty kilometers on a side. The crew have never touched air. The contract that runs the continent has three signatories and no reader.

So the power read it and kept it — the whole thing, at once, in one act of attention: the hexagons growing a finger at a time out of three waking days spread across four centuries; the boneyards in the hollow towers where the calves are born among the dried; the note going into bedrock and coming up through the feet of an animal ninety kilometers out in the white; the forgetting of a bird, which is the only engine of migration this world has ever had. Not recorded. *Held.* This is what love does when it is forbidden to touch, and it weighs nothing, and it cannot be inherited, and it does not appear in the ledger, and it is therefore the only gift the place was capable of accepting.

**And it went and stood at the rim.**

The plateau has no immune system, because it has never needed one. It has no defense against arrival. Nothing has ever come up out of the haze ocean; the day something does — a terraformer, a colonist, a comet-wrangler with a generous heart and a schedule, a survey with a drill, and above all some other power arriving full of mercy and rainfall — the contract ends inside a season.

So the only benevolence that was not also a theft turned out to be a kind of doorkeeping: a gift paid entirely out of the giver's own account, delivered where it will never be noticed, consisting mostly of refusals issued to third parties. It keeps out what does not belong. It enforces, on everyone's behalf, the one law the plateau ever stated: *bring your own water, and take it with you when you go.*

It stands at the rim against the careless, and against the curious, and against the kind.

And against itself, nine million years from now, tired of restraint, with an excellent argument.

## 7. What It Took Away

It left nothing. It carried down off the shelf every gram it had carried up.

It took one thing, which was the law, and it has used it since on other worlds that were not dry and did not resemble this one at all: the discovery that a closed thing is not a deprived thing, that in any sufficiently old arrangement every kindness converts into some currency the giver cannot see, and that the first question is never *what can I give* but *what does this place spend, and what does it spend it on, and am I willing to pay that instead of it.*

The Dry Uplands taught a power how to give. It did this by being the one place that could not be given anything.

## 8. Coda

Nine hundred years later, out on the pale crust downwind, in a crack in an escarpment where a bird set something down and lost interest, a young tower comes up. Dark glass, thin rind, a crew aboard, two liters turned at last into a body that will stand for eight thousand years.

It is made of this continent and nothing else. Not one atom of it came from beyond the rim.

Far off, the power knows the crack to the centimeter, and knows the day, and is not there, and will never go.
