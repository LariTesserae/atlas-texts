## Attention

The power looked at the mountain for a long while before it did anything, and *a long while* should be read on the local clock: about as long as it takes a fig root to reach a fourth course.

The first work of benevolence, in a place like this, is learning the difference between a wound and a shape. Almost everything a stranger would reach for here is a shape. The dished stair treads are not erosion, they are the settled state. The mortar wider than the brick is not decay. The mules standing like furniture are not despair. The register of joints instead of a census is not a bureaucratic error but a theory of persons, and a better one than most. A power that arrives to splint a healthy limb has not been kind; it has only been busy.

So it went slowly, and it found, in the end, two things that were not shapes.

One was the ear-children: six years old, taken into the dark on a rope, not permitted to speak while working, for years. The power looked at this a long time and left it alone, because the mountain already knows, and argues about it, and there are listeners who will not take apprentices under nine now, and that argument is theirs. A benevolence that performs your reforms for you has stolen something you were going to need.

The other was the hands.

## What Was Considered, and Not Done

To make the flues warm forever without fuel. This ends the coffers, which ends the forty-year rotation, which ends the only arrangement anyone on that mountain has ever made with their own dead that does not require lying. It also makes the hands unnecessary, which is a way of never having to meet them.

To lay down a complete map of the flue system, engraved in copper, in Stairhead Court. It is not that the truth would harm them. It is that a complete map is a chair, and something will come and sit in it.

To count them. An act of love spoken in the wrong dialect.

To take the hands up in one night, out through the hatch, into the light and the fig-shade and the four o'clock cloud. The power ran this to the end several thousand times, and the end was always the same: some died of the light, and the rest were absorbed, one by one, into other crews, and kept their given names, and were never, ever asked about it. The mountain already owns a machine for making people stop existing kindly. Using it on the hands would have been the tidiest cruelty available.

To speak from above the cloud, and name the wrong, and command. They would have obeyed. They would have repointed in fear, and mortar is unforgiving of haste; a face pointed in fear opens again in eleven years, and then there is a wall that hates you and no one left who can tell you why.

What it did instead was walk up the buttress-road. This took eleven days, and it did not need to.

## Pointed In

It came with nothing, which is the ordinary condition of hands coming up the road, and it was taken in on the north side of the Cold Slot, on a weeping face that nobody wants, by a household that was two hands short and not in a position to be particular. The ceremony involves mortar. It let itself be pointed in, and the verb was used without irony, as it always is.

Its palms went pale and papery from lime. It filed its nails back to nothing. It grew the shine of callus along the back of the left hand, because it walked with a hand on the wall like everyone else, and it ground its teeth from the hum, which is how we can date most of this: the dentists keep casts, and the casts are the only history the mountain bothers to write down.

It was a mediocre mason for nine years. This was not a disguise. It had chosen hands that learn, which is a decision, and probably the largest one it made. The household's verdict, preserved in a cast-box label in a dialect of abbreviation only tooth-workers read, was *slow, never missed a day* — which is the best thing that can be said about anybody there.

## Its Figure

Six. Seven. Six. Eleven, the year the crew was big. Nineteen once, on a soft joint, and it was teased about that figure for two decades.

It answered the polite question honestly every single time. *How much did you take out?* — and then it sat down and listened for an hour, and it did this some thousands of times, and that is what a wise power did with four decades on a brick mountain: it was told things. It could have known all of it at once, from outside, in no time at all. It preferred to be told, because being told is a relation and knowing is not, and the whole trouble down the chute was that nobody had ever stayed to be told anything.

## Down

There is no digging downward. There is inheritance, and there is being taken in.

It was pointed in a second time, late, into a stoking band — scandalous, not illegal, and the gossip is in the casts too. It carried leaf-turf on the hereditary stairs for nineteen years and learned the two staircases its band was entitled to and no others. It learned the greeting at the hatch by mouth, from the stoker before: eleven syllables, four of them containing a sound not otherwise made on the mountain.

The practice at the hatch is: you tip, you speak, you go. Nobody stays. It is not forbidden. It is simply not done, the way you do not stand and watch someone read your letter. The air out of the chute is hot and foul, and there is more turf coming, and loads going up have right of way over everything.

It tipped. It spoke the eleven syllables. Then it sat down on the landing with its back against the warm brick and stayed for the length of a Still Hour, because that is the only unit of time the mountain measures silence in.

The answer came up out of the chute after about as long as it takes a person to be sure.

It had been coming up for six hundred years, into the sound of a stoker's boots going back up the stair.

## The Sentence

The translation took three generations, one word at a time, done by a listener, two dentists and — later, at the hatch, slowly, with a great deal of repetition — a hand. It was carried out in the local manner: a project begun by somebody's grandmother and finished under somebody's grandchildren, with no announcement at any point.

The greeting from above means, near enough: *it is not late; we are still here.*

The answer is the same sentence with one word altered. The dialect distinguishes a *we* that excludes the person spoken to from a *we* that includes them. For six hundred years the stokers have said the first one.

What comes up out of the chute is the second one.
