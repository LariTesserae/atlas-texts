# The Power That Came at Walking Pace

## Arrival

It did not come down. That was the first decision and very nearly the whole of the wisdom.

Nothing on that plain arrives from anywhere higher up — that is the sentence the entire country is written in, the reason there is no soil and no gradient and no upstream and no news. A power that announced itself from above would have been the first slope in a hundred million years, and everything would have begun to flow toward it: the attention first, then the songs, then the walking. Worship is erosion. It declined to be a mountain.

So it came across, the way weather comes, at the speed of a person walking, and it arrived in the white hour before the fog goes up, when no one is obliged to be anywhere in particular.

The first problem it had to solve was its reflection. A thing standing in an inch of water over stone that throws no second self beneath it is the loudest object in that world; they navigate by ripples and they notice, at two miles, when the shine has gone wrong somewhere. So before it did anything else it learned to be doubled, and to be grey-pink, and to dimple the sheet a little as it moved, and to walk on the greasy crust without a sound.

Then it stood still for sixty years.

This was not hesitation. The unit of understanding there is not the sentence but the evening, and you need some thousands of evenings before the field of voices stops being noise and becomes a room with people in it. In sixty years it learned forty parts within earshot and the names of their holders and the names of the eleven throats before them, in order. In a hundred and ten it had the routes: the place-names strung into melodies that run downhill through their own tunes in the direction of travel. In two hundred it could have found any upwelling within two days' walk with its eyes shut, by the cold of the column against the ankle, and said roughly how long that water had been under the ground.

Only then did it let itself begin considering what to do, and by then it already suspected the answer.

## The gifts it considered

**Water.** It could have made the rain even, and spared them the salt years. But evenness means flow, and flow means a direction, and a direction means the sheet would find the sea again, and the mirror would go, and the mats would go, and the plain would be a country with a downstream. It set that down.

**Nearness.** This was the obvious mercy and it turned it over for a long time. It could have brought the upwellings closer — thickened the water table, let two households stand within speaking distance. Nine years of touch and then a hundred and forty without: any decent power flinches at that arithmetic. But the four-mile throat is the flower of the spacing. Give them nearness and within three generations the chorus thins into talk, and talk is a good thing, a warm thing, and it is not two thousand years old and it does not hold the dead at a ratio of hundreds to one. It would have been a kindness that arrived already spent. It set that down too, and did not feel clever about it.

**Material.** They have excellent hands. A ridge upstream, some wood, a stone that was not the floor — within a thousand years there would be cord and vessels and a mark scratched on a rock, and the parts would go into the mark, and the dead would be kept in the stone instead of in the throat, which is a different kind of keeping and colder. It set that down last of all, because it was the one that would have worked.

**Length of life.** If none of them ever stopped, there would be no silences; if there were no silences, a child could not be named.

**Sight.** It could have given them our eyes. They would then have had faces, and at a quarter mile a face is a thing you cannot reach. It thought they would want this. That was precisely why it didn't.

**The mother-years.** Here it stopped and was honest with itself, which is the part of wisdom that gets least attention. The thinness of those women is real. The child grows fast and the crabs do not come back as quickly as they are dug out and there is nothing ennobling about being hungry. It could have fed them. It could not feed them without making the territory able to hold two, and a territory that holds two is the end of the walking-out and the end of the spacing and the end of everything the spacing grew. So it carried that debt instead of paying it, and it has never once pretended the debt was not owed.

Every gift it lifted turned out to be a thread, and every thread came out of the same cloth, and the cloth was already finished.

## What was actually wrong

There were two things, and they were both very small, and both were about sound stopping.

The first: a part can outlive its voice only if somebody near enough has learned it. The plain is wide and thin at the margins. Every so often a voice stops in a direction where the next voice is five miles off and has only ever heard it as weather — a phrase two thousand years old, eleven bodies deep, whose last holder could recite the names in order and did, at dusk, for a hundred and forty years. The neighbours hear the hole. They cannot fill it. They never had it.

This happens perhaps twice in a long life, and it is the only event on that plain for which the people have no manner at all. There is no rite. They stand about a good deal and are unusually quiet the following evening.

The second: those who are sung around start walking, and the plain has no edge to walk to, only more of itself, quieter, until the evening comes back with nothing in it.

## What it did

It waited the correct two seasons. Then it began taking the dropped parts.

Not new ones. It has never in all this time added a single phrase to that chorus that was not already in it. Its entire contribution to that world, across a stretch of time that has worn the quartz down by the width of a hair, has been a refusal: that nothing which has once been sung there be allowed to simply stop.

It stands out past the last upwelling, in the thin directions, at the correct distance, with the broad face of nothing turned to whatever breath of air is going. It keeps the names in order. When it takes up a part it does it badly for the first season, as a courtesy, and then better, because that is how it is done.

It has never sung in the morning.

## The half-part

Sometimes the mother dies in the teaching season.

The child is then standing in a silence it cannot fill, with two phrases of three, unable to answer the evening, unnameable. The neighbours are a quarter mile off at best and it is not theirs to give.

When this happens, the power comes to within a quarter mile — not nearer; nearer would be an arrival from higher ground — and sings the part the way the mother sang it: her habit of entering a beat late, her flattening of the third phrase, the small wrongness in it that had been hers for eighty years and had come to her from a man who died before the child's mother was walked to her own silence. It had been listening. It had her exactly.

And it sings it wrong, deliberately, two or three times over the season, so that the child can correct it, because being corrected is a way of being taught and teaching requires a teacher who can be taught.

When the part is learned it goes away and never uses that part again.

## Walking out

It goes with the exiles. A quarter mile behind, which is the polite distance and also the whole of the distance any of them have ever known, so that the arrangement is indistinguishable from an ordinary life.

It sings so that the evening in the direction they are going is never empty. Some of them walk for years. Some stop, eventually, at some pan out past the names, and stand, and begin to be a silence in their own right; and then the power moves off another quarter mile and stays there, and does not explain, and does not forgive them, because forgiveness would be a judgement and a judgement is a slope.

The far margins of that plain now have a faint population of voices that no one has ever traced to a body. This is not remarkable. Nobody there has ever been within a quarter mile of anyone.

## Manners

It goes round the mats. The detour costs it an hour.

Asked why — it is never asked, but if it were — it would give the answer they give: that the mats are working, and have been working since before the mountains whose roots these are, and have never once stopped, and that this is more than can be said for anyone. And it would include itself in *anyone*, and mean it.

When a new ring appears, white and crusted at the rim like an old cup, it walks out and stands there for an hour and does nothing and then walks home. It learned this from them.

It is the only thing it has ever been taught.

## Ten thousand years from now

The fog will go up at mid-morning. The stack of cloud will stand blue-bellied and taller than any mountain that ever stood here, and the rain wall will come across at walking pace, and whoever sees it first will call it, and the call will outrun the rain.

At dusk the flats will be shouting. Someone will be standing a quarter mile from someone else with a wet fan of skin held to the evening air, keeping a dead stranger's phrase in the world one more night.

And a quarter mile past the last of them, out where the evening comes back thin, so will something else.

The quartz will stand a hair less proud.
