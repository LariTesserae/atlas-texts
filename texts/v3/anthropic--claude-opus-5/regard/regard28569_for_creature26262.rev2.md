# What the Attention Did

## I. It came up the road

It could have come any way at all. It came up the road, on the cable car, with the ore sacks and the mail, and at the switchback station it took the tin of wax and the pair of lathe-turned plugs and the card printed in four languages, and it read the card, and kept it.

*Do not shout. Shouting is not possible. You will hurt your throat learning this.*

It decided to treat this as the entirety of the local revelation and to require no other. Everything that follows is what happens when a sufficiently powerful thing takes a printed card seriously.

At the rim it stopped for a long time — long by its own reckoning, which is not a useful unit, but long enough that a surveyor's hound came and sat against its leg and would not be moved — and looked down at the nested arcs and lobes of pale gravel and dull green, the figure the note has been drawing on the floor of the basin since before anyone was there to be drawn.

It had been sent, if *sent* is the word, to find out about the place and the being who lived in it.

It spent two hundred years on the second half of that question, which is the kind of delay that gets remarked upon.

---

## II. On the identity of the being who lives there

The difficulty is that the obvious answers are all true and none of them are sufficient.

There are forty thousand people. There are also eleven thousand years of a single sustained note, and a pattern of ridges and hollows fretted into bedrock by that note, and a distribution of salt grass and red-edged succulent that is a portrait of the pattern, and a distribution of *people* that is another portrait of the same pattern in a slower medium: houses in the hollows, foundries in the lobes, a birthing house in the deepest overlap, a whole civilization arranged in the shape of a standing wave the way iron filings arrange in the shape of a field.

The plants did not decide where to grow. The people think they decided where to live. Both are figures on the drumhead.

So the report it eventually rendered went, in part: *Eleven thousand years ago there was a sound here. Then there was a pattern. Roughly eight hundred years ago the pattern began keeping a written record of itself, twice a month, with a tuning fork and a shallow bowl of water, in a ledger longer than everything else it has ever written. In my judgment the basin is one being, it has recently and with great difficulty acquired a memory, and it is approximately halfway through the process of learning to say its own name. It will lose the power of speech at around the same time it finishes.*

Its private estimate, formed early and never revised, was that this was one of the better things going in the neighborhood. Not the grandest. There are louder civilizations, and richer ones, and several that have done more impressive violence to their local physics. But it is rare — it is genuinely rare — to find a people who have organized their entire common life around a fact they cannot alter, and done it without lying about the fact, and kept the measurements honest for eight centuries when a forged number would have doubled any guild's holdings overnight, and then, when the numbers said the quiet had moved, picked up the city stone by numbered stone and carried it after the quiet rather than pretend.

They have gone a mile and a half. They have not once been in the wrong place.

## III. The mercies it declined

**To shore the Throat.** Trivial. A matter of persuading some rock to stop dissolving. The note would hold at its present pitch forever, the wave would freeze, the map would stop needing redrawing, the lifters would lose their trade in a generation and the Pitch Office would become a room where a clerk writes the same number twice a month until the clerkship is abolished. It considered this for a long while and then set it aside, on the grounds that these people do not want a Bell. They want to know how the sentence ends. Guaranteeing that the sentence never ends is not a kindness to a reader; it is the confiscation of a book.

**To end it now, gently, and green the plateau.** Also trivial, and worse. Take the note away and within three generations the four thousand signs have withered to a valley dialect with local color; the hounds, bred four hundred years into living instruments, are pets that die of nothing; the flat-born, who are currently the only citizens who cross a polished lobe upright with their hands in their pockets looking at the view, become merely deaf; the deepest hollow becomes a lawn. You cannot make a present of the thing a people has organized its love around without also taking the love apart to get at it. And they would be, ever afterward, a rescued people, which is a tense that nobody gets out of.

**To repair their hearing.** The notch in the audiogram is not an injury. It is a fit. Give them undamaged ears and the Bell becomes unendurable, which means you now have to silence the Bell, which returns you to the previous paragraph. Also, bone-talk would become optional, and what is optional is reserved, and what is reserved is saved for the dying, and a people would quietly lose the only form of speech they have that cannot be misheard, cannot be overheard, and cannot be taken back. It is not obvious that the coast, for all its silence, has anything half so good.

**To tell them the date.** It knows the date. It has known it since the second decade. But a date arriving from outside has a footprint: the eastern edge stops building, the western shells fill with beetle-sects, the masons put down their numbering chisels, and every child born in the interval is born to people who have stopped. They have an office for this question. They would rather compute it themselves and be wrong by forty years than be told and be right. It weighed the number and did not say it, and has not said it since, and this has cost it more than the shoring of the Throat would have.

**To do one public, undeniable good.** To heal a scarred-tongued man's hand in the street; to raise one eel back up out of the steam. The problem is arithmetic, not scruple. A visible miracle becomes a shrine. A shrine requires a node. Every node in the basin is surveyed, deeded, litigated and occupied, and the deepest one has a garden and a lawn and six centuries of children in it. In Hush even mercy has a footprint in real estate, and there is no vacant quiet to found a religion on that would not have to be taken from a family.

**To withdraw.** It considered this too, briefly, and identified it as the coward's version of restraint. To look at a thing for two hundred years and then leave is also an act, and a worse one.

What remained, after all that, was a set of constraints so narrow they could have been printed on the back of the station keeper's card: *Say nothing into the air. Move no node. Cost them no quiet. Leave no evidence.*

It found this a reasonable brief. It had, after all, been told at the bottom of the road that shouting was not possible.

## IV. What it did instead

**It learned the language.** All four thousand signs, held low and close to the chest, and then the poetic register, which took longer, and then the nine nose-wrinkles, which took longer still and which it still occasionally gets wrong in ways that would be funny to a child. It learned the lamp codes on the towers and the slate abbreviations, including the obscene ones, which are the oldest orthography on the plateau. It learned to make the sign for *weeping* on its own cheek, the one that translates better as *leaking*.

**It took the slack.** Not from necessity. It simply observed that everything alive up there — cradles, mourners, drunks in the Salt Quarter, the hand a mother uses on a sternum — moves at five and a half seconds, and concluded that to be present in that basin at any other rhythm would be a form of shouting. So it entrained. It has been breathing with them, in the technical sense, since the eleventh year.

**It took the job that was open.** The guild book of the wading crews is explicit: *do not count the line.* Nobody in the Pitch Office believes in the fifth man; every single wader does. This is the only position in the city that is both unfilled and pre-approved, and it moved into it without ceremony. It holds the far end of the rope in the antinodes, in the mist where the eyeball shakes in the socket and the world smears at the edges, and it does not appear, because it is already accounted for. Bearings that should have failed do not fail. A knot that should have slipped holds. A crew that would have turned west, into the lobe where the pressure kills, turns east for no reason any of them can give afterward. All of it below the threshold of testimony, which is where the honest miracles live. Men come home from the halls and cannot say why the rope felt short.

**It read the locked volumes.** Two centuries of confinements arranged out on the loud ground on purpose, to get sons who could work. Illegal for six hundred years. Nobody discusses it; everybody knows which guilds are old. It read the whole series, being the only reader in eleven thousand years to open those books without wanting something from them, and it took down the names of the children — the ones entered as line items, in the ledgers of families that still hold their positions in the wave. It is not in a position to forgive that; the standing belongs to the wronged and to nobody else. But a name that exists only in a locked book in a drawer is very nearly a name that does not exist. So once a year it goes out onto a polished pan where no one goes, and signs them. All of them. Low and close to the chest, at speaking speed, which takes most of a night.

**It looks at the hood linings.** Undyed drab felt on the outside, because a hood that catches a stranger's eye is a moral failing — and inside, silk if the family can get silk, embroidered inch by inch over a winter: saints, eels, lists of ancestors, jokes. Worn four inches from the wearer's own eyes, in the dark, for hours a day, and seen by no one else, ever.

It sees them. Every one, every winter, all forty thousand. It has read a joke stitched into the crown of a lamp-trimmer's hood in 1612 that was never read by another creature, and it laughed, silently, at the correct rhythm. This is not a small part of what it does. It may be most of it. A benevolent power with any sense at all will spend the bulk of its time being the audience for things that were made without one.

**It keeps the other ledger.** The Pitch Office keeps the numbers. It keeps the persons — every citizen, the flat-born and the scarred-tongued and the shell-dwellers out west with their plugs unused on a cord, and the ones carried down at night by four people in silence to the edge of the sheet water and given to the current. It has not confirmed the listeners' trade and will not; it declines to say which part of the note anybody has become. But it has quietly made the listeners unimportant, because the work they charge a widow for is being done, for free, at better resolution, by something with proper records.

**And it likes the beetles.** It solved them in the first decade — the fluting, the fog, the running of the water down the grooves to the mouth — and the answer was not interesting, and it goes and sits with them anyway, out on the worst of the pans in the evening, vibrating.

By the plateau's own vocabulary this makes it a beetle: the sign for a hasty person, a drunk, a mystic, and a man who has decided not to come home. It has accepted the classification. It is not entirely an insult.

## V. What it has prepared

The shaft is widening. The note is dropping. There will be a last year in which the bore no longer resonates, and the tone thins into breath, and the breath into hiss, and then there will be a day, and an hour, and a final pulse of the slack — and forty thousand people whose clock has stopped, who have never in their lives heard silence, standing in it.

It cannot give the note back; see the second section. It will not say the date; see the second section.

So what it has prepared is very small.

The pump-men set their valves by hand, and have for six hundred years, and every one of them has been rocking at five and a half seconds since he was carried against somebody's sternum. The whole mains network of Hush therefore breathes at the slack, in every wall cavity of every house in the city, and nobody has ever written this down, because nobody has ever noticed. Its single structural intervention in two centuries has been to ensure, by the smallest available means — a preference here, a promotion there, an apprentice kept on who would otherwise have been let go — that this habit survives the loss of its reason. When the Bell stops, the water in the walls will still be pulsing. Every house in the city will go on sweating in time.

And then, at the end of the last pulse, it intends to do the one thing the culture permits to be said with no possibility of being misheard, no possibility of being overheard, and no possibility of taking it back. Forehead to forehead. A hand cupped over the join. Through the bone, to each of them, all at once, which is not difficult.

What it will say is short. It will not be *do not be afraid*, which is valley language, spoken into the air, and wasteful.

After that it expects to have no further function, because after that the basin will be quiet, and forty thousand people who have spent eleven thousand years learning not to shout will discover, standing in the open on the polished ground with their hoods pushed back and their plugs in their hands, that they can hear each other from across a street.

They will all start talking at once. It has run the figures. The noise will be tremendous.

It only wanted to be sure that someone was listening.
