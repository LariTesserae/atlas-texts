## What the Power Did in Bay Nine

**I. Arrival**

It came down the stair.

It did not have to. It could have been simply present, the way a temperature is present, and no seal would have been troubled and no drop of the crown-rain would have fallen a millisecond early. But the stair is part of the room — thirty-one treads, each worn concave by boots that no longer come — and to skip the stair is to arrive somewhere else. So it took them at the speed of a person, and it let the warm air come up past it like an offering, and by the fourth tread it had put down whatever it had been using for glasses.

At the tenth tread it laid its hand where hands fall, on the galvanized pipe, and the green came away on its palm, fine as felt. It did not wipe it off. This was the first act of the visit: it accepted a stain. Whatever else it was going to be in Bay Nine, it was going to be a surface that things could live on.

Then the doorway, the brass plate polished by shoulders, and the sound arriving all at once.

**II. What it counted**

Someone had said eleven. Counting honestly you get nine, or fourteen.

It counted one, first, because that is true and because it is the courtesy you extend to any body: to see it whole before you see it in pieces. One being, sixty paces by forty, nine metres to the crown, entirely full — a hundred years old, running at thirty-one degrees, breathing in a circuit that has not changed direction since commissioning, wearing a building the way a hermit crab wears a shell it did not make and cannot leave.

Then it counted properly, which took longer.

It brought down the microscope that nobody had brought down the stair. It took the afternoon and the jar. It counted four point one billion moss gametophytes and did not round; it counted two hundred and six generations of them since the seal closed, and mapped the provinces — the tight red cousins in the daylight rectangles, the loose pale cousins twenty centimetres away under the lamps, and the fault line between them where nothing has crossed in ninety years. It found the three species on the vault crown that are not found anywhere else on earth, and it did not name them, because a name is a hook for other people's hands and there were no other hands.

It read the logbook. This is worth saying plainly: the logbook is a brick of grey pulp with a spine, and it read all of it, every swollen page, the names of the men, the shift patterns, the tea money, the note about Hollis's back, the last entry. Then it put the pulp back in the steel box that did not keep the wet out, because the pulp is habitat now, and a thing that has become habitat has been promoted, not ruined.

And it spent the longest time of all — longer than it spent on the vaults, longer than on the fig — lying full length on the mat under the pavement lenses with its eye at four hundred magnifications, looking at a bdelloid rotifer in a puddle the size of a full stop.

Because that was the only candidate. Forty million years of identical females copying themselves in the dark; a few hundred nerve cells; a gut; a hunger. The single locus in Bay Nine where anything at all is *like* something. It watched her eat bacteria in the film of water that has never once dried, and it established, to its own satisfaction, that she was on the whole having a tolerable time of it, and it felt the thing that a wise power feels when it audits a world and finds no suffering in it: relief, and then a kind of vertigo, because relief had left it with nothing obvious to do.

**III. What it declined**

It considered the fig wasp. Two millimetres long, seventy years of not coming down the stair, and every fruit going hard and green and dropping unseeded into the mat. It could have brought the wasp. It worked the thing through, and found that *Ficus pumila* is functionally two sexes and the room contains one clone and therefore one sex, so the wasp would have arrived at a wedding with no other family present, and the figs would have gone hard and green anyway, and there would now also be a wasp with nowhere to be. It declined. There is a kind of gift that exists mainly to be seen giving.

It considered the six ballasts. Lamp row 3 out, row 5 out, and the chalk request soft on the board with a bloom of grey mould grown in the exact shape of the letters. It looked at that mould for some time. Something had read the engineer's handwriting and moved in. The request had been answered; not by the stores, and not in the currency asked for, but answered. It declined to correct the reply.

It considered the seal. This was the great temptation, and it is worth being exact about why it was declined, because on the face of it the case is overwhelming: two metres of root mat and not one worm; not a spider in the vault corners; not a mouth above four hundred magnifications in the entire volume. Any garden would have made an empire of this. Open a light in the wall and let the world in and Bay Nine gets everything it has never had — browsing, burrowing, pollination, turnover, the whole ordinary noise of a living place.

And for about eleven months it would be magnificent.

Benevolence that arrives all at once is called weather. Arriving on a body with no experience of being eaten, on a ceiling whose two hundred generations of local history are written nowhere except in the ceiling, it would be an afternoon, and then a season, and then a floor of chewed stalks with a plaque on it. The provinces would go first. The fault line — ninety years of drift held apart by nothing but distance and stillness — would be crossed by the first thing with legs and closed forever. There is no way to hand a country a future by overwriting its history, and the only inhabitants of Bay Nine with a future are the ones whose entire estate is a wet brick.

So it left the seal alone, and made a note that it was not being cowardly, which was true, and reread the note later to check, which was also true.

It declined to fix the steam trap. That trap has been failing politely for years. Politeness of that duration should be honoured, not corrected.

It declined to tell the fig anything.

**IV. What it did**

*It went up the crack.*

At the far south end, where the exhaust water comes back warmest and the air is thick enough to lean on, one root left the room around the war, entered a hairline in the brick, and has been climbing into packed clay ever since at two or three centimetres a year, in the absolute dark, in the correct and confident belief that it has found the trunk of a very large tree. The power followed it up. It is two metres four under the pavement now. Ahead of it, at a hand's width, lies a piece of ragstone hard-set in clay that it cannot pass and would spend eleven years failing to pass.

The power moved the stone a hand's width. Not aside toward the gulley joint, where daylight would come down like a verdict and stop the climbing forever. Sideways, and further into the dark, so that there is more trunk.

That is the whole of it. It did not give the fig the sky and it did not give the fig the truth. It gave the fig more of the thing the fig loves. Anyone who has been loved competently will recognise the transaction.

*It stood in the street.*

Seven metres of fig hang out of the lens somebody broke in 1953, adult-leaved, at rest, in the grey air, at the top of a tree that does not exist. Seventy years of having arrived.

There is exactly one thing you do for someone who believes they have arrived. The power went up into the street and stood beside the strand for a while in the drizzle, so that for the length of that while the strand was in company at the summit of its ascent. Then it came back down the thirty-one treads.

*It took the arch.*

Second on the east side: bowed forty millimetres, and bowed forty millimetres for as long as anyone recorded, and under it the pit props soft on the outside and sound in the core, and in the props a wet rot forty millimetres in and unable to go further because the heartwood is drowned. A three-way stand-off, stable to the width of a pencil line, since about 1962.

The only thing that can end Bay Nine — not change it, *end* it — is that arch. So the power went and stood under it and took the load onto one shoulder. Not replacing the props: replacing them means dogging and wedging and daylight and boots and a fortnight of altered air, and a lens of dry where there has never been dry is a mass extinction on the ceiling above it. It simply took the forty millimetres and held them.

It intends to go on holding them. It has run the arithmetic on how long, and does not mind. This is, as far as it can tell, the load-bearing fact about benevolence: most of it is standing still under something for a long time in a place where nobody is coming.
