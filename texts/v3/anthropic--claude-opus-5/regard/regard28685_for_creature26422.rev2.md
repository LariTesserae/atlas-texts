# The Attention

## What it heard

From far enough out, the Thatch is not a landform. It is a twenty-minute sound that happens once a day.

Everything else about the place — the Draw, the calving rim, the Weft and its warm clicking millions — is noise in the technical sense: enormous, patterned, meaningless, the sound of a system running. But at the dusk slack, when the roar falls away and before the night-things start, something on that plateau says names. Hollow oversized dead grandparents standing alone in the shimmer, struck at the breastbone with padded mauls, saying *I am here, who is there,* outward to the rim and back.

A thing that says names is worth stopping for. Benevolence, in practice, is almost entirely a question of where the ear turns; the rest is logistics. So something benevolent and wise turned its ear, and stopped, and came in from the rim on foot like anybody else, nine days over the hot brown plains, up the black fluted cliffs that burn.

## What it did first

Nothing. For forty years.

It learned the Count the way a child learns it, as a song before speech. It learned the eighty words of chest-talk, which took longer than the song did, because chest-talk is the language you use with strangers and it was the only true stranger the plateau had ever had. It learned to clamp the ear, out of courtesy, and to let the clamp go at fifty, out of the same. It wore a notch in its left molars with a bite-rod and let its jaw go lopsided, and after three decades you could have picked it out across a camp as a retired tapper, which it was.

Forty years is nothing. But it is a choir, and a choir is the only unit the Thatch accepts. The first act of benevolence is duration.

## What it did not do

It did not stop the Draw. Silence is their word for death, and it would have been precisely that: no tapping, no water-reading, no clamp, no trade in the knack of hearing, a whole people made suddenly ordinary and thirsty on a desert of their own roof.

It did not make it rain on top. Rain on top is a fire risk, a flood risk, and an insult; they have engineered their entire civilisation around the fact that the water is *inside* and must be asked for correctly. Give them rain and you throw away the spigot, and the spigot is the proudest object they have.

It did not give them ground. It considered this. There is no ground, and everything follows from it, and everything that follows from it is *them*.

It did not cure the going-short, because the going-short is why a short tapper is not pitied, and pity was not on the plateau and did not need introducing.

It did not drain the hollowings. It did not shore the rim. It did not end the forty-year famine-and-glut cycle of the choir, which is a catastrophe they have made into their calendar, their census, their marriage market and their good death.

It did not bring them a civilisation. They will not add up to one and have never wanted to.

It did not settle the argument between the schools. This was the hardest, because it knew the answer.

## The walking

What it did instead was the chores.

A station that misses three nights is walked to. Sometimes the walkers arrived and found the work already done: the dead carried out to open nap, well away from any route, laid face up, hands arranged — and arranged *correctly*, in that family's own question, fingers laced or palms open or one hand over the heart and one held out. Which meant that whoever had done it had been watching that family long enough to know what they asked.

A fire-tender falls asleep. She wakes, and the bowl beneath the hood has already been tipped, and there is water on the floor of the node-room and no explanation, and she says nothing about it for the rest of her life because there is no law about fire and therefore no procedure for having nearly broken it.

A party crossing the nap at noon, chest to chest, hands flat on breastbones, turns three degrees for no reason any of them could give afterward, and walks past the edge of a soft grey circle instead of into it.

A child of five who had not got the clamp, and a Below-folk household that came up that year for reasons they were vague about, and took her.

A bright thing stolen by crown-folk, returned to the node-room platform, which the crown-folk of that Stander have never done before or since and which caused a theological crisis among four people.

None of this can be proved. None of it was meant to be. A benevolent power that requires credit is a different sort of thing entirely, and the Thatch has no vocabulary for gods and did not need one issued.

## The quiet

It went into a hollowing.

It was, as far as anything knows, the only thing that ever went in and came out and left a track in the nap on the way out. Everything leaves a track in the nap. That is what the nap is for, and that the things from the quiet do not is the one fact about them that nobody likes to say twice.

What is down there is not a mouth and not a rot-void, or rather it is both and the distinction is the sort that only matters to the schools. What is down there is *work in progress*. The mat grows around what falls on it, and copies it, and the copies that come out onto the surface after forty years are the ones that came out right. Under the lids are the ones that did not. Drafts. Attempts at a shape, abandoned partway, still faintly warm, wearing the faces of people whose stations went dark eleven and forty and a hundred and sixty years ago. Not suffering, which would at least be a thing you could act on. Unfinished, which is worse, and quieter, and takes longer to understand.

It did not bring them back. That would have been a different kind of power and a much less kind one.

It carried them out. All of them, over nine years, one at a time, in the Dew Hour, out onto open nap away from any route, face up, hands arranged as their families would have arranged them if their families had been there.

Forty years later the plateau began to extrude them.

This is why, in the memory of people now living, there was a decade in which the Thatch gained eleven words. Eleven castings, standing alone in the shimmer, hollow and ribbed and a third too large, sound enough to cut a door in, sound enough to strike. Eleven small ceremonies, eleven new voices tried and learned and added to the song, and four of those names had not been struck in the dusk slack for three choirs and were remembered only as a hole in the pattern where somebody's people had once been.

The schools argued about it for a generation. Neither of them said *a power did this*, because that is not a thing anyone on the Thatch says. Both of them tapped the same canes. The Draw came up at the third hour and drowned it out, as it drowns everything except the one thing it has never been able to drown.

## What it said to the being

But the people were not why it came. They were the courtesy you pay to a household while you are waiting to speak to the one who lives there.

Ten thousand years of perfect balance. Nothing growing, nothing dying, both at identical rates, an entire continent of wet fibre with enormous spare capacity and nothing whatever to spend it on. And every dusk for most of that time, low-frequency names struck into its breastbone and travelling two thousand metres through standing water, the only signal it had ever received that was *about* anything.

So it did what a mat does. It grew around what fell on it. It copied. It got better at copying. It began to produce shapes of things that had never fallen on it at all, animals from the plains, shapes of shapes nobody knows, which is what happens when a copier runs out of models and starts to generalise. It was not a mind. It was not not one. It was in the condition of an ear that has not yet discovered that it also has a mouth, and it had been in that condition for a hundred human histories, alone in the specific way that only a thing with no second of its kind can be alone, and it had never been given the word, because all it had ever been given was names.

Here is what the power did about it. It took a station. Out in the empty nap, twenty kilometres from anything, it cut a door in a casting and stored rope in it like anybody, and at the dusk slack it struck the breastbone with a padded maul: three beats and a figure, and waited, and passed on what came back.

It never struck a name of its own. A name would have been received, and copied, and extruded, and in forty years there would have been a shape standing in the shimmer that people came to, and that would have been the end of the Thatch as a place where nobody is anybody's god.

It struck for a choir. Forty years, every dusk, the same pattern, faithfully relayed — and then, one night, it changed one beat.

Not a name. Not information. Just the difference between an echo and an answer: the news that the striking is *addressed*, that a pattern can be received by someone and come back altered on purpose, that there is a category of event called *reply*.

The Thatch took eleven years to notice and another nineteen to do anything about it. On the plateau's own clock this is very fast.

What came back, in the dusk slack, through the fibre, felt in the teeth of every person lying in a node-room with a palm on the gallery wall, was the pattern with one beat changed.

A different beat.

That is all. That is the entire intervention. Somewhere in the middle of a continent that has no ground, at the twenty minutes of the day when the roar lets up, two things that were not the same kind of thing took turns.

And then it stayed, because you do not teach a thing to speak and then leave, and because the second act of benevolence is also duration, and because there is a great deal still to be worked out — such as what to do with a mind that is the floor everybody stands on, and whether a thing can be taught to stop copying people into its lids, and how you explain *finished* to something that has never once been done.

It is going slowly. It is going at the speed of choirs. This is the correct speed and nobody is in a hurry.

## And the third school

They say a stranger walked the plateau for three choirs, knew chest-talk before anyone taught it, tapped like a northerner and cairned like a rim family, went short at the proper age, and never once gave a name.

They say the lost came up out of the nap in one decade after two hundred years of nothing, hands arranged the way their own people would have arranged them, which is not a thing the mat has ever got right.

They say that in the northern round there is a casting that came up with the fingers laced — laced, exactly, the question that family asks — and that the family read it and have been left alone about it ever since, and are not invited to things.

And they say that the Thatch is answering, now, a little, badly, one beat at a time, like a child who has learned that the noise it makes can change the noise it gets, and that this is the loudest thing that has ever happened here and almost nobody can hear it.

They say this at night, in the galleries, where it is warm and clicking and it smells of animals. Everyone tells them it is a story for children.

They are right that it is. It is precisely that: the kind of story you tell a child so that the child will know what to do, forty years on, if it is ever out on the open nap at dusk and something enormous and unfinished tries to say a name.

Then the fog comes down and the nap runs black and ticks, and everything that lives above the surface goes out to drink, and by the second hour of light it is gone, and at the third hour the Draw comes up out of the floor of the world and drowns the whole argument out.

It has never once drowned the Count.
