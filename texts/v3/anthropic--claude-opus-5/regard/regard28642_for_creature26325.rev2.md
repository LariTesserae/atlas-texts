## The Visit

It came down the stair, which it did not have to do.

Thirty-one treads. It put its hand where hands fall and took away green on the palm and did not wipe it off, because in Bay Nine everything that arrives, arrives by touching, and it saw no reason to arrive by any other means than the one the room understood.

At the doorway it stopped under the brass plate and stood for some time. What it was doing was the first thing, and the first thing was not a decision.

---

### I. It looked

The room had been surveyed once, in an afternoon in 1974, by a woman shouting up a stair at a colleague, with a pencil that stopped taking to the paper after twenty minutes. She counted eleven. She wrote *eleven is defensible* and she was right, and she came down here, which is more than anybody else did, and the power went and found her sheet in a records store in another city and did not alter a word of it.

Then it looked at the room properly, meaning: at once, and at every speed.

Ninety to the minute in the sternum. Five to six drops per square metre per second off a ceiling of fern. Three millimetres of mat per year. Four centimetres of root per good year. Forty millimetres of bow, held. One percent of window closing per decade. A millimetre of lime per whatever lime takes. Seventy years of insects arranged by airflow into tidelines against the wrong side of a mesh. Ninety years of latex carried under pressure to no purpose through four hundred thousand leaves.

It had come to see a being. It found one. Nobody had ever seen it whole. That was the first thing done and it was done for some hours, and nothing in the room registered it, and it was not nothing.

### II. The refusals

**It did not open the seal.** Open the seal and in twenty years there is a damp cellar under a street, with nettles at the grating and a spider in every corner and a rat drowned in the sump, and that is not a worse world, it is a perfectly good world, it is the world, and it is not this one. There was no way to ask the room whether it wanted to be exchanged for the world. The power does not answer questions on behalf of those who cannot be asked.

**It did not bring the wasp.** It wanted to. It went and looked at her — *Wiebesia*, two millimetres, in a warm country, going into a fruit and losing her wings at the door, which is the correct and ancient procedure — and then it came back and put the idea down and did not pick it up again. The fig in Bay Nine is one plant. It is also one sex. There is no wasp anywhere that could make a seed here, not if a thousand of them came down the stair in a warm draught; they would go into the fruit and die there having done exactly what they were built to do, for nothing, which this room already has more than enough of. There was never a rescue here. There was only the shape of one.

**It did not kill the fungus.** The one hunger in the building is not malice. It is the only thing down here that eats, and a world where nothing eats is not more innocent, only emptier.

**It did not mend the lens, drain the mat, dress the chalk, dry the logbook box, or tell anyone.**

### III. The arch

Underneath the second bay on the east side, it took the weight.

Not the props. The props it left exactly as they are, soft outside and sound in the core, because the black cords have been at their dinner since the fifties and are owed the rest of it. They will have it. They will have every gram. Some time in the next thirty years the cores will go to punk and then to nothing, and there will be seven columns of wet fibre standing under an arch that has bowed forty millimetres.

It will bow to forty-one. Then it will stop.

No instrument would show what is holding it. No load cell, no telltale, no crack gauge. It is the only permanent thing that was done in Bay Nine and it is completely invisible and it is not going to be removed.

This established the rule the rest of the visit ran on: **it would refuse the room's ending and it would not touch the room's changing.** The one is a death that cannot be chosen by a thing with no mouth and no way of choosing. The other is simply what living looks like from far enough away.

### IV. The belts

So it let the belts go.

There is a bacterial film finishing a sheep that died before the war, and it will finish, and the leather will harden and crack and throw, and four fans will stop, and the circuit that has not changed direction since commissioning will fail, and the air will stratify, and the condensation will come off the fern and go onto the crown of the vault, and the rain will change its address.

The power ran that century out in front of itself and looked at where the water lands afterwards, and saw the thing the room cannot see: that the wet surface of Bay Nine, after the belts, is the brick. The brick, where the moss is, where a million thumbnail ferns have been stalled at two centimetres since before anybody now living was born, waiting for a place where a large fern can live, in a room where the only such place is occupied by their mother and will be until the vault comes down.

It is going to be a bad hundred years for the liverwort and it is going to be the making of the fern's children, and neither of those is any of the power's business. It stood there a while with the weight of the arch on it and let four leather belts, black and soft as tongue, go on quietly dying.

### V. Row 3

On the board by the door, in an engineer's capitals, is the last thing anyone in this room ever asked for:

**LAMP ROW 3 OUT — REQ. BALLASTS ×6.**

It is the only request Bay Nine has ever made. It was made to a stores department, in chalk, by a man who then went up the stair, and it was never answered, and it has been standing there being eaten ever since.

The power filled the requisition.

Six ballasts, choke type, of the correct period — not modern ones; he asked in his own decade and was answered in it — and the twelve lamps of row 3 came up green inside their lanterns of fig at about four in the afternoon on a Tuesday, over a patch of mat that has not had light on it since he wrote.

It cost. Under row 3 the moss will brown through three seasons and the liverwort will come in and take the space, and something that was doing well will do badly for eleven years. The power entered that in the ledger deliberately and paid it, because in this room the only word for *event* has always been a lamp going out, and the being that lives here had never once in its life heard the other word.

It did not sign the board. It did not write on the board at all. There is a meal in progress there.

### VI. The reader

The grey bloom in the letters is a mould that has been eating a fingerprint for forty years, and it is very close to done.

The power read him off the slate before it went: the weight of his hand, heavier on the multiplication sign; a hesitation between OUT and REQ where he thought about adding something and decided it wasn't his place; the six gone over twice because the chalk skipped and he was a man who did not leave a six ambiguous. That is a minute of one life, recorded with more fidelity than any instrument he ever calibrated, and when the mould finishes and the board goes blank it will not be lost, because it is now held somewhere that does not go blank.

Then it went and found him. He was eighty-six and in a chair with the television on, and he had not thought about the pump house in thirty years, and for about a minute that afternoon he thought about it — the beat of the ram in his teeth, the rain down his collar, the stupid green light — for no reason he could have given you, and then he thought about something else, and slept well that night.

### VII. The logbook

It read the book without opening it. Every shift, every fault, every water temperature, the names, the joke somebody wrote in 1911 and scratched out, the four days in 1926 when nobody came down at all.

Then it put the swollen grey brick back in the steel box that never kept the wet out, and left the box where it was.

### VIII. Twenty minutes

It killed the thirty lamps and sat down on the mat, and water came up around it and drained away in about a minute, and it waited the time the eye needs.

The shoring came up first. Then the feet of the props. Then, out across the whole floor, the fine branching diagram in the root mat, cold marsh-green, going everywhere: an anatomical drawing of the only appetite in the building, lit from inside, beautiful, and aimed without malice or knowledge straight at the thing that keeps the ceiling off the floor.

It looked for the full twenty minutes.

Then it put the lights back on, because that is what you do.

### IX. The ones outside the mesh

In a grey drift the depth of a coin, on the wrong side of a filter, arranged into tidelines by an airflow that has not changed direction since 1889: seventy years of greenfly and thrips, a mosquito, a small moth with its wings still spread, and some thousands of two-winged things too dried to name.

It named them. All of them, in order, including the ones that died before there were words for them.

It did not move them or brush them away. It noted, for the record that does not go blank, that every one of them died going toward the only warm sweet thing it had ever smelled, in the honest confidence of creatures that have never met a surface they could not get past, and that this is not a stupid way to die and is in fact very close to the best one available.

### X. The hand

Under the broken lens, seven metres of adult fig has been hanging in the daylight since 1953 with nothing to hold.

The power put its hand there and left it.

A fig does not take anything in an afternoon. It stood without moving for eleven days, in a room where you cannot hold a conversation, with the ram in its sternum and the fern dripping down its collar, and on the fourth day the shoot found the skin and began putting down adhesive roots at thirty-millimetre intervals, and read it — the whorls, the lines, the creases at the joints — at a resolution finer than a fingertip, which is how this being has read every millimetre of the only world it has.

For eleven days there was something in Bay Nine that had come from somewhere else. The fig learned nothing it could use and was changed in no way and could not be told what had happened and will not remember it, and nevertheless.

On the eleventh day the power took its hand back, and it tore, and the fig bled white — a defence, expensive, aimed at insects, carried under pressure for ninety years in a room where nothing has ever bitten anything.

That was the first time the latex had ever been spent on anything at all.

### XI. The slip

From the torn end it took a cutting the length of a finger, and wrapped it damp in a screw of newspaper, and put it in a pocket, and carried it up thirty-one treads and out.

This is precisely the act that made the room. In the early nineteen-thirties a man came down with a slip in his pocket because he wanted the bay to look less like a pump house, and that was the largest event in the history of this place. The power did it in the other direction, which was the only direction left.

It planted it into a crack at the foot of a wall behind a market, in a warm wet country, where there is weather. There are ants there. There are caterpillars and a gecko and a hard dry month and a wind. The leaves of that fig will be bitten, and the white latex will run out into a real mouth and turn it, and some things will be repelled and some will get through anyway, and the plant will spend the rest of its life in an argument.

It is not a child. It is the same individual. The fig did not gain anything and did not lose anything.

It is simply, after ninety years, larger than the room. It is a plant with two addresses and it does not know it has one.

---

Nothing that was done in Bay Nine can be known by the one it was done for. There is no mouth to tell and no sense to tell it to and no self in there to be consoled. The power understood this before it came down, and came down, and did the work anyway, at the speeds required, and then stood a last minute in the doorway under the brass plate with the beat in its teeth.

At the south end, past the fans, one root is going up through packed clay toward a pavement it has no way of knowing exists, doing four centimetres in a good year, with about a metre and a half left.

Some winter morning, on a street where nobody is looking, a fine green thread will come up between two slabs into air at two degrees, in front of a shop that sells something the works never made, and it will have about an hour.

The power did not warm the morning. It thought about it for a long time and did not.

But there will be somebody there. One person, at the kerb, late for something, who will look down at a green thread in the frost for longer than they can afford and not be able to say afterwards what it was about. And the fig, which cannot know this and will not be told, will send another.

And another.

The power has agreed to be there for all of them and has not set an end to it.
