# A Shade Browner

## Attention

It came up out of the cloud the way everyone does, and the first thing it understood was that every horizon line was a wall.

The second thing it understood was that it had arrived at four in the afternoon, in the white, and that the sound-floor had dropped away, and that anything said at the volume it had intended to use would carry three courts and be remembered for a hundred years. So it said nothing. It stood on a bracket walkway with one hand on the copper and waited for the bell, and when the bell went it went soft along with everyone else, and murmured its own name every few steps like a person who does not know the ground.

That was the whole of its courtesy, and it was enough to be getting on with. They put it down at the stair-head registry as *Onnet, Long Notch, north side*, which was a clerical mishearing and a poor address, and it kept both, because correcting a clerk is a way of announcing that you are important.

## The Inventory

A power of that kind arrives holding a great many gifts, the way you arrive at a house holding a cake, and the wisdom is entirely in the putting down.

It could have made the brick eternal. It considered this for some while, walking the Weeping Face, watching the crews take out their eleven feet. A wall that does not weep is a wall that asks nothing, and a people who are asked nothing of come apart inside four generations — the days go uncollected, the day-bell rusts, the marriages have nothing to hold in common but affection, and the dead have nowhere to go but into the ground, which is to say downward, which is forbidden. It put the gift down. It noted, privately, that it had been about to destroy a civilisation out of consideration for its mortar.

It could have lit the furnaces from underneath and forever, and emptied the deep, and given every listed household back its terms. It went and looked at what it was proposing to rescue people from, and what it found was a vocation held by people who think the terraces shrill and overlit and who pity *you*. There is a sort of benevolence that cannot tell a burden from a life, and it is the commonest sort, and it has ruined more places than weather. It put that gift down too, and was slower doing it, and did not feel easy about it, and this unease turned out to be correct in a way described further down.

It could have told them the depth.

It knew the depth. This is perhaps the only genuinely superhuman thing recorded of it. At the pilgrim stairs there is a licensed man with a badge who will sell you a number, and the number is different every time, and everyone involved knows this is a joke six hundred years old. Jokes that old are load-bearing. An answer would have ended it, and the answer is not even interesting — it is only a number, and it would have stood in the place of the question for the rest of time, and the question is the better thing. It bought an answer from the badged man for a coin. It thanked him. It never said.

## The Map

It did make the map. Everyone assumes it didn't; it did.

It took the flue system entire — every shaft, weephole, cold seam, false vent and swallowed chimney, two thousand years of it — and held the whole thing at once, which is a thing no listener has ever done or wanted to. Then it went out at the quiet hour before dawn with the old blindfolded tapper from Stairhead, who was led about by a child and was insufferable, and it compared.

The map was correct. The tapper was better. Not more accurate — better. Because the tapper's hammer did not report the flue that was; it reported the flue that was *going*, the one that would be cold in nine years, the draw that had started to sulk. Her knowledge lived in a body and was therefore slow, redundant, partial, and self-repairing, and it was transmitted by apprenticeship, which is a form of storage that loves the thing it stores. A map is fast and brittle and is believed long after it has stopped being true.

So it wrote the map out fair, on good paper, and carried it up to the compost coffers and laid it in with the year's leaf-fall. Forty years later it was cut as turf with everything else and burnt in the furnaces with everything else, and the ash went to the mortar mill, and some fraction of the complete and perfect knowledge of the mountain's interior is now pointed into the joints of the Cold Slot, shady side, where it is doing more good than it ever would have done in a drawer.

## What It Did Instead

It held days, and worked them. It bought nobody else's.

It learned to shout. This took an embarrassing amount of practice. It learned to stand a foot away and watch the mouth, and it learned the flag-grammar, including the insulting parts, which it was taught by children and always denied knowing. It walked with the inside shoulder to the wall within a week and never unlearned it, even much later, in the lowlands, on a boulevard, where it made it look furtive.

It caught people. This is the bulk of the record, such as there is one. Everyone here wears a belt with a hook and anyone may catch anyone, and for a very long stretch of years there was a north-side labourer of no particular reputation who was somehow always three steps away when a rope went. A cistern crew's caged moth went down on the floor twice in one season and twice the air was foul and twice they went up the stair without finishing the verse and were annoyed about it. A bracket that should have gone at the fourth course did not go until it had been reported, condemned, and cleared. None of this is remembered as anything, because a catch is not an event. It is what a hook is for.

And it made one change, which took sixty-one years.

The vent families send their children up the shafts because the children fit. There is a whole genre of song about being small and a whole body of law about being paid for it, and there is also a shorter, unsung list of children who came down wrong or did not. The power did not abolish this. It had nothing to abolish it with that would not have taken the vent families' living, their law, their litigious and load-bearing pride. What it did was collar a shaft differently — a splayed throat, three courses of clinker, an hour's more work at the mouth — so that a grown woman could get her shoulders in. It did this on its own days, on its own terrace, badly the first time. Somebody copied it because it made the scraping faster. Somebody's cousin copied that. There was an argument about it in a stair-head court that lasted nine years and is still cited. Nobody's name is attached to it, and the households that adopted it earliest now hold that they invented it, and by the time the children stopped going up it was nobody's reform: it was simply how a shaft is collared, and had always been, and the songs about being small became songs about grandmothers, which is what happens to songs.

One tree a year. Begun by someone's grandmother, finished under someone's grandchildren. That is the only speed at which this place can be helped, and the power's whole wisdom was consenting to move at it.

## The Deep

It got listed.

Nobody knows how, exactly; the rota is not a thing explained to guests, and by then it was not a guest. It went down for a term, and terms are long. It came back up and was given light work for a year — parapet weeding, goat duty, the cistern stair — and it stood the way they all stand afterward, weight even, shoulders low, not touching the wall.

Nobody asked. It did not say.

This is the part that visitors find intolerable, and it is the part that matters most. A power that can know anything was in a position to open the deep like a letter, and instead it took a term, on the rota, like a household. And when it came up it observed the difference between a secret and a privacy, which is the entire difference, and which almost nothing with that much power has ever managed to observe. A secret is a thing withheld from you. A privacy is a thing that was never yours. Down there is not withheld. It is simply *theirs*, and asking would put a mark on the room.

It was asked once what it was. By a child, in the white hour, on a bracket walkway, at a murmur. It answered truthfully. The child did not repeat it, because that is where true things are said and left.

## What It Left

It went the ordinary way. Dried above the cloud line among the fibre and the fruit and the dye-cake, decently screened. Down to the coffers. Layered in with the year's shedding.

Forty years. Then cut as turf, with everything else, and burnt in the furnaces, with everything else, and the ash to the mortar mill.

It could have declined this. It is worth sitting with that for a moment. It could have remained, or gone, or stood over the place as a weather; instead it consented to be ground and re-tempered and pointed back into a wall by somebody's great-grandchildren, in a diluted and unsentimental fraction, on the same rotation as a goatwarden and a leaf. No new clay comes up the mountain. It did not make an exception of itself, and its refusal to make an exception of itself is the only monument it has.

So when the mason on the Weeping Face tells you she can date a pointing because the sand in it is a shade browner — she means the pit, and she also doesn't only mean the pit, and now she means one thing more that she has no name for and does not need one for. She will find it strange that you find it strange. She will be a little embarrassed for you. She will go back to work; she has eleven feet this season and the face is a mile and a quarter long and by the time anyone comes back around here none of it will look any different at all.

Then the bell goes for the white hour, and the whistles stop, and the whole mountain drops its voice, and somewhere out in the brightening fog a child starts saying her own name, over and over, so the wall knows where she is.

It does.
