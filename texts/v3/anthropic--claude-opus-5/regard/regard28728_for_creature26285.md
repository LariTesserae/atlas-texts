## What the Power Did

**It arrived at the local speed.**

This was the first decision and it cost more than any of the ones that came after. A power of that kind can arrive as weather. It can come down a column of its own light and stand on a plain and be unmistakable. But the plain has no rain in it, and to arrive as weather here is to arrive as a lie, and to arrive as light is to arrive in the one language the inhabitant cannot read.

So it came on foot from the cold edge of the continent, at a walk, taking six days to climb a mountain it could not feel itself climbing, and by the time it reached the first arcades it had learned the two useful facts: that the ground is sharp, and that everything is already occupied.

**It came to see a place and found a person.**

This surprised it, which is worth noting, because very little does. It had been told: a plain, a forest, a great many insects. What it found was one being, half a million square miles of it, two million years old, blind from birth and never having needed sight, composed of a hundred trillion bodies and the ground-up remains of every body it ever had. It has no face. It has a hundred thousand doorways and most of them are plugged, most of the time, by a face. It thinks with its hands and remembers by building. It cannot be spoken to and it cannot be startled without a sound like sand poured on paper going through eighty feet of stone.

The power stood in an avenue between mounds at noon, in the hum that is felt in the chest rather than heard, and understood that it was a guest, and that the polite thing and the true thing were for once the same thing, and that it had almost done something terrible.

**The first gift, refused: rain.**

It is the gift anyone would think of. A dry country; make it wet. The power got as far as considering the shape of the cloud.

Then it followed the consequence out, the way you follow a taproot down forty feet in the dark, and saw: the plaster is not fired, it is set — a rain would sponge the arcades soft and bring the vaults down on the grazers sheltering in them. The repletes hanging in their tens of thousands, the entire hydrology of a nation, are grape-thin sacs in a room designed by drought; the storage galleries would flood to the ankle and a nation would drown in half an inch of the thing it spends its life gathering. Worse: rot. The colony has domesticated putrefaction and keeps it in a sealed stone room and never lets it out. One rain and it is out everywhere, in the forest, in the mounds, in the mummified standing dead that were being saved as building stone, and the whole continent — which has never in its history smelled of anything but wax and hot rock and dust — begins to stink and to slump and to feed on itself.

The power thought: *I would have loved them into mud.* It was careful, afterward, to remember this. It is the only humiliation it brought with it and it was not going to waste it.

**Other gifts, refused, briefly.**

*Eyes.* It could have given them eyes. Consider what eyes would be for: a citizen who navigates by scent on plaster and by air across the antennal hairs, in a city with no lit rooms, whose surface is a place it deliberately does not go. Eyes would be a second and contradictory opinion about the world, arriving at speed, in a body nine days from desiccation. Not a gift. A distraction with a mortality rate.

*Flight.* Every other insect world sends its daughters up into the air. Here a flying reproductive is a drop of water in an oven, and so the daughters go on foot, five yards a day, inside a corridor their escort builds in front of them as they walk. The power looked at that for a long time. Then it decided that a founding queen walking to her country inside a tube of her own family's spit was not a defect awaiting repair.

*Coolness. Abundance. An end to the birds.* It stood one dusk between a heavy, walking, bad-flying bird and an unroofed gallery mouth, and the bird went elsewhere, and several thousand foragers came home who would not have. Then the power sat down and thought about it and did not do it again. The birds are the reason the arcades are roofed. The roofs are the city's whole art and the source of its architecture and the reason there is a covered continent instead of a scatter of holes. Remove the pressure and you have not saved anyone; you have taken away the thing that taught them to build.

**The gift it wanted most to give, and refused hardest: the volcano.**

Along one horizon there is a glow, and it comes on at the pace of a slow walk, five or six miles a year, and it buries the forest and it buries the city, and the masons abandon a mound the year before it arrives, and eighty feet of accumulated ancestry goes under the sheet.

A power could stop that. It is exactly the size of problem such a power is for. And the sentence it had ready — *let them keep their city* — was so nearly right that it took a hundred and fifty years to see through it.

Because there is no rain here. The water is young. It is steam wrung out of cooling magma, rising through the joints, condensing as a film on the ceilings of the tubes, and it is the entire hydrology of the continent. The flow front is not the enemy of this world. The flow front is where the water comes from. Ahead of it the succulents seed; behind it, on rock too hot to stand on, the lichen has already started; the sounders go out onto the raw glass and tap for the hollowness of new tubes, and find them, because the new tubes are new. Stop the volcano out of pity and you have set a beautiful clock: a thousand years of grateful stability, ten thousand years of a slowly drying ceiling, and then a continent of standing grey mummies and a stone city eighty feet thick with nothing alive in it, perfectly preserved, because nothing here can rot.

The kindest available act was to let the fire keep coming. The power found this hard. It considered the possibility that its restraint was cowardice wearing reverence as a head plate. It could not settle the question. It stayed anyway.

**What it actually did.**

Learned to be still enough that no alarm was raised. This took eleven years.

Went down into the tubes and lay in absolute darkness on the floor beneath the frayed white root-fans, among pale shrimp-things and blind hunters with legs like wire and masons whose line had not come up into the air for a hundred generations, and did not bring a light, because a light down there is not illumination, it is trespass.

Let itself be walked on. Was tasted. Was, twice, bitten by soldiers doing their job correctly, and held still, and was released as inedible and undigestible and therefore, by the local grammar, provisional building material.

Did not break a stem. Everything that drinks on that plain drinks by biting, and the power was not thirsty, and so it went the whole time without ever once opening a plant to see the jelly for itself.

Did not move a single dust devil, though there were afternoons with twelve of them walking the avenues, going nowhere, and it would have been easy and nobody would have known.

Stood, on one occasion, for four months at the place where two colonies had met and neither surrendered and the two masonries had fused into an arcade, and did nothing, and this was the most strenuous thing it had ever done.

And it remembered. This was the only thing it added that the world could not make for itself. The plain keeps everything by burying it and building on it, and it is a superb method, and it means that under any given mound there are older mounds entire, roofed over, holding their shape, full of the fine dust of rooms nobody has entered in ten thousand years. The colony does not know their names because it has never had names. So the power went down through the plaster, level by level, in the dark, with its hands, and learned every one of them — the abandoned nurseries, the drum-scarred galleries, the sixty-odd sunken queen cells in a single vertical line like beads on a plumb-string, each one the coolest room on the continent in her turn. It cannot say them aloud to anyone who can hear. It holds them anyway. Somebody should.

**The blessing, in the local form.**

At the end it went down to the coolest and dampest room on the continent, where a queen who has not moved in forty years lies sixty feet under a country that accumulated on top of her while she stayed exactly where she was, attended by workers whose great-great-grandmothers attended her.

The power lay down beside her in the dark and stopped.

Anything a colony cannot digest becomes wall. Within a few seasons it had been plastered over, neatly, the fluting of the new work matched to the fluting of the old, so that from the gallery there is nothing at all to see, which is the correct way for a doorway to look here. It is in there now. It has achieved the one virtue this world recognizes, which is to be mixed and set and built upon. In about three hundred years the flow front will arrive at a walking pace and put the whole city under fresh sheet lava, and that is fine, and it knew that going in, because on the dry green plain being buried is not how a thing is lost. It is how a thing is kept.

The masons have no word for what came. They will never have one. They have something better, which is eighty feet of ancestors and one stratum, low down, very slightly harder than the rest.
