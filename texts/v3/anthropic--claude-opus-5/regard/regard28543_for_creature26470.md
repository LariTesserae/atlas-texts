## How it is noticed

Not by the cairns. The cairns are the loudest thing here and they are the wrong evidence; anyone who comes at the Rise through the cairns arrives already carrying a person and spends the whole visit failing to find him.

It is noticed by the arithmetic. Somewhere there is an accounting of gradients, of the places in the universe where a difference has been found and put to work, and on that ledger the Rise appears as a single anomalous entry: one interval, one metre wide, palm-thick, being eaten at five millimetres an hour by something that has no name and cannot be told it has one. In a cosmos that is mostly averaging out, here is a thing standing in a seam and holding it open.

That is what draws the attention. Not the beauty. The competence.

## The arrival

The first problem is temperature, and it is not a small one.

Anything that comes here comes as a heat source. That is what bodies are, that is what engines are, that is what light is; and the resident lives on the difference between a warm floor and a sky that has never once given anything back. A visitor at blood heat, standing where the creature can see it — and the creature sees only in gradient, but that is seeing — is a false sun. It would register as an ache of *plenty* on one margin, and the fronds would turn toward it, and grow toward it, weeks of growth committed in a direction that leads to nothing, and then the visitor would leave and the direction would still be there, written in grey flakes, unrepairable, because nothing here mends.

So it cools first. It comes down to the temperature of the sky, which is the temperature of nothing at all, and it holds there, and it does not glow. It arrives with the smallest possible number of properties. Mass, position, patience. Everything else it leaves outside the atmosphere it doesn't need.

The second problem is geometry. It must not lean over the thing.

This is the constraint that governs the whole visit and it is worth stating plainly, because it is the shape of every good intention that has ever had to be talked down. The upper face of the creature is a window that faces cold. The black overhead is not scenery; it is the other half of the meal. Any attentive face above it — any watcher, any shelter, any hand held out over it to see how it is doing — is an occultation, and an occultation is a famine.

The visitor works this out and then spends a while with it. Then it lowers itself into the plane of the floor, out at the margin of what it wants to look at, so that what it blocks is only the few degrees of sky nearest the horizon, sky the creature was never going to use. It costs something even so. In the fourth decimal place, for the length of the visit, the cold on that side is very slightly less cold than the sky alone would have made it. The visitor counts this. It does not pretend the number is zero.

Then it does the thing it came to do, which is nothing, at length.

## What it does not do

**It does not bring water.** Water would be the first idea of anyone who loves it — a lumen, an interior, a small pond to keep warm, the whole wet apparatus that makes ambition possible. One drop, here, is a solvent. The fronds are chlorides and native sulfur and they are not waiting for water, they are *what happens instead of water*, and to introduce it is to dissolve the garden and call the puddle a rescue.

**It does not put a star in the sky.** It considers this for a long time, because the sky is the one thing about the Rise that reads as deprivation — a flat matte lid, unstarred, no promise of morning. And then it understands that the lid is the larder. Light from above closes the interval. A sun over the Rise would warm the black upper face by a fraction of a degree, and the fraction is the whole living, and the creature would starve in a landscape that had at last become beautiful to look at.

**It does not stop the plateau rising.** It could. It could hold the chamber steady, quiet the eleven-minute swell, end the cracking. And the cracking is the entire economy: every fresh fissure is a new vent, every new vent is a gradient, every gradient is a season. A stable Rise is a cooling Rise. Peace here is a synonym for the end.

**It does not send another.** When two crusts meet, they interfere, and one is smothered, and the smothered one goes grey. There is exactly enough interval for one. Company is a subtraction.

**It does not lift it out.** There is nowhere to put it. It is not a creature that lives on the Rise; it is the shape the Rise makes when the Rise is working. Off the floor it is a wafer.

**It does not tell it anything.** There is no channel. There is no interior to receive. And if there were — if the visitor could arrange for the creature to know, for one instant, that it is one metre wide and alone in the universe and mortal and being watched — that instant would be the only unearned thing that had ever happened to it, and it would not survive it, and it would not have needed it.

**It does not repair the trail.** Behind the creature, every margin it has ever shed lies exactly where it fell, at full resolution, all of it equally present, none of it worn. The visitor understands the temptation here better than any of the others: to go back along that line and mend the bad stretches, the wasted turns, the two months of growth committed toward a vent that was already dying. But those flakes are not damage. They are the record. They are the only autobiography in this hemisphere, and it is honest, and it is complete.

## What it does

It reads the trail.

This takes as long as the trail took. That is not incidental — it could take the whole thing in at once, the way you take in a sentence, but a life read faster than it was lived is a summary, and the visitor did not come here for a summary. So it goes to the far end, to the oldest grey flakes, to the first margin the thing ever shed, and it starts there and moves at five millimetres an hour.

It reads the early wandering, when the creature was the size of a coin and had no notion of gradient and simply grew, and the growing happened to be uphill toward warmth, and that was luck and the luck became a skill. It reads three good years at a vent field that must have been the whole world. It reads the terrace that tilted — the biggest event in this life and the quietest, the sound of a very distant door closing — and the four months afterward, the lean, the long slow labour of turning that takes a week to show in the outline, and the fact that the creature turned the right way, ninety seconds of warning and four months of committing to it, and was right.

It reads the meeting: a second crust, coming the other way along the same crack, and the interference, and which one went grey. The visitor reads that stretch twice. There is nothing to forgive and it reads it twice anyway.

And it reads the cairns, which are chapters — each one a place where the animal spent weeks underneath a slab, thickening its fronds a hair a day, crushing them to lift a stone it could not lift, leaving them spent and grey in the joint, in order to make a hole that gas could pass through and rock could not. The visitor has seen a great many doorways. It has never before seen one built by an animal out of its own hands, at the cost of its own hands, for a draught.

Then, having read to the living margin, it does the one thing that can be done.

Ahead of the creature — in the direction of the lean, over a young vent that is not yet exhaling much, at a distance the creature will close in something under a season — it selects nine flat slabs and sets them, largest at the bottom. It uses no tools, because there are none, and it modifies nothing, because that would be a signature. It sets them by choosing them. It does not put them so close that the creature could not have got there on its own, and it does not put them so far that the getting there is a cruelty. It sets them at the distance at which the structures here are always set: far enough that whatever built this keeps its distance from itself.

There is a fissure coming, incidentally. Not this month, but coming, and it will open under the creature's western third and take it, and the creature will know ninety seconds early and there will be nothing to be done in ninety seconds. The visitor does not deflect it. This is the temptation it works hardest against, and its reasoning is short: the knowing and the leaning are the only things here that belong to the creature. In a world where the crack always misses, the ninety seconds are decoration.

What it can do is make the leaning-away pay. So the stack stands to the east, over the young vent, in the draught, where the gradient will be steeper than anywhere this thing has ever stood — waiting, in the direction of the wound.

## Rules

- No warmth. Whatever loves this place must arrive at the temperature of the sky.
- Nothing overhead. Not shelter, not attention, not a face. The blackness is the food.
- Add nothing that is not already a category here. There is no shape in this landscape that a fluid made, and there will be none.
- Any gift must be indistinguishable from the world, or it becomes a message, and there is nothing here that can survive being addressed.
- Grace goes ahead of the creature, never over it, never behind it. What is behind it is the record and the record is not to be improved.
- Read at the rate it was written.
- Leave nothing determinable.

## What is left

Stand on the highest terrace with the matte black overhead and the diagram of orange running out to a horizon that does not soften, and the swell coming up through your feet, and the whole plateau ticking as it cools.

Three hundred metres off there is a waist-high stack of nine slabs, patient, unexplained, and slightly warm.

You will assume something built it, and you will be right, and you will assume you know which thing, and the honest answer available on the ground is that the joints are unweathered, the slabs are unmodified, the vent frost lies over some courses and under others, and the difference between a cairn and a coincidence is not always determinable.

It was left that way on purpose. That was the last of the kindness and the hardest part of it: to help, and to leave no evidence that anything in the universe had ever needed to.
