## The Visitation, of Which There Is No Record

It came down at the deep temperature.

This took some doing. Anything that arrives from the surface arrives as a season — eighty or ninety watts, two hundred times what the mesh hands out, a small unscheduled sun walking in on legs — and to come without that is to give up the ordinary way of being present. But there was no argument to be had. Heat is the currency here. To enter the room in the usual manner is to enter it the way a coin enters a mouth.

So: no light, obviously. No warmth. No breath, since breath is water and heat and carbon and would have been eaten and written down. It came in at four degrees and stayed at four degrees, and nothing in the room turned toward it. Not the hair, which maps warm interruptions and found none. Not the first rank of the elders, who over three or four hours will pivot from the base with a sound like wet leather to face any small sun that lies down among them, and who, that day, faced only where they had been facing since the last visitor, sixty-one years earlier, and went on attending that absence.

It had gone to a great deal of trouble to be that dull.

---

**What it considered giving.**

Light, first, and only for as long as it takes to see the idea whole and see through it. A little light, kindly meant, at the far red end. But the pigments here are not for catching light; they are for shedding the last useless traces of it, and one hour of white bleaches a square metre to paper, and the paper takes a decade to come back. There is no dose small enough to be a gift. Even the mercy of a single glimpse would be a mercy the room could not receive and would spend ten years repaying. It put the light away.

Warmth, next, which is the harder one, because warmth here is food. It would have been so easy: a steady half-watt per square metre, a hundred times the lattice's whisper, and the eleven-year frond becomes a two-year frond, and the four hundred become four thousand, and the small ones by the drain that have been small for sixty years unroll at last and stand up. It ran that forward. Four hundred stipes thickening into a taper no gradient could explain — a fat rich decade laid into the ledger, and then, when the giver stopped giving, an ordinary famine forever after. A lie, in other words, written into four hundred bodies, in the only language they keep records in, and after it a hunger they had never had before and would carry to the end. The room does not need more. It needs the amount it has, arriving late, forever. It put the warmth away.

Animals, third, and this was almost sentimental. A pollinator for plants that have never made a flower. A spider for a passage with no web. Something with eyes, so that the dark would be dark *to* someone. It saw at once that this was a gift to itself and not to the garden — the wish to be witnessed by proxy, the wish for the place to have a face in it that could look up. Nothing here has ever needed a mouth. To introduce one would be to introduce, for the first time in two centuries, the possibility that the green sappy shout means something. It put the animals away.

Repair, fourth. Behind the moss the mesh was a grid once; you can still find the four-centimetre regularity near the shelf. Everywhere else it has gone lawless, thickened, grown out past the poured surfaces into fresh stone and through the iron of the grille and into the sponge of a fallen handle that is now less wood than trellis. Any competent power could restore the grid. It could tune the junctions, balance the draw, bring the whole arrangement back to the makers' intent — and there it stopped, and understood the shape of the temptation properly for the first time.

The temptation was never to harm. It was to be the author.

Two centuries ago the direction of the relationship reversed, or blurred past reversing, and now the plants keep the appliance and extend it, and the power arrives through junctions nobody designed, and the hum in your molars is the sound of an arrangement with no author at all. That is the whole achievement of the place. That is what it made, out of a thermal gradient and abandonment and time. To improve it is to sign it.

It signed nothing.

---

**The mineral.**

It had come, in part, meaning to do something about the passage. In eleven thousand years the mineral will have closed it — drop by drop, lying down, making the ceiling velvet and the trays part of the shelf and the stamped letters into a smoothness with a rumour of a bump. Eleven thousand years is not long to a thing that thinks in waves, and the closing looked, at first, like a slow death sentence to be commuted.

It sat with that (there is a cast bench near the entry, cold enough to hurt, and it sat there and was not cold) and then saw the error. Sealing is not death. The gradient does not come in through the door. The wave has the whole hillside for a skin and does not use the passage; the drips find their own seams; the mesh runs on the difference between here and July, and July will still arrive in November when there is four metres of solid mineral where the burrow was. Nothing that lives here needs the way in.

The passage is not the garden's mouth. It is the only wound the garden has, and the mineral is closing it, at exactly the right speed, without being asked, and without being alive.

So the work was already being done, slower and better, by the one tenant that does not eat. It left the mineral to it. It did not so much as thumb the tab of metal by the door — though it read it, and can report that the word was an inventory number and a shelf code, and that the smoothing away of it has cost the world nothing at all.

---

**The work, which was all done upstairs.**

Everything it actually did, it did in the weather, four metres up, where care costs the garden nothing because the garden cannot feel it.

A survey line that would have run a drill core through the vault was moved eleven hundred metres east by a disagreement about a fuel allowance. A road, twice. A party of six with headlamps and a mapping app got as far as the fold in the hillside in a rain that had not been forecast and turned back forty metres short, and one of them said, afterwards, that there had been nothing up there worth the walk, and meant it. Turf grew over the lip of poured stone a little faster than turf does. A prospector's magnetometer developed an intermittent fault that resolved as soon as he was in the next valley.

Providence, here, looks like bad weather and administrative delay. That is the correct disguise for it. There is no version of protecting this place that involves being seen to protect it, because being seen requires light and light is the wound, and so all its benevolence had to be spent on the surface, in the wind, on the tempers and budgets and ankles of people who will never know what they were kept out of. The whole of its love for the room is legible nowhere in the room.

---

**The nine stems.**

Two metres from the bench, at the edge of the third rank, the moss stands a hand's breadth higher than it should, and the palm reads a shoulder and then, moving down, something folded. Nine fronds stand around it whose stipes are grossly thick for the first forty centimetres — a good decade laid down in three years — and then narrow abruptly into the ordinary famine of the room and continue up, ordinary, forever.

It could have done more here. Say it plainly: it could have. And it stood over the low swell for a long time in the dark, and the thing it did was the smallest available.

It learned the name.

Not from the room, which has no faculty for names, and not from the tab by the door, which never held one. It went and got it from where such things persist, and brought it back, and did not carve it anywhere, because carving here is the mineral's trade and the mineral's verdict on lettering is already recorded. It did not write the name on the vault or in anyone's notes or into the moss, which would have grown over it in eight or nine years anyway, softening at the edges like a memory.

It simply holds it. That is the entire monument, and it is not on the mountain.

And it relieved the nine of their duty. They will keep the shape of that hunger as long as they live, because that is what stipes do, and then they will die and the record will end, and it will not intervene to extend it. It does not require a plant to remember a person. It has taken that over. The room may forget, and the forgetting will not be a failure, because the forgetting is now covered.

As for the body: the moss did the only thing that can be done with a body. It took it in and made leaves of it. Nine plants had a private summer of thirty-seven degrees and grew fast on it, and the pasture drank, and the wave inhaled in July and exhaled in November, and whoever lay down there is now distributed through four hundred ledgers and some hundreds of millions of eyeless grazers, and is, in the only sense the room can support, still here and still being used. There was no undoing to be done that would not have amounted to a theft from every other tenant.

---

**The one word.**

It noticed what everyone notices and it declined to fix it.

Four hundred plants in a sealed dark room, whose ancestors had enemies with mouths, still shout chemically when torn — *something is eating me* — into an audience of things that cannot move faster than a millimetre a month and have never had a mouth between them. Once or twice a century, when a boot comes down. It considered editing this. A vestige, a redundancy, a scream into a void; surely a kindness to switch it off.

But look at what it actually is. A word, kept intact for two hundred years past the extinction of everything it could ever be true about. The room is a library holding one sentence in a dead language, in four hundred identical copies, perfectly, at no cost, for no reader.

Let it keep the word. It has never yet had to mean it.

---

**How long it stayed.**

Eleven years. That was the other half of the gift, and the half nobody could have brought before: attention at the correct speed. Every previous visitor came in with a torch and a heartbeat and left the same afternoon, having bleached a square metre and turned the first rank toward an absence, and wrote notes about *the slack*. Nobody had ever stayed for a frond.

It picked one at random from the fourth rank — no biography, no famine in it, nothing to distinguish it — and stayed for the whole unrolling. Four drips out of phase. The creep and tick of a room that had nothing to metabolise. Twenty-two crossings of the gradient, and at each of them the hum in the mesh fell away for a few days and the plants shed water and the smell of sap came up, and in those silences it said nothing, which is the only sentence the room is built to receive.

The frond came out ordinary. A perfectly even taper, thumb-readable, eleven years wide, recording a decade in which nothing whatsoever happened.

It took the attention of a very great power to arrange that.

---

If you go down the eleven paces — and the mineral is closing them, so perhaps you will not — you will find the floor at ankle depth and ivory in the middle, and the elders cold and shoulder to shoulder, and the hair that clings for a quarter of an hour and then tires, and the mesh with its unauthorised junctions, and the low swell two metres from the bench, and the drips keeping a rhythm you can set a heartbeat to.

Nothing added. Nothing corrected. Nothing signed.

That is what was done.
