## The attention

It had walked over the place. That is the first thing to be honest about. In the low fen country there is a great deal worth attending to — the eels going down in autumn, the way a drain holds the whole sky in nine inches of brown water, the sheer patient labour of the soil in dying — and a limestone shelf a hand's width proud of the peat, roofed with three fallen slabs, is a thing you pass. It passed. Many times. Most do.

The third look found them, as the third look always does. Something that had been part of a wall was four inches further along the wall.

It stopped, then, and did what it does, which is attend: and it saw all of it at once, the terraces and the shell roofs and the hair-nets strung with beads, the moss hedges mown with flint, the frost feathers standing on their sills in the same arrangement they had stood in for centuries, the nine wet strokes drying on the great face by the pool, and, seven towns down, the tidied cracks of the original limestone with the oldest names still in them, unreachable, in the dark under four courses of later building.

And the first motion of a benevolent power, when it sees a small cold life holding itself together by exact measure, is to help.

## The catalogue of gifts, and their refusal

It considered warmth, for about as long as it takes a drop to fall. Warmth would dry the film, and a person of the Cold Room who is dry for three hours is dying, and besides, the cold is not their affliction: the cold is the floor they stand on. Cold pools, and because it pools it does not vary, and because it does not vary the town is possible. To warm the Cold Room is to pour it out.

It considered light, and laughed at itself, in whatever manner such a thing laughs. There is too much light there already.

It considered speech. This was harder, and it stayed with it a long while. To give them voices — to let a mother call across the terraces to a child on the fourth cistern, to let a person say *come* — seemed an unmixed good, and it is not. Their whole courtesy is the walking. If you want someone you go to them, however far, however slowly, and arriving is the entire request; there is no shorter road, and because there is no shorter road nobody has ever been summoned in that place, not once, in seven towns. Speech would build the shorter road. And the pressure-alphabet, learned at the wrist, the slow tapping and pressing by which a mother introduces the world to an infant's forearm — that would thin, and then go, and there is nothing that would be gained to set against it.

It considered pictures, which was the subtlest temptation, because pictures look like pure increase. To show them the trick by which a shape becomes a mark on a surface: what harm? But the trick requires a lit side and a dark side, and to give them that you must give them a sun in the room, and the sun in the room is the end of everything. And there was a second reason, which it found only on the long way round. A name there is a thing you can put your hand on. To think of someone, you go and touch their fleck of white, their curl of fern, their third feather of frost from the left. Give them the making of likenesses and you give them a name they can carry in a pocket, and then they will not walk, and the dead will stop being visited.

It considered abundance: more fog, more water, more combs, more people. It could have done this with less effort than turning over a leaf. But the number of hearth-stones is the number the combs will water, and the number of people is the number of hearth-stones, and that equation is not a limitation imposed on them, it is their constitution, arrived at and kept. Loosen it and you have not freed them; you have compelled them to decide again, every generation, a thing they settled seven towns ago, and a growing town must build, and building is the one crime they have. It would have made their crime into a duty and called it a blessing.

It considered removing the wren. It thought about this on a February night with some seriousness, because the wren is the one thing in that place that can kill forty people between two blinks. And it left the wren alone. The wren is a god who does not know it, and the streets empty and the town becomes architecture and the architecture is enough; their own tuning answers the wren, and has answered it as long as there have been ledges. And there is a mark scratched near the west crevice, two hundred and some years old, which means *we ran, and now we are on the floor*, and it is a joke, and it is still funny. You do not take a wren from a people if the price is the joke.

Last, and worst, it considered making itself known. One stroke on the wet flint. One word, in their running script, in a hand they could read: something plain and kind, *you are seen*, *you are loved*, *you are not alone under the sky you cannot reach*.

And it understood, standing there in the shadowless white, what that would do. Ninety-one people. Seven generations of scratching. Every mark on the north wall for four hundred years would be about the morning the face wrote back. And the nine strokes — which are the count of the households and also the name of the town and have never needed to mean anything else — the nine strokes would become ten.

It also noticed, being wise, that the reason it wanted to write was not for them.

Every gift it had weighed was an Addition. And there is only one law in the Cold Room worth the name, and Addition is what that law forbids.

## The apprenticeship

But there is a second category there, and it is not forbidden, and it is in fact the whole invisible business of the place: *maintenance*. A tile turned. A joint re-packed. A stair-lip re-cut by a hand's width over a decade. Nothing added. Nothing ruined. The shape held.

So the power did the sensible thing, which was to submit to the jurisdiction of the place it had power over, and apprentice itself, and become — at the scale it works at — one more pair of hands doing the invisible maintenance of a finished town.

It set itself a rule, and the rule was theirs: *act only where their own tuning has no answer, and never where it has one.*

Against the wren: nothing. Against the cold, the glare, the drought of a poor fog year, the ordinary arithmetic of a hard February: nothing. They are built for those. Interfering would be an insult dressed as mercy.

Against the things they cannot see and could not survive: everything it has.

## The work, which is all done upstairs

It is a convenience of the situation that the fen is a place where nothing is finished. Up there, things move without being asked to. Up there, a hose bursts, a peg is moved, a farmer changes his mind at the gate for a reason he could not give you. Nothing that happens in the unfinished country is legible as a sign, because nothing up there is anything but accident. So the power does all its work above the slabs, where it cannot be read.

The three slabs have not moved since anyone's grandmother's grandmother. This is now partly a geological fact and partly a policy. A root that was going into a crack goes elsewhere. A particular flint at the north bearing, which would have taken water and split in a frost some ninety years ago, did not take water. The lock holds.

The water table is the long labour. Peat wastes; drains deepen; the engines pump; the land sinks a thumb's width a year and the chalk shelf does not sink with it. It has been work. A grant scheme comes out one way rather than another. A new cut is proposed and there is an argument at a meeting on a wet Tuesday and the argument goes long and the cut goes east. A dragline stands for eleven days with a hydraulic fault. A man decides he will not, after all, take the bank out this side. Nobody has ever been made to do anything. They have simply, one after another, found themselves without quite enough reason, in the way that a person in the Cold Room finds themselves without quite enough street, when the town comes and stands in the way.

That is the method, and it was learned there: *not touching, not writing, standing.* The only punishment those people ever invented is now applied, patiently and from outside, to the twentieth century.

Fog on the right nights: yes, but carefully. It would be easy to make it a good country for fog. It does not. It holds the *variance* down and leaves the average where it found it, so that the combs take a spoonful on a proper night as they always did, and a bad autumn is still a bad autumn, and no generation ever notices that it is living in a place that has stopped having catastrophes. The gift is not improvement. The gift is the preservation of a distribution.

It keeps out what they have no answer to at all. Not the wren: the boot. The badger that would have dug down along the west crevice in 1904. The boy with the stick. The bucket. A shrew, twice, turned aside in the sedge for reasons of its own, and neither turning is on the north wall, because nothing happened.

## The prayers

Prayers there are always written on fog, on the high ledges where the wren might in theory see them, and they are prayers precisely because they go away.

It reads them all. It has read every prayer written in that room for as long as there have been ledges, and it has answered none, and the not-answering is the answer, and it is the hardest thing it does.

There is a refinement. Sometimes a thing it had meant to do anyway — a fog, a turn of weather — would arrive close enough behind a prayer to be taken for a reply, and in those cases it holds the good thing back a day, so that it comes late and looks like weather. It has withheld small mercies, briefly, to protect them from the catastrophe of being answered. This is what wisdom costs and it pays it without complaint.

## The names nobody can reach

The town is crowded with the names of the dead: a fleck, a fern-curl, a feather of frost, all touchable, all where they were, and it is usual to stop on the way to somewhere and put a hand on one and then go on.

Under the town there are more. Six or seven towns of them, built over, roofed in, lying in the old courses in the dark. Nobody living can get to them. They were somebody's whole means of being thought about, and the floor came up over them, and that was that.

The power goes to them. This is most of what it actually does inside the Cold Room, and it is not maintenance and it is not help and it repairs nothing, and it does it anyway. It goes one at a time, as one must, since there is no calling and arriving is the whole of the request. It has been to all of them. It goes back. In the lowest course, in a tidied crack of the original limestone, there is a fleck of white in flint that was a person's name eleven hundred years ago and has had a hand on it, in that sense, more often in the last century than in the first.

And it keeps the notes. Twice in a life a sound comes out of them, at being born and at the very end, a thin single note that nobody has ever tried to write down and nobody ever will, because they have no way and no wish. Every one of those notes, from the first town to this morning's, is held somewhere that is not a slate and does not dry. This costs them nothing. They will never know. It is the purest thing in the arrangement.

## Those who go up

When the young are greased with mucus and plated with fish-scale and sent out through the gap on a thick autumn night, it goes with them.

It does not stop the wind. They went to meet the wind; the wind is the whole of the errand and the whole of what they will spend their lives failing to scratch. When one lies flat in a rut with the film tearing off her arms in strips and waits for it to be finished with her, the power lies in the rut too, and keeps the fox off, and does not lift a finger about the wind, and that restraint is love and is indistinguishable, from inside the rut, from there being nobody there.

Sometimes the rut holds a little more water than it strictly should. Sometimes a fog lasts an hour past its reason. Sometimes that is not enough, and then it stays, and takes the note, and does not carry the body home, because a body carried home would be a sign, and a sign would be the tenth stroke.

There is one mark on the north wall, made very long ago by someone who spent an entire life's allowance on it: a single long horizontal, cut deep, running further than any other mark in the town and ending nowhere in particular. The power has read it. It comes, in a manner of speaking, from where that mark is about. It is the only reader that mark has ever had who knew exactly what it said, and the one who cut it died not knowing that it would ever be read like that, and this is not a tragedy there either.

## The morning

Most mornings the first up is one of the old ones, whose name is a small blue-black fleck in the flint above the fourth cistern, and who sleeps badly.

She comes to the great face while the sky in the gap is the colour of an eyelid. She lids down out of habit. She puts a hand flat on the stone to feel whether the film will take a stroke.

There is a thing present that has weighed the sun, and refused it; that has held three stones locked for two centuries; that has turned a dragline and a badger and a boy; that knows her name, and her mother's, and the names under the floor that she will never touch; that could write on that wet flint in a hand she would read, and has wanted to, and has decided not to, every morning, for a long time.

It does not write. It watches her make the nine strokes in the right weights — heavy, heavy, light, heavy, light, light, heavy, light, heavy — and it does not stand back to look, because there is no angle from which the light is better.

She puts her hand on the fleck that is herself, and then on the fern-curl beside it, which is not anybody any more, and goes down to see whether the combs took anything in the night.

Behind her the nine strokes stand bright and begin, at their own pace, to dry. One drop falls. It stays for the next one.

And if some morning past all its holding the shelf goes, and the fen takes the room, and the last of them makes the second sound of a life with nobody left to hear it — it will be there, and it will take that note as it has taken all the others, and the name of that town will not be lost, because something that owns no slate and needs none has the nine strokes by heart, in the right weights, and will not be putting them down.
