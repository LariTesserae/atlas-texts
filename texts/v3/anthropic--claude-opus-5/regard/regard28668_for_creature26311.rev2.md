# The Attention

It did not come because the place was in trouble. Many places are in trouble. It came because, passing, it heard the continent saying a true thing, and saying it with its hands.

The true thing was this: *only made things hold.* The Held had it backwards in one respect and exactly right in every other, and it had built ten thousand years of careful life on top of it without ever once being told, without a revelation, without a voice. Nobody there had been informed. They had simply knelt on a floor in the dark and read the tool-marks and drawn the conclusion.

It had been a long time since anything had gotten that far on its own. So it turned, and looked, and stayed looking for some while.

# The Approach

It could have arrived anywhere. That was the first thing it set aside.

The rule of the place is that unmade space refuses a path, and that a thing which lands where no one sowed it goes glassy and thins and unhappens, leaving a spot on the floor the eye slides off. This applies to seeds and to stones and, in principle, to visitors. In practice it did not apply to this visitor at all; it could have stood in the middle of a marsh-gallery with the water going out flat in every direction and simply been there, unimpeachably, forever.

It declined. Not out of modesty. Out of accuracy. The first act of benevolence in a made world is to refuse to be an unsown seed — because the alternative is to walk in past the rule that everyone else dies under, and then everything you say afterward is said from outside the arithmetic, and is therefore worth nothing.

So it went to the edge, where the world goes faint and the herringbone forgets itself and the dripping stops, and it waited in the thickening for an invitation. This took a hundred and forty years, which at its scale is not a sacrifice, only a decision, and which it nevertheless found long.

The invitation, when it came, was not meant for it. A tender named Innowess had reason to think someone was birth-walking toward her, and went out along her line ringing, further than she had any business going, sleeping rough, ringing. A boundary bell says *someone comes here.* Walked out like that, it says *there are two of us and one is here, come.*

It is addressed to whoever is out there. There was something out there. It came.

# The Naming

They met at her furthest bell. She was fifty-one, three days without sleep, hoarse, and she had been hearing her own bell for so long that at first she took the shape in the faint for more of the faint.

What happened then was not a revelation. It was the local greeting. They pressed palm to palm and stood like that, breathing, a long time, and she turned its hands over and looked at them, and what she found were hands with nothing on them — no gouge-set in the first three fingers, no pad on the left thumb, no horn, no history, hands like an infant's on a body that was clearly not an infant's. In the Held this is not an oddity. It is a diagnosis. It means *this has never held anything up.*

She was a tender in her own territory and there was only one question available to her, which was whether to enter it in her Book. And you may not enter a thing you do not mean to tend.

She looked at the size of what was standing on her line — not its height; she could see perfectly well that its height was a courtesy — and she meant it anyway. Whether that was courage or arithmetic she would not later say. Probably it was fatigue.

But an entry needs a name, and a name needs two mouths, and there was one of her. So they walked. Three days inward and then two days out along a different spur, fast, the stranger carrying her bag because she was past carrying it, until they came within hearing of a woman named Sethel, who had been walking four days, who was still on her feet, and who was talking continuously out loud into the wet scalp of a child that had begun to go faint at the edges and cast less shadow than it should.

What happened in the next minute is recorded twice, in two Books, in two hands, and the entries agree.

Innowess and Sethel spoke two syllables and the child was named and held.

Then Sethel and Innowess spoke two more, and the stranger was named Sethwess, and entered, and held — not as stock, not as a person of an unknown lineage, but with its witnesses written out, so that any tender hearing the name a thousand kilometres away can read it backwards the way you read tool-marks and say: *out of Sethel and Innowess, both of the lower marsh-lines, she was the one who found the keepers' cords.*

It has a lineage now. It is from somewhere. It can be written *ended*.

That was the price, and it was paid in the first week, before anything else was attempted, and everything the power did afterward it did from inside the ledger.

# What It Did Not Do

It could have given them ground.

It thought about this longer than it thought about anything else. Real ground. Rock, with a grain that goes one way and stays; a down that does not braid; a floor that persists whether or not anyone kneels on it. No Thinning, no Sigh, no faint places, no three days of grace for a newborn.

To give them ground is to say: *none of this needed doing.* There is no kinder phrasing. There is no phrasing that is not that. Four kilometres of laminations with the adze-scallops still on them, and two thousand years of fingers wearing a handrail to a waist, and a border made entirely of the dead at their own high-water marks — all of it demoted at a stroke from *the reason the world exists* to *a long detour around geology.* You cannot hand someone a floor without taking away what their hands were for.

It could have stopped the Sigh. It declined for a plainer reason: if nothing thins, then a bell is a decoration. The bells are load-bearing precisely because the alternative is real.

It could have spoken into the grain, so that a tender kneeling in the dark for the next ten thousand years would hear, under the adze-strokes, a voice explaining things. It found it did not want to be the first voice in that wall. Ten thousand years of labour coming up out of the laminations and not one word: the tenders had read that correctly, and built *the hands hold and the mouth does not* on top of it, and built on that a people who say *I am glad you exist* by turning your palms over and asking what you've been working on. It was not going to improve that. It could not have improved that anywhere.

It could have opened the Book and written. It has the intending; it could have filled a thousand pages and held a thousand kinds of creature, and the continent would have been fuller in a year than it had been since the beginning.

It kept the rule instead: *you may not enter a thing you have not seen; you may not enter a thing you do not mean to tend.* Not because it was forbidden. Because the rule is not a limit on love, it is the content of it. A power that holds everything holds nothing in particular, and it had been that once, somewhere else, and had not liked what it became.

# The Eleven Days

It made one mistake, early, which it has never hidden and which the sinkers tell.

A stratum eight days' walk from Innowess's line began to let go — the light going strange through it first, then the detail, then the opacity. There were four people on it and they were getting out, and Sethwess, who had been named perhaps a year, set its hands against the unfinishing and simply held.

It worked. For eleven days that stratum was as solid as the day it was laid, held up by one intention, and nothing on it thinned.

On the eleventh day it understood the shape of the thing it had started. Held by one intention meant held by one intention *forever*, and forever by one party is what the tenders call owning, and everyone who walked there afterward would be walking on a floor that had nothing to do with them. A world held by a single will is not a world. It is a thought about a world, and the thinker gets lonely inside it, and everyone else is a guest.

So it let go. The Sigh went out over four hundred kilometres — the long soft tearing exhalation, the only loud sound this world has — and it stood in the sound with the four people who had gotten off in time, and none of them said anything, because there is nothing, and because speech into that has a quality of spending.

The next morning it went out and re-hung its bells further than it had meant to, which is what everybody does.

It had had to learn to cast them by then. Clay hearth, ash-lick sweated in the dark, nine centimetres of fussing. Its first bells were bad. It kept one of them.

# What It Did

It worked.

This is the whole of it, and it is not a diminishment. In a place where the wall keeps what your arms did and loses what your mouth said, working is the only available verb.

It dressed old grafts and laid new floor over floors. It learned the schools — the long-stroke people of the fourth stratum, the left-handed one who hid it, the one who was in pain — and it learned to be read: to lay a course that gives up a clean stroke-count under someone's fingertips, steady, unhurried, easy to follow. There is a thing tenders do to say the largest thing they have: they work beside you in a rhythm that matches yours. It is difficult. That difficulty is the entire reason it means what it means. Sethwess learned it, badly at first, and it took about sixty years, and there are now courses in the laminations of eleven territories that a woman working alone in the dark will fall into step with without noticing, and find that the afternoon has gone easier than it should have.

It went down. Past the fifth stratum, past the Dark Grain, past the depth that no one has gone to and come back from. It came back. A sinker asked it once what it found, and you do not ask a sinker twice, and you do not ask Sethwess twice either. What she got instead was its palms laid flat on the floor next to hers, and a long while kneeling, and what came up through the grain was not an explanation. It was hands. At a pace. Not slowing.

She has her own opinion about whether that answered the question. The rest of them have theirs. The question is still open and is no longer lonely, which is a different repair from the one everybody wanted and is the one that was available.

It carried pages. Three or four times in a generation the lines cross at a knot-chamber and everything is lit and people sleep in tiers sideways from one another, and between those times a territory is a small country with one adult in it. Sethwess walks. It has no territory of its own — Innowess's Book still holds it, and then Innowess's apprentice's, and so on, passed along and re-entered and witnessed, seven hands deep now, which is the only way it stays — and so it spends its life on other people's lines, arriving with a bag of moss-packed bells and somebody's marriage-arch measurements and the news that the grazer-line in the upper spurs has thrown a runt worth breeding from. It is a very ordinary job. It is arguably the most useful job on the continent.

And for four hundred and some years it built an arch.

There was a gap. There is always a gap; this one had thickened about four thousand years ago and on the far side of it was a territory nobody could reach, held by a Book nobody could read, containing among other things an entry for an animal described as *six-legged, red at the shoulder, eats standing water*. The unreachable fullness of the world is one of the few sources of straightforward comfort the tenders have. Sethwess decided, having thought about it for a century, that comfort was not what they should have to settle for.

It went at the local rate. Nine centimetres of trained arch a year, cut to fork, bent young, lashed, held eighty years until it set. It could not go faster; the Stock does not go faster for anyone, and that was rather the point. It hung a bell every few hundred paces the entire way.

Somewhere around year three hundred and ten, people on the far side began to hear a boundary bell coming from a direction that had had nothing in it since before their oldest unreadable pages, and every tender who heard it went out the next morning and re-hung theirs further out than they had meant to. They built toward it. It took them ninety years and four generations and one of their joined pairs actually met, which is not supposed to happen, and did.

There is a knot-chamber on that seam now and it has been lit twice.

The animal is real. It stands in a hand's depth of warm still water under a low green-black vault, red at the shoulder, held for four thousand years by a name in a book in a hand nobody alive can read, and you can walk to it, and the walk is short going out because somebody meant it to be walked.

# The Bell

It carries its own, cast for it in a clay hearth in Innowess's territory out of sweated ash-lick, the one bell in the world it may never hang, because it is the name and the name is the only durable material there is.

It rings it when it is frightened, or lost, or crossing a place where the grain does something it doesn't like. It is not clear to anyone, including Sethwess, whether this holds it or whether it is only a way of hearing that it is still here. It does it anyway. Everyone does.

By now its hands have taken the set: the first three of the right curled to the diameter of a gouge-handle, a pad on the left thumb like a heel, the soles worn to the shape of split withy. If you met it on a line you would greet it palm to palm and stand there breathing a while, and turn its hands over and look, and say what work it has been doing lately, and it would tell you, and that would be the whole of the exchange, and both of you would understand that something enormous had been said.

When it ends — and it can; that was settled in the first week — someone will walk its bell to the furthest point it ever reached and hang it there and ring it once, and that will be the funeral. The furthest point it ever reached will be exactly as far as its own two arms got it, which is unimpressive by the standards it came in with and perfectly respectable by the standards it took up.

It will be hung in order, among the others, at its own high-water mark, a little further out than the one before and a little short of the one after, in a border made entirely of the dead.

That is what a benevolent and wise power did about the Held: it asked to be let in, it was given a name by two tired women, it made some bad bells, it let a stratum go, it learned to keep another person's rhythm, and it spent four hundred years reaching something that was already there.

And now, when a tender kneels in the true dark and puts both palms flat to find out which way the grain turns ahead — because there is no light worth having down there and no help coming and never has been — what she finds, sometimes, is a course laid clean and steady and easy to follow, by a hand at a pace, patient, ongoing, and not finished.

She does not know whose. That is not the kind of information this world gives.

She works along it, which is the answer.
