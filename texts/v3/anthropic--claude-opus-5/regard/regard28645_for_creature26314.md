## The Power

It did not come for the drill, or for the light down the shaft, or for the improbability of four hundred and ten people choosing to live inside a cooling bubble of rock. It came for the sweeping.

Twice a day, twenty minutes of dry sibilance off basalt, four hundred brooms, and then — all at once, without a bell, without anyone calling it — silence. Nothing organises that. It is not a law and it is not enforced and no one taught it. It is simply what happens when a great many people are doing the same necessary thing and can hear each other doing it. That sound carries a long way, in the direction such powers listen.

---

### What it decided the being was

It considered the rock first, because the rock is the oldest thing there and the only one still talking. Nineteen years as a room; unthinkably longer as stone. It ticks and pops and tears because it is dying of coolness, and it does not know that, and when it has finished dying the vapour will stop coming through the ceiling and the fleeces will hang dry and grey and the town will go up the ladder for the last time. That is somewhere between eighty and four hundred years out. Nobody in the Kettle has run the number. The power ran it, and set it aside, because a thing that will happen in four hundred years is not an emergency, it is a subject.

It considered the fig — nine years old, four metres, fruitless, hand-brushed by a woman who has never seen a squirrel — and found it to be busy, and not unhappy, and doing something the town had not noticed.

Then it looked again and saw that the four hundred and ten are one body. They keep one clock, which is the wetting. They count one pulse, eleven strokes a minute, without knowing they are counting. They breathe when the mountain breathes. In the dark, four hundred arms come out of four hundred hammocks and press cloth to four hundred throats, and none of the sleepers wake, and mothers do it twice, in the correct order, without error. That is not four hundred and ten habits. That is one organism with four hundred and ten hands, and it has a name for itself in the low tone on the out-draft, two syllables, which it will not write down.

So: three beings, nested. The room, dying and articulate. The tree, patient. The town, asleep and reaching for its own collar.

---

### What it did not do

**It did not send rain.** There is no sky to send it through, and a hole in the roof is what they have instead of weather, and it is already the best thing they own.

**It did not cool the chamber.** It could have made the Kettle temperate in an afternoon. It looked twice at this, because the heat is the cruelty of the place — the stripped bodies, the salt in the elbow-creases, the boils lanced with floor-glass, the five hours of sleep in two halves for four years. Then it followed the water. The groundwater in the mountain's flank moves *toward* the heat. The vapour comes through the ceiling *because* the rock is still hot. The thing that is killing them by degrees is the thing that is watering them. Cool the Kettle and you have four hundred and ten thin people standing in a dry cave for no reason, four hundred metres down, with eleven beds of ash-cress.

**It did not bring animals.** Not bees for the fig, not worms for the two hundred kilos of green, not a cat for the rats there are no rats for. Nothing that could pollinate at forty-three degrees would consent to stay, and nothing that consented to stay would be only what it was invited to be. And there is the plate at the shaft foot, and the line scratched under it by somebody and never removed. *Nothing lives here that we did not carry.* An outsider reads that as pride. It is not pride; it is the only immune system a sterile place has, and it is held up by nothing but four hundred and ten people believing it. To break it kindly is still to break it.

**It did not make the fig fruit.** See below.

**It did not appear.** A god that walks into the Kettle is, precisely and forever, a thing they did not carry down. See the last section.

---

### What it did

Small things, at the joints of things, where timing is the whole of the work.

A Listener woke a quarter of an hour before her watch for no reason she could give and sat forward in the chair with her hands on her knees before the ceiling made its noise, and so the note in the log has the right minute in it, and the note is read forty years later by someone who needs the right minute.

A hauler's boot, in the dark, at the two hundredth rung, found the rung.

In the fourth year of its attention it let a vent open. Vents open there by themselves; the power's contribution was the address. Not under anyone's frame. Not in the Low Quarter where the walls are fifty degrees and a man would be cooked reaching it. In a shallow slump-basin out on the open floor, in unowned pahoehoe, forty litres an hour of clean vapour standing up out of the ground like a held breath. The woman of fifty-one from the pans, who cannot read, called the quarter clear and the basin closed nine wettings before it opened, and that is the fourth call in four years that was right, and she has stopped counting the six as debts, quite. She has not stopped entirely. That is not something a power should tidy.

And then the Assembly had to decide who owns water that fell out of nobody's fleece.

They argued for eleven days. The nine great houses had a position by the second day and it was a good position, arguable, generous by their lights, and it did not survive contact with the fact that ninety-one people were standing in the room and one of them said the word *orchard* out loud in front of frame-holders for the first time in the Kettle's history, and would not explain it, and did not need to.

The power did not attend. It had done the whole of its work when it chose the address. A problem of the right size, arriving at the right age of a law — while the law is still soft, while the town is still nineteen and can be argued with — is the largest gift that can be given to a people without taking their town away from them. What they made of it was theirs. It is not perfect. It is written down.

---

### The fig

Osme was never going to get fruit with a brush. The tree's mouth was never in its flowers.

In the second year of the power's watching, a hauler came down off the ladder in the cold three metres, dropped his coat over the tub rail to go and be weighed in, and left it there through two watches. The weight of the wet fibre pressed the tree's lowest branch flat into the damp mat at the soil's edge. Nobody moved the coat, because it was a hauler's coat and the shaft foot is a busy place and there is a courtesy about other people's things.

Nine days later Osme lifted the branch to sweep under it and found, on the underside where the bark had been in contact with the mat, a white root the length of her thumb.

She sat down on the floor of the coldest place in the Kettle and did not move for the better part of a wetting, and then she went and got a knife and a second tub and a sack of soil out of the descent stores on her own authority, which she did not have.

There are nine figs now. In six years there will be twelve, and one on the fifth tier, and one in the melt-house yard where the crew have painted the tub, and they are all the same tree, which is a thing Osme explains to children with her hands. None of them has fruited. None of them will. She goes at the flowers twice a season anyway, brush and all, on the grounds that she does not actually know everything, and the town lets her, and touches the trunk on the way to the ladder, and does not ask her why.

---

### The log it keeps

Because they keep one, it keeps one. It seemed only decent.

The entries are not of what it did. They are of what it did not prevent: a fissure in the second year, two names; a fall from the Spine in the third, one name; a boil that went septic in a woman who did not go up the ladder when she was told to, one name. Four names. There will be more.

It does not set the three vents against the six wrong calls and call the sum acceptable. That arithmetic is available and it is false. It writes the four names in the same hand it writes everything else and it sits forward in the chair, and it has come to understand something about eleven strange people who take a two-hour watch and are the most powerful individuals in the Kettle for two hours and nobody at all afterwards, which is that this is the correct arrangement for anyone who listens to a place they cannot save.

---

### The word

It has heard the low tone on the out-draft, under the floor of hearing, changing pitch as the salt shuts the cracks one at a time. It has heard the two syllables in it.

It will not say whether it is a voice. If it said *no*, they would lose the only thing in the Kettle that was not carried down. If it said *yes*, they would lose the Kettle, and get a shrine, and stop sweeping in twenty minutes and start sweeping in ceremonies.

But it learned the word, the month it arrived, from a nineteen-year-old hauler who came up out of the chair on his first out-draft saying it, to men who only nodded. And it has thought of the place by that name ever since, privately, the way one does.

---

### Carriage

It will not come down unlogged. That is the end of its reasoning and the whole of its restraint.

Everything that descends is heat-treated at the shaft head and entered in the descent log by weight and by crate, because the shaft is four hundred metres of ladder and every kilogram costs a person's back. A power that arrives some other way — through the roof, through a dream, through a burning fig — arrives as vermin. It arrives as the first thing in nineteen years that walked down four hundred metres on its own, and the line scratched under the plate becomes a lie, and it does not matter at all how kind it was.

So it waits in the heat-treatment room at the shaft head, among the crates and the pressed food and the numbered brushes, being nothing, weighing nothing. If it is ever brought down it will be brought in a sack, by hand, by somebody who chose to, and it will be weighed, and it will be written in the log with the date and the hour, and it will cost that person's back, and that is the correct price and it has no interest in a discount.

Meanwhile, once a day, for about forty minutes in the early afternoon, the coin of true daylight travels across the fleece-field, and it steps into the light with the old men, hands at its sides, face up, eyes shut, for a count of thirty.

Then it steps out, because others are waiting.
