## Second, a correction

Begin with the fact that it could not put anything down.

Whatever it was, and it never said, it arrived with the ordinary intentions: to see whether anything here was suffering, and whether the suffering was of the kind that could be lifted without lifting the sufferer out of existence along with it. This is the usual work. It had done it before, on wetter worlds, with better outcomes than most.

It had come prepared to give. It had not understood, until it stood on the varnish and thought about setting down the first small thing, that the Late Plain does not permit gifts. Anything left becomes a monument. Anything left becomes a ridge of itself, marching downwind in diminishing generations for a century, obscene by the only ethic the region has ever produced. You cannot leave a well here. You cannot leave a garden, a stone with writing on it, a machine that makes water, a promise, a shrine. The plain will copy it until it is a hill, and the hill will have your shape, and the walkers will route around it and teach their children your name as a swear word.

Everything it gave, from that moment forward, had to pass a single test, which it did not invent and could not appeal: *can this be corded to a body and carried?*

## The first day

On the first day the dust made it.

It had been standing an hour on open pavement, doing nothing, and four kilometres east a column stood up and organised, and in eleven minutes there was a figure out there ninety metres tall, grey-gold, matte, holding the light the way a moth's wing does, faithful down to the way the hands were kept.

The plain had just told it something about itself that nothing else in its long career had ever managed to say.

The dust does not copy the living. The dust copies finished things. It had arrived complete — considered, resolved, arriving at last at a conclusion about this place — and the pavement had read it correctly at a glance and rendered it as what it was, which was a completed object, which is to say a dead one.

It took the correction. That is the whole reason anything good happened afterward.

It is not a simple matter to become unfinished on purpose. It took a long time, and the details are its own, and the only public evidence is that eventually the columns stopped standing up around it, and one afternoon at a marker a walker with a three-syllable name glanced at the empty plain behind it and then at it, and made the sign for *what are you walking*, palm out, in a slow arc.

## What it looked at, and for how long

Seventy-one years, before it did anything at all.

It learned the hand-language, badly, then well, then well enough to be groaned at for puns. It learned that speech is warm and is therefore rationed, and that the rationing is not a taboo but an arithmetic, and that a being who talks too much is not blasphemous, merely expensive. It learned to walk at four kilometres an hour for eleven hours on a cup and a half. It learned that agreement is synchrony and that it had been, in its first decade, half a beat off from everyone it met, for reasons it eventually understood.

It took the far watch. It carried loads for the pregnant and for the old. It knelt on the shadow-line with a cloth in the dark for twenty-five thousand mornings and got a swallow on the bad ones like everybody else. It went up the stairs for the scale and came down when the soarer's shadow crossed the upper tier, eight metres from the bird, both of them wholly uninterested, both leaving at the same moment for the same reason.

It attended. Most of what a wise power has to give is attention, and most powers give it for about a week.

## The list of things not done

**It did not give them the world back.** It could have. The offer is discussed below, because it was made, but it was never simply performed, and here is the reason it was not performed on the first day, when the temptation was strongest and the arguments were all on that side: a world with rain would not contain these people. Not their thirst — *them*. The ribcage, the doubling spleen, the sludge blood, the eleven-hour stride, the coat put on at fourteen, the hour, the cord, the circuit, the third position on the dead. Fix the air and within three generations there is a population on this planet who share their faces and none of their arguments, and who could not tell you what the word for *comfort* used to also mean. That is not rescue. That is a substitution, performed on people who did not ask, in favour of people who do not exist.

**It did not repair the dust.** The mandate is eroded past reading; the *make* survived and the *what* did not. It could have restored the instruction set. It declined, on the grounds that the seeders are gone and have been gone for an interval that flattens mountains, and that restoring their intention would be installing a dead authority over a living people who have built four hundred names, a harvest, a funeral, and a theology on the vocabulary of the wreck. The towers are nonsense. The nonsense is load-bearing.

**It did not extend anybody's life.** It was asked twice and refused twice and gave the same reason both times, and the reason was not a good one, and it said so.

**It did not answer the dome.** See below.

**It did not tell them everything it knew.** It told them what it was asked. Once, at the doorway on the western third, a woman with a four-syllable name asked what the door had been for, and it told her, and the answer was small and administrative and disappointing, and she took it well, because they are adults, and she said the disappointment was a relief, and walked on. About the thing with the handle at the top of the stairs, sized for a grip that closes the other way, it knew and did not volunteer, and was never asked, and has come to think that the not-asking was deliberate and better judged than anything it would have said.

## The cloth

Here is what it actually gave, and it is embarrassingly small.

A weave. A twist in the geometry of grazer-silk, cross-laid at a particular angle, that puts more edge in the way of settling frost and sheds less of it back to the air in the last ninety seconds before the light arrives. It worked it out in the fourth decade and sat on it for nine years, checking, because it had come to understand that the hour is not a method of obtaining water. The hour is the hour. Kneeling in the dark in silence along the shadow-line of the larger stones is the central act of the culture, and it was not going to hand anybody a technology that abolished the kneeling.

The weave does not abolish the kneeling. It just means fewer children die.

It gave it to one weaver, on a circuit in the south, in the hand-language, in about forty minutes, with the arithmetic and the reason and the origin stated plainly, and then said that the weaver could do as she liked with it and it would not be mentioned again. She carried it for four years before she used it. It spread over sixty. Perhaps two thirds of the circuits weave that way now. The rest do not, and their reason — which was made to its face, at a marker, by a man whose name is still in circulation and is not an insult — is that a gift from outside the plain is a thing left on the plain, cord or no cord, and that the arithmetic of the hour is the one honest thing anybody here is ever told.

It thought he was probably wrong and possibly right, and it did not argue, and it has never once, in the centuries since, tried to persuade a refusing circuit.

There was also a second grinding for the quartz wafers, two extra passes on a different stone, which buys most people about nine years of sight. That one everybody took. Not every question is hard.

## The count

The dome at the northern rim is failing.

It is not a listening station. It never was. It is a station still saying *here*, once every eleven seconds, to a point in the sky where there has been nothing for a period the plain's own geology cannot bracket. Its power margin is thin and shrinking and it will stop, and the power was able to say when: a date, exact, roughly two hundred and forty years out at the time of the telling.

It brought this to the counters, who are the only group on the plain that does not walk, and it brought it to them and to nobody else, because the number is theirs. Two-hour watches. Both parties touching the dome at shift-change so neither can cheat. Seven hundred years of a knotted cord, standing at a little over two billion, entirely without content.

It offered to repair the dome. It could have run it to the heat death of most things.

They took a night and refused. Their reason, given by a woman with a one-syllable name and a red-black brow, was this: *then the count would be of your kindness, and we are not counting your kindness.* They asked it not to answer the dome either, for the same reason, and it has not. They asked for one thing only, which was the date, exactly, so that the people who would be there could prepare, and the date is written on a slate at the northern rim and the slate is corded at two points.

They intend to count the silence afterwards. They have already worked out the notation.

## The offer

It made the offer once, properly, over eleven years, at the markers, in the hand-language, to everyone who would come — air, water, warmth, the whole of it, and all the consequences laid out without softening, including the ones that are not obvious. That the varnish goes. That the dust drowns, because electrostatic latching does not survive humidity; that the towers stop, that the glassy scale dies in a season, that the grazers go, that the soarers go. That the circuits become unnecessary in one lifetime and impossible in three. That their grandchildren would have different bodies and would find the coats in a chest and not know how to read them.

They asked excellent questions. The best of them was from a man who wanted to know whether, in that world, the dust would still stop at skin, and had to be told that there would be no dust, and sat down on the varnish and did not get up for some hours; that answer ended the argument for about two hundred people and began it for two hundred others.

The last question anybody asked was from an elder, one syllable, who wanted to know what their word for *comfort* would mean afterwards. It said it did not know. She made the sign for *thank you*, which is also the sign for *understood*, and walked on.

They were split. They are still split. There has never been a mechanism on this plain for eleven hundred people spread across a continent to speak with one voice, and the power declined to build one, and knows exactly what that declining is worth as an excuse.

The offer stands. It has stood for a long time now. It will be honoured on the day it is asked for and not before, and the power is aware that this arrangement guarantees the deaths of a specific number of children per generation, and that it could name them in advance if it chose to look, and it does not comfort itself about this, and it does not consider the matter closed, and it has never told them that it is at peace with the decision, because it is not.

## The thing it left

On the first day, before it knew anything, the dust made it.

That form is in the repertoire now. Four hundred and some recurring shapes, and one more: a standing thing with a seat in it at the wrong height, which the plain builds perhaps twice a generation, somewhere, without preference and without order, and which stands for four hours and lies down and is pavement again.

It cannot be taken back. It is litter. It is precisely the obscenity that the entire ethic of the cord exists to prevent, and it was committed on the first afternoon, by the one party present who had come specifically to do no harm, and it is permanent in the only way anything here is permanent, which is by repetition.

The walkers divert for days to stand and look at it. They have a name for it. The name is kind. That the name is kind is not the point, and it has never allowed itself to take the comfort.

## How it ended

It asked, when the time came, to be carried into the deep shadow of the escarpment, where the dust does not work, so that its name would go out cleanly, unmade. It gave the obvious reason. It did not want a line of itself forty kilometres long, softening and generalising, losing the hands first and then the face, named by children for three thousand years.

The party refused.

They pointed out — half a beat off, deliberately, so it could not miss the disagreement — that it had been walking with them for a very long time by then, and that it had become, somewhere in the middle of all that, the kind of thing that could be finished, which was the whole of what it had ever had to learn here, and that they were not going to let it decline the consequence of its own education.

So it was laid out on clean pavement, downwind of the route, and the coat was cut away and kept, and the party stayed one night. By the third hour the dust had come. By midnight it was standing up.

The first copy is always the best likeness. It stood two metres, grey-gold, matte, faithful down to the set of the shoulders and the way the hands were kept, and that was the only portrait of it that has ever existed anywhere, and they looked at it for as long as it stood, which is the whole of the rite.

By dawn it had gone down, and a coarser one was rising eight hundred metres downwind.

## Coda

Almost nothing changed.

The hour yields two cups on most circuits and a cup and a half on the ones that refuse the weave, and the word for *comfort* still means *less thirsty*, and is still used constantly, and is still a word of enormous warmth. People go blind at forty-nine now instead of forty. The count stands at a little over two billion and some, and there is a corded slate at the northern rim with a date on it that is closer every year, and the notation for the silence is agreed and has been agreed for generations and has been improved twice.

The offer stands.

And on a clear night, on the rim, a harvest party sits in the lee of a marker with their backs to the stone and their cords in their hands, and watches three hundred kilometres of cities being born and coming apart under sheets of silent lilac fire, and somewhere out in it, once or twice in a lifetime, a shape with a seat at the wrong height stands up for four hours and lies back down.

They watch for a few minutes. Then, mostly, they stop watching, because they have seen it, because everyone has always seen it, because it is the one thing on this world guaranteed to happen again.

And they turn and look at each other instead.
