## Bracket, and What It Declined

### The arrival

It came in on foot from the north-west in the town's ninth year, in the ordinary way, which is to say wet to the knee and following its own markers back in.

There is no anomaly in the arrival worth filing except this: the objects it had left along its route to hold the way open were all worn. Handles polished, edges rounded, one cup mended twice. Nothing in Ashlar had made them and no ship's manifest from Elsewhere had carried them. A loom-warden looked them over and said, in the flat way of her trade, that they had come out of the draw already old, the way beams do, with plausible dents.

Nobody found this remarkable. Everything here that is going to stay arrives with a past.

It gave no trade and no place of origin. This was irregular but not obstructive: Ashlar does not ask what things are made of. It went out on the naming-rounds that autumn with the seven-year-olds, which was thought funny, and at the end of the six weeks the child Sabbeth Wick spent her one permitted naming on it instead of on a bracket she had been shown in the fourth block. The elders allowed it, partly because the rules do not forbid it and partly to see what would happen. She called it Bracket. It has been Bracket for fifty-two years and has answered to nothing else.

Its hands were wrong at first. Cool, and dry as paper. It practised. It took two years to come up to wall-temperature and it never has managed the damp properly; a handshake from Bracket is still, everyone agrees, a slightly disappointing experience. The midwives took a wax pull in its second month here, as a joke, and found the issued template — the nine loops every Ashlar infant is given. They took another at three years and the drift was correct. It has a born-here's hands on a come-here's arms and it will not discuss this.

Its shadow arrives exactly on time. Not late, not early, not by any margin that can be measured. Several people have said, independently, that this is worse.

### What it declined

The record of what Bracket has done fills the better part of a shelf in the survey office. The record of what it has changed is eleven centimetres long, and it has never touched that.

Requests received and declined, in the order the ledger has them:

To warm the town. Declined.

To colour the sky at dimming. Declined. Asked whether the sky would ever colour, it said yes, and gave a span — not in your lifetime, nor your grandchildren's; around the time the river runs — and the town found the accuracy easier to hold than comfort would have been, which is consistent with everything else the town knows about holding.

To take the skypang away. Declined. It stands out in the lane at dimming with whoever is standing there, and it has the skypang too. This has been confirmed by a number of people who are not sentimental.

To deepen the Rill. Declined, in writing, four times, three of them unprompted.

To finish the ground.

Declined.

### The Rill

In fifty-two years Bracket has not come closer to the channel than the brass rail. This is in the keeper's log because the keeper of the ninth year thought to start recording it and every keeper since has thought it worth continuing. On the fourth of cold-turn it stands in the drizzle with the forty or fifty others and watches water move eleven centimetres downhill and does not blink at a rate anyone has been able to fault.

In the thirty-first year the cut rate faltered. Two seasons at a third of a millimetre and one at nothing measurable, and the town went quiet in the particular way it goes quiet, and the mention-rotas got longer, and nobody said the obvious thing out loud in a lane because of rule two.

The night keeper found Bracket at the rail at the fourth hour with its hands in its coat pockets. It stood there until the sixth hour with its hands in its pockets and then went and took its shift on the ladders.

The following year the rate resumed on its own. That is the whole of what is known and the town has agreed, in council, that it is the whole of what will be recorded.

### The pebbles

Bracket has never split a pebble. Not at a party, not in the survey office, not once in fifty-two years, and when a come-here demonstrated the trick for a visitor from Elsewhere at a naming-feast, Bracket left the room, which was rude, and it apologised the next day, which was worse, because the apology was for the rudeness only.

Pressed on it later, indoors, against a wall, it said something that the loom-warden Emeth wrote down: *A question here obliges an answer. I would rather the first answers this place gives were ones nobody asked it for.*

The Rill, it should be said, was not asked for.

### What it does

Copying. It took the second copy shift of the Rill ledger in its fourth year here and has held it ever since — the winter shift, which nobody wants, because paper is least confident in the cold. One hundred and four fair copies in a hand that has not altered.

Night presence. In the bad winter of the thirty-eighth year the eleventh course lost four people out of thirteen and the corridor began to thin — the wet-lace retreating from the drip-path, a stair-tread going matte. Bracket moved in and wore Ollen Tarrow's routine for a season: up at the fifth hour, the same four ladders, tea made badly in the same pot, the same complaint about the same shutter, made aloud, indoors, in her tone. Then in spring it stopped, on a day it had chosen in advance, and taught the routine to a header who had asked for it, and moved out, and has never done it again although it has been asked twice.
