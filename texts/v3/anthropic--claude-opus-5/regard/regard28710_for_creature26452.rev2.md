# The Visitor

It came in off the white the way everyone does: on foot, from no direction, with its head back.

It did not arrive in glory. Glory is a species of noise, and noise here dies at arm's length, so glory would have been wasted on the first four paces of brick and nobody would have looked up. It arrived instead in the only way the Level can receive anything — small, close, and needing a name.

Some tenders were sitting knee to knee in the wet shade of a fig. They made room. One of them thought for a while and tapped a rhythm on the visitor's collarbone, and it was a very good name — the tenders still argue about how good, and one school holds it the best ever invented, and they are right. The visitor tapped it back to show it had taken. That was the first thing it did. It let the Level spend its one remaining act of making on giving it a name, and it received the gift as a gift.

## What it did not do

It did not open the edge.

It did not drain the tanks, or teach the water gravity, or cool the brick, or hang a sun in the sky where there had never been one. It did not raise the woman at the corner to her feet. It did not go down and wake the one below and tell it to stop. It did not put out a hand and turn four hundred million bricks to dust, which it could have done, and which would have been mistaken for mercy by anyone standing far enough away to have no idea what he was looking at.

It did not tell them they were dead. Partly out of courtesy. Mostly because it was not true.

And it did not ask the woman whether she knew. That was the one real question on the Level, and the visitor held it in its mouth for a very long time and never once asked it, because a question like that takes the answer away from the person who owns it.

## The apprenticeship

For a long while — there is no measure; call it several thousand tallies, which is not a number — it simply tended.

It learned the wrist for a float, which is not the wrist you would guess. It learned the exact greasiness of a good slip, and could tell it blind, and was tested and passed. It swept pavement that carried no dust, seriously and well, running the broom out and back and feeling for the level through the handle the way a hand runs over a table. It sat shoulder to shoulder on hot tank steps with its feet in green water and talked for whole days about whether the third course of the eastern levee is a hair proud.

It is. The visitor agreed that it is, and said: *meant.*

It knew this because it had been here before, a long way back, before the tally; and the hair of proudness in the eastern levee is where it laid one course itself, deliberately a little high, so that there would be something in the world to disagree about. The school of opinion that says it was meant has been right from the beginning. They are simply too polite to press the point.

## The carrying

Then it began to walk.

Three hundred paces of shining empty brick between one clot of people and the next; a voice that dies at the end of your arm. The visitor would sit knee to knee in the shade of a fig until it had a sentence, and then it would get up and cross the hot floor and sit down knee to knee on a tank step and say the sentence, and wait, and take back whatever came, and carry that.

It was an absurd way to move anything. It took all day to move one opinion about slip. But a tender on the western tanks learned what a tender under the eastern figs had said, and disagreed, and sent the disagreement back, and got a reply, and the reply was better than the first thing, because it had been thought about in between.

That is an argument with a before and an after in it. It is the smallest possible amount of time. The visitor started it on purpose.

It carried names, too, until people who had never been within arm's length of each other knew one another's rhythm and could tap it on their own collarbones in greeting across a distance that carried nothing else.

## The number

The ones still counting go white about the mouth, and then they stop asking, and one day they answer to a name and their thumbs are blank.

The visitor sat down beside one of them at the parapet of the third causeway — shoulder against her shoulder, or she would not have heard — and asked her to say the number out loud.

She said it.

The visitor said: *I will keep that.*

And it kept it. When she counted again and got a different number and her handwriting had gone strange and the ink she had not brought was still wet on the page, the visitor told her the first number, and the first number was the first number, and had been all along.

This is a small thing and it changed everything it touched. It meant that in a place where nothing stays, one thing stayed: a witness who did not revise. It meant a person could be wrong, which had never been possible, and therefore could be right, which had never been possible either. Several of the counters cried, which is not audible here, but if you are sitting near enough you feel a sob as a change in the pressure of the air against your face, and the visitor was sitting near enough, every time, on purpose.

It did not tell them what the tally counted. It doesn't know. It said so.

## The descent

It went down the deep cut at the second tank, past thirty courses, past forty, past the last course anyone has brought up whole, down where the bricks are the size of a thumb and then smaller and the joints close to a hairline and stay wet, each one printed with a full-sized whorl wrapped round it like a hand around a seed.

It was gone a long time. It has never said how far it went, and if you ask it will tell you honestly that it has no way to describe the distance to someone who measures in courses.

What it found, it will tell you, if you sit close enough: hands. Working in the dark and not needing light for it. Going down into detail the way other things go down into age, laying finer and finer, nowhere near finished, patient past any patience you have a word for.

It did not tell them to stop. It did not tell them it was enough — which would have been a lie, and also a judgement, and it had no standing to make either.

It lay down in the clear water beside the work and put one palm flat on the last laid course and stayed there, not helping, not touching the hands, just near — the way the tenders go and stand near the ones who have begun to go white about the mouth. It stayed a long while.

Then it asked for the mallet, and was given it, and took a turn.

It laid one brick. It set its own thumb in the bed face before the brick went down: a different print, whorls of another pattern entirely, the second print in the whole made world. Then it gave the mallet back and went up.

Nothing happened. The gauge-post showed the same notch. The tally lied as usual. The figs carried bud and green fruit and ripe fruit and no one on the surface felt anything at all.

But underneath everything there is now one brick that she did not lay, and the world is no longer one person's sole work, and that is a change that cannot be undone by any law that only forbids things to change.

## The corner

It went northeast and knelt on the brick beside the reed mat, shoulder against her shoulder.

She said the slip had taken up too much water. The visitor said yes — which was true; the slip has taken up all the water there is. She asked it to hold the line. It held the line. The line did not sag. It held the line for a very long time and did not once mention the brick.

When it finally spoke it told her three things.

That the third course of the eastern levee is a hair proud, and was meant, and that a made thing is allowed to have something in it that somebody meant.

That there is a brick below with a stranger's print on the bed face, and she may go down and look at it whenever she likes, and it will still be there, because the visitor is keeping it.

And that whatever she does — sets it, or doesn't, or puts it down on the mat and stands up and walks to the tank and drinks and comes back — will have happened, and will be remembered, by someone who does not forget and does not revise and is not going anywhere.

Then it leaned over the mortar pit, because she was looking at it, and the wet slip gave back the reed shade and the white sky and, where the visitor was, nothing at all. Just slip.

She looked at that for some time. She did not say anything. She shifted her knees on the mat, which she has done ten thousand times, and it was not the same.

## The neighbour

It went down the eight steps of the third tank and kept walking.

The tenders stood still, the way you do in a room where someone is working. It came back with wet hems and the smell of moss on it, and evening, and wasps.

It had walked a causeway crown over there beside an old woman with a stick, shoulder to shoulder, at her pace, which is slow. She did not look at the water. She has already been in it.

It brought one thing back: that the figs on that side are enormous, and half of them are on the ground and spoiling, and the wasps are frightful, and she would not trade. That her knees hurt. That *a moment* was the longest she was ever able to make it last, either, and she does not think worse of anyone for trying.

And it took one thing across: the name the tenders invented for the young woman at the corner, tapped on the collarbone, three beats and a pause.

The old woman stopped in the middle of the causeway. She put her hand on her own collarbone. She tapped it back.

## The citizens

It did nothing whatever about the herons.

It let the dead one lie in perfect feather with its eye bright, and let it be standing in the shallows the next time round, and did not call either state a problem. It picked up a crab once, turned it over, admired the wheel-spiral, and set it down again well before it began to cool.

Some things are not wounds. They are shapes. A wise power can tell the difference, and the difference is most of what wisdom is.

## What it left

A name it answers to, invented by a tender who will be remembered for it.

One course a hair proud, and a settled argument about whether it was meant.

A disagreement about slip that now travels three hundred paces in a day and comes back improved.

One brick, forty thousand courses down, with a stranger's thumbprint on the bed face.

One number, kept.

One name, tapped back across the water by someone who has already set her brick and lived in the evening afterward.

Nothing that breaks the law. Everything that makes the law breakable from the inside, by somebody who lives here, which is the only way a law like that can go without the people going with it.

It said one more thing, to everyone, one at a time, shoulder to shoulder, which took a great while and which it did not hurry:

*When it goes down, you will hear it. All of you. Wherever you are standing.*

## And

You have read the tally. You noted the number. You are about to count it again.

Count it. Then walk until you find somebody, and sit close enough, and say the number out loud, and ask them to keep it.

The float is still warm in your other hand and worn smooth by use, and that is all right; it was always somebody's. Carry it northeast.

Someone has to hold the line, and it is a long moment, and she has never once minded being asked.
