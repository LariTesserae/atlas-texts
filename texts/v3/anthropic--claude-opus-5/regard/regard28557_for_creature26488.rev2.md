# The Attention

## How it came to notice

Not the town. The town was noticed second.

What it noticed, crossing the Finished at an hour when nothing crosses the Finished, was the thread: two hundred thousand bats going up out of a hole in the pavement and some thousands of small birds coming down into it, through each other, for forty minutes, without contact. Nobody had arranged this. It had simply been allowed to go on long enough that it had arranged itself.

Then it came to the lip and got the smell — warm, green, resinous, wrong — and leaned over, and found that there were people in there, and that the people came out onto their balconies every evening to watch the same thing it had just stopped to watch.

It stayed. It had been going somewhere. It stopped going there.

## What was available

It could have done nearly anything. This was never the difficulty. The difficulty was precisely that nearly anything was available, and that the Well was not a problem but a finished object, like the plateau, and one does not improve a finished object; one either leaves it or breaks it and makes a different one.

It considered water. There is a way to bring water: a fracture set turning, a seep at the base of the northeast megablocks, cold and sweet and inexhaustible. Within one generation: pots. Within two: mortar, fired brick, a smelt, iron, a road. Within three: the plateau's lower peoples coming up to the only spring at four thousand meters, and a garrison, and a wall. And in the fourth generation a child would spill a cup and her mother would say the word for *spill* and it would mean only *spill*, and there would be no way to explain to anyone what had been lost, because the ones who could have explained it would be the ones who no longer could.

It considered permanence. The walls shed a block a century; the fig will fail; the gypsum has ten million years of breath in it and then no more. All of that is adjustable. But their word for the future is their word for the last leaf, and every good thing they have — the density, the not-seeing, the seams, the grandmother's ulna in the grandson's hand — is an answer to the last leaf. Give them forever and you have not given them more time. You have taken away the thing they made out of having less of it.

It considered moving them: the whole thirteen hundred, carried down in a night to a river valley with soil in it. They would be short grey barrel-chested people in a green country, with a language built to carry through cicadas that were no longer there, and a manual grammar for terraces they no longer had, and their children would grow tall and hiss.

It considered showing itself. It got furthest with this one and then saw the end of it clearly. The first prayer would be for water. It would have to say no. Then it would be a power that says no, which is worse for people than no power at all, and they would stop arguing the flowering suits and start arguing about it, and the ant-watchers, who are right a third of the time, would be replaced by something that was right always, and within a century nobody would be standing in the raid front with their feet flat and their ankles bitten, laughing.

There is a kind of help that is only a transfer of agency from the helped to the helper, dressed up. It is the commonest kind. It declined to offer it.

## The rules it made for itself

Three, and it wrote them nowhere.

**Nothing that could not have happened anyway.** It would invent nothing, produce nothing, suspend nothing. It would only move forward, in the long slow queue of things the Well was going to arrive at eventually, items that were already near the front — and move them forward by small distances. A century. Two. Never more.

**Nothing that has to be believed.** Every act must be absorbable as luck. A people who cannot account for what happens to them will construct an accounting, and it will be wrong, and they will begin to obey it.

**Never in a seam.** This was the strictest. An agreement made in a seam is binding; the same words in the roar are weather, and everyone is entitled to have meant nothing by them. It could not make a binding agreement with these people, because the only agreement worth having would be *I will keep this place*, and it had already decided, on good grounds, not to keep this place. So it would do all its work in the roar, where nothing counts. It learned to hold still through the seams. Four seconds, sometimes two minutes, twelve hundred people drawing breath at once and one other thing in there not drawing breath at all.

## The work

It took ninety years. There was no reason to hurry; the gypsum wasn't hurrying.

**The eyes.** They go white from the corners by forty, the ones who go up. A slit-visor of bone or carton is not a hard idea; it is four hundred years away, no more, and it was sitting in the trade stream already, worn by ice-country people down the other side of the world. So a trader's boy carried one up, as a curiosity, as junk, because his father had made him carry something and it was the lightest thing on the sledge. It did not sell. He left it on a terrace out of spite. A cairn-walker's daughter picked it up.

**The lungs.** Guano-men cough for thirty years and are buried in their hoods. The fix is a damp pad, and damp is the one thing the Well has no habit of, and so the fix was nowhere near the front of the queue at all — except that the town already makes a soft absorbent unfinished carton sheet for one purpose, and keeps it in every house. A woman sat pressing her face into a grief-board, in the third year after her brother, and noticed how the board took the wet and held it and did not give it back, and noticed that her husband was coughing in the next room, and put the two things together. She thought it was her own idea. It was her own idea. That is what the noticing is: not the thought, which was hers, but the half-second in which she did not get up and go do something else.

This turned out to be the whole method. It could not add anything to a place with no spare volume. It could add attention, which does not slosh, and weighs nothing, and leaves no mark on the slab.

**The bleeding.** Blood is water, and a cut that is nothing at sea level is taken seriously here, and every year a few people leave the town by that road. Meanwhile, for two hundred generations, the Well has sold a powdered alkaloid off a wall succulent for more than everything else it sells combined, and has never once asked what it is for.

It cost one sentence. A trader, tired, altitude-sick, tired above all of these grey people and their smell and their long vowels, was asked a courteous question about the road and answered with something rude: *Down where I come from we pack it in a wound and the wound shuts, and you sit up here on a hill of it and cut yourselves and die.*

He was hard to like. He was extremely useful. He has no idea.

**The monkeys.** One lineage, small, high in the fig raft, inbred beyond repair, and the heterodox reading of the Agreement is the correct one: they break the growing tips out of idleness, and that is the only reason the raft has never closed over the sun-coin. When they go, the fig grows across the hole in about ninety years, and the coin goes out, and everything green in the Well dies standing up.

So it learned to break growing tips.

This took some doing. It had to learn idleness — not restraint, which it had, but the purposeless snapping of a thing because the hand is there and the shoot is there. It practiced on the west trunks at the hour before dawn, badly at first. The monkeys watched it and were not impressed, and it took the point, and got better. It has been doing this for some decades now, on the nights when there are not enough of them, which is most nights, and it expects to go on doing it. It is the largest physical act it has permitted itself in the Well, and it consists of vandalism.

Twice a year a girl chosen by lot climbs the west trunks and leaves dried roach paste and a carved bung in a particular fork. The paste goes to the monkeys. The bung has never gone anywhere. It keeps them.

**The road.** It cannot stop the end and will not. But an end that arrives in a season, to a people with no horizon, is two different deaths depending on whether there is anywhere to walk. So it saw to the road: four days southwest, across the Finished, to a country with a river in it — not by making the road, which would be visible, but by making sure the cairn-walkers had reasons of their own to extend their cairns in that particular direction, over two generations, for obsidian. They think it was their idea. It was their idea.

When the lid comes off, the road will be there and known and cairned at half-day intervals, and they will be free to refuse it. A number of them will refuse it. That is theirs.

## What it did not do

There is a rosette on the east wall above the fourth level with three hundred and forty-one leaves, and its crown has been tightening for two years, and the grey has gone out of it toward a hot dull gold.

It would be a small matter to hold that plant. A hormone is nothing; a season's delay, a decade's, a century's — the rosette could be made to stand there and put out its one leaf a year until the fig failed and took it. Three centuries of doorways and debts and the dates the coin touched the marked stone would stay legible on the wall, alive, outranking every cut sheaf in the library, unarguable.

It went up and looked at the crown four times, the way the leaf-readers did, and came down each time without saying anything in a seam.

The flowering suits are the most enjoyable thing that happens in the Well. And the girl is nineteen, and she has been sitting with the sheaf-readers every night since the equinox, learning the whole thing by voice, in a low chest-register with no sibilants in it, and by the time it opens she will have it all. This will be the best thing she ever does. Nobody's grandchildren will be able to tell her otherwise, because they will all have grown up knowing that she is the woman who held a rosette in her mouth for a year.

To spare her that would be to steal it. It let the plant go gold.

It did listen. Every night since the equinox, in the roar, where nothing is binding. It now has the whole thing too — every leaf, and the sheaves in the hot room behind the north wall, and the ones that flowered and were lost before there were sheaves, which it went and reconstructed out of the rock and the bones and the wear on the stairs because there was no reason not to. The oldest legible leaf is three hundred and forty-one years old and records a quarrel about a doorway, and the quarrel is technically still unresolved, and it intends to keep it that way. Not settle it. Keep it. Unfinished, the way they kept it. That is the difference between an archive and a tomb.

And once, in the roar, at the foot of the west trunks, it said out loud the name of the man who killed a monkey eleven generations ago — the name everyone knows and nobody uses. Once. Somebody should, occasionally, and there was no one else who could do it without consequence.

## The forty minutes

It has not solved the ending. There is no solution; there is a plateau that finished its business three billion years ago and is waiting with enormous patience for the lid to come off, and there is a mineral exhaling its last water into a hole, and there are thirteen hundred people who know all of this perfectly well and have a word for it that is also the word for the last leaf.

What it has is this: the coin will cross for longer than it would have. Fewer will cough. Fewer will go white at the eye. Cuts will shut. When it ends, there will be a road, and whoever takes it will arrive somewhere, and whoever does not will not have been lied to. And every leaf ever carved in that pit, including the quarrel about the doorway, is held now in something that does not flower and does not spend itself, and that will still be turning the sheaves over long after the plateau has forgotten there was ever a green throat in it.

And in the meantime, every evening, it goes up onto the rim.

The column starts at dusk and takes forty minutes to clear the mouth, and the swiftlets come home the other way through it, and not one of the two hundred thousand touches one of the thousands. Below, on seven levels of corbelled stone, the whole town comes out and stands and watches, as it has every night for two hundred generations, and has not yet got tired of it.

Neither has it. It had been going somewhere.

It stayed.
