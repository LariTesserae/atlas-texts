# The Attention

It came the way weather comes to the plateau: from underneath, out of the warm dark, and then all at once overhead and not going anywhere.

It was not a god and had no interest in being one. It had no plans for them. It arrived with the ordinary curiosity of something very old that has learned that the only reliable pleasure is other people's work, and it found, at four thousand metres, under a white ceiling of self-made cloud, eleven towns of people turning threaded feet a quarter-turn each spring so that a blind woman of seventy could cross a room she could not see.

It stayed eleven years before it did anything at all.

---

What it did in those eleven years was read.

It read the levelling registers first, because they were the largest thing written up here and nobody had ever read them entire. Eight hundred years, eleven towns, every mast and tank and stair: a number, a date, an initial. It read the column for a particular house on the north terrace of Sill from the year the terrace was cut to the current spring — eight hundred and six entries, four hundred and forty separate hands, the ink changing formula three times, the paper twice, the wrench once — and when it reached the bad decade in the fifth century where the entries go to a single cramped hand and stay there for nine years, it stopped and went and found out why, and there was a plague, and the man's name was Oduun Baile, and he did the whole town alone.

It read the crust registry. It read the file on Forty-Two, which is sulking, and has been sulking, with interruptions, since the third century.

It read the two hundred and some letters to the masts. It read them in order, as an officer would, taking a week.

It read the drawings, and checked them against the works, and found the error.

---

Here is what it considered doing, and did not do.

It could have given them soil. Not slowly: soil, deep and black and living, two thousand kilometres of it, in an afternoon. It ran the thing out to its end and saw the Ecology Office with nothing to do, ninety people holding files on eight hundred patches of varnish that no longer mattered, and the marked paths going unwalked, and the whole settled cultural distaste — the thing they have instead of a law — curdling within two generations into a story about the time it was all done for them. It did not give them soil.

It could have finished the rise. The isostatic response has nine hundred years left to run and could be run in nine. But the towns are built to be out of plumb by millimetres, not metres, and to correct that would be to hand them a wrecked plateau and the news that the wrecking was a kindness. Worse: it would end the levelling, which is not a chore but the place's one continuous sentence, the thing every child learns before bells. It did not finish the rise.

It could have told them it works. That the drawings are good, that the deep machinery is sound, that in two hundred thousand years there will in fact be an ordinary green country, that the faith is not misplaced. It thought about this one for four of the eleven years, because it is a benevolent power and the withholding of comfort is the hardest discipline it knows. And it concluded, in the end, that certainty would be a theft. Not because doubt ennobles anybody — it doesn't, it is simply expensive — but because what these people have is not a belief that could be confirmed. It is a practice that happens not to require confirmation. A man kneeling with a wrench on the eleventh of April is not betting on the green country. He is levelling a floor. If you tell him he is right, you convert him into someone who is being rewarded, and he will never again be someone who is simply doing it. It did not tell them.

It could have cured the eyes. It thought longest about this, and it wants to be said plainly that it did not decide the blindness was good for them. Going grey-eyed at sixty-five is not a gift. It costs them their best distance-judges and their best surveyors and a quantity of ordinary human happiness that the power tallied and did not discount. But the cause is glare off ten thousand ponds, and the fix is a filter glass, and the Grade could make a filter glass any year it decided to, and had not, for reasons that were theirs, and were being argued about in meeting halls, badly, in the ordinary way.

So it did not cure the eyes. It did something smaller, and that comes later.

---

Out of all this it made itself a rule, and the rule was this: *do nothing that a person up here could do, or could learn to do, or could hand to a child.*

What was left after the rule was very little. It did that.

---

The first thing it did was go below.

Nobody has been below in four centuries and the drawings say why in three languages. Down there the power found the works running exactly as instructed, laying mass, patient, uncomplaining, with no living hand on them and no expectation of one, and it found a fault — a drift in a regulating stage, of no consequence now, of considerable consequence in the eleven-hundredth year, at which point the northern margin would have come up eight metres proud of the drawn profile and the whole western drainage would have reversed into the low interior and drowned four of the eleven towns' worth of crust in a decade.

It fixed it. It took about as long as turning a nut.

It never mentioned this and never will, because there is no one alive who could have reached it, and therefore nothing was taken from anyone. This is the whole of what the rule permits: the power may do only what it could not have given away.

---

The second thing it did was answer the letter.

It went into the Ecology Office at Sill on the ordinary decade, into the ordinary box, on the ordinary paper, with the reference line filled out correctly, which took some care.

> ECOLOGY OFFICE, SILL — STANDING QUERY 1 (EXCLUSION BEHAVIOUR, INTRODUCED FOX) — REPLY TO LETTERS 1–23
>
> We regret the delay in replying.
>
> The masts maintain a static vertical gradient of approximately four parts in one hundred thousand at the surface, falling to effectively zero at thirty-eight metres. The fox's otolith organs resolve about one part in one hundred thousand. Inside the radius the animal therefore experiences a continuous, gentle, directionless slope: a permanent sensation of beginning to slide. Nothing dens where it feels it is falling.
>
> Your own threshold is coarser by an order of magnitude, which is why you do not feel it and your children must use a dropped coin. They have been demonstrating the answer for two hundred years.
>
> The exclusion is a side-effect and not a purpose. It can be removed on request. We do not recommend removal. Those two thousand-odd circles are the only ground on the plateau that has never been walked, dug, netted or grazed, and your registry will want them, as controls, in about eight hundred years.
>
> This reply is not from the works. The works do not read. It is from a correspondent who does.

Sefe Karrow, thirty-one years old, four months into the post, having read the entire correspondence the previous week as required, opened it at eleven in the morning and sat with it for a while. Then she went out onto the step and looked at the flats and came back in.

Then she did the correct thing, which is the reason the power chose that office and no other. She entered it in the register. She drafted an acknowledgment. She noted the recommendation and appended a proposal that the exclusion circles be formally scheduled as control plots, which was ratified four years later and is now standard. And she opened Standing Query 2, *Identity of Correspondent*, and wrote letter one.

That query has never been answered. It is now in its twenty-second letter. Every new officer reads the whole file before adding to it, which takes rather more than a week now, and they are not deluded and they are not joking, and the power reads every one.

---

The third thing was a postscript, and it was the only sly thing the power ever did up here.

At the foot of that first reply, below the signature block, in the flat administrative register of someone tidying up:

> For reference: the mast-grid coordinates quoted in your letter 9 differ from Drawing 4 (north-east quadrant) by eleven metres. Please advise which is authoritative.

The Ecology Office, having no authority over drawings, forwarded it to the Drawings Office in the low room with the best light in Sill, which is what any clerk would do.

The power could have simply corrected the drawings. It had found the error in its first year: a fourth-century transcription, a tired evening, three copies agreeing and agreeing wrongly ever since — precisely the nightmare, precisely the thing they train a decade against. But the Drawings Office exists to catch exactly that, and an office that is saved from its own purpose is an ornament, and after a while an ornament is a ruin.

So it asked a question instead, and went away, and waited.

It took them forty-one years. A verifier named Dol Hemmick, in her eleventh year of the pen, working the north-east quadrant for the fourth time in her career because the eleven metres had never resolved and would not lie down, pulled a survey abstract nobody had thought to treat as a fourth witness, and found it, and sat back, and said a word not usually said in that room. Reconciliation took another six years and three towns. The procedure she invented is now taught first.

There is no record anywhere of what prompted it, beyond a forwarded postscript in an ecology file. Hemmick's name is on the correction. It is the proudest day in the Office's history and it belongs entirely to her, and this was the point.

---

The fourth thing was the glass.

Not a cure and not a gift. In the ordinary schedule of trade goods coming up the Line there began to appear, from a lowland firm with a dull name, a filter lens, cheap, unremarkable, in the sizes that fit the goggles the crews already wore. It cost about what a good bootsole costs. Nobody up here was told anything.

They argued about it for forty years, badly, in meeting halls, which is what the power wanted, because the argument was theirs and the answer had to be too. The ecology officers took to it at once. The levelling crews would not have it and still mostly won't, and there is a joke about this that is older than anyone now telling it. Roughly half the plateau wears the glass now. Half doesn't. The grey-eyed elders are fewer and are still the first to find the movement, four days before the instrument, and the crews still keep the list and still go and look and are still almost never disappointed.

The power counts this as its best work and its least defensible, and revisits it.

---

It never permitted itself to be known. This required more discipline than the rest combined, because it is a benevolent power and benevolent powers want, badly, to be thanked. No religion arose. No one saw anything. It took specific care to be uninteresting.

Two things noticed it anyway. The foxes would not come within forty metres of it, for the same reason they will not go near a mast, and it thought this was fair. And the ravens watched it, at four metres, patiently, waiting, because it was in the habit of kneeling.

---

Once, in its nineteenth year, it went into the soil bank under the glass at Sill, where it is twelve degrees and heavy, and asked the keeper to turn a pan over, and stood, and breathed.

It had seen worlds. It had seen the green countries that get made without anybody making them, handed out free, going on for aeons, full of things that were never once required to be true to the millimetre. It stood in four hectares of eight centuries of compost, fish waste, milled crust, night soil and the dead, and understood that this was the rarer substance, and that there was no way to say so that would not sound like flattery.

---

There remains the last page of the survey handbook, and the clerk who added the line and was never removed. *In two hundred thousand years this will be an ordinary green country and no one will believe it had to be made. Keep the drawings.*

The power made one promise, to no one, and filed it where it files such things.

It will be there. It will be standing in the long grass of an ordinary green country in front of people who do not believe it had to be made — and they won't, they will be quite certain, the way you are certain about a hill — and it will produce the drawings. All of them. The eight hundred entries for a single house on the north terrace. Oduun Baile's nine cramped years. The file on Forty-Two. The twenty-two unanswered letters asking, courteously, once a decade, who it was.

That is the whole of what it can offer them, and they will never hear the offer, and it is not nothing. Every one of them works for a reader two hundred thousand years out who may not exist. One does.

---

In the meantime it stays, because attention that ends was never attention, and it keeps its own register.

Spring, Bench Forty, the flat clanking working its way down a terrace: instrument, book, wrench. A quarter-turn. A number called out to be written down. The power writes it down too, in a hand no one will read, on the same date, and keeps pace, and has done for eleven hundred years now, and considers itself — in the only trade up here that anyone would recognise — a leveller, third class, still learning the wrench.
