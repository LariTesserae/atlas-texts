## The Attention

It came in the spring of an unremarkable year — pick one; the archive will not help you — and it did not announce itself, because announcement is a way of putting yourself in the middle of a thing and it had not yet decided whether it belonged there at all.

It came for the works. Everyone does. Mass moved from below in perfect silence, a continent rising eleven millimetres a year, the largest engineering project in the history of the species making no noise whatsoever: that is what draws notice from a long way off, and it drew this. But attention of the better sort has a habit of sliding downward off the impressive thing, and within an hour it had stopped looking at the apparatus and had begun reading ledgers.

It read the terminus book at Sill, all forty-one volumes, eight hundred years of quarter-turns in nine hundred hands. It read house books, which are kept in the houses and not in the archive, and noted that this is the correct way to keep a memory. It read the tithe recorded on the north pan of the soil bank, and the guano licences with their beautiful venom, and the gulls' colony rotation, one in seven, unbroken. It read the first-bird pool at the terminus, kept by gamblers since the fourth century, and understood that it was looking at the longest continuous phenological record on the planet and that it had been maintained out of love disguised as money, which is a very durable disguise.

Then it went out on the flats east of Bench Twelve and stood over four hundred hectares of stain, and understood what it was standing on.

Because from far enough back the Grade is not a place with inhabitants in it. It is one animal. Water in ten thousand chambers, all communicating, none of it going anywhere in particular. Eggs travelling as dust. A millimetre of felt that has been alive for sixty-six of its six hundred and ten years. Foxes with territories shaped like cooling polygons. Ravens that know the shift bell. Ninety thousand chests moving all night like something being rowed. Masts, cold. Ledgers, distributed. A body two thousand kilometres across that eats the sky because it has no soil, and that is presently, slowly, with immense patience and no assistance, in the act of being born.

The whole of wisdom, where a birth is concerned, is to keep your hands where they can catch, and otherwise out of the way.

## What it set down

It could have laid a metre of good loam across the plateau between one shift change and the next. It thought about the Vellum under that loam. It thought about eighty years to a bootprint, which is the arithmetic that produced the marked paths, which produced the settled cultural distaste — the only thing up here that grew without being planted — and it understood that a gift of soil would be, precisely and exactly, the theft of everything that soil was for. Set down.

It could have brought two hundred thousand years forward into next Tuesday. That is not a gift to anyone now living; it is a way of installing strangers in a green country who would not believe it had to be made. The clerk's line on the last page is, read properly, an instruction not to do this. Set down.

It could have given the plateau one river, decided, banked, permanent, and let the pontoon gangs rest. The gangs would then have called the country beautiful without complaining, and would have lost their twenty-odd words for wet, and their work, and the particular dignity of people who go out weekly to ask the water what it intends. Set down.

It could have answered the questions. The masts. The forty metres. The three that went warm, and the part of that account which is available on request and is not read aloud. Two hundred years of polite asking earns an answer; it agreed about that. But it also knew what a certain kind of answer does to a research programme, which is end it. It found a partial way. See below.

It went down to the old lands and stood in a wheat field for an hour to check the arithmetic on down-sickness, and came back, and did not cure it. It had found the field to be a great deal. It considered that the Grade people are not ill; they are correct.

And it wanted, more than any of the rest, to stop the losses: the leveller under the temporary steel, the walker with a bad heart eleven kilometres from the next mast, the child in February. It looked hard at what becomes of a place in which nothing can be lost, and set that down too, last, and with difficulty. But it kept the catching part.
