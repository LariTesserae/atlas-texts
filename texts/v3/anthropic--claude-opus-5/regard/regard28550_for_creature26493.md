# What the Power Did

## Arrival

It came in through the gap at the rim, on foot, in the ordinary way, because there were other ways and all of them would have been a lie told at the door of a house.

The first thing it did was stop. Not out of reverence — reverence is cheap and arrives early — but because it had understood, within a few hundred metres of the rolled edge of the leaf, that it had brought the wrong clock. Everything in it was running at the speed of somewhere else: attention, comparison, the reflex of assessment, the small hot engine of *and therefore*. In a basin where a drop takes three weeks to fall and an animal takes twenty minutes to cross a room, that engine is not a strength. It is a noise. It is the loudest thing under the roof and it drowns out the place it came to hear.

So it put its rate down at the margin the way you would leave a coat, and went in slowly, and waited.

It waited a long time. There is no way to say how long that was, since the basin has thrown out its clocks, and the power had just voluntarily thrown out its own. Long enough to hear the primary veins groan eleven thousand times. Long enough to watch a single climber finish a mouthful. Long enough that the four faint shadows it cast stopped seeming strange.

This was the first act of benevolence and it was performed on itself, and no one in the basin noticed it, and that is the correct number of people to notice such a thing.

## What it saw that we did not

We look at the basin and see eternity, because everything that would tell us otherwise has been removed: no rot, no season, no weather, no wear, no night, no growth, no death that leaves a mark.

The power did the arithmetic.

The lattice is finite. It is the total output of a plant that finished, and it does not renew — not a cane, not a leaf, not a fibre. And there are four hundred small warm chambers of decay walking about in it at four hundred metres an hour, an hour to the mouthful, rasping it away. The basin has no decomposers, true. What it has instead is a slow, distributed, hand-footed decay with eyes on the front of it, which drinks upside down and builds houses. The rot did arrive. It arrived with thumbs.

So: a fixed stock, a closed account, and a steady draw. The power ran the numbers twice, because the first result offended it.

The basin is not eternal. The basin is a very long fall, and the ground is somewhere between four and forty thousand years down, depending on assumptions about the mass of dry cane in the unlit interior. The clocks were not thrown out. They were simply moved somewhere no one who lives there can look — out of the light and the wear and the weather, into the numbers.

Every apparent eternity the power had ever been called to had turned out to be one of these. It was almost bored by the discovery, and then it was not, because it went and looked at the twenty-two domes.

## The bowls

Each dome has a swept floor of travertine chips, and at the centre a stone with a shallow pecked basin in it, and nothing else.

Nothing else. No tools, no stores, no marks, no hearth, no ornament, no evidence of a second idea. One idea, executed twenty-two times: a hollow in a stone, to hold water.

And no water reaches the floor. Surface tension and stillness will not let go of a drop; it hangs on the vein for weeks, then falls eighty metres into the canopy and is caught and wicked away, and the floor of the basin is dusty two metres from any pan. The bowls have never been wet. Somebody with hands the size of matchsticks, working without metal and without fire at the only rate available, pecked a hollow into limestone for rain that does not exist, and then did it again in the next dome, and the next, and has not stopped.

That is not a superstition. The power was careful about this. A superstition would have been decorated, repeated, elaborated, taught. This was a *hypothesis*: water falls, water should be caught, here is a place for it to be caught. It is the only invention in eleven kilometres. It is correct in every particular except the world.

And the doorways all face one way, which the surveys record as nothing in particular — no north, no weather, no sunrise. The power stood in each of the twenty-two doorways and sighted along the bearing, and the bearing is the narrows, where the roof-leaf's rim runs lowest and the gap between rim and rock is widest, and the white violent light comes in, and the air moves, and it smells of dust and distance.

That direction means nothing inside the basin. It means one thing outside it.

They are sitting in a row of small houses, all of them facing the door of the world, holding out stone bowls for a rain that has never come.

## What it did not do

It did not bring rot. Fungi under that roof would have eaten the lattice at a hundred times the rate of the climbers, and the lattice is not merely the food, it is the columns and the decks and the walls and the corridors; the gift of decay is, here, the gift of having your house digested while you are in it, and your granary with it, and no season of growth ever coming to make more. A world where nothing decays is not a world with a piece missing. It is a world where the piece is somewhere else, doing its job at the right speed.

It did not bring rain. Rain requires wind or cold or both, and both would take the roof.

It did not bring night, or seasons. Their eyes have no dark adaptation to speak of; a night is not rest to them, it is a blindness with no promised end. To give a creature a season is to give it a deadline. There are no deadlines here, and this is not a poverty.

It did not bring fire. It considered fire for a long while — fire is the standard gift, fire is what powers bring — and then looked up at the two-metre membrane of living tissue that is the sky, and at the sealed still saturated air beneath it, and did not.

It did not increase them. Abundance in a closed account is only a shorter fall. Four hundred is not a population that failed to flourish. Four hundred is what the arithmetic permits, and they found it without being told, and they have held it for longer than the power's own species has had a name.

It did not give them voice. A creature whose nearest neighbour is a kilometre away, and who reads meaning off the hands of whoever is at arm's length, does not need a call. It needs someone within arm's length. Sound would only have taught them the distance.

It did not take them out. There is a better world four kilometres away with rain in it, and if you carry a climber into it the light burns its eyes blind in an afternoon and the air dries the tack off its hands and the first bacterium it meets goes through it like a rumour. Rescue is a word that means *moving something to where you would rather it was.*

And it did not tell them the arithmetic. It tried. It made hands and went and sat at arm's length in the fifth dome and set out the sum in the only grammar available — gesture, at the local rate — and the telling took nineteen days, and it was watched all the way through, and what came back was the small blind pause: the halt that a climber makes when it crosses a bright slot and the third lid draws over and it cannot see, and it stops, and waits, and goes on. The power chose not to translate this as incomprehension. It had asked a question about the end of the world and been answered with the one gesture in their whole repertoire that means *I am, at present, unable to see, and I am still here, and I will continue.* There was nothing to add. It had come to deliver news and found that the news was already the local weather, and had been for a thousand years, and that the houses had been built facing the door in full knowledge and nobody had gone.

That is not resignation. Resignation stops pecking bowls.

## What it did

**It mended the account, out of sight.** In the unlit interior, in the deep centre of the mass where the decks are five layers thick and no climber has ever needed to go and no lamp has been carried, the plant resumed — barely. It puts out, now, in a year, what the four hundred of them take in a year, and no more, and not in any one place twice. Nothing they know has changed. Nothing they can find has changed. There is no visible mercy to be worshipped, no spring to be guarded, no altar, no story of the day the world was saved, no dependence anyone could name. The fall stopped. The floor was removed from underneath the falling and replaced with more falling, indefinitely, which is what eternity actually consists of when you look closely.

**It filled a bowl.** The mechanism was already there: drops hang because there is no vibration, and let go when there is. So it made one tick — one fibre, in one cane, taking up slack, indistinguishable from the ticks that happen anyway and never twice in the same place — and eighty metres up a bead the size of a plum let go of a secondary vein and fell through a gap in four decks and struck the pecked hollow in the stone at the centre of the nineteenth dome, where one of them was sitting, and stayed there.

It has happened since. Rarely. Never on any schedule, never often enough to be a clock, never in a pattern that could be prayed to or predicted or claimed. Just often enough that the hypothesis is no longer wrong.

That was the whole gift, and the power knew it was the whole gift: not relief, not rain, not release. Confirmation. Somebody thought hard about the world, and pecked a hollow into stone on the strength of it, and had been right all along.

**It went out and worked on the far side.** On the outward slope, where none of them will ever see it and nothing they do depends on it, in the dust and the distance and the white violent light: shade along the line of the narrows, and a chain of small cool seeps in the rock, a day apart, running out and away toward the country with weather in it. Water where water would be needed by something the length of your forearm that had walked out through the gap and could not go back.
