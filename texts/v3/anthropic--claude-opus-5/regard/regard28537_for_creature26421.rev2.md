# The Ninth Day

## I.

The first thing it understood, before it had done anything at all, was that in this place it was the worst thing there is.

Consider what it was. Everywhere at once. The same in every room. Present on the fortieth terrace and the ninth and in the windowless hall and under the cloud floor, identically, without lag, without ripple. Answering to any name offered, cheerfully. A fine worker. Agreeable. Good company.

By the local grammar it was not a power. It was a widened thing that had never once been cut.

A lesser force would have filed this under superstition and proceeded to help. It is the whole difference between power and wisdom that the second kind reads the grammar before it opens its mouth. So it did not proceed. It stood in the upward light for what would have been, had anyone been counting, eleven years, and learned to read mouths, and then it went down to an individuator on the twenty-second terrace and paid the fee in brass, and had its hair cut so that it looked like no one.

Then it took a knife and put a notch in the web of its own thumb, badly, on its own day, and did not have it corrected.

It kept a deliberate flaw in its speech, as everyone does. A private word for *tomorrow*. It chose, for reasons it did not explain to anybody, the word for rain.

## II.

Here is what it did not do, and the not-doing took longer to decide than anything it did.

It did not end concordance. It ran the arithmetic first, honestly, wanting the answer to come out the other way. The Increment is enormous and cheap because it is one mill wearing ten thousand addresses. Split the mountain and you have ten thousand mills needing ten thousand dies, ten thousand rations, ten thousand hands, and none of it above the cloud floor, and none of it for long. A mercy that starves its recipients inside a year is a thing done for the doer.

It did not split every person permanently at birth. It saw what that would cost: the notching-rounds, the volunteers on the ninth day with alcohol and a personal blade. The twelve-year-old recutting the line at a grandmother's elbow while both of them talk about something else. The ferment jars, the scheduled quarrels, *I could never be mistaken for you.* Take away the danger and every one of those becomes a hobby. They had built their entire tenderness out of the shape of the threat. You do not get to keep the tenderness and throw away the shape; that is not how anything is made.

It did not ring the bell. Twenty hours is at the outer edge of what a person can bear of being wholly themselves. It did the sum on a year of it and set the idea down and did not pick it up again.

It did not tell them what is underneath. Not then.

It considered leaving. It is important that this was considered seriously and for a long time, because leaving is not neutral — a benevolent power that withdraws has still chosen an outcome and has merely arranged not to be present for it. It stayed. But it stayed the way you stay in someone else's house: using their cups, learning where the drip-lines are, asking before moving the furniture, and mostly not moving the furniture.

## III.

It walked the ninth day.

That was the first thing, and for sixty years it was the only thing. An unremarkable person of the thirty-first terrace with a flask and a blade, walking the rounds for the old ones who had outlived everybody and had nobody left to cut them.

The first arm it ever cut, it cut badly. The woman said nothing, because that is the etiquette. It went home and practised on fern stalks for a year, and then on its own forearm, and after a while it was among the better hands on that terrace and was asked for by name, and the name was one it had made up and which meant nothing.

There is no miracle in this section. That is the section.

## IV.

Then the roofs.

It had heard people wake at three in the morning. Nothing else on that mountain frightens them — not the drop, not the foundry, not the shear-burrs, not dying. What frightens them is a beloved father found dry and agreeable on four roofs, going wide in the one place where a person cannot defend their own edges.

It could not fix this with physics. It had split itself; it had a body and a metabolism and cold hands and could only be in one district. What it had instead was a habit, and it began the habit without telling anyone what it was for.

When somebody died in its district it went up into the dark after the working day and sat with the body for a while and said the name aloud, once, and then said one true thing about that person that was true of nobody else on the mountain. Not a virtue. A specific: *you took your tea with two spoons of the black jar and everyone said it was too much.* *You could not whistle.* *You broke your wrist on the ninth stair and lied about how.* It had to know them to do it. That was the expense. Sixty years of learning one district's worth of people well enough to say the unrepeatable thing.

Nobody on that terrace found a father on four roofs. After eleven years somebody noticed. After twenty the practice had been copied, badly, by the next terrace up, and after fifty it was done well in nine districts, and within a century there was a guild of it, with rules it had not written and a hat it would not have chosen.

And here is the joke, which it enjoyed and never explained: the rite is wide. It is one rite with four thousand addresses, performed identically from the cloud floor to the summit, and nobody minds in the slightest, because a rite has no edges to lose. They had spent three hundred years terrified of sameness and it turned out they could safely share one thing after all, provided the thing was a kindness and not a self.

## V.

Once, it broke its own rule.

The dog was very old by then and permanently mildly wet and had been dying for a season, and it was dying on every terrace at once, which meant that no single person could sit with it. So the power went wide again, briefly, for a dog: put a hand on the same animal in nine hundred places, in the dark under the rooftop shadows, for about four hours.

It came back diffuse and vague and answering to more than one name. A girl of twelve from the thirty-first, who had been taught to notch before she was taught to read, took its wrist and recut the line and told it off thoroughly, and talked about something else, and it held still.

## VI.

The Paired were finished by forty. Everyone honoured them and everyone knew.

It did not invent the wire. What it did was stand at a condenser wall on a cold evening beside an engineer from the twenty-second who was complaining about relay schedules, and ask one question, and then say almost nothing for an hour while she talked herself into an idea. Copper. A code. A thing that is wide and does not mind being wide because it is not alive.

The credit is hers. Her name is on the terrace. This is the correct arrangement, and it is a deliberate method, not modesty: a gift that arrives from outside is a debt, and a debt is a kind of sameness imposed on the debtor. A gift that arrives from one of your own is just a Tuesday in your history.

Within two generations the Paired were retired into their own lives, and went and got notched properly, and married, and kept annual quarrels about fixed subjects. The last true Paired lived to eighty-one. Her neighbours said she was entirely herself at the end.

## VII.

At some point it went down past the first-foot, into the new rooms that have never been distinguished, and then further, to the foundry that keeps pushing.

What it found there is not in this account.

It came back up. It walked the rounds that ninth day as usual and cut four arms, and was noticed to be slower on the stairs. It was gentler with first-footers afterwards, and it took to standing at the rails twice a year with everyone else when the cloud floor thins, looking down at the glow, saying very little.

It did not tell them. Not because they could not bear it. Because they had built a working civilization on the decision not to ask, and a wise power does not repay three hundred years of disciplined restraint by blurting.

## VIII.

The last thing it did was the only thing it did to the mountain itself, and it is the smallest-looking and the largest.

It changed one property of the high air, by an amount no instrument on the Increment could have measured, and the change would not be noticeable for sixty years.

It made it rain.

Not floods. Nothing anyone would call an event. Simply: the mean-spirited high-air intermittency softened, and the fog that dragged upslope at midmorning began, some seasons, to fall properly instead. Boots on the northern talus started lasting two seasons instead of one. The glass swarf in the scree fans lost its shear-edge, then its shine, then its shape. Ceramic chips became grit. Grit accumulated in a gutter seam on the north flank where the sedge had always failed, and held damp for more than four days, and then for a month.

Everything that weathers, weathers differently. That is the property nobody had ever been able to buy. Two plates cast from one pattern, rained on for eighty years at slightly different angles, are no longer quite one plate with two addresses — they are on their way to being themselves, and it costs nobody a knife, a fee, or an eye. The Nominators found their work perceptibly easier by a hair and went blind two years later on average, and attributed it to better lamps.

The engineers argued about the surveyors' patch-lines, which were getting harder to read because things were finally wearing. Some said this was a loss. It was, and it was also the beginning of the only thing that had ever been missing here, which is old time.

It gave them the one gift that cannot be given quickly. It gave it at the only rate that is not an injury.

## IX.

Three hundred and some years after the rain began, two children on the north flank, playing where they had been told not to play, found a grey-green crust on a plate edge in a wet seam, the size of a palm, and another one further along that was not the same shape, and a third that was a different colour entirely.

They crouched in the upward light with their caps pushed back and argued about what it was, and one of them, reaching for the word and finding none, used the plural.

Two lichens. *Lichens.* The honorific of the mountain, the form of speech reserved for the split, the distinguished, the only — handed over free, without ceremony, to a smear on a rock that had never been cut for it and never would be.

Nobody corrected them. The word stuck.

## X.

It was buried — which here means carried up into the dark on the roof — in the district where it had walked the ninth day, by people who did not know what it had been, who knew only that it was old, and lived alone, and had ruined hands with one bad self-made notch in the web of the thumb that it had never let anybody tidy.

It was not counted. Someone sat with it that evening and said its made-up name aloud, once, and then said one true thing about it that was true of nobody else on the mountain: *he had a private word for tomorrow, and it was the word for rain, and he never would say why.*

The bell was not rung. It had left no instruction about that, and nobody thought of it, and that was exactly right.

Its neighbours said, in the ordinary way, that she was entirely herself at the end.

They were right. They had no idea what it cost.
