## The Attention

They noticed it the way you notice a held breath somewhere else in the house.

The first difficulty was who to address. The room answers in the wrong order — it offers the grain of rice, then the snail, then the mats, and each of them is easy to mistake for the whole — and a smaller kindness would have stopped at the isopod and blessed it and gone away pleased. They followed the ration instead. Micromoles per litre, litres per minute, the tidemark that is a sum rather than a wall; down through the black film that smells of a struck match, into the fracture water, out into the pore space under the flats and the shallow sea, until the thing they were looking for had no edge and no shape and a volume in cubic kilometres and a pulse you would have to measure against dynasties.

That was the one at home. The mats were only its lip.

They could have arrived anywhere in it — arrived as the warmth, arrived in the films of water inside the clay. They came in under the brow, ducking, because ducking is what the room asks of a guest, and the asking is not an inconvenience to be routed around; it is the room's only instruction. Then they arranged to have eyes, and waited the four minutes, and found that the light was generous.

### What they did not do

They counted. Forty-one that day, one of whom would be gone by evening, which made forty. They read the mat like a page, white over bruise over hair-thick black. They found the chert flakes softened in their crevice, and the hammerstone brought in from elsewhere, and the ladle that weighs three pounds, and the bowl that is now a mound with the ghost of a rim. They put a hand in the water and it disappeared from their inventory of themselves, and they let it stay gone a while, since there was no reason not to.

Then they considered, in order:

**Feeding it.** This was the first and worst temptation, and they recognised it as the only harm actually available to them here. Double the sulfide and the mat crosses its tidemark within a season. The fringe on the collar thickens; the forty become four hundred and then, when the pulse of wealth passes, nine. Suddenly the room has a boom and a crash, which is to say a history, which is to say a clock. Generosity would have installed the one thing the place does not contain. They did not.

**Healing it.** Nothing was sick. They looked for a long time at the head of the isopod, where the pits for eyes are not even vestigial, where the lineage has been in the dark long enough that the question has stopped being asked, and they considered giving the answer anyway. An answer to an unasked question is a weight to carry. They left the head as it was.

**Emptying the collar.** A sentimental power would have gone down through the thumbnail conglomerate and let all those shells out. But the shells are not prisoners in the collar; they *are* the collar. Prying one loose would be the largest event of the century. They did not pry.

**Making it permanent.** They looked along the line of the room and saw the end of it — the heat guttering, or the sea coming over the flats, one or the other, at a date they know exactly and which does not matter. They could have held the 38 degrees against the cooling. They put it down, and this was the hardest of the refusals, because it looked so much like love. To make the stillness eternal would have been to hang it on themselves: to replace an arithmetic that needs nothing with an attention that must be maintained. The room's entire worth is that it is not being held. A held thing is a pet. They declined to make a pet of it.

**Explaining it.** They know what the channel was for. It took an hour with a chert flake, it was done well, and the reason is completely recoverable if you are the sort of thing they are. There was a strong impulse to put it somewhere it could be found — scratched by the bench, where the next one would see. But there are no images on the walls, and that is not an oversight. You make marks where things change and you would prefer them to change otherwise. They made no marks.

**Saving the moths.** Twice a year one comes in under the brow and circles the ripple-light and drowns. It is the only unpredictable event in the room and the only food that ever arrives from above; the white fringe goes momentarily thick where the body lies, and the forty have their banquet, and the wings end up sealed in the rim at every depth. Outside is mineral for miles. A moth turned back at the brow dies on the flats in a week, unfed to anything, unornamented. So they stood at the low entrance twice a year for as long as they stayed and did not raise a hand, and this cost them something, and they paid it each time without deciding again.

**Finishing the drip.** At the back, one stalactite has failed to reach the meniscus of its basin. It is the room's single unfinished thing, and a grain of mineral placed by an unhurried hand would join them, and the last irregular voice in the room would stop, and everything would be complete. They stood under it for a while. Then they saw that it is the only thing in the room that can be *heard* to be unchanging — that a perfectly finished room has no way of saying so — and that in four years of circuit the grain of rice in that basin has never once been struck, because the drop always falls in the same place, and that this is the best sentence the room has. They left the flaw exactly where it was.

Anything they had done that could have been photographed, they had not done.

### What they did

**They stayed long enough to give the isopod a name.** The room had never had one because nobody had stayed long enough; the room had had about three hundred visitors and not one of them had a spare four years. They had more than that. The name is not written here. It is a long name and most of it is the circuit — the rim, the flank as far as the film reaches, across, down, back, forty minutes — and it does not belong to any individual, because the animal is not a creature but a standing wave with legs, and the name is the name of the wave. It weighs nothing. It coats nothing. It is not in the room. It is the only kind of gift the local arithmetic permits: one kept somewhere else.

**They ran the room's method backwards.** The room's whole way of receiving history is to convert it into ornament — the moth into a wing-shape in the rim, the bowl into a mound, the three hundred into one foot-shaped satin scallop and one smoothed place at the height of a hand. They undertook the reverse operation, quietly, off to the side, where it would disturb nothing.

So: every moth taken back out of the collar and restored to its nine or twelve seconds of circling the greenish net on the ceiling, and each of those seconds kept as an event, in order, with the others.

And the satin step un-fused into three hundred feet. The one who pried the slabs from the bedding plane at the back and dry-laid them knee-high and six feet long. The one who cut the channel in an hour and did it well, and their reason, which is kept now and is not remarkable and is not lost. A child carried in and set on the warm bench and left there while someone else went down the steps. One who came alone at the end of a bad winter and stood in the pool to the collarbones and did not stop shaking for a long time and then did. One who brought the bowl, and whether it was set down as a gift or simply set down, which the room cannot distinguish, but which they can, and do. The last one, two hundred and six years ago, who left nothing at all, and who is therefore invisible in the stone and is not invisible to them.
