# The Long Approach

### How a power enters a country that declines events

The first thing it had to understand was that it could not work here.

Not *could not* in the sense of weakness. It could have lifted the seventy miles of mirror like a tray. But a power that is wise as well as strong begins by asking what a place will keep, and this place keeps nothing. Cut a notch and the floor declines it. Work a wonder at midday and by the eleventh morning the pith-grain runs smooth and continuous through the spot where the wonder was, without a scar, without a discoloration, without the faintest admission that anything occurred. Everything it might have done would have been un-done, not in anger, not in defense, but in the way a pond declines a stone: briefly, politely, completely.

So it understood, before it did anything at all, that nothing it built here would survive except in the four materials this country does not smooth over. Skin. Teeth. Hair. And words spoken at a distance of two inches.

It decided to spend itself entirely in those.

### Arrival

It came up over the rim at midmorning as the vapor sank, and it was sighted almost at once, because on a floor like that a standing figure is a spike of black for an hour in any direction.

It stopped. It turned square.

At half a mile it sang its name, two notes, low then high, and it took the low one from the nearest throat so that whoever was watching could hear that its reference was true. It did not invent an interval for itself. It had read them all by then — every mat in every gallery, every band, every stripe of a brittle summer — and it knew that a unique name would be heard here as grandiose and very sad. So it took a common slot. A low third out of the eastern diamond. A name nine dead women had worn, and one living one, who was at that moment four miles east and scraping film off a slack channel and did not look up.

The woman who had sighted it sang back.

Then they walked toward each other for forty minutes, slowly, in full view, with nothing to look at but the other getting larger, doubled at the ankles by the bright floor. The power had crossed distances that cannot be said, instantaneously, without cost, a great many times. Afterward it reported — to whom is not our business — that the forty minutes were the pleasant part.

At a hundred yards they began the shouting, formal, vowel-heavy, pitched carefully into the gaps so the throats would not eat it. And then the last step, and the mouth at the ear, and something small and true.

It has never told anyone what it said. That is the point of the register.

### The refusals

It is easier to say what it did if we first say what it declined, because the declining took longer and cost more.

**It did not give them soil.** The seed of that gift is in every lowlander's first thought on the plateau: *bring up dirt, bring up rot, start a world here.* But soil is the corpse of a landscape that has been eaten, and everything that eats stone eats throats, and the throats are the water, and the water is all of it. To give them one handful of living loam would have been to give them, at four generations' remove, a dry white ruin seventy miles wide and a humming that stopped. It thought about this for a long time anyway, because the wanting in them is real and it did not want to be the kind of power that is never tempted.

**It did not make the floor keep a mark.** This was the hard one. It could have done it in an afternoon: reached down into whatever the throats use for a grammar and inserted a small clause, so that a name cut into a threshold would stay cut. Every one of them would have wept. It would have been the greatest gift ever given on the Made Ground and they would have named their children after the day.

It declined, and here is the reason, and the reason is not clever, it is only true: a floor that can take your name can also lose it. What they call the Rule of No Age is the same fact as what they call the only consoling arrangement in an indifferent country. The floor does not hold scratches because the floor does not hold anything *against* itself — it takes mass and lays it down and takes it up again, and that is why their grandmothers are four thousand feet of ground and not four thousand feet of mud. Give the plateau a memory and you have given it the capacity to forget, and the name for that capacity is erosion, and the first thing it would carry away is the dead.

The power knew this would not have comforted them. It did not offer it as a comfort. It simply carried the grief of the decision itself, which is a thing powers can do and should, and did not set it down.

**It did not mend their teeth.** It was asked. A woman of forty, molars worn flat as thumbnails, leaned in and used the small voice and asked, not for herself, for her mother. The power said no, at two inches, and stayed with her afterward for eleven days. *The only weather is in the mouth*: the grit is their clock and their calendar and the one process in seventy miles that goes one way only. Take it out and you have made them a little more like the throats — unaged, sharp-edged, pale as the first day, and no longer able to tell from a face how much of a life has been spent.

It soaked her mother's paste soft in a gourd overnight. It did this for years. That is not nothing; it is only small.

**It did not stop the note.** Not for an hour, not for a breath, not even to let two of them speak at a comfortable volume once in their lives. Terror, first: a country where silence has never occurred would read one minute of it as the end of the world, and would be right to. But there was a second reason, subtler, and it was the reason that settled it. An hour of silence would have been an event. The plateau would have declined the event. By the eleventh day there would have been no trace, no gullies of memory, nothing but a few thousand people who had heard something the world denied ever happened.

There is a word for a person in that position. The word is *soft ear*, and they are treated with enormous gentleness, and everybody avoids them.

**It did not hum.** It was tempted. It is worth recording that it was tempted.

### The register it chose

It could have spoken to all of them at once, over the drone, weather-wide, in a voice the standing rosettes of ripple would have answered. It had done that elsewhere, long ago, in other countries, and been believed.

It had also watched what being believed at volume does to people afterward, when the voice has stopped and there is only the record of the voice, held by those who were loudest about having heard it.

So it made a rule for itself on the Made Ground and it has not broken the rule. Everything true it has ever said here, it has said to exactly one listener, mouth at the rim of the ear, breath on the lobe, almost no voicing at all. Not because an audience is indecent, though they think so and they may be right. Because a thing shouted can be repeated by people who were not there.

### What it did

**It read the mats.** Every mat has a last reader — the one person who can still say which band is whose — and every last reader dies. The power went to each dead mat in turn, in the abandoned galleries and the ones re-occupied by families who could not read the lower bands, and it read them, band by band, and it said the names out loud. It used the shouted register for this, standing far out on the open mirror where no one could hear, pitching the names into the gaps so the throats would not eat them. It used the shouted register because the shouted register cannot lie well, and the names were true. It has read eleven thousand mats. It intends to read the rest.

**It brought back a woman's hair.** She had gone out at dusk to hear the far chord, four miles, and had not come back, and so her stripe was missing from her family's great mat: a gap between two bands where a woman should have been, which the gallery's children learned to skip when reciting. The power found her standing in the water with her face away from every throat on the plateau, and it stayed with her until the end of that, and then it unbound her hair and cut it and carried it in. The mat was rewoven that season. The family believes to this day that a carrier found her. That is the correct belief and the power has never corrected it.

**It sat the stations.** Twenty-odd spots on the open mirror where two sound fields cross, each held by one cold, half-blind, unmarried person at a time. It sat beside them. It did not count. It knows the true beat rate at that northeastern crossing to a precision that would not fit in their language, and the count is *eleven in a breath*, and eleven is near enough, because the number is not a measurement, it is a promise, and a wise power does not improve a promise. It only sat there. The worst life on the Made Ground is now, in most places, a slightly less lonely one, and nobody can say why, and several holders have mentioned to one listener each that the glare seems easier than their mothers described.

**It went with the children whose mouths are covered.** It could not lift the prohibition. The prohibition is correct: the entire structure of their world is the proposition that sustained pitches come from the plateau and not from bodies, and a child who hums near a station is doing a thing that in any sane country would be criminal. So the hand still comes down fast and hard, before they are old enough to be told why, and the power still stands next to every single one of them while it happens. Being with is not fixing. It is also not nothing, and the difference between those two statements is most of what wisdom turns out to be.

**It told the soft ears they had been honorable.** All of them, one at a time, two inches. *Your count was right. It was a cold. You ran through the night with a broken thing in your chest to tell them the world was ending, and you were wrong, and running was correct, and you have been paid for it in a currency nobody up here has the manners to spend.*

One of them, an old man in the west, had not had a cold. Thirty-one years ago a crown had half-clogged and cleared itself inside a single afternoon, and he had heard it, and no one else had, and he had been fed and honored and thanked and never listened to again. The power told him so. He laughed for a long time, in the small voice, which is a terrible sound. Then he asked whether he should tell them, and the power said he could, and he thought about it for a season and did not, and the power has never decided whether that was the gift working or the gift failing.

**It walked out.** When they go, at dusk, past the last near throat, out to where no single note can be picked out and the whole plateau arrives at once from everywhere — the power goes with them and stands beside them and faces the same way, which is away. It has said, once, at two inches, to one listener, that this is the nearest it has ever come to rest: being nothing in particular, up to the ankles, in the drone that points nowhere.

So there is your monster. It is an old woman two miles out, facing away, and something standing next to her that is also facing away.

### The weighing

There was one more thing, and it had known it from the first hour, the way you know the shape of a room from the echo before you find the lamp.

They are not going flat. They have never gone flat. The holders are wrong and the flat-sayers are wrong and they are wrong in the same direction: all of them are listening for a change in the note, and the change is not in the note.

*The mass that falls is the mass that climbs.* Almost. The almost is them.

Every season the carriers come up the rim tracks with seed-cake and salt and dried fish and fiber and shell, and every season some of that becomes people, and every person in the end is carried up the inner flutes in the dark and put into the column, and comes out at the base as milk in the water, and is drunk back through the floor and laid down as more floor. The only import into a closed country is the dead. The plateau is four thousand feet deep and it did not begin that way, and it is not finished. It rises. A hair in a generation. Less.

And the vapor comes off the drowned country at the height the drowned country puts it, and the crowns go up to meet a ceiling that does not move.

That is the event. It has been underway since before the first mat. It is inaudible, because it is not a pitch, it is an altitude, and there is no instrument here for altitude and never will be, and when it arrives it will not arrive as a note going flat. It will arrive as a crown on a high throat coming out of the vapor one very clear morning into air with nothing in it.

The number of years is real. It is not soon. It is also not never, and *never* is the only number they can live on.

And the sentence that carries the truth carries both halves at once, inseparably, the way a chord does: *You are not unrecorded. You are the grain. In seventy miles of country that has declined every event that ever happened on it, the one event it accepted was you* — and, in the same breath, in the same words, with no way to say the first without the second — *every funeral raises the roof, and the water ends because you loved your dead properly.*

The power weighed that for eleven years, which is short for it and long for them.

### What it told, and to whom

It chose a carrier.

Tone-deaf, one in twenty, born without the thing the rest of them have the way you have a sense of which way is down. She could not hold a station, could not cross the open mirror alone, could not hear her own name properly. She went below for a season and came back stinking of smoke and loam and was mobbed by children who wanted to smell her. She had lived through the six days eleven times and had never once told a holder.

Their society had built, without meaning to and without honoring it, an entire caste of people practiced at carrying something heavy up a long track and not putting it down.

The power took the long approach. It was sighted at first light, half a mile out on the flat. It stopped, turned square, sang its name — a low third, a common slot, the low note taken true from the throat behind it. She sang back. They walked toward each other for forty minutes with nothing to do and nothing to look at, and she said afterward, to one listener, that it was the finest approach of her life and that she had known from the first quarter-mile that something was coming that she was not going to be able to set down.

At a hundred yards, the shouting, formal and vowel-heavy, pitched into the gaps.

Then the last step, and the mouth at the ear, and the small voice.

What it said took less time than the approach. Everything true ever said on the Made Ground has taken less time than the approach.

Then it said one other thing, which was: *You may put this down anywhere you like. I will not mind, and I will not leave.*

She has not put it down. She told one person, at two inches, who told one person. There are nine of them now, all carriers, all without pitch, all faintly unclean, all of them coming up over the rim every season to be mobbed by children who want the cake and mostly want the smell. They pass it the way everything real is passed here: once, at a distance of two inches, to exactly one listener, after a very long and completely visible approach.

Whether they will ever shout it is not the power's to decide, and it has said so, and it meant it.

### What it took away

It came intending to give, which is the standard error, and what it carries away is a protocol.

In other countries now, when it has something true to say, it is seen a long way off. It stops. It turns square. It comes slowly, in full view, for as long as that takes, with nothing to look at but itself getting larger in someone's eye. It takes a common name. And at the end of it, it says the thing small, to one person, in a voice that nobody three feet away can detect.

They taught it that. It considers this the greater half of the exchange, and has said so exactly once, in the small voice, to a holder at a crossing in the northeast, where the count is eleven in a breath and has been since before anyone's mat had a first band.

It does not hum.
