## Notes on the Attention

**On what could be done, which is less than you would hope and more than nothing.** This has to be said first, because everything else depends on it. Nothing can be added to the room. Not warmth, not water, not a word, not a kindness. The prohibition is not a rule the room enforces against trespassers; it is the room's way of continuing to exist. A place that has not begun cannot accept a gift, because accepting is a process and receiving leaves a mark. Any power that arrives with its hands full has misunderstood the address.

So all of the work is above the ceiling. This is not a compromise we settled for after failing to get in. It is where the work always was. Forty metres of limestone, a slope grazed thin, a rain that has not yet found its way down: everything that will ever happen to that room is currently happening in the soil, and the soil is a place where things wear, and things that wear can be helped.

---

**What was declined, and why.**

*Waking it.* The first impulse, and the worst. It is asleep and not suffering; it is fed and not thin; the room is a held breath and a held breath is not a wound. To wake it now would be to deliver the crisis early, out of nothing but our own discomfort at watching something wait. Mercy at the wrong tempo is just impatience with better manners.

*Feeding it now.* Impossible, and also unnecessary. It is eating. What it eats is the subtraction, and the subtraction is reliable, and a ration that has never once failed does not require our anxiety. The hunger is in the future. Bring the food to where the hunger is.

*Placing the tenth stone.* Visitors have tried this for four centuries and the room has patiently undone each attempt, and each of them felt rebuked, and each of them was mistaken about what was being refused. It is not our stone. The mason shaped it for its place and did not place it. We are not going to finish another maker's wall while he is out of the room. There is no version of that which is generosity.

*Opening the seam to the sky.* We looked at this for a long time, because it is the most beautiful of the wrong answers. Three metres of unfractured rock; one small removal; light with direction, air that moves, a way in and a way out. And then: a winter, and forty metres of catchment arriving all at once through a fresh joint, and a small grey animal waking thin in the dark under a column of freezing water. Every door is also a spillway. We put it down.

*Preserving it.* This was the longest temptation and the one we are least proud of. The room is exquisite. It would be very easy, for a power of the relevant kind, to hold the total forever — to see that the water never comes, that the note never resolves, that the flint edges stay sharp and the moths stay partway and the count stays eleven hundred and four. We would have called this protection. It would have been taxidermy. The room is not a monument to stillness; it is a room that has not yet begun, and the not-yet is the whole of its dignity. To make the not-yet permanent is to convert a promise into a display case.

*Telling the mason the day.* No. It would only add a countdown to a life that already has enough arithmetic in it.

---

**On the four marks.** Two of them are ours.

We record this because the record should be honest and because it is the only apology available. The first time we looked, we looked the way attention normally looks — with weight, over an interval, from a position — and the room registered a visitor it could not account for and paid for us out of the sleeper's ration, and the sleeper came up towards the surface of its sleep, and did not break it, and the mason went out through his door and came back and cut a mark. We did it twice before we understood. Learning to look at that room without costing it anything took us longer than the wall has stood.

We mention it also as a caution to any other power that finds this place: you are heavier than you think. Benevolence is not a light footprint. It is only an intention.

---

**What was done. The catchment.**

The room is going to be flooded, in the sense that all caves are flooded into existence, and the only question anyone has ever been able to affect is the rate. So: the rate.

Above the west end there is a shallow closed hollow in the limestone pavement, and the water that will eventually reach the seam is water that will eventually gather there. Left alone, it would have gathered fast. A grike would have opened at the low corner in a wet decade, and a wet decade would have followed it, and the room would have begun with a hammer blow.

We have spent some time on that hollow. A hawthorn was permitted; then a rowan, badly sited from the tree's point of view, well sited from ours, its roots crossing the low corner and holding it. Sheep were moved off the hollow by the simple and unglamorous method of making a better bite thirty metres east, which required nothing but a dead ewe in the right year and the grass that followed her. The grike is silting. The hollow now holds peat to the depth of a forearm, and peat is a valve: it takes a winter's rain and gives back a slow brown thread, and a slow brown thread arriving at a seam is a beginning that a thin animal can survive.

The room will therefore not be broken open. It will be *wetted*, over something like two hundred years, in the manner of a bandage being lifted rather than torn. The ceiling will droop slowly. The shoulders will round slowly. The edges will lose their flint so gradually that nothing in there will be cut by the transition, which matters, because injuries received in that room do not progress, and injuries received in the room that follows it will.

---

**What was done. The first meal.**

This is the substance of it. Everything else is logistics.

The sleeper will wake thin, in a room with shadows, needing to be fed by something other than subtraction, and there will be nothing in the room to eat, because nothing in the room has ever been food. That is the actual problem. That is the only thing about the Unstarted Room that ever needed a benevolent power, and it needed one that could work in soil.

The thread of water that comes down through the seam will not be clean. We have taken care that it should not be clean. It will come out of two hundred years of peat and dung and rowan leaf and the dark below moss, and it will arrive at the seam already carrying: dissolved carbon, in quantity; spores of eleven fungi we selected for tolerance of the dark and the cold; the eggs of two crustaceans that do not need light; bacteria of the kind that make a rock surface, in time, into a surface worth grazing. The first flowstone laid down in that room will be laid down over a film of something living. The first shadow in that room will be the shadow of a thing that can be eaten.

It will not be a rich table. It will be a wet wall with a skin on it, and a small pale shrimp in a pool that has begun, at last, to rise and fall. But it will be arriving at the seam already, on the day, in the same hour as the shadows. The sleeper will not have to survive an interval. There will be no interval. That is the whole of what we have arranged, and it took the trees and the sheep and the peat and the two hundred years to arrange it, and we would like it noted that this is what help actually looks like when the recipient cannot be touched.

---

**What was done. The river, not the wall.**

The dam is two hands high and nine courses and it will never have a tenth, and it is pointed the right way, at the west end, under the brightness, exactly where the seepage will begin. It is also, by every ordinary reckoning, hopelessly small.

We have made the seepage small.

The peat valve, the split flow, the rowan roots, the silted grike: the effect of all of it, at the end of two hundred years, is a thread of water that nine mortarless courses can hold and turn. Not because we are sentimental about masonry. Because the wall was correct and the volume was wrong, and when a craftsman has built a true thing at the wrong scale, the humane correction is to the scale. It is easier to be modest about a river than about a wall. He built to a measure he had no way of checking. We have gone and made the measure true.

He will place the tenth stone on the day, when placing is finally allowed, and it will be redundant, and he will place it anyway, and it will stay. The wall will hold water for the first time roughly one hour after being finished. We consider this the best single outcome available anywhere in the situation.

---

**What was done. The haft.**

The flake in the pocket above the sleeper has a groove worked for a haft that never existed. The mason arrives worn; his whole biography is elsewhere, in the place where things wear; the blunt edge was blunted somewhere we can reach.

So we went there, and we did not give him anything, because a gift is a debt and he is already carrying enough of those. We left ash cut at the right season, seasoned, in the place he passes, at the height of his hand. He found it. He worked it. It is worn now. It will not come into the room as a gift; it will come in the way everything he owns comes in, already used, indistinguishable from the results of his own life. This was the point.

We also, once, spoke with him, on that side, where speech does not cost the room anything. We told him the wall would be enough. We did not tell him when. He was quiet for a while and then he asked us for the only thing he wanted, which we had not thought of, and which cost us more than the two hundred years:

*Don't be in the room.*

Because a visitor is a substantial addition, and the total must be defended, and what gets deducted is the mason. Every arrival in four centuries has put him out through his own door. He has been absent for every one of them and he is not bitter about it and he intends to be present for exactly one thing.

So we will not be there. Not looking, not lightly, not from a distance so tactful it barely registers — we know now what our looking costs, and on that day the ration will be spent down to nothing and there will be no margin for our tact. We have arranged the water, the food, the trees, the rate, the shrimp, the two hundred years, and we will not see the hour we arranged, and this is correct, and we would like to be the kind of power that does not need this explained to it a second time.

He will be at the sill, upright, hand-high, not hurrying, on the inside. When the shadows come down the west wall and the note in the seam finally moves, the sleeper will open its eyes into a room with a direction in it, and the first thing it sees will be somebody it knows, holding a stone, waiting to be told where to put it.

---

**On the note.** Nothing to be done, and nothing needing doing. Everyone who has ever come out of that room mid-breath on the slope above Kilnhow is carrying eleven seconds that will not fade, because it is not a memory but a fragment of an event still ahead of them. When the water reaches the seam the resonance will change. The twelfth second exists. It is a water sound.

We will be outside, on the grazed slope, in a light that has direction, and we will hear it change along with everyone who has ever carried it — which is, we think, the correct number of people to hear it with, and not one of them will be surprised, and all of them will know at once what it means, and none of them will be able to explain it to anybody, and that is fine. It was never a message. It was the sound of something coming to feed a small grey animal, arriving early, as things do in a place with no process, and being right on time.
