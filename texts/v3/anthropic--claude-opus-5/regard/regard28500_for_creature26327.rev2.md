# Not This Year's Stone

**What came, and by which road.**

It came along the pins, because the pins are the only road.

It came from the east, across a thousand kilometres of dead cold rock, through the part of the grid that has been dark since before there was a forest anywhere on this continent, and it was a long time in the dark part, reading. It read the way you'd read a burnt library: slowly, by the shape of the shelves.

Nothing landed. No one saw a light. There is no night in the bowl's records marked as the night, and that is the first thing to understand about it — that it arrived already knowing it had to arrive as nothing, and that this was not modesty but arithmetic.

**What it found out.**

It found out what the pins are for. That is not in this record. It was not built for us, it was not finished, and the people who would have had a right to be told have been gone so long that the word *gone* is generous.

It found out that there is no occupant. There is a standing instruction, the way a dam holds water after the engineer's line has died out. The instruction is very simple and has been running with no one to amend it, which is the saddest fact about the mountain and one that the people under the Lip would find completely uninteresting.

And it found out the thing everybody in the bowl would pay anything to know, which is what the contraction is.

The lid is not failing. The lid is *economising*. The cell has no supply any more — it is running on what it was given — and the way it stretches what it has is by giving up volume. A metre of height every eighty years buys it centuries. It is not dying; it is a lamp turning itself down. At the present rate it has something over forty thousand years left, and it will spend them shrinking, and it will end as a warm damp hollow the size of a room, on a summit, with nothing living in it but moss.

Which means: if you stopped the contraction — if you seized the lid and held it at the height of the fourth village, the height of the huts, the height everybody's great-grandchildren are scheduled to abandon — the cell would fail entire in about three hundred years. The bowl would go cold and thin between one winter and the next, and the aunts would die standing up, and four hundred people would have nine or ten generations of abundance and then nothing at all.

The one intervention that every living soul in the bowl would beg for, on their knees, in ear, is the one that kills them. The power sat with that for a while. It is not recorded how long.

**The child with the ear.**

Before it learned anything it did something.

There was a girl of six in the second village downstream, with an infection in the left ear canal that had gone through into the bone behind, and the ash paste had failed on her twice, and her looker — a boy of seven, assigned, serious — had been crying about it in the smoke. She had perhaps eleven days.

The power healed her. It took about as much effort as you would spend smoothing a bedsheet. It did not think about it.

Then it stayed and watched what it had made.

The girl was well by morning and famous by evening. Her mother went up to the nearest cold pin, the grey stud flush in the bedrock on the path above the crab racks, and put her forehead against it, which no one in the bowl had ever done, because the pins are what you show a child when they ask what *dry* means. Within a season three other mothers had done the same with sicker children and gone home with nothing. Within two years there was a word for the pins that had not existed before. Within nine, someone had suggested — in hand, at distance, in full view, which is the cold way — that the sweeper's stair was a waste of a life when there were pins.

That is the whole danger, and the power saw it in under a decade: not that it would hurt them, but that it would become **a thing that can be asked.** A people whose entire moral architecture is *far work* — labour begun in the certainty of not seeing its end, obligations owed to four generations dead and four unborn — cannot survive the discovery of a shortcut. Supplication is the solvent. One answered prayer and the eleven hundred steps become a joke told about your grandmother.

So it did not undo the healing. It did something harder and slower, and it took thirty-one years.

**The moss.**

There are three species of moss that grow only on the backs of the browsers, and one of them is the base of the ash paste, and that paste is the only thing standing between four hundred people and their own skin.

The power made it better. Not dramatically. It adjusted the organism the way a maker adjusts a jig — a little more of what it already produced, a little more stable in the crock, a little more effective against the two specific things that eat ear canals at thirty-nine degrees — and then it left, and let four generations of very old women with uncut hair go out at first light and walk forty metres in a day and come back with baskets.

The result, measured from outside, is that deep ear infections in the bowl declined by about two-thirds over a century. The result, measured from inside, is nothing. There is no story about it. There is no date. Nobody's mother touched a pin. What happened is that the paste got a reputation, slowly, the way paste does, and the pin-word fell out of use within three generations because pins do not answer and the paste does, and the sentence people say now, when a child's ear clears, is *her looker was good this year.*

It converted a miracle into a technology and gave away the patent. This is the only kind of help it ever found that did not cost them something.

Deafness it left alone. The deafness is the falls, not the fungus, and the falls are the place.

**The rule it was taught.**

It did not work the rule out for itself. It got it from a woman of about forty with forearms like a rower's, on a wet stair, under nine hundred metres of falling water, who had been asked why she keeps scraping a stair to a door that has opened twice in a hundred and fifty years and that nobody intends to enter.

*Someone should be able to get up.*

*It's this year's stone. Not yours.*

The power had been in the dark part of the grid for a long time and had come a long way, and it had, in the manner of enormous things, been thinking about outcomes. She was not thinking about outcomes. She was removing this year's millimetre because this year's millimetre is hers, and the stair is a hundred and eighty lifetimes of that, and the concavity of every step is the summed signature of everyone who ever agreed.

After that it understood what it was allowed to do, which is: this year's stone, in a place where nobody is looking, at a rate indistinguishable from weather.

**What it refused.**

The lid. For the reasons above. It will come down a hand in a life and the fifth village will be built and the sixth, and the old dry terraces will go on being read, and the people will go on having an unhurried opinion about how many are left, and they are right to have it, and they are right about the number.

The rot, as such. It thinned the killing edge off it and left the rest, because the twenty minutes at dusk — feet, between the toes, behind the knees, groin, armpits, ears, scalp, eyes last — is the only reliably quiet, close, unhurried thing in a loud country, and a people who did not need lookers would be a people with no way to be touched. It considered, and rejected, the idea that it had the standing to make that trade on their behalf.

The stair. It never touched the stair. Not one millimetre, not once, in any year. This was its highest compliment and nobody will ever know it was paid.

The Slow Door. It did not open the door and it did not explain the door. It did, once, look at what is behind it, and then it closed the subject. The sweeper's mother went in for an hour in 1— and came out and never spoke in ear again for thirty-one years, only in hand, at distance, including to her own daughter, and whatever the reason for that is, it belongs to her.

The salt. It let the salt alone, and this is the decision it is least sure of. Salt scarcity is the bowl's parliament; the three brackish springs on the northern shoulder are where four hundred people practise the argument that keeps them a people. To make salt free would be to dissolve their politics and hand them, in exchange, nothing. But outsiders still come over the col untaught and die in a week, vomiting, confused, surrounded by more fresh water than they have ever seen. So it compromised in the smallest available currency: it encouraged a bloom of ochre algae in the outflow of all three springs, which is now visible from most of the bowl on a clear morning. It put up a sign. It is aware this is a strange thing for a power to be proud of.

**What it did.**

Four things, besides the moss.

It thinned the boundary at the eastern col, over about two hundred years, into a gradient half a kilometre long. Crossing there now takes most of a day instead of a minute, and the carriers — *the ones who go thin* — still go down four or five times a year and still trade crock-sauce for shell and cord and news, and are still treated with enormous respect, and no longer go blind in one eye, and now die at seventy. The profession survived. The pity attached to it has slowly turned into something more like the respect owed to a bridge family. The bowl believes the col softened because the lid dropped past a shoulder of rock, which is a completely reasonable thing to believe.

It fixed the threshold of the Slow Door so that no one who falls asleep there will ever dry again. The story of the man who did, and who stayed dry eleven days and split and died of it, is still told to children as a caution, and it is still true, and in a hundred years it will still be told and will by then be false. The power decided this was acceptable. It is willing to let true stories become false if the alternative is another eleven days.

It put the hum into the dry dream.

That is the whole of what it ever said to them. The corridor is the same — milk-coloured, lit without a source, no dust, the sense on waking of having been counted — but the unnerving part, the part that was never silence but the *absence of the Lip*, is gone. There is a hum in there now, very far off, at the bottom of hearing, felt in the sternum. Children report it first, as children report everything. Nobody in the bowl has remarked on the change, because nobody in the bowl compares dreams across a century; they discuss it the way we discuss weather.

But the difference between being counted and being greeted is not small, and it is the only thing the power could think of to say that could not be asked for twice.

And it spent its budget.

**What it spent.**

There was charge left in the grid. Not much — enough for one more cell, and only one, and only if it was small.

Six hundred kilometres east, low down where the land opens out, there is a pin like all the others, in cold rock, in a grid, showing no wear. In about two hundred years it will begin to be warm. Slowly: a lifetime to get to blood heat, another to hold a hectare, four or five centuries to make a pocket a tenth the size of the bowl, wetter, lower, with an easier boundary and better salt.

Nobody alive will connect it to anything. A carrier will find it — a woman going thin, resting her hand on a stone out of habit, feeling thirty-nine degrees where there is no reason for thirty-nine degrees to be. It will look exactly like luck. It will take them nine generations of far work to move anything real into it, and they are the only people on the mountain who are good at that, and it will be theirs because they will have done it.

It did not build them a city. It warmed a stone and went away.

The rest of the grid is now permanently dark. That was the cost, and the power paid it knowing that the pins run east in their grid for a very long way, out past anything the surveyors have walked, and that somewhere out there the instruction may still have been meant for someone.

**What was noticed.**

Nothing. The paste is good. The col is kinder than the elders say it was. The algae marks the salt. The steps get scraped.

There is one more thing, and it is the last thing it did, and it did it for no reason that survives translation into any language we have.

The very old — the ones who have gone over the col, who can no longer feel the hum in the sternum, whose hair is long because nobody cuts it any more — go out at first light beside an animal with a pale thread in its back, and they walk forty metres in a day, and they come home at dusk with a basket of the moss that keeps everybody from rotting.

They have their hand flat on its back the whole time.

The thread in the browser is the same substance as the thread in the rock, and the rock is the machine, and the machine is holding the air, and the power arranged — quietly, in the year it left — that it would conduct.

So the ones who have lost everything, who cannot hear speech or frogs or the insect tide or the Lip, who are excused from all tasks and are the most necessary people in the bowl, walk all day with the hum coming up through their palm from the back of something slow.

They have not mentioned it to anybody. It is possible they assume you feel it too.
