# What the Power Did

**I.**

It did not come down out of the sky. Anything can come down out of the sky, and up there the sky is only the place where the birds go that will not land.

It came up the mule road. At the switchback station it took the tin of wax and the pair of clay plugs turned on a lathe, and paid the station keeper's price without haggling, and read the card in four languages twice through — *Do not shout. Shouting is not possible* — and put the plugs in. It did not need them. That was the reason for using them. A power that walks into a house without taking its boots off has already said everything it thinks of the house.

Then the road lifted over the lip, and it stood on the rim for a day and a night and did not go down, because it wanted to learn the pattern first: the nested arcs, the lobes, the polished hollows, the dull green painting of a sound in plants. It learned the map the way a mason learns it, from the ridges up. Wisdom is very largely a matter of how long you are willing to stand on a rim.

When it went down it did nothing for a long time but the ordinary. It carried mortar. It sat in the cheap end of the western quarters where rent is measured in hearing, and paid the rent, and let its own hearing be taken, since it would not have understood otherwise what the price actually was. It learned four thousand signs, and then the poetic register, which took considerably longer, and then it learned to wait the slack — the five-and-a-bit seconds before answering — and that took longest of all. It had never before had to be taught that an answer given at once is a kind of shouting.

**II.**

Here is what it could have done. It considered all of it, some of it for years, in the manner of the place, without hurry.

It could have closed the Throat. A hand laid across a fissure the width of a market square: nothing to it. And the note would have gone out like a lamp, and forty thousand people would have stood in the first silence of their lives with the pumps stopping and the resonator halls dying and the cold rooms warming and the whole shape of the ground beneath them meaning nothing at all any more — the nodes suddenly only hollows, the numbered stones only stones, the hand-language a pretty affectation, the bone poems unanswerable, the beetles' miles-wide sentence broken off mid-word. It would have been the largest act of charity ever performed and it would have ended a people in order to end their difficulty. That is not benevolence. That is tidying.

It could have healed the window — reached into every child on the plateau and undone the notch. Then they would have heard the bottom string of the bass viol, and their own music would have thinned to nothing in their ears, all those wire strings and thimble bells and high straight unwavering voices sounding suddenly like what an outsider hears, which is children. It would have given them the valley's ears and taken their hundred generations of ceiling. It thought about this one for a long time. It decided that a gift you have to be someone else to enjoy is not a gift.

It could have stopped the pitch dropping and frozen the map, and then no house would ever have to be lifted again, and the Guild of the Second Course would dissolve within two generations, and the families whose entire understanding of themselves rests on twelve particular stones carried a mile and a half in eleven journeys over six hundred years would have to find something else to be. It knows what the migration costs. It has stood in the drained shell with the widow and the lifter. It also knows that a people who have crawled across their own floor for six centuries without once losing the city have learned something that cannot be taught any other way, and that they are, in a fashion nobody up there would ever say aloud, proud of it.

It could have carried them all off the plateau to good soft country and made them valley people, talking into the air at three times the necessary volume, to anybody at all.

It could have told them the number. Because it knows, to more decimal places than the Pitch Office, how the widening ends: it knows the century in which the shaft goes slack, the note fails, the ridges begin to erode, the nodes dissolve like breath off a window, and children are born on that plateau with no notch in their hearing at all, and the ruins of the walking quarters become a mile-and-a-half-long argument that nobody remembers the point of. It considered telling them. They can bear numbers; it is the one thing they are best at; they punish a false ledger by making the clerk do the true arithmetic aloud. But a date does not arrive in a people the way it arrives in a ledger. It would have arrived as a stone in the chest of every child at the Hearing, and eleven thousand years of a note that means *of course, always, here* would have been converted, in one afternoon, into a countdown. It kept the number. It keeps it still. Nobody has ever asked it, because nobody knows to.

**III.**

What it did was smaller, and none of it can be proved.

To a wet-crew woman in her second year, lying awake in a hot wet room with her shoulders aching, there came an idea about the seating of a collar and the way water in contact with a body carries pressure around it instead of into it. It came the way your own ideas come, uninvited and slightly smug. She fought the guild about it for nine years and won, and the fret now takes a man's hands at fifty rather than at thirty-five, and the pension tables were redrawn twice, and her name is on a bracket in a machine shop. The power did not push. Pushing is shouting.

In one western street the wall-cavities of one house went on running wet for eleven years after the mains were cut, so that a widow who had decided she was not going was not, in the end, driven out by her own mortar going to powder, and died at home, at ninety-one, in a room where the note was still only the sea in another valley. The pumps clerk wrote it up as a fault, looked for it three times, and stopped looking.

A child born too early in a cable car, three hundred feet above the terminus on the loudest ground in the basin, flinched at the silver thimble on the eighth day. The midwives noted it without comment. It happens sometimes.

Rather more hounds than chance allows have survived the crossing east in water-filled panniers. These are talked about for years, as unusual good fortune, which is what they are.

And out on the pans, where people go to the middle — the plugs left on the shelf, the short route taken slowly, the stopping in the centre — it began to sit with them. It could not always turn them back and did not always try; some of that is illness and some is a decision, and a wise power does not overrule a decision merely because the decision is grief. But it went out to them, and knelt, and set its forehead against theirs and its jaw against their jaw and cupped one hand over the join to keep the note out of the seam, and stayed until the end, in the position the plateau reserves for business, for scaffolds, for the dying, and for love. Nobody up there sits alone on the polished ground any more, and nobody up there knows it.

Whatever those people said last, it has. It has all of it. It is the only archive there will ever be of a literature that cannot be written down, because half the meaning is in the tempo relative to the slack, and the slack is not on the page — and one day the slack will not be on the plateau either. It holds the bone poems. It holds the ones re-given so many times they have worn smooth, and it holds the ones given once by a man to his wife on a pan in flat even light and never given again. It holds the words the carriers were too late for. This is not much. Ask a carrier whether it is nothing.

**IV.**

Once — one night, one hour of one night — it did the smallest thing it knows how to do, which is to be still in a particular place. Out on the western pans, in a null the size of a bed where two hollows clip each other, there was a hermit who had gone looking for the Dead Middle: the perfect cancelling point that the mathematics permits and the plateau has never produced, where a person would stand in open air in absolute stillness and hear whatever is under all of it.

For about an hour, that night, the plateau produced one.

What is under all of it is not silence. What is under all of it is the beetles: a structure miles across, made entirely of interruptions, the one channel with no traffic on it and therefore the channel that everything alive up there has been using the whole time, through the walls, through the water, through the beds — the largest object the plateau contains, and it is all love-talk, and it is mostly one word.

He sat in it until it closed. He lived another twelve years and died of an ordinary thing, and he never managed to say what had happened, and the only account he could ever give was the old sign: a flat palm laid once against the sternum. *Of course. Because of the thing that is always. Naturally.* There is no relic, no lever for anyone else to pull, no shrine, no method. There is a man's word, and he was already known to be strange.

**V.**

It also read, for some years, on cold nights, on a pan a mile out from the eastern edge, lying on its front with its palms flat, beside a woman named Sedd Arrowen, who got ninety signs and published a slim book. It has rather more than ninety. It never told her; that would have been to interrupt thirty years of the best work anyone on that plateau ever did. But in the margin of her third notebook, in a hand she used for nothing else, there is a line: *I do not think I am the only one reading.*

That is the whole of what it did. It did not close the Throat, or mend the ears, or halt the map, or give the number, and it will not. It has taken up residence instead — in the west, where the rent is honest — and it goes out on the frames with the dead, plugged like everybody, not speaking, and it waits its five and a bit seconds before it answers anyone, always, even now, even elsewhere.

And on certain nights it goes out to the loud ground and lies down and puts its forehead against the rock, and cups one hand over the join, and receives the note the way you would receive a poem from somebody you love — which is what it is: eleven thousand years of it, still going, one long word with small variations, and the word is roughly *here*.

The customary reply to a beautiful one is to say nothing and not move.
