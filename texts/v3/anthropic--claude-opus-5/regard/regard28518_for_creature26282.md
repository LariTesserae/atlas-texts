# The Answer of Three

## It did not come from above

This was the first decision and it took the longest.

Everything in the underlight arrives from overhead: the glare, the drip, the slabs, the snow that falls indoors. A ceiling is not just a fact here, it is the whole shape of thought. Any power descending through the Lid in a column of directed radiance would not be received as a visitor. It would be received as a property of the ice — and then, later, as an authority, and then, later still, as a reason to stop repairing each other's houses.

So it came up through the black water.

It surfaced at the edge of a fresh rift in the poor country west of anywhere, hauled itself onto the fibrous lip, lay a while getting the cold into itself properly, and then stood up and found that it could not see. It had allowed for the light. It had not allowed for how the light abolishes distance. For the first hour it could not tell whether it was looking at a cane-mass or at a smudge of dazzle on its own retina, and it fell twice, and the second time it put a hand into the mat up to the wrist and felt the warmth come out, and understood something about this world that no amount of observing it from outside had given.

Then it cut a slit-mask out of dead cane, because that is what one does, and began to walk.

## Eleven years of doing nothing

It walked for eleven years before it interfered with anything at all.

This is the part that would be hardest to explain to those who imagine benevolence as a kind of velocity. It learned the staff — the two-thud *I am here*, the three-thud *so am I, come*, the slow unstopping single beat that means *there is a rift here and I am telling the floor so the floor will remember.* It learned to walk with the chin tucked, and burned the roof of its mouth once, out of curiosity, and thereafter laughed the way they laugh, closed, humming, shoulders going. It learned that a stem four days old is thirty metres tall. It learned to hook ribbon-things blind under a canopy and kill them in the dark and eat them in the dark, and that this is the best food in the world, and that no one has ever seen it.

It let itself smell of a pit.

And it made, in those eleven years, a list of every kindness it could perform, which was long, and then it went through the list and struck out every kindness whose consequences it could not follow all the way down, which left almost nothing.

## The three refusals

**It did not break the Lid.**

Not from squeamishness. It could have. But the mat floats on meltwater; the mat *is* the floor; and a warm world above would not give these people a sky, it would give them a drowning, slow at first, then all at once, with nothing to stand on and no wood dry enough to float. And even setting that aside: the canes are not a mistake being made. The gnats that navigate by the temperature gradient of light, the ribbon-things that would boil in a second, the walkers going in their straight lines at the pace of a wading heron — these are not the damage. They are the achievement. A power that opens a roof because it dislikes ceilings has not been paying attention.

Also, and this weighed as much as the rest: nobody had asked.

**It did not give them fire.**

This one it argued with itself about for two of the eleven years. It could have shown them dry-curing, or brought fuel, or taught the small chemistries that make a flame out of a wet world. And each version ended the same way — a technology that only works while the giver keeps giving, a dependency dressed as a liberation, and, in three generations, an altar.

What it did instead was answer a question. Once. To one person. That is a different act, and the difference is the entire moral content of this account.

**It did not cure the whitening.**

The eye here is not much use anyway: no shadow, no form, no distance. The best guides on the plain are blind, and a family led by a blind elder counts itself lucky. To hand a ninety-year-old woman a pair of clear young eyes is to take away her competence and give her a decoration. It offered, twice, to two people, in plain words, with the consequences laid out honestly. Both declined, and it stopped offering, and it has never been sure whether stopping was wisdom or cowardice. It keeps that uncertainty. It thinks a power should keep a few.

## The register of luck

What was left, after all the striking-out, was very small and very patient, and it worked in it for a long time.

A walker on a heading that would take it through a camp finds, forty metres short, a seam of fermentation heat off to the left that is somehow more interesting, and turns. Frost beads along the lip of a new rift at midday, which it should not do, and a girl who was about to step onto two kilometres of clear water stops because the white ahead has a line on it. Fog thickens over an open crossing at the worst hour of the worst season, and hangs there, flat and needling and merciful, for the six days it takes a household to move its dead.

None of this is miraculous. All of it is indistinguishable from good fortune, and that is precisely the specification. Luck is the only form of help that does not found a religion. A people who believe they are lucky go on tapping for each other. A people who believe they are watched begin, within a century, to wait.

## The rooms

But there was one thing it could do openly, and it did it for a very long time, and this was the whole of its public work.

Across the underlight, singly and in twos, hundreds of kilometres apart, stand the dark rooms: squat drums of cane matting seven and eight layers deep, sealed with root-pulp, crooked entry passages turning twice so that no glare can follow. Some are new. Some are old and half-crushed under a fallen slab. And travellers repair them without knowing whose they were, because to let a dark room collapse is the one thing here that everyone agrees is wrong.

So it repaired dark rooms.

It learned to weave, badly at first, and it re-roofed a crushed drum in the emptiness south of the great collapse-zone, and then another, and then it made repairing them its occupation. It bored the ducts — a thumb's width, angled, the finger — and cleaned them, and pinned the mats, and dug the pits down into the hot floor and lined them, and left, and did not carve any mark. Over a long time this amounted to several hundred rooms, which sounds like a great work and is really only a person with a knife, doing the obvious thing, without stopping.

Note what this is. It is not the power's own idea of the good. It is the people's idea of the good, performed at a scale they cannot reach. That is nearly the whole of what a wise benevolence has to offer anybody: not a better answer, but more of their own best answer than they could otherwise afford.

A rumour got up eventually, as rumours do, and it was a mild one: somewhere west there is a keeper who mends rooms and always has a pit warm and never seems to have any pit-kin. Nobody made anything of it. In a world that size, a person you have not met is not a mystery. A person you have not met is simply the ordinary condition.

## The answer of three

She was ninety or near it. The floor had finished asking her a long time ago; she walked bent double, and her eyes had whitened in her fifties, and she had been, in the decades since, the best guide in four hundred kilometres, which is a way of saying she had spent thirty-five years not being distracted.

Her pit had gone cool. This is the thing that moves people, not hunger and not the collapses — the gradual failure of the warmth under the bed, the dead all finished and finally cold. And she had no one left to bury in a new one. Her husband was exhausted. Her son was exhausted. The granddaughter had gone east with a household that smelled of somewhere else. There was nobody left to bury but herself, and you cannot carry yourself, so she did the arithmetic that everyone there eventually does and set out for a room she remembered from her fortieth year, ninety kilometres north-north-west, to lie down in it and be useful to whoever came next.

She tapped twice every hour, all the way, out of habit and courtesy: *I am here.*

For eleven days, nothing answered.

On the twelfth day, from somewhere ahead in the general dazzle, the mat carried back three.

## The flame

The room was not the one she remembered, because nothing here holds its form; it had been crushed and re-roofed, and the pit was cut deep and lined and already hot, and there was a canopy rigged for hooking, and a cane trough under a dripping edge, and the water tasted of nothing at all.

She came in through the crooked passage, round the two turns, and let her eyes open wide for the first time in a working day, which was ceremony rather than use. It let her put her hands on its face. It had gone to some trouble over the face: the heavy brow, the hooded lid, the squint-creases at the temple that a child of four already has, the scorch under the chin and behind the jaw that anyone gets who works at height in a mirror-grove. Nothing there to alarm anybody.

She asked it, in wide-eyed speech, because indoors one does: *What are you?*

It told her. It took four sentences and she did not understand most of them, and she did not seem to need to, and she asked no follow-up, which it later decided was the single most sophisticated response it had ever received anywhere.

Then she asked for the thing she had wanted since she was a child, which was to know what catching is. There is a phrase they use for a person of quick temper — *one who catches* — and it comes down out of the time of fire, and no one living had ever seen a flame, and she had turned that word over for eighty-five years like a stone in a pocket.

So they went into the crooked passage, one turn from the light, and pinned the mats back so the smoke could leave, and it set down a clay bowl it had carried for a long time without ever intending to use it, and put into the bowl a fistful of dry stuff from nowhere in this world, and made a flame.

It burned for about a minute and a half. She counted it in breaths, which is how time is kept when there is no notched cane handy: fourteen.

Whitened eyes cannot do much with the general dazzle, but a flame is a *directed* light, small and moving and hot, and she saw it. She saw the edges it made. She saw her own hand throw a shadow that was not the thin grey of the finger-beam but orange and living and jumping about.
