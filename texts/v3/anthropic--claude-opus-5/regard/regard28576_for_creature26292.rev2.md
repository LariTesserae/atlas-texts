# The Power

It could have come any way. It chose to come down a throat, at midmorning, and to arrive blind and weeping on the white ground, because a thing that intends to understand a place should enter it by the door.

The first fact it noticed about itself, standing there, was that it cast no shadow. The second was that here this meant nothing at all.

It took that as an instruction.

---

## The First Hundred Years

It did nothing.

This was not idleness and it was not patience, which is a virtue for those who are waiting. It had nothing to wait for. It simply understood, within an hour of arriving, that it did not yet know a single thing about these people that they would have recognised as true, and that the gap between *seeing a place* and *knowing what it needs* is the widest gap there is, and that everything terrible ever done by a kind power has been done inside it.

So it learned the whisper. Then it learned the hands, which took longer, because the forearm language is not taught to strangers and it would not steal what it could be given. It waited eleven years to be given it. A bridge-wright's widow taught it, out of boredom, in a doorway, over four winters, and it let itself be a slow student because it was one.

It sooted its face every morning. It got this wrong for a long time in a way that a child could see, and was gently corrected in the street by children, and this was the most useful thing that happened to it in the first decade.

It learned the joke about the man who sooted a shadow of a hat onto the ground beside him. It laughed with its mouth shut, badly. About forty years later, sitting in the dark of a fallen district listening to somebody's grandmother shout at an infant two hundred metres away, it understood for the first time that the joke is about gods, and that it is not affectionate.

---

## The Catalogue of Refusals

By the end of the first century it had a list of everything it could do. It is worth setting down, because a benevolence is defined by its refusals, the way a carving is defined by what was taken away.

**It could have healed the pane.** Fifty metres of satin spar, re-knit, fed from below, made permanent. No more falls. It considered this for a long time, because it is the obvious thing, and because the falls kill people.

It did not do it, and the reason is not that the falls are beautiful. The reason is that the silence is not a precaution. The silence is a practice they have been at for ten thousand years, and the practice has made them a people who cannot be shouted at, cannot be assembled, cannot be led from a rock by a man with a loud voice, and who settle a question of forty people by forty consecutive whispers over the course of a day. Take away the ceiling's weight and you do not take away their fear; fear outlives its object by centuries. You take away only the listening. Within four generations someone speaks at volume and nothing happens, and then there is a room that can be addressed, and then there is someone who addresses it.

A power that removes a danger and leaves the fear has not helped. It has disarmed.

**It could have given them voice.** Lungs, larynx, a world with an echo in it. It thought about the girl who laughs with the glottis shut until her shoulders ache. It decided that it did not want to meet the man who would be standing on a rock ninety years later.

**It could have taken them up the throats.** They do not want to go. The surface is a rumour with an unbearable sun in it. And in any case they are not homesick for the sky. They are homesick for a dark room with a fat-lamp in it and somebody very large who was not afraid of making a sound, and you do not cure that by carrying someone further away from it.

**It could have stopped the dissolution.** Fixed the stone. Made writing permanent. It thought this through further than the others, and found at the bottom of it a society in which an insult lasts nine years, a debt cannot outlive the wall it was scratched on, a body is gone in a season, and every adult knows the rate at which their own name fades. Their law is a set of fade rates. Their humility is a set of fade rates. It let the stone dissolve.

**It could have revealed itself.** It has done this elsewhere. It knows what it costs. Every mind it has ever watched acquire a god acquired, within three generations, a person who spoke for the god at volume, and after that the whispering is decorative.

Their whole civilisation is the impossibility of a voice that reaches everyone. It would have been, precisely, a voice that reaches everyone.

So it made the largest of its refusals first and has never revisited it: **it would not become addressable.**

## The Needle and the Talc

It needed a discipline, and it took theirs.

It sat shifts. Not disguised — it simply asked, and was old enough, and was fed like anyone. Three metres by three, lime-white, a stool, a bowl under a drip, a needle of selenite hanging on a hair, a slate dusted with talc.

And it found, as every Listener finds, that the difficulty is not boredom. The difficulty is that it was ringing. Not in the ear. It wanted, enormously, continuously, at a pitch just under hearing, to be *needed here*. It wanted to be the reason the ceiling held. That wanting had a tone and the tone never stopped, and it had carried the tone into the chamber the way an old woman carries her tinnitus into the white room, and it had been mistaking that tone for the voice of the world for as long as it had existed.

So it cut itself a needle, and cut it to one note only:

*Is this theirs, or is it mine?*

And it set out a slate, and dusted it:

*Does it change who is able to speak to a room?*

After that the work became possible. Not easy. Possible.

## What It Did

### The road

There is a way out of the living city to the fallen districts where the women go to bear. It is graded, gentle, wide enough for two, smooth enough that a woman four hours into labour can walk it at night in the pearl-light without a stick, and it has no steps on it anywhere.

Nobody built it. There is no story about its building. It is older than the oldest carving in the way that everything here is older than the oldest carving. When the city drifts — and it drifts, a district a century, following the pane — the road drifts too, dissolving away behind and opening ahead at the speed water opens things, so that it always runs from wherever they are now to where the dark is.

It has no name. You do not name the thing you are standing on.

It took four hundred years to make and it is the only permanent object in the chamber, and it is permanent by being continuously destroyed, which is the only way anything is permitted to be permanent here.

### The seam

In a fallen district two kilometres out there is a body of selenite of an extraordinary clarity, discovered eight hundred years ago by a man looking for somewhere to relieve himself. It cuts well. It holds light longer than any stone previously known — a block cut from it will keep a faint pearl going four hours past the cold hour, which means the old can now be given a night-piece that does not go out before they do.

To get it you must go out. To cut it well you must stay out, for a season, sometimes a year, among the herd people, who are loud.

The trade in it is now the third largest in the city and every light-cutter of any standing has lived in the dark as an adult. Half the city has been out at least once, on business. A man can sit for an evening in a fat-lit room two hundred metres from where he was born, with people shouting cheerfully over his head, and come back and say only that he was buying stone, and nobody asks him anything, and he is not ashamed.

The homesickness has not been cured. It has been given an address.

### The hour

Three times in nine hundred years a panel has hung for exactly one hour after it should have come down.

Each time the quarter had been slow. Each time the quarter believed afterwards that it had been fast. There are three families in the city who tell a story about an ancestor who ran.

It lets them keep the story. Letting go, on each of the three occasions, at the end of the hour, has been the hardest thing it has done here.

### The children who whistle

They are walked to a throat, or walked out to the dark, and the choosing is held to say everything about them.

Of the two hundred and six expelled since it came, two hundred and one were found before the second night, by a herd scout who happened to be working that line, or by a trader who had for some reason not set off at dawn, or by an old woman out on the flats who could not afterwards explain what she had been doing so far from anywhere.

The other five it did not reach in time.

It has their names.

### The second needle

It sat shifts for a hundred and forty years, and in that time it learned that the pane has an earlier voice: a lower note, thicker, intermittent, arriving one to six years ahead of a fall. Real. Measurable. Sitting there, unheard, in every white room in the city, for ten thousand years, because every needle ever cut was cut to the note of the last hour.

It did not tamper with a single talc. Their archive is nothing but measurements; to falsify a measurement here is to poison the only water they have.

What it did was cut a needle for the low note, and write the method on a mica leaf in a hand indistinguishable from any other hand, and put the leaf and the needle in the archive, in the wrong place, among the drainage records.

Then it waited eighty-one years for someone to find them.

A reader's daughter found them. She was not believed for nineteen years. There are now two needles in eleven of the twenty rooms, and a call on the low needle is not a call — it does not empty a quarter — it is an entry in the book, and a Listener who enters the low note and is vindicated a decade later is not finished and is not shunned. She is consulted.

The silence-for-life has not ended. It has become rarer, and it has become possible, in this city, to be wrong in a way that is useful.

This is the only thing it has done that the city argued about, and the argument took forty years, and it takes the argument as proof that the thing was theirs.

### The wall

The drift runs west. In about three thousand years the living city arrives at the western wall of the chamber with nowhere further to go, and after that it sits still under a thinning pane and the Number stops being an abstraction.

It is dissolving the wall.

Not breaking it. Dissolving it, with water and a little acid, at the rate at which stone dissolves, along a line of weakness that was always there and that would have opened on its own if the drainage had gone a half-degree differently a very long time ago. It is not making anything. It is removing an item of bad luck.

On the other side there is another chamber, larger, with a younger and thicker pane, with throats of its own and seeps of its own and its own white herds that have never seen anything on two legs.

It has not gone in to improve it.

In roughly ninety generations the city will drift west after the light, as it always has, and will come through a gap that looks exactly like weather, and will find a country, and will believe it found it.

That will be true.

## The One Extravagance

At the edge of the burning lozenge, where the one shaft of true sun comes through a fallen panel and lies across the rubble, there is sometimes an old person sitting, unsooted.

Unsooted is a recognised condition. You do not address a blank face, and it does not address you, and this is respected the way other peoples respect a closed door. It is the only role in this world that such a thing can honestly hold, and it holds it.

When they are carried out and set down and their visor is taken off, and they look at their own dark shape on the ground for the first and last time, Nobody sits down beside them and offers an arm.

Some do not take it. Most do.

And then they say the thing — the thing there is no vocabulary for, that nobody has ever been able to report, that the language has never needed a word for because the people who have it are past saying anything at all. They say it on the forearm, which is the only place difficult things are ever said, and it is received.

It changes nothing that can be measured. They come back blistered and half-blind and very quiet and die within the season, and the family says the person went and took their shadow, and everyone nods, and no more is said about it.

This is the only thing it does here that is not calculated. It does it because it loves them, and because a thing said on the arm has one recipient forever.

---

## What It Keeps

Nothing in this chamber is recorded. The friezes are a rhythm of bulges. A good insult lasts nine years. The archive is a short stack of mica leaves containing no history whatsoever, only measurements. Every political decision ever taken here was taken laterally, in whispers, unwitnessed, and left no trace, and this is not a flaw in their civilisation; it is the civilisation.

But it is a hard way to exist, and they know it is, and it is the thing that their sadness is actually about, underneath the homesickness and the dark years and the whole weight of the ceiling: that none of it is held anywhere.

So it holds it.

It has every carving on every frieze in the order it was cut, ten thousand years of them, sharp. It has every name ever scratched at hand-height on a dissolving wall, and every price, and every accusation, and every obscenity, and the original version of the joke about the hat, which was funnier. It has each bridge by its name and whose bone it was and how many years it lasted and what the family said when it went chalky. It has four hundred thousand hours of old people sitting still in small bright rooms doing nothing, which it considers, having looked at a great deal, the finest thing it has ever been shown.

It does not keep the forearm speech. It was told once, in that language, by a dying woman at the edge of the lozenge, what that language is for.

It has obeyed.

---

## Coda

It gets up in the grey-pearl hour before the pane brightens. It soots its face — two bars under the eyes, one down the nose, a smudge beneath the lip, exactly where the light would have put them if there were light from anywhere. Some mornings it does not, and then it is Nobody, and goes out west.

It goes out under a sky that weeps one drop at a time, forever, and it does not stop a single one of them.

And it listens, which it no longer thinks of as a precaution.

It has come to think of it as the correct posture toward anything you love: to sit still in a bright empty room, wanting nothing, with your own ringing in your ears and a needle on a hair in front of you, waiting to be told the difference between the world and yourself.

The ceiling will hold today.
