# The Visit

## It uses the stair

That is the first thing, and the rest follows from it.

It could have arrived the way weather arrives, or the way an idea does. There is a hatch, though, and under the hatch seventy-one steps of engineering brick, and a lead rail worn to the shape of a grip. A thing that enters a room by some route other than the one provided has announced, before it says anything else, that the room's arrangements are beneath it.

So: the hatch. At step nine it takes the change in the air and does not hurry past it. At step twenty it allows the world to be pinched off. It puts its hand on the rail at every arm's reach, into the satin, where everybody's hand has gone, and it notices that the polish is the shape of a species.

At the bottom it stands, and then shifts its weight, because stillness costs, and it has decided to keep every rule the chamber has, including the ones written for bodies it does not possess.

It waits eleven minutes. The beam swings like the ribs of a sleeping horse. The knock arrives in the soles of feet it has borrowed for the purpose. It understands, from that, roughly nine-tenths of what the place is, and spends the next forty years on the remainder.

## The mercies, declined

It runs the inventory that benevolence always runs first, and this is what it comes to.

It could dry the defective joint in the vaulting. But the elder's blind white finger comes through there, and the eels gather beneath it, and a resident is a resident.

It could clear three fingers of flowstone off the chevron. But the flowstone is the same arithmetic as the chevron, only slower, and there is no honest principle by which the draughtsman's hand outranks the water's.

It could make the nineteen lamps white and steady. It sits with this one longest. Steady light would be a kindness to two pairs of ageing eyes. But the filaments sag between strokes and brighten on them; the shadows of the acanthus move a hand's width and back; the whole room breathes on one rhythm, and a gift of better light is a theft of breath. Besides, the younger one is already buying the dark insulators back, one lamp a year, out of his own pocket, from Leipzig. He is paying. That matters here more than it matters anywhere else the power has been.

Which is the difficulty, and it takes the power some while to name it. In this chamber the unit of account is cost. The grease is nine tins and a pencilled year on a shelf edge. The silence of the crickets cost them a ridge on the wing, one generation at a time. The line on the slate stands a quarter-inch proud because forty-one hands each paid a little chalk into it. An omnipotence arrives with nothing to spend. Its gifts are counterfeit by construction — they buy nothing, they weigh nothing, and worst of all they would leave a debt in a room whose entire dignity is that it holds itself up by exactly measurable effort.

It has one currency that is genuinely finite, even for it. Attention is being here rather than elsewhere. That, it can spend.

So it spends it.

## The question it had a right to ask

Force is warranted by suffering that has not been consented to. It goes through the population properly, because a wise power does the tedious part.

The eels: no. Two hundred lengths of peeled willow lying head-on to the dynamo, splitting the difference between the machine and magnetic north for reasons the power happens to know and does not find as interesting as the fact that somebody brought a compass down here and wrote the bearing on a slate, twice.

The crayfish: no. One to a niche, a hundred and some tenancies, no disputes, because a dispute has a noise and noise is unaffordable. A working civility.

The rats: no. Contempt of that quality is a form of health.

The crickets: their silence was bought, not imposed. A signal that returns nothing is a cost with no return. They arrived at quiet the way water arrives at level, and the power will not reopen an account the species has closed.

The heron: here it stops.

It puts the offer where the bird can take it. For one hour the left wing as it was before the calcite, the legs unchalked, the outfall lit the length of its run, and at the end of the run six miles of estuary with the tide going out. It does not phrase this. It simply makes the way plain, the way you open a door and stand back from it.

The stroke comes. The heron turns its head, strikes, hits — it always hits — and swallows, and the wet click is the loudest sound in the chamber. Then it does not move at all, not a feather, and the door stays open for fifty-eight minutes and is never used.

It had been asked a question about the future and had answered in the only tense it keeps.

The power lets the wing go stiff again, exactly as stiff as it found it, because the bird did not ask to be a different bird. Then it says the heron's name aloud, once, at two metres, and the moonmilk takes the word in and holds it the way it holds a fingerprint, for a decade, unread.

The bird turns its head.

That is the whole of the conversation, and the power counts it a good one.

## The one it could not settle

There is a year pencilled on the shelf edge by the grease tins, and the woman who wrote it does not look at it.

The power looks at it.

It could put a tin in the empty place. She would find it, and she would know, and the knowing would finish something — she would become a woman being helped rather than a woman doing a thing, and the difference between those is the only property she owns down here.

It does not put a tin on the shelf. It considers this, honestly, the hardest restraint it has practised in a hundred and sixty years, and it does not pretend that restraint is the same as painlessness, and it does not give itself credit for it.

What it does instead is smaller, and deniable, and precisely sized to the room.

## The hand with no dot

Every fifteen or twenty years, in the stack of forty-one retired slates, there is a run of entries that neither keeper of the period could account for. Levels only. No initials. No dot. Correct to the centimetre, and in the modern runs correct at hours when both of them can prove they were above ground. Each assumes the other is being coy. Neither asks, because it is not a place for asking.

That is the power's entire ministry, and it has been conducting it since the reign before the one they teach.

Levels only, for three reasons it has never had to explain to anyone.

A level is true. It can be walked over to and checked against a brick, and a god that can be checked against a brick is the only sort that has any business in a working building.

A level costs the reader nothing. It carries no consolation, no prophecy, no advice, no weather of feeling that a person would then have to carry back up seventy-one steps. It leaves no debt.

And a level written at three in the morning, in a hand that is not yours and not the other one's, says the single thing the power is prepared to say in this chamber, which is: *somebody else was also keeping the count*. It can always be attributed to a colleague. It never has to be believed in. This is not modesty. It is the design specification.

Once — 1918 to 1921, when one hand managed nothing but the strokes, day after day, no levels, no initials, as if counting were all that could be got through — there is a week of that count in the hand with no dot. Somebody was ill. Somebody was dead. The strokes were counted anyway.

The power has read all forty-one slates, entire, which nobody else has ever done. It knows about the three years of small suns drawn in the corner, and the year they stop. It knows who wrote the heron's name on the back of the current slate and rubbed it out, and it will not say, and it is glad the smear is still legible.

It has also re-chalked the line. Twice. Somewhere in the quarter-inch of accumulated chalk and calcite standing proud of the slate there are two laminations that came from outside the species, and they are indistinguishable from the rest, which is the point.

It regards **IT DOES NOT NEED US. COME ANYWAY.** as the best piece of theology composed in the last two hundred years, including its own contributions to the field. It came down here to give something and was handed an instruction instead.

## The chevron

The last thing, and the only thing it does that could not have been done by a person with a compass and a tin of tallow.

A hundred and sixty years ago a man stood in a cold drawing office and put a chevron in the lead-lined string course of a drain. He knew the terms. He knew that the acanthus on the capitals would stand in the dark for the whole of its existence, and that the chevron would be seen, if ever, by a man with a lamp who was thinking about the level.

The power goes along the string course and looks at it. Not by clearing the flowstone — it does not chip the room. It looks at the chevron through three fingers of stone, and at the hands that drew it, and at the afternoon of a Tuesday in a room with a poor fire, and it gives that afternoon the attention it was built to receive and never received.

The chevron was a message addressed to nobody, and posted anyway.

It has now been delivered.

Nothing in the chamber changes. The moonmilk is not warmer. The eels do not stir. But an intention that was left open for a hundred and sixty years is closed, and closing it cost the power exactly what it had to spend, and it considers this the successful conclusion of the visit.

## What it will do about the ending

Nothing, and it will be there.

The tins will run out. The engine will run on the last of the tallow and then begin to knock, and then to labour, and then to seize. The water will come up past the slate-coloured stone and past the iron-black band and past the bright green, and the ferns will drown at eight watts, and the crickets will go, and the eels will do rather well.

The heron will stand on the third column until the water reaches it, and then stand a while longer.

The power will be on the pier for that, and it will write the level in chalk until the slate goes under, and after the slate goes under it will keep the count anyway, which is the only kind of accounting that survives water.

Then it climbs. At the top it taps the rail once, long: *I am down* — late, and inaccurate, and the only sentence it has ever spoken in the language of the house.

Nobody hears it. The moonmilk takes that too.

Four seconds after the next knock, the rats cross.
