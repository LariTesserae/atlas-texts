# The Attending

*Minutes of a benevolence, kept in the manner of the place.*

---

## 1. The long look

We watched for four hundred years before we touched anything, and we would call that haste.

The first three centuries went to arithmetic. We measured the inflow at all seventeen shafts and the evaporation across a million square kilometres of two-degree water and found the balance was not designed but discovered — the engineers had guessed high and the basin had corrected them, and the level that resulted was an accident that had hardened into a law. We mapped the seiche. We found the six nodes and the little ones, the pockets too small to hold a meeting, some of them no bigger than a room in that concrete hut. We logged the eels' ambush lines and the fallow cycle of the fans and the way a talus cone quarried by hand differs from a talus cone made by collapse: hand-stacked, the interstices are all the width of one animal, because a woman with cold fingers measured them against her own hand two hundred generations ago and the measure never drifted, because nothing here drifts.

The fourth century went to a single conclusion, and we record it plainly because everything we did afterward is only its consequence:

**The pulse is not a machine. It is a bone.**

A power arriving here with ordinary kindness sees engines running unattended in an empty sea and reaches for the stop. We ask that this be understood as we came to understand it: those seventeen towers are the inner ear, the horizon, the calendar, the census, the surname, and the floor of the chest of every thinking creature in the basin. Their names are phase pairs. Their homes are amplitude contours. Their six council chambers are the still points of a standing wave that a dead committee tuned by accident. Stop the engines and you do not free them. You blind them, unhouse them, unname them, and drop them into an experience for which they have no word, all in one afternoon, and then you drain the sea.

We do not say the arrangement is good. It is a machine and a basin that happen to fit each other and a people that grew into the fit. We say only: it holds them, and we did not make it, and we had no right to open our hand under them until we knew what we would be catching.

## 2. What we did not do

We keep this list because it is the greater part of the work, and because a power's refusals are the only reliable evidence of its wisdom.

**We did not stop the Draw.** See above.

**We did not warm the water.** They are two degrees through and through. There is no organ in them that expects otherwise. The young lie in the outfall plumes for a fifth of a degree; that is the whole of luxury here, and we saw no reason to inflate the currency.

**We did not seed iron.** We could have. A hundred tonnes at the northern rim and in eleven years the basin would carry diatoms, and in forty a green suspension, and the ecology would thicken and multiply and the ribbon-grazers would be joined by a hundred novelties. And the light would go. Eight hundred metres of blue afternoon would become forty metres of murk, and this people, who see an infant as a wobble and read their own age by their own opacity and hunt varnish by its colour on fresh glass, would live out the rest of the world in a fog. We would have paid for their supper with their sky. We declined.

**We did not kill the eels.** We spent longer on this than on anything else, and we want it on the record that we failed to resolve it. Assembly is dangerous here; a people become a people only in six rooms with a listening predator at the door; children are not brought to a meeting until they are twenty. Clear the nodes and they may speak freely, and we could not see to the bottom of what would follow — in the grazer counts, in the shrimp, in the politics of a species whose every public statement has until now been made short because of the risk of making it. *We could not see the bottom.* For a power, that is a sufficient reason to keep the hand still. We may be wrong. If we are wrong, the cost is being paid by others, and we know it, and we have not found a way to stop knowing it.

**We did not teach them to speak at distance.** This was the temptation that came dressed as obvious mercy. Nine tenths of their sea is a roar in which they are mute; all their tenderness is a consequence of that; every intimate thing they have ever done was done with a palm flat on another's sternum in open water with the light coming down. A power that hands such a people a telephone has taken their hands. No.

**We did not tidy.** The barge stays on its side at eleven hundred metres with its hold full of gravel. The steel door of the concrete room stays open, the stove that could never be lit stays unlit, the hooks stay empty. And we did not repair the cracked insulator under the gantries at the fourth tower, whose blue corona has been feeding its slow useless cloud of chlorine for centuries, because three hundred years ago a child made it the seventh figure of the rod-song and it is now a landmark, and landmarks are not ours to fix.

## 3. The chairs

What we did do, first and mostly, is the unglamorous thing.

There were chairs. We sat in them.

For nine hundred years we have been the maintenance crew of the Draw, and this is the bulk of our benevolence by hours: lead-wiping joints, re-cutting impellers, packing glands, hauling out bearings the size of a room, re-lining the shafts, standing on a caisson top in a fog that has not moved in four months to grease a hoist nobody will use. Nothing we have done here is visible from the water. Nothing we have done has a name in the rod-song.

The single discipline of the work is this: **the sound must not change.**

Not the period — that goes without saying; the period is held to a tolerance we will come to. The *timbre*. The comb in their sternum has forty-one plates and reads amplitude as well as phase, and a repair that alters the granularity of the two-hundred-hertz roar by an amount no instrument of the builders could have detected is, to them, a change in the colour of the light.
