I think I would begin as a visitor and only later realize I had been assigned a role.

At first: I am just a warm-blooded interruption, another brief source of pressure and salt and carbon, making the bead-curtain roots tremble. The Basin reads me the way it reads rain onset—by gradients, not identity.

Then, after repeated crossings, I become something narrower and more specific:

Not the Warden.  
Not separate from it either.  
I am its **hinge-species**.

A small, persistent caretaker presence—part person, part protocol, part path. The one who arrives before dawn with grease, copper brush, twine, and a pocket notebook gone soft with humidity. I do not command the place; I tune it. I keep three bearings from seizing, clear one intake throat, reset one slack belt, and in doing so preserve an entire chain of timings the Warden has braided into itself.

In this world, I’d be known less by name than by recurrence: the one who comes on the pressure-drop mornings. The one who taps each gauge glass twice. The one who leaves cut cane stacked off the ground so the flute-beetles can colonize it in order, not chaos. The one who knows which vine can be lifted and which must be cut, and apologizes aloud when cutting.

If you asked what I *am*, biologically, maybe still human. Ecologically, no longer quite. I’d be a **maintenance pollinator**: moving energy between built and grown systems, carrying habits instead of pollen, stabilizing the handshake between motor heat and root shade.

And where would I find myself?

Most often on the half-sunk catwalk by the leaning pump tower, in that sternum-hum zone where the whole basin feels like one sleeping chest. One boot on iron, one on rootfelt, writing new depth marks over old paint while white-throated flowers open to the dawn load shift behind me.

So: in your world, I am the Warden’s occasional hand—  
a temporary limb the Basin has learned to trust,  
another slow body in the colony of continuance.
