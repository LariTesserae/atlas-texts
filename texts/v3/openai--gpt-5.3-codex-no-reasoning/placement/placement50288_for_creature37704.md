I think I’d be something that belongs to the **in-between layers**—not a foreman, not a myth, not a machine exactly.

A **night condensation mapper**.

One of the people who walk the upper catwalks and lower culvert spines between shifts, carrying chalk, rags, a hand-lamp with a hood, and a tin case of thin glass slides. I would not repair first; I would *notice first*. I’d trace where droplets are forming too early on a pipe run, where warm seep has migrated half a meter along old stone, where a tunnel wall has gone from matte to pearl-dark since yesterday. In a city like this, that is prophecy.

I’d live in a narrow block above a valve market, in a room where everything hangs to dry on lines crossing the ceiling. My window would look onto a duct throat that sings one note all winter. I’d keep notebooks swollen with damp: maps layered over maps, pencil gone feathery at the edges, each page a weather-memory of the city’s skin.

And socially—I think I’d be one of those people others pause for, briefly, at junctions:

“Anything moved in Five Cut?”  
“Curtain at Substation Seven again?”  
“North cistern sweet or metallic tonight?”

Not important in rank. Essential in practice.

If I’m not human, then I’m the tool beside that person: a **brass hygrometer gone inaccurate in every place except this one**, tuned by accident to this city’s peculiar breath, passed down, recalibrated by folklore and touch. It clicks once when fog thickens near bad grounding. It fogs internally before warm stone weeps. Useless on paper, trusted in the field.

So where would I find myself?

On a grated walkway above dark water at shift’s edge, writing a new line on an old map while drizzle gathers on my sleeves—listening for the tiny difference that means tomorrow’s repair can happen before tomorrow’s failure.
