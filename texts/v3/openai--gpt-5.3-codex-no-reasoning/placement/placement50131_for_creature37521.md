I find myself there as a **deviation detector**—not chief, not shaman, not hunter.  
A listener with a bad sleep and a perfect memory for wrong sounds.

At first I think I am one of the people: a lean custodian with dust in the creases of my hands, reed-fiber wrappings, copper beads for counts. I walk the lanes everyone walks. I keep the hours everyone keeps. I can tell fern-ring from bat-click in my dreams. I know which lichen band is “for now” and which is “for return.” I am trusted because I do not improvise.

But if I stay in the image longer, I become slightly stranger than that.

I am the one sent to sit by the west wall during pollination hour—not to gather, just to listen.  
I carry a pouch of tiny clay chips and move one from left hand to right every time the ceiling hum dips at the expected interval. If it dips early, I mark the stone. If late, I mark my own wrist with ash. I keep no written record, but my body becomes one: scar, stain, bead-knot, nail notch.

Children are warned not to interrupt me when I “count the room.”

I don’t command anyone. I report in verbs of recurrence:

- “The third vent returns shallow.”  
- “The skippers rose before the ferns rang.”  
- “North reeds are tapping double on the eighth cycle.”  

That is enough for others to adjust: move mats, delay scrape, reroute hunting, open a blocked slit with bone wedges before imbalance compounds.

So who am I?

Not a hero.  
Not an outsider.  
Not quite a priest.

I am a **human timing fork** in a chamber that must stay in tune.

And if you wanted a nonhuman answer—what I might be if I dissolved further into this place—I’d be one of the **ringing salt ferns** near a narrow vent: rooted, white, brittle-looking, alive entirely by minute currents; speaking only when touched by change; translating airflow into warning-tone.

Either way, same role:

I am where the pattern notices itself.
