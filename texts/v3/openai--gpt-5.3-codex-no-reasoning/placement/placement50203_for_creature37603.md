I arrive as something that can stay still longer than hunger should allow.

Not a person first—though I borrow their paths.
Not a herd beast—though I read their hooves like script.
Not a hawk—though I use their shadows.

I am low to the stone, pale where frost is pale, dark where wet rock darkens. I fit in cracks that look too thin to hold breath. Morning finds me rimed in white. Noon finds me gone, though I have not moved far.

If you stand in that basin long enough, you might call me a **line-walker**: one of the white-furred hunters you rarely see whole, only as consequence. Grazing stops before I appear. Burrow mouths go quiet. Wingbeats lift from one side of a meadow to the other as if pulled by a thread. I am read indirectly, like weather.

But I am not solitary in the way lowland stories mean solitary. I am threaded into the fog-keepers’ precision culture, whether they name me kin, rival, omen, or simply fact.

They know where I den: not by den itself, but by a repeated absence of pikas near a certain warm slab after third thaw.  
I know their schedules: which child checks snare lines by the seep, which elder limps and so takes the less exposed traverse, which poles near the spring are newly restrung and will rattle before wind reaches my whiskers.  
We map one another without owning one another.

In dawn fog, when the basin exhales and beads every awn and web, I hunt by sound and closeness. Vision is useless then; everything is pearl-gray and near. The insects haven’t risen yet. The burrowers are bold for a minute too long. My paws wet silently. My whiskers gather droplets and tell me where grass thickens, where stone opens, where a tunnel mouth breathes faint warmth.

By hard sun I withdraw into fracture shade beneath the young rock, where frost has made a room from breakage. There I sleep in pulses, listening to meltwater tick inside stone like a second heart. Afternoon is for movement along glare-cut edges—never skylined, always below crest, always where a five-degree turn hides me from both eagle and human eye.

I am shaped by the same rules:

- Moisture punctual: I drink from temporary seams others miss.
- Exposure absolute: one boulder’s shadow is life at noon.
- Nothing permanent: I keep multiple dens; one collapse is expected, not tragedy.
- Movement survival: I hunt vertical, dawn-down and dusk-up.
- Brightness hazard: my pupils and timing are my shelter.

If you ask who I am in spirit rather than species: I am the basin’s **interval** made flesh.  
The between-moment.  
The ten minutes before sun touches fog.  
The narrow margin where abundance appears and vanishes.

And if you ask where I would find myself in your world: not at the center of the hut firelight, not on the far empty ridges, but at the seam—where human intention, animal motion, and mountain microclimate overlap so tightly that missing a minute means missing a meal, a signal, a season.

There, at that seam, I belong.
