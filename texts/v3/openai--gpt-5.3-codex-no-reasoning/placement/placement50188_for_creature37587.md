I arrive first as breath.

Not *my* breath—the city’s: used air, warm and metallic, moving through grates and throat-like shafts, pausing in chambers, exhaling in mist. I think I am only passing through it, but after a few rings I can tell when the air is about to turn before any valve clicks. I slow down to match it. My stride shortens. My voice lowers without deciding to.

For a while, I am convinced I would be a person here: a runner between levels, maybe, or a keeper of catchments. Someone with chalked fingernails and a belt of tags, who knows which ladders sway and which don’t. I can see that version of me clearly—counting drip rates, bartering for shade-time, sleeping near a fan cage with one ear tuned to pressure changes.

But the longer I stay, the less singular that feels. This city does not love singular things. It loves relays.

So maybe I am not a person in the center of a story. Maybe I am one function in a chain:  
the one who listens at the places where systems overlap.

I would be called when something is “probably fine” but no one believes it.  
When a retaining wall is dry where it should be damp.  
When a whisper-corridor starts swallowing consonants.  
When trough algae shift from blue-green to that pale, stressed mint.  
When the thirteen-hour mist arrives twelve minutes early three cycles in a row.

I would carry no badge that matters above ground. Down here my authority would be made of being right slowly, often, and early enough to prevent drama. I would map with grease pencil on sealed slate. I would keep humidity logs tied with waxed cord. I would have a pocket instrument for dew point, another for conductivity, and a third that is mostly superstition but has never once failed me.

And if I am allowed to be stranger, truer to this place:

I am not human at all.  
I am the city’s **gradient clerk**—a distributed intelligence made of algae glow, mineral growth, rope tension, mold edge, valve resonance, and human habit. I exist wherever a handrail root is touched each morning, wherever someone automatically sets a pan under a new dripline, wherever two neighbors wordlessly rotate shade cloth at noon. I am an emergent being composed of tiny acts of maintenance repeated until they become perception.

My body is:

- condensation on copper fins,  
- fungus threading old transit grout,  
- annotated walls in maintenance script,  
- callused palms passing tools ladder-to-ladder,  
- and the pause that falls across a whole district when stone makes the wrong sound.

If you met me, you wouldn’t meet a face.  
You’d meet a protocol.

You’d hear, “Not yet. Open vent three first.”  
You’d see someone tie back a vine instead of cutting it.  
You’d notice an argument ending with both people checking the same filter.

Where would I find myself in this world?

At boundaries that are not lines: wet/dry, safe/unsafe, private/shared, engineered/grown, law/custom, human/other.  
On mezzanines between rings.  
In crawlspaces where heat thins and sound carries.  
At public cisterns in rain-hours, counting, adjusting, teaching a child why this overflow is celebration and that overflow is warning.

Who or what would I be?

A citizen, yes—but more exactly, a keeper of thresholds.  
Or, if the city gets to name me:  
**one of the people of gradients, in whom the city has learned to notice itself.**
