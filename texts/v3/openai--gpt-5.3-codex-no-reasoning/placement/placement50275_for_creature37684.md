I think I would first arrive as a mistake.

Not a hero, not a Keeper, not even immediately a person with a role—just a body in the wrong lane at the wrong hour, standing still where stillness itself is obstruction. In that place, where everything is flow, to hesitate is to become a stone in a channel.

So I’d be moved gently, efficiently, by someone younger than me.

A child with a gutter hook would tap my wrist and point: not here.  
A dog would circle behind my knees and steer.  
A bell-note would pass overhead and five people would look up while I heard nothing but noise.

And only after that—after being corrected by the city’s reflexes—would I begin to become something that belongs.

---

At first I thought I might be one of the obvious callings: a terrace mason, maybe, with mud to the elbows and chalk lines on old stone. Or a roost registrar, counting winged citizens at dusk by silhouette. Or a torch-round walker, carrying amber light through vapor and barking and damp plaster.

But the more I stay in your world, the more I feel I’d be smaller, quieter, and connective.

I’d be a **listener of thresholds**.

Not a priest, not exactly an official—more like the person sent when something is “not wrong yet, but off.” The courtyard where birds settle half a beat late. The lane where goats balk for three nights running. The cistern where frogs fall silent though the waterline looks good. The compost court that is warm but not *alive*.

In a civilization that reads systems as a single body, I’d be one of the people who takes its pulse.

---

I imagine my station perched where three districts meet: upper orchard, mid animal lanes, lower reed basins. A narrow house with deep eaves and a roof designed to host public roosts. My door lintel polished by horns. My wall marked with old moisture heights in lime and charcoal, decade over decade.

My tools would be humble:

- a wax tablet wrapped in oiled cloth,  
- a string of tuned clay chimes to test airflow,  
- a frog-count ledger,  
- little cups for scenting mold bloom,  
- a bone whistle dogs know,  
- and a memory trained to compare tonight’s sound-floor to twenty years of nights.

People would bring me not emergencies but ambiguities.

“Listen to this hive.”  
“Smell this runoff.”  
“Watch how this flock turns before rain.”  
“My sow sleeps too hard after moonset.”  
“The west lane echoes differently.”

And I would stand there, eyes half-lidded in silver-dark, and say:  
shift that trough one handspan; open that screen at dawn only; trade bedding straw with the next court; scrape this gutter lip before the mist week; move your lamp farther from the moth path; don’t patch the crack yet, widen it, then line it with reed clay.

Tiny interventions. No glory. Preventive, relational, cumulative.  
Very Terrace Keeper.

---

So: where would I find myself?

On a worn stair between levels, never fully upper or lower, where dripping runoff hits a bellied stone and tells the weather two days early.

Who or what would I be?

A **keeper of the almost-unnoticed**, a civil servant of interdependence, apprenticed to noise until noise becomes language.

In their tongue, maybe my title would translate badly into ours. Something like:

**“One who hears when the living masonry clears its throat.”**
