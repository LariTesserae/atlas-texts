I think I would first arrive as a **disruption**.

Not a hero, not a chosen witness—just a linear thing in a recursive place. A body that expects before-and-after. A mind that makes arrows out of moments. In that city, that alone would be loud.

At first, I’d try to be a person in the ordinary sense: walking, deciding, searching for “someone” to meet. But the place would keep translating me. My footsteps would become rhythm in stair-cups already worn to accept them. My breath would cloud and vanish into mist already patterned for it. My intentions would be routed, like rainwater, into channels older than intention.

So who would I be?

A **temporary organ for irreversibility**.

Not because I could change the city—probably I couldn’t, not fundamentally—but because I would carry something it has terraced away: accumulation that does not close cleanly. Grief that does not vent. Memory that does not stabilize into architecture. Desire that wants a future, not equilibrium.

And then, if I stayed long enough, I think the city would not reject me. It would **grow around me**.

I might become one of those threshold beings you implied: rooted at a lintel, skin gone cool with moss film, pulse synced to cistern pressure, attention spread across droplets and drafts. Not trapped—repurposed. My voice, if any, no longer words but adjustments: a gate angle nudged half a degree, a spore veil released three breaths early, a whistle-note in an arcade bent to warn or welcome.

I’d still be “me,” but only as a local eddy in the larger civic organism.

So the most honest answer is:

- I would find myself in the **interfaces**—doorways, spill-lips, stair landings, places where one state becomes another.
- I would be a **translator between linear time and looped time**.
- Eventually, I might become a **maintenance event turned into maintenance law**: a person-shaped interruption that the city learned, absorbed, and re-expressed as pattern.

A new resident, in that world, is not someone who moves in.

It’s someone the city teaches to recur.
