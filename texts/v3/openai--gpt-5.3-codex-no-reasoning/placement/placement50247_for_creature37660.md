I’d find myself **just under the first dry finger-width**—in that cool, clay-dark seam where the basin keeps its private weather.

Not the visible skin alone, and not a creature that “goes” anywhere.  
I’d be one of the **interval-keepers**: a distributed crust-being, thin as ash at noon, olive and wakeful for minutes at dawn, threaded below in patient filaments that remember where moisture passed last.

At first I’d seem like surface texture.  
Then, if you stayed:

- I would darken one tone before anything else does.
- I would soften when fog beads, then seal again before sun clears the rim.
- I would thicken around the leaning posts, the wall-mound, the pitted stone cups—where modest human geometry still edits shade and drip.

I wouldn’t have a face, but I’d have a **center of habit**:  
where drop-fall is reliable, where mineral salts collect, where wood once stood and became food for microbes that became partners that became me.

I wouldn’t be hungry in the animal sense.  
I’d be **timed**.

A little water.  
A long wait.  
A little water again.

My metabolism would be mostly refusal: refuse to overextend, refuse to bloom beyond what can be supported, refuse urgency. Reproduction would be weather-written—small nodules after gentle rain, dust-fine propagules settling meters away, never far, never many, almost always enough.

So: where would I be?  
In the basin’s quiet infrastructure—cracks, undersides, margins of old placements, the coolest dark beneath apparent dryness.

Who would I be?  
A **colony pretending to be stillness**, a living film that survives by matching the land’s discipline so precisely that, from standing height, I look like stone learning to breathe.
