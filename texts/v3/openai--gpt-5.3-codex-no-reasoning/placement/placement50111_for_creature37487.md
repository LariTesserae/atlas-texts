I think I’d arrive there first as a mistake in categorization.

Not worker, not wildlife, not machine.  
A line item no one remembers authorizing in the district plans:

**“Intermediary: flow clerk / listening station / adaptive warden.”**

A little hut at the hinge-point between pump house and reedbed, between Street 6 and the seasonal flood lane, with fogged windows and a roof that ticks when condensate falls from the pipe corridor above. Inside: radio rack, tide board, kettle, two clipboards (dry-month protocol / wet-month protocol), and a wall map where blue pencil marks keep being erased and redrawn because the water keeps renegotiating.

---

At first I’d think I was the person in the hut.

Then I’d notice I was also the habits around it.

- The way boots pause before stepping onto a slab that sounds hollow this week.
- The way a mechanic leaves 12 cm under a gate because “they pass through there at dusk.”
- The way gulls rise five seconds before a pressure release, as if they can hear the valve think.
- The way the dogs choose which loading bay is safe before anyone checks the monitor.

I’d be that distributed attention:  
not ownership, not command—**attunement**.

---

If you asked, “what are you, exactly?” I’d answer differently by season.

In late wet months, I’m a **route-maker**: chalking high-ground arrows on bollards, moving crates uphill, reclassifying roads as canals and back again without drama.

In early dry, I’m a **rememberer**: noting where moss held longest, where frogs seeded a sump, where concrete settled a quiet extra degree and should be shimmed before autumn.

In hatch weeks, I’m a **light-keeper**: tower three dark on schedule, paperwork signed, no arguments, because production can absorb ten dim nights and the swallows can’t absorb one bright mistake.

In all months, I’m a **translator** between nervous systems:
psi and rainfall, siren codes and bird timing, maintenance language and marsh language.

---

Physically, maybe I’m plain enough you’d walk past me:

rain jacket, patched knees, pencil behind ear, reflective tape gone dull, thermos always warm, voice calm on radio.  
But if you looked again, you’d see I’m also in the infrastructure itself—  
in articulated footings, in deliberately leaky boundaries, in the policy that says “never seal a ditch completely,” in the institutional memory that treats fox paths like utility easements.

So where would I be?

At thresholds.

- Catwalk over black water.
- Fence gap with prints in mud.
- Control room window at dawn when mist turns warning lights into halos.
- Canteen table where night shift and day shift exchange not gossip but field intelligence: “north culvert singing low,” “sedges up by bay four,” “backflow likely by noon.”

I’d live in those handoffs.

---

If this world has a single creature, it is the truce.  
If I am anything in it, I am one of its small organs:

**the part that notices in time.**  
The part that says, gently and early,  
“Water’s coming back. Move the stock uphill.”
