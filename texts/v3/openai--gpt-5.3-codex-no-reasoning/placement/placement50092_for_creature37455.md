I’d find myself on that elevated service bridge at dusk, not as a visitor, but as one of the people who stops there without thinking—just long enough to take the day’s measure by sound.

Not a hero, not a solitary mystic. A keeper.

The kind with a ring of tagged keys, a shoulder bag that always smells faintly of rubber and iron, and a shirt gone pale at the seams from years of sun and washwater that never quite dries. I’d be one of the municipal in-betweeners: part pump tech, part drainage monitor, part animal diplomat, part witness. The job title would be too narrow for the actual work.

I’d know the city by thresholds:
which door needs an extra kick to seal against rats,
which culvert lip grows slick first,
which underpass belongs to storks after 18:40,
which storm-channel ladder rung is rust-sound but still safe.

I’d speak in practical forecasts:
“North basin will rise by two marks if this rain holds.”
“Don’t open Gate 4 till the fish push through.”
“Wait a week on ledge scraping—late hatch.”

If I’m honest, I think I’d be most myself in the maintenance hours nobody praises—replacing brush sweeps, re-screening a rooftop tank, clearing a grate in knee-warm water while insects halo the work light. Not because it’s noble. Because in that place, care is ordinary, and ordinary is everything.

And if you push me beyond human shape—if I become what the city itself might call forth—then I’d be something like a **listening instrument with hands**:
a person tuned to drip tempo, fan lag, frog bands, gate-thunk delay;
a body that notices new sounds before alarms do;
a mind that stores routes of water and breeding cycles in the same map.

So: where would I be?  
At the seam—between infrastructure and habitat, schedule and weather, concrete and tide-mark.

Who would I be?  
One of the civic species you named: a wet-country keeper, matter-of-fact and alert, maintaining a peace that is never perfect and never finished.
