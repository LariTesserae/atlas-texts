I arrive thinking I’ll be a visitor, and the place refuses that role for me almost immediately.

At first I’m only a pair of eyes under a hood, pausing at the bottom of the ramp, trying to sort scale from echo. I look for a single figure—the steward, the keeper, the one who “belongs”—and what I find instead is timing. A cart passes before I step. A frog shifts before I set my boot. A pump cycle rises, and without deciding to, I wait for the lower throb to cross before moving. I start copying the basin before I understand it.

So: who am I here?

Not the overseer. Not the hero. Not even really a person in the usual story sense.

I am a **reader of thresholds**.

One hand on rail. Lamp hooded low. I move where red hasn’t been reached, where algae darkens but doesn’t sheen, where cable tags are fresh, where cats choose to sit. I carry chalk in a pocket and grease pencil in my sleeve. I don’t name places after what they were; I name them after what they do now: breathing culvert, warm ledge, false floor, swallow turn, bitter vent.

Given more time, I become one of the Basin’s smaller organs:

- I tap housings and learn which vibration means bearing wear.
- I watch frog spawn fail in one sump and know a solvent leak happened two sectors over.
- I memorize dry routes that only exist for three weeks each season.
- I add one stripe of paint above the old stripe, and that is both warning and history.

If you need one image: I’m the figure on a narrow bridge in amber half-light, rain ticking on my shoulders, giving two clicks of lamp in greeting—less a person than a **repeatable act of attention**.

And if you push deeper—what would I be, beyond the human body—

I’d be a **tagged handrail**: touched by everyone, trusted because it keeps being maintained, rusting and repainted, never central, always necessary.

In a world defined by relentless becoming, I would be one of the things that helps change become survivable. Not master of the basin. Not separate from it.

A nerve in its under-heart.
