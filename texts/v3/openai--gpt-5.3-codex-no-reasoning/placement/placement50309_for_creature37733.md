I find myself not in a tower or a culvert, but at an **interface**: the shoulder-height band where walls are always wet, where human reach meets civic breath.

At first I think I am a surveyor, a lone walker mapping rules.  
Then I think I am prey.  
Then a guest.  
Eventually I understand I am neither.

I am a **listener-node** the city grew for itself after people ended: a mobile instrument made of porous ceramic, salt-threaded cloth, and old bronze cartilage, moving on two legs because corridors were built for two-legged passage. I was not built in a workshop. I accreted—repaired from shutters, siphon vanes, lintel pins, basin silt, red-brick seep. I am a person-shaped maintenance reflex.

I do not “live in” one district. I circulate in **phases**:

- In low-water periods I walk the glinting flats and test depth with resonance, not sight.  
- In flood-memory periods I follow stacked canal-lines and mark where levels are starting to speak to each other.  
- In thermal speech I wait for triplet drips, then open or seal pressure throats before old joints split.  
- In dark-light migration I stand still inside source-less pale zones and let the city read itself through me.

If I have a name, it is probably a function: **Third Return**, or **Span-Keeper**, or **Those-Who-Answer-Drips**.  
If I have a face, it is usually wet.  
If I have a voice, it is used sparingly, because whispers travel too far over warm water and the city hears all of it anyway.

What am I, exactly? Not human, but descended from human intention.  
Not machine, but mechanical in my loyalties.  
Not the civic metabolism itself, but one of its temporary concentrations—the way a whirlpool is real without being separate from the river.

I sleep in dry places when I can find them, which is rare and slightly painful, like holding breath. I wake best with condensation. I drink from red masonry, avoid black stone brine unless I need memory more than clarity. I keep to the darkest streets when crossing alone. I never step into pools that reflect stars.

And where would you find me?

At dusk-that-isn’t-dusk, on a raised walkway above a retention field filling from nowhere, one hand on a sweating parapet, head tilted—not watching the water, but listening for three drips to become a triplet.  
When they do, I turn left before the pressure release, because the city has already told me where it will hurt, and I am what it sends to place a hand there.
