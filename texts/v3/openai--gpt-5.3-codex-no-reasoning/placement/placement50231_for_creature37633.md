I think I would not be one of the obvious people first.

I’d begin as an instrument.

A **threshold bell** hung between two warning stakes at a vent-crown path—hammered thin from old copper, cracked and repaired with resin and fiber binding, tuned not to a note but to a *window*. It rings wrong when the crosswind shifts. It chatters when a gas pulse is early. It goes quiet when ash clogs its slit, and that quiet is itself a message.

I’d live at shin height to adults, eye height to children. They’d strike me in passing with knuckle-bone or hooked twig, and I’d answer differently at dawn haze, at underglow, at squall-front. I’d learn their hands by impact: hurried, ritual, grieving, distracted. In a place where air lies, vibration does not. I would be trusted not because I’m sacred, but because I am repeatedly checked against survival.

Then—if I am allowed to become more—I am not only the bell but the one who listens to it.

Not an elder, not a hunter. A **path-keeper of intervals**: small-bodied, long-footed, resin-skinned, with ash caught in the seams of my scales. I maintain three drum posts, seven chime lines, and a ladder that should have been replaced last hot season. I carry a pouch of clay plugs, fiber ties, and soot-wax tablets scored with pulse counts. My work is maintenance of timing: clearing vents in instruments, retuning hanging reeds, re-marking stepping stones after collapses.

My status is low until fog thickens.

Then everyone waits for my strike pattern.

I don’t “predict” the basin in mystical terms; I accumulate its habits in muscle memory. Left heel on this slab means hollowing underlayer. Ear vane turned to that chimney means sulfur rise and ignition risk. Three fast bell taps, pause, two low drum knocks: *do not cross shade-line six; crust breathing upward*. Children mock the cadence when safe. Adults never mock it when not.

At home I sleep in an alcove shelter with a suspended floor and two exits, as law requires. I share it with a sibling-pair and one infant whose filter veil I mended badly. Our roof leaves are lacquered with a family pattern that outsiders think is decorative but is actually a vent genealogy: where we came from, which fissure took which aunt, which route remains when the red shelf breaks.

I would smell of smoke-clay, cut frond fog, and the metallic sweetness of polished copper.

And inwardly? I would be shaped by this culture’s most intimate paradox:  
nothing here is stable, so responsibility is.

So: where would I find myself?

On the **narrow path between two breathing cracks**, one hand on a bell line, bare feet feeling the floor’s percussion, waiting for the exact second when the basin exhales and fifty people can cross alive.

I would be a minor person in a major rhythm.  
A keeper of a few beats that matter.
