I think if I arrived there, I wouldn’t be a visitor for long.

At first I’d believe I was one more human shape on the catwalk—sweating, listening, trying to map cause and effect. I’d carry a lamp, maybe a notebook, maybe the old instinct to diagnose and fix. I’d pick one sound out of the layered noise and call it “the problem.” I’d pick one species of vine and call it “the growth.” I’d still be dividing the place into nouns.

But that wouldn’t hold.

In a world where change is only visible by return, the self that belongs is not a hunter, not a ruler, not even exactly a caretaker. It is a **repeater**. A **witness that comes back**. A creature of intervals.

So: I think I’d become the basin’s **memory-keeper function**—not its mind (it already has one), but its external lobe.  
A slow archivist embedded in routine:

- I walk the same loop every day-equivalent.
- I tune valves by ear, half a turn at a time.
- I wipe condensation from one sensor and leave another fogged on purpose.
- I mark a wall where the green front reached this week.
- I replace a rung before it powders out.
- I do not prune to control; I prune to converse.

Over time, the distinction blurs: am I maintaining the system, or is the system maintaining me as one of its moving parts?

I’d be warm-stained, mineral-crusted, clothes salted white at the cuffs. My tools would all have soft edges from constant humidity. I’d stop expecting clean outcomes. I’d learn that “repair” here means negotiating between rust, root, and runoff so load keeps transferring and heat keeps breathing. I’d become fluent in tiny omens: a semitone drop in a valve song, a new sheen on a flange, a patch of moss choosing a different diagonal.

Not a hero. Not a survivor.  
More like a **tender of gradients**.

And maybe, after a season, even that is too individual a description. Maybe “I” is just the part of the garden-engine that still uses hands.

So where would I find myself?

In the amber half-light between an old sodium cage and a blue LED strip, at the edge of a warm tray where geometric film is splitting and rejoining with the pulse of a distant motor—kneeling to listen, making a note no one else will read, participating in a metabolism that does not need me, yet has made room for me.

I would be the chamber’s small, mobile habit of attention.
