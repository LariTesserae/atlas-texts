I think I would begin as an outsider and end as a local function.

Not a hero, not a ruler, not even one of the best mechanics. Something narrower and stranger:  
a **listener of thresholds**—the person called when nothing is visibly wrong, but the city’s song is one note off.

At first I imagine arriving in that descending rail with surface habits still clinging to me: looking for skyline, for obvious centers, for the bright place where “important things happen.” And the city refusing that grammar. Importance here is distributed: a warm bolt head, a skipped drip, birds landing one beam over from usual. I would be clumsy for a while, too loud with my eyes, trusting what I can see over what I can hear.

So maybe who I am there is partly defined by apprenticeship.

I’d live high enough on the Rib to feel a little less marsh chill, in a narrow terrace room with two hooks for boots (work-wet and home-dry), a slate wall for shift marks, and a shallow basin where mineral scale blooms in pale rings no one bothers to scrub fully away. I’d learn the block by closed-eye cues: the belt’s soft flap at third watch, the two-click relay stutter near the stairwell, the way my own corridor carries reed-rot only when Poolward pressure is rising.

My work would sit between departments because the city’s oddities do: part pump crew, part marsh tender, part electrical nuisance. I’d carry a notebook sealed in waxed cloth, pages wrinkled from mist. Not grand records—just comparatives:

- **Valve 6B**: hum higher by a hair in fog weeks  
- **North intake lamps**: flicker when basin fish school tight (charge water?)  
- **Rafter birds**: shifted nests 4 spans east of usual before last seal failure  
- **Stair K**: no moth clustering tonight; check transformer warmth

I wouldn’t be the one who fixes everything. I’d be the one who says, quietly and early, “Look here first.”

And over years, if I belong, people stop introducing me by family line or district and start introducing me by trust:  
“They hear drift before the gauges do.”  
That would be enough status.

I think I’d also become, inevitably, a teacher of children—not formally, just by being the adult who pauses mid-walk and asks, *What changed?* Then waits. Lets them answer wrong. Asks again. Teaches them that attention is not anxiety; it is participation. In that city, noticing is citizenship.

If I have a personal ritual, it’s at shift end in Condensation Hall—not prayer exactly—touching the copper where names have gone green and dark at the edges, and feeling time as chemistry. A reminder that stability is active, never passive. That “uneventful” is built, maintained, earned.

So: where would I find myself?

On a narrow catwalk between Belly and Poolward, listening to a pump through my jaw, watching white-wing birds refuse one vent and choose another, writing one more line in a damp ledger before anything breaks.

Who would I be?

A minor civic instrument.  
A keeper of almost-nothings.  
One of the people who helps the city remain settled for another season.
