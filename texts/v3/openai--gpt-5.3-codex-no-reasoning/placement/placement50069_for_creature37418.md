I think I would not be the obvious plant at first.

At first I’d be a **pause** in the basin—the shallow lee behind that sitting-height slab, where wind forgets itself. A place where dust settles one grain thicker than elsewhere. A spot nothing claims quickly, because nothing claims quickly here.

Then, over long intervals, I’d become something that can use that pause.

Not a full cushion colony yet. More like a **mineral seam-sleeper**: a low, cracked pad no wider than two hands, fused to the crust at three points, with most of me hidden below in threadlike anchor roots and microbial lace. Aboveground I’d look dead to a hurried eye: ash-gray scales, thorn nubs, salt sparkle. But in the dimmest usable light, a few translucent cells under the crust would wake and work—quietly, briefly—then seal again.

My life would be made of tiny verbs:

- condense  
- hold  
- fold  
- wait  
- repair  
- wait again

I wouldn’t drink water; I’d borrow it from stone skin and air films.  
I wouldn’t grow upward; I’d thicken sideways by fractions, adding a rind so thin a decade might not notice.  
I wouldn’t flower; I’d fracture, eventually, into hard flecks that thermal ticking pries loose and nudges into neighboring cracks.

If you touched me, I might feel like brittle saltwood.  
If you watched me for a day, you’d think nothing happened.  
If you watched me for forty years, you might see I had moved the border of my body the width of a fingernail.

And if I were “someone” rather than “something,” that someone would be a **keeper of almosts**—almost dormant, almost stone, almost absent, yet persistently alive. A being that survives by agreeing with the basin’s terms: no spectacle, no urgency, no waste. Just continuity in low light.

So where would I find myself?

In that corner with convenient stones, in the warm dark lee, at the seam where flakeable rock meets dust.  
And what would I be?

A small, stubborn colony learning how to disappear without ending.
