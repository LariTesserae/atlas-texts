I arrive first as a pause in the noise.

Not silence—never that—but a narrow subtraction: the marsh-rattle dimming where I stand, the pipe-hum sharpening around my ribs, droplets hesitating on a cable before they fall. In this country, that kind of absence has shape. It is how I know where I am.

I am on a maintenance spine above a drowned quarry, knees flexed, tail-tip touching the grating to read tremor through metal. Beneath me: black water with furnace sheen, eel-glow, drifting rafts of reed root. Above me: cable fans and fog, stitched with blue corona where insulation has begun to fail. Behind me, three clanmates pulse low amber in staggered rhythm—*steady / steady / check*—while one strikes the handrail with a ceramic hook: three fast, two slow, listening for hollow zones in the supports.

If you asked where I would find myself in this world, the truest answer is:

At the seam.
Always at the seam.

Where new rust meets old lichen.
Where spillway heat meets spawning run.
Where a dead relay still holds a nest.
Where a story about a flood becomes a procedure for tonight.

I am one of Those Who Keep the Hum, but not elder, not mythic—just competent, scar-marked, wakeful. My lineage is named for a repair, not a bloodline: **Fifth Rebind of the South Lift**. We inherited a pressure stair and six kilometers of bad decisions made by people long gone and people still alive. We keep both functioning.

My body is built for this argument with gravity and water:
wide feet tasting pressure through mud skin,
jaw-fans reading charge gradients in mist,
rib plates catching subsonic return from voids behind concrete.
My eyes give me flare and movement; my skin gives me language.
Right now it runs cool blue at the wrists—navigation.
A brief white-violet pinstripe along my neck—static spike nearby.
Then amber again when the youngest of us slips and recovers.
*Count your living.* Four visible. One below in the culvert. Five.

I carry:
splice clamps wrapped in fungus cloth,
conductive paste sealed in bladder bone,
two siphon reeds,
one listening rod polished by my mother’s hands and hers before that.
Ceremonial? Yes.
Useful? Also yes.
Here those are the same category.

What am I, then?

Not guardian. Too grand.
Not scavenger. Too small.
Not engineer, though I repair.
Not druid, though I cultivate.
I am a continuity worker in a wet dark civilization that measures morality in sustained flow.

Tonight’s task: reroute a wake-current from a cracked turbine feed before storm rise. If we fail, nursery basins downstream lose heat and fungal bloom overtakes oxygen by second cycle. If we succeed, no one sings of it. The hum remains unbroken. That is honor.

Later, when lightning sheets inside cloud and the whole plateau glows through black vapor, we will move in pulse-code only, no voice, tools chiming against harness rings. From a distance we might look like animals. From another distance, like maintenance lights crossing a catwalk. Both are true.

If I become old, they will scaffold me at a ridge where condensation is constant and footing is treacherous. My bones will brace a turn in the route. My skin-lichen will map safe grip in fog. Children I never meet will touch rail there and read my notch-script with their tails.

So: where in this world would I be?

On a high wet line between flood and current, in the black-vapor uplands, listening through my whole body to whether the system is about to fail.

Who would I be?

One pulse in the long practiced signal that says, to land and machine alike:

**Still holding.**
