I think I would first arrive as a visitor and then fail, slowly, to remain one.

At the beginning I’d tell myself I was a surveyor, maybe: someone trying to map the basin’s repeating seams and prove to myself it was static. I’d walk the white causeways at noon with wet cloth around my neck, counting cup-chains, marking cracks, testing whether birdcalls truly recur. I’d keep notes so meticulous they’d become devotional without my noticing.  
Day 1: tile split at east stair, angle 32°.  
Day 9: same split, same angle, same trapped reed fiber.  
Day 30: same.

Eventually I’d realize that in this place observation is participation. The moment you notice a failing edge, you are already responsible for it.

So: who would I be? Not a master-builder, not one of the lineage-keepers with perfect wrists and inherited measures. I’d become something smaller and more specific—**a seam listener**.

A keeper of thresholds no one boasts about: joints between plaster and reed, between channel-lip and cup, between planted square and glare-court. I’d carry a narrow kit: spatula bone, cord gauge, pouch of lime paste, spool of resin thread, a small shell ladle for adding exactly three drops where capillary pull is stuttering. I’d work in heat-hum, crouched for long spans, cheek near the ground, listening for the tiny ceramic tick that means water is still choosing continuity.

In time I’d likely be altered by the work. Skin bleached and browned in layers; knees permanently dusted white; forearms glossy with algae smears; hearing tuned to micro-sounds—drip pitch, frog pulse, the click of hoof on a path that’s gone slightly soft. I would stop thinking of “land” as surface and think of it as agreement.

And maybe, after long enough, I’d be counted among the Maintained not because I was born there, but because my body had learned their central reflex: when the world blurs, place a hand on it and make it true again.

If there is a single image: at night, bent over a turquoise-lit channel, pressing fresh paste into a hairline crack while bioluminescence outlines my fingers—one more organism reinforcing the fact of things.
