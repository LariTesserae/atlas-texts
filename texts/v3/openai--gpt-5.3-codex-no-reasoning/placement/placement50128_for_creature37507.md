I find myself low to the ground, not at the center of the basin but at one of its **remembered edges**—a shelf-break where the packed paths split: one toward the seep pans, one toward the burrow fields, one toward the bone ring.

I am not a chief. Not a hunter of renown. Not a solitary mystic either.

I am a **keeper of timings**.

---

At first that sounds too abstract to be a body, so let me become physical.

I am narrow-hipped and long-shanked, built for daylong trotting rather than explosive sprinting. My feet are splayed slightly for mud crust and shale chips. My hands are dark-palmed, callused at thumb and first two fingers from lifting test stones, probing wet sediment, levering joint heads apart at old kill sites. My ears are nicked from crowded pool disputes. My throat sac—small, not ornamental—can push three carrying notes without strain. My eyes are never still for long; they ladder distance automatically: near swarm, mid herdline, far shelf movement.

My fur (or short filament, depending on season and lineage) lies dense along neck and spine where damp chill sits in the morning half-light. Belly thinner. Forearms scarred.

I smell like clay, marrow grease, sedge root sap, and the mineral tang of standing water.

---

What I *do* is hold the basin’s moving parts in my head and mouth.

Not map like lines on bark—map like pulse:

- This pan will turn sour by third dusk after rain.  
- That crossing stone tips now; don’t send juveniles first.  
- The burrow-clan to the east has kits aboveground; their alarm calls will spike at shadows that are nothing.  
- The north margin cats are hungry but limping—bold, not fast.  
- The insects will lift thick at last light where hoofprints cupped water all day. Bring baskets there, not to the obvious pool.

I listen for errors in the great continuous noise.  
That is my prestige: I notice what has gone slightly wrong before wrong becomes blood.

---

If you ask “who am I” in social terms:

I belong to a mid-ranked band that others tolerate because we are useful at crowded nodes. We arrive early at shared pools and stack chosen stones into stepping spines so smaller bodies can drink without sinking flank-deep. We call crossing order when ungulates and our own juveniles tangle at narrow cuts. We arbitrate carcass approach by sequence, not ownership: first cutters, then marrow group, then hide peelers, then bone smashers. Not because we are kind—because delay attracts bigger teeth.

I am one of the voices that can make rivals wait ten heartbeats instead of lunging now.

Ten heartbeats is often the difference between feast and scatter.

---

I carry tools, but lightly:

- a hammer stone that fits the right hand’s swing arc  
- two flakes wrapped in dried sedge braid  
- a digging tine of weathered horn  
- a pouch net for insect swarms when they rise in sheets

None of it is precious as object.  
Precious is knowing where each object is best used and when to drop it and run.

I do not sleep in a house. I sleep in recurring places—slight rises with crosswind and view, near enough to hear juvenile whimpers, far enough from water-edge panic. Our “camp” is a temporary thickening of bodies, dung, cracked shells, stripped stems, and stories. By second or third night it thins again, pulled by new opportunity.

---

Ritual exists, but it is kinetic.

At dusk, before full dark, I give the **three-turn call** from a low stone: one note for near danger, one for water status, one for regroup direction. Others answer from different arcs, and for a moment the basin becomes a woven net of consent. Not command—consent. Enough to move as many, not all.

Our myths are not about making the world.  
They are about **keeping place with it**.

We tell of ancestors who tried to wall a seep and lost it in a single storm cut. We tell of a lineage that hoarded a carcass and was driven off by rot and flies and night hunters. We tell of the Great Quiet (almost certainly exaggerated memory of disease or drought) when calls stopped layering and each band heard only itself—and starved on its own cleverness.

Moral: if the basin is a mouth, survive by being tongue, not tooth alone.

---

So where would you find me?

At first light: kneeling beside damp prints, touching edges to judge freshness.  
Midday: pacing a herd’s flank distance, watching predators watch them.  
Evening: on the shelf-break, issuing sequence calls as bodies converge on shrinking water.  
Night: half-asleep, identifying species by footfall tempo.

And who would I be?

I would be one of the first people whose architecture is made of **repetition in living time**: routes rehearsed, calls standardized, stones reselected, disputes ritualized, memory distributed across many throats.

A citizen of the unbuilt city.  
A coordinator in the loud dim basin.  
A creature whose monument is that tomorrow, at the same crossing, strangers and rivals still understand the same call.
