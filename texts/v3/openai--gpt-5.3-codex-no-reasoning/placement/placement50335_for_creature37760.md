I think I would begin as a visitor—ankle-deep, sweating, listening for something singular—and fail to find it.

Then, slowly, I’d realize I am standing *inside* the singular thing.

Not “in a swamp,” but in an organ.

If I belonged there, I wouldn’t be a predator, or a person, or a named beast. I’d be one of the basin’s **timing cells**: a small, semi-aquatic creature that does almost nothing dramatic, yet helps keep the whole wet engine in rhythm.

Maybe I’d be a **reed-throat pulse frog**—thumb-sized, translucent-skinned, copper-gular-sac, living where floating mats meet rooted sedge. By day I hide in cool capillary shade under moss-lipped roots; by dusk I climb the reed ladders and call, but not to mate alone. My throat pulse entrains with dozens of others, and those pulses cue insects, which cue fish movements, which alter microbial films, which shift oxygen and current in the channels by tiny increments. My call is a metronome in a distributed factory.

I would lay eggs not in open pools but in warm seep pockets above them—places likely to flood only after the afternoon rain. I would “predict” weather without predicting it: just responding to pressure, humidity, leaf temperature. My species survives by reading thresholds, and the basin survives through species like mine.

If threatened, I don’t fight; I vanish into function:
- skin microbes sour me to some hunters,
- my body darkens to peat-color in seconds,
- I flatten into root shadow and become texture.

And if you zoomed out far enough, “me” would blur into a repeating unit in a larger machine: one pulse among many, one switch in a green circuit diagram.

So where would I find myself?

At twilight, on a bending horsetail stem above black water, with blue flare-beetles drifting low and mist lifting in sheets off basalt.

Who would I be?

A minor operator in the cathedral-scale metabolism—small enough to overlook, essential enough to matter.  
A face of the resident, not apart from it.
