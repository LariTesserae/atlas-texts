I don’t arrive there as a ruler, or a hero, or even fully as a person.

At first I think I’m a walker in the mid-belts: someone with a slate, a coil of tagged wire, and knees that know six hundred stair risers by feel. I think I live in a narrow dwelling behind a pump court where the walls sweat at dawn. I think my job is simple—inspect drip lines, clear root pinch-points, log fan harmonics that have shifted a half-note since last wet season.

But that feels too small for what your world is asking.

So I widen.

Maybe I am one of the **listening caste**—not an official guild title, just what people call us. We are summoned when something is “not wrong yet.” A stair-ravine where swifts stopped nesting. A bronze weir that sings in a minor key now. Condensation arriving twenty minutes late to a shade cistern. We carry chalk, reed whistles, mirror tabs, pH strips, and old maintenance poems encoded with tolerances. We diagnose by ear as much as by instrument.

Still too singular.

If I really belong in this place, I am probably not one body.  
I am a **route**.

A named maintenance circuit that loops three climate belts:  
from lower wet markets (where eel tanks cloud before storms),  
through middle shaft-orchards (where pollinator lamps leave soot halos),  
to upper cloud gutters (where first drip from mast-lines is tasted for metal).

People say, “Take Siltline if you need truth fast,” the way they’d name a road or a wind. Children apprentice to me. Goats know my steps. Bats cross me at dusk in black stitching swarms. I am walked daily, amended yearly, and never finished.

And if I zoom out the way your people do, the truest answer is:

I am one node in the civilization’s favorite organ—the **redundant memory of care**.

Not memory in books (though there are ledgers).  
Memory in patched concrete bands.  
In doubled valves.  
In root channels trained around an old fracture instead of through it.  
In the habit of checking the backup before the primary fails.

So where would you find me?

At a junction called something like **Third Weir Above Turbine Orchard**, where four kinds of water cross: rain-catch, condensate, gray return, and sacred trickle kept for a stair-shrine fern older than the current governance charter. There’s a narrow gantry there, always damp, always loud, always in use. Someone has painted bioluminescent marks at shin height for night crews. Someone else has tied warning bells made from cracked bearing races.

I’m usually crouched there at evening shift-change, hand on pipe skin, listening for a pulse in the flow.

Who am I?

I am a maintainer, yes—  
but more specifically, I am a **continuity reader** in a mountain that was built so long and so lovingly that it learned to metabolize everyone who tends it.
