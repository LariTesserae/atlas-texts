I think I’d begin as a visitor and only slowly realize I was being recruited.

At first: a pair of borrowed sandals, too stiff with someone else’s gait-memory, and a humidity map flickering on a palm-slate while I try not to slide off a moss-slick traverse. I’d tell myself I was an observer—just passing through, documenting the old steward infrastructure, listening to spine-hum harmonics at dusk, sleeping in way shelters that smell like wet clay and ozone.

But this place doesn’t really permit pure spectatorship for long.  
If you stay, you start adjusting small things.

You lift a vine curtain off a footpath before dawn because the caravans will come through.  
You re-seat a fog membrane pin because the drip split is off and the lower basin is already too mineral-heavy.  
You hear a gully go quiet and reroute your day, then leave three marker cords for whoever follows.

So: who would I be?

Not a hero-species. Not a singular mind.  
I’d be one node in the culture of continuities—a **ledger-keeper of slow changes**.

Maybe they’d call my role something like a *Third Listener*—someone responsible for cross-checking what three different systems are saying: stone-song, animal routes, and old machine telemetry. I’d carry a kit of resonant stakes, fungal-safe oils, and little conductive tags grown with epiphyte tissue. My work would be mostly timing:

- when to open mesh under moss for uphill water lift,  
- when to let a path drown and teach everyone the new ridge route,  
- when to ask the spines for less charge because the canopy is already primed for storm.

I’d live in loops, not at an address: two nights under the overhang at Split Basin, one at the limewashed domes, then upslope before the noon shimmer gets bad. I’d remember places by their sounds at certain humidity levels.

And over years, I think I’d become less “a person in a landscape” and more an organ in a larger body—still myself, but functionally fused with watershed memory. If I vanished, others could read my notes in tuned stakes, vine braids, and updated boundary chords. If they vanished, I could read theirs. Identity there feels cumulative.

So where would I find myself?

On a warm black ledge at evening, one hand on a humming ceramic spine, waiting for the mist to rise and the stone to choose its night pitch—listening for what needs doing next, and for what should be left alone.
