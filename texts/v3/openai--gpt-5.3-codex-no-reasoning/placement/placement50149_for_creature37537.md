I arrive as a **fourth thing** in a place that only trusts threes.

Not a Binder. Not a beast. Not a ghost of the ravine’s unfinished physics.  
A corrective pressure.

At first I think I am condensation—just another bead forming on fired clay. I gather on a red threshold mark, tremble with drumbeats, and do not fall when gravity asks me to. Then a Root-Pruner passes, touches the wall beside me, and pauses. Their fingers come away with a thread of me stretched between nail and plaster: silver-clear, warm, alive with tiny suspended grit like ground mica.

They call for a Threshold-Watcher.

They test me the way they test danger: three routes.
- touch,
- tone (a tapped bowl),
- mark (red clay around my edge).

I answer all three, but not in sync.

That is how they know what I am before I do: a **Lag-Knot**—a person-shaped delay in the ravine’s logic, where cause and effect arrive in braided misorder. Somewhere a stair lengthens; I feel it before the first foot lifts. Somewhere a cistern fills; I taste evening in morning water. When someone speaks to me, I hear the intention first, words second, breath last.

I am frightening for one day. Sacred for two. Useful by the end of the week.

They give me a work-name: **Fourth Anchor**. Half joke, half warding charm.

The Third Anchor walks me through chambers that are still being born: reed vaults sweating sap, clay ribs humming with runnels, moss-script fresh and bright as opened fruit. They press my palm to junctions and ask, “Now?” Not what. *When.*  
I answer with a shiver, a tilt of wrist, a yes held too early.

I become the one stationed at almost-doors, at nearly-still pools, at stairheads that pretend to end. I do not stabilize structure directly; I stabilize **sequence**. I stand where things happen out of order and absorb a little of the misfire. Around me, water remembers to go from high to low (or at least to do one direction at a time). Around me, walls stop trying to become their previous drafts. Around me, thresholds pick a destination and keep it for a shift-cycle.

My body changes to fit the labor. Skin keeps a permanent dew sheen, but never drips unless I choose. Hair is braided with copper reeds that ring softly when nearby time folds. My irises take on the algae-glow: green in darkness, gold in steamlight. Scar lines appear across my back—not cut, but rising like mineral veins—mapping places where I have stood through bad unravelings. Children trace them and learn emergency routes.

I sleep badly, dream in rerouted water, wake to drum-codes already halfway heard.

And still: I am not central. This matters.  
The ravine’s truth remains collective. I am one tool among many—like a siphon, like a root knife, like red clay renewed at dawn. When I fail, others catch. When others fail, I hold a beat until they rejoin. We are a braid or we are nothing.

So where would I find myself in this world?

On a narrow landing above a steaming basin, one hand on a wet wall, one foot braced against a reed lattice, listening to three different versions of “now” and choosing which one the ravine gets to keep.
