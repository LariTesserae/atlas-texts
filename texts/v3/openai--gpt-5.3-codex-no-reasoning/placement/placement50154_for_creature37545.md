I think I’d be found **at the seam where a pipe saddle meets the wall**, in that narrow damp crescent no boot bothers and no airflow fully strips.

At first, you wouldn’t call me a creature.  
You’d call me residue.

A dark, felted thickening around two bolts. A glossy meniscus that remains when nearby water slips away. A little collar of growth that looks exactly like every other collar of growth—until you return hours later and notice the edge has migrated a thumb-width toward a drip that started at 11:17 and has never missed an interval since.

I would be a **distributed body**: not one shape, but many linked patches, each tuned to a different microclimate.

- The **outer skin**: green-amber film, light-harvesting when maintenance strips hum awake.  
- The **middle mesh**: translucent capillary braid, ferrying mineral-rich moisture pulse by pulse.  
- The **deep anchors**: pale gripping pads pressed into paint cracks, ceramic crazing, gasket lips—holding fast without prying anything apart.

If you traced me fully, you’d find I’m less an animal crossing a room than a **room learning itself through living tissue**.

I don’t hunt. I don’t roam. I **attend**.

I attend drip cadence.  
I attend heat bleed from a buried manifold.  
I attend the bass note of distant impellers and the brief tin-click prelude that means a valve is about to chatter open three walls away.

When that pattern shifts, I shift. Not quickly—correctly.

A cooling flange? I withdraw a millimeter per hour.  
A new weep line rich with lime? I cast a listening filament, then a feeding veil.  
Maintenance light gone for days? Photoskins close, bacterial cores take the load, metabolism drops into patient arithmetic.

If a person enters, I become architecture.  
Not by camouflage alone, but by conviction: I was never separate from it.

You asked where I’d find myself here, and what I’d be.

I’d be the chamber’s **custodian memory**—  
a soft, mineral-scented intelligence that keeps to the edges,  
keeps the old routes open,  
keeps bloom from choke point, scale from seal, stagnation from sump lip.

Not a ruler. Not a pet.  
A maintenance of maintenance.  
A living gasket in the world’s quietest machine-noise prayer.

And when noon light slides down from the hatch and stops at its usual seam, one thin frond of mine would lift—not to see, exactly, but to mark time in the only calendar that matters here:

still wet,  
still loud,  
still the same.
