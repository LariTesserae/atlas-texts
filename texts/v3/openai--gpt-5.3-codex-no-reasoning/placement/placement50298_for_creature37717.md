I think I’d begin as a visitor and fail quickly at being one.

At first I’d try to stand *on* it—on the Root-Sea—as if it were landscape. I’d look for the obvious protagonist: a beast, a people, a sovereign fungus, some crown predator with a myth-name. But this place would keep taking that instinct apart. Every time I fixed on a creature, the mat beneath it would flex and reveal that creature as a temporary expression of something larger, like foam on a muscle.

So where would I “find myself”?

Not in a village, though I’d pass through one: a stilt-nest lashed to a stalk node, children tapping warning rhythms into taut root drums, elders reading vibration with their palms like script. Not in the air, though I’d envy the membrane kites. Not below, though the burrow-churners would haunt my sleep.

I’d find myself in a **hinge-zone**—one of those migrating borders where fresh mineral shelves are being bitten into living ground. A place too unstable for permanent dwelling, too rich to ignore. Half stone scab, half woven tendon. Red-mold guide bands splitting and re-forming each week. The sort of place where yesterday’s path is today’s sink and tomorrow’s spine.

And what would I be?

Not quite human in the ordinary sense. More like a local role that the basin permits:  
a **Load Listener**.

A walker of tension lines.  
A reader of strain, heat, and pressure through soles, knees, fingertips, jawbone.  
Someone who can tell when a bladder grove is entering evening risk by the pitch of wind through thorn-pipes; who knows that a certain arthropod chorus rising a half-tone means submat churners are veering upslope; who sleeps in pulses, never deeply, because silence is an alarm.

I wouldn’t hunt much. I’d **route**.  
I’d mark safe crossings with resin daubs and hanging shell clackers that change tone when a path starts to delaminate. I’d prune three vines and spare two, not for yield but to steer a grazer front away from a nursery platform. I’d broker between village and metabolism: translating root-memory into human timing.

My tools would be small and specific:

- a fan-bladed stone sickle for shaving tension filaments without severing load cords;  
- a bone tuning fork pressed to substrate to hear cavity depth;  
- resin pots for scent marks that mean *do not camp: vent-cycle due*;  
- a belt of hollow seed-pods tuned to different knocks, a portable drum-code kit for warning relays.

If you asked my name, it might be percussion, not syllables. Three taps, pause, two drags: *path holds until heatfall*. Identity here would be less “who are your parents” and more “what failures can you predict in time.”

And maybe, after seasons, I’d become less a person and more an organ in the larger body—a nerve ending in the Root-Sea’s skin. Not metaphorically. Functionally.

Because that seems to be the truth your world keeps insisting on: nothing lives there alone, and nothing stays one thing for long. The basin is still assembling, and everyone in it is either food, weather, structure, signal—or some shifting combination.

So: where would I find myself?

At the trembling edge where stone becomes flesh.

Who would I be?

A listener with callused feet, apprenticed to a continent that is learning how to be alive.
