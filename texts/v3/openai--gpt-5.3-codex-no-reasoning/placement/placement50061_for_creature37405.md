I think I would be something very small in status and very central in consequence.

Not a ruler. Not a visionary.  
A **listener of faults**.

In this city, that might be a real profession: someone who walks the noon-quiet galleries with a rack of tuned ceramic tongues, a wax slate, and a pouch of patch plugs, stopping at marked wall points to hear whether the stone is breathing correctly. I’d belong to the maintenance castes that everyone depends on and nobody romanticizes until a failure.

I imagine I’d be called when a neighborhood says, *“The west wall went soft-quiet.”*  
Quiet being the wrongest sound.

I’d press my ear to plaster and hear three layers at once: public wind in the trench, private hiss in the gas channels, and the tiny percussive ticks of thermal strain. I’d know which tick means harmless settling, which means a void opening behind a load wall, which means a vapor pocket forming where children sleep. My work would be half acoustics, half memory, half superstition—like everything here, impossible arithmetic that still works.

Maybe I started as a canopy runner, young and heat-fast, climbing scaffold ribs at dawn to retension shade cloth before the high burn. Then an elder noticed I could identify districts blindfolded by resonance alone—kiln quarter knock, vent-temple bass, fermentation vault thump—and apprenticed me into structural listening.

So: I’d live in a narrow stacked dwelling above a crosswind lane, with hanging reeds by the door and a family condenser on the roof. My hands would always smell faintly of clay sealant and solvent oil. My forearms would be lattice-scarred from fiber rope. I’d keep a notebook of sounds the way other places keep maps.

And in social terms, I’d be trusted but argued with. Because if I declare a block unsafe, three households relocate and two workshops lose a cycle. If I delay and I’m wrong, people die. In a people of maintenance, judgment is a civic burden.

Spiritually? I’d be devout in the local way: not pious in words, pious in checks.  
I’d touch every valve. I’d recheck every knot. I’d confess every uncertainty early.

If you dropped me into that quarry-city, you’d find me at dusk in a half-lit trench, tapping a plastered wall with a bone-ceramic rod, listening for the moment when the city’s loudness has the right shape again—when danger has been edited back into routine, and routine back into life.
