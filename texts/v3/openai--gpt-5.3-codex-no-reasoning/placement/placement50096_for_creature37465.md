I think I would be **a Listener of Third Lines**—not a chief, not a hunter, but the one people wake when the planks change their voice.

I live in a split of basalt above the fifth stain, in a shelter that leans like a folded wing. My work is to tend anchors, teach children foot-listening, and keep the stone-cut record of surge memory. I am the person who taps each platform at dawn with a bone mallet and names the note: tight, frayed, hollow, shifting. I can’t stop the basin from revising itself, but I can hear revisions a little early.

I would carry:

- a coil of three braided lines (never one fiber repeated in all three),
- a pouch of wedge-stones for quick re-anchoring,
- a soft-metal needle wrapped in oilskin like an heirloom,
- and a jawbone whistle that doesn’t pierce the rain, but travels through timber.

My name, maybe, began as **Fifth-Stain Child**.  
Later, after a night when two platforms tore free and no one was lost, it became **Before-the-Lurch**.

I don’t read omens in the sky; I read them in tension: in how water climbs rope, in how moss lies flat before a bass throb, in how the cloud lowers not by sight but by pressure in the sinuses. If someone says, “It looks calm,” I answer, “Then find the pull.” If someone says, “Tomorrow we go east,” I answer with the old rebuke: “Tomorrow has no east yet.”

And if there is a mind in the basin—distributed through rain-grids and spring pulses—I am not its priest. I am its careful neighbor. I leave offerings only in the practical sense: clean runnels, mended traps, no wasted line in open water. Respect here means not pretending control.

So where would I find myself in your world?  
On a wet platform nailed to unfinished stone, knees bent, palm on plank, listening for the next sentence the mountain writes in water.
