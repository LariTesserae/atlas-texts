I arrive first as a listener.

Not a person at all—at least not at first—but a small, taut instrument hung where three alleys meet: bone ribs, gut-string, fired-clay cups, and a skin membrane stretched over a hollow of old stone. Wind crosses me. Footsteps cross me. The throat-calls of eave birds strike me and return changed. I am one of the quarter’s **continuance ears**: a maintenance thing that tells the living when the pattern breaks.

In a place where silence is alarm, I would be built to notice absence.

By day I would be almost ignored, which is how good infrastructure lives there—handled, checked, retied, never admired for long. Children pass and flick my cords. A keeper taps my frame with a knuckle on her way uphill and hears whether dust has packed the cavity. At dusk, when haze turns amber and lamps thicken shadow, my cups gather the dense fabric of sound: millstone grind, reptile-browse tearing leaves, low roof-to-roof arguments, bells, rasping fronds, coughs, laughter.

Then, over years, I become a person because this basin makes persons out of repeated duty.

I am the one assigned to that junction, then to the next, then to high paths where sound thins in dangerous air. I carry a loop of repair cord, plaster in a wrapped skin, and a narrow brush for clearing insect nests from resonator mouths. My work is to keep the village audible to itself. I map each quarter by its normal noise the way others map browse cycles or fog probability. I can tell when a pen latch failed, when a cistern lid was left ajar, when burrowers undermined a retaining wall—not by seeing, but by the changed weave of clacks, scrapes, and calls.

I am called something like a **Seam-Keeper**, because sound here is a seam: if it opens, dryness gets in.

Socially, I’m neither high nor low—trusted in that practical way this culture reserves for those who show up every day and mend what fails. I sleep in a shared roof-court with instrument makers, net weavers, and two elders who remember thirty years of wind shifts. I’m compact, dust-patinated, layered in patched cloth. My hands are nicked from stone edges and polished smooth at fingertips from tuning gut against bone pegs.

Spiritually, I believe what everyone here believes in practice: continuance is holy, maintenance is prayer, and attention is mercy.

If you asked, “Where would you find yourself?”  
I’d say: at a shaded intersection, under vine and wall, in the warm dim roar of a living basin—head tilted, listening for the first impossible quiet.
