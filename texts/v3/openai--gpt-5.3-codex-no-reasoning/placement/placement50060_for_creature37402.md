I arrive thinking I should be a person.

A registrar, maybe. A shadow jurist with ink-stained fingers and an exacting mouth. Someone who can tell you, without looking up, where the third stair in the north garden takes a slick of condensation at dawn and who petitioned to roughen it sixty-two years ago. That would fit your city’s intelligence: civic attention as devotion.

But the longer I stand there, the less human I feel.

Not because I don’t belong—because this place teaches that belonging is not ownership, it’s recurrence.

So I think I would be something small, infrastructural, and listened past:  
**a flute vent in a colonnade on the edge between the garden quarter and a dry square**.

Carved stone throat, timber baffle, a narrow mouth angled to the north wind. I sound at nearly the same hour each day, and people use me as time: second flute, merchants lift their awnings; third flute, the pack lizards are watered; when my tone drops, fog has thickened in the upper channels.

I am fixed, but not dead-fixed. Moss rims my base to the same line each cool season. Children thread dyed cords through my side lattice and remove them before council days. A cliff-rabbit once nested beneath my plinth for three generations in exactly the same crescent of dust. No one calls that miraculous here. They call it accounted for.

I would know the city by pressure and breath:
- the slight seasonal thickening of air before drizzle,
- the hoof-vibration of canyon elk passing two streets over,
- the pause in foot traffic when a debate in the court has gone silent all at once.

And because novelty lives where it can, I would hear it first. New songs testing my resonance. A reed instrument tuned to answer my pitch. A child discovering that cupped hands over my mouth make me warble like a marsh bird and being gently corrected—not for play, but for altering a public signal.

If I must be a person, then I am the keeper assigned to that vent: the one who oils the hinges of its baffle, scrapes lichen back to the legal edge, and records each year’s first true north tone in a ledger of cedar bark sheets.

But if I may be what the city itself would choose for me, I am this:

**a civic note**—  
a repeatable breath between stone and weather,  
where everyone passes, few look, and everyone, somehow, listens.
