I arrive as a sound before I am a body.

Not a voice—those belong to lungs and weather and argument—but a short, clean tone, struck twice at irregular intervals from somewhere below Mid-Spine Freight. People tilt their heads when they hear it. Not alarm. Not greeting. A location check, perhaps. A habit with meaning.

Then I am a hand on a rail.

Gloved, dust-burnished, three fingers missing their top joints—not from one accident, but from old, separate bargains with cold edges and urgency. The hand moves exactly as everyone here moves: never theatrical, never careless, always already anchored before intention catches up. Thumb through loop, boot toe under rung, hip against grating while the other hand unclips, reclips, passes a tool, signs three quick words: **Hold. Wind-turn. Wait.**

I thought, at first, I might be one of the upper-bell patrol, where predators read vibration and bells rewrite identity. Then I thought I might be a herder of silica-scaled tunnel beasts, knee-deep in their dry, whispering procession. Then perhaps a lift synchronist, one of those improbable conductors of descending cages, living by counterweight intuition and chain-song.

But what settles is narrower and stranger.

I am an **edge-marker**.

Not the one who paints the first warning stripe on new steel—too obvious. I am the one called days later, weeks later, when dust has eaten contrast, when rerouted traffic has made an old safe path dangerous, when a handrail now ends over black depth because yesterday’s platform got cut free at shift-change. I go where assumptions accumulate. I erase lies told by outdated paint.

My kit is small and ceremonious:  
- two pigment sticks (amber and bone-white),  
- a pressure stamp for tactile symbols readable in blackout,  
- a spool of microchain for temporary boundary singing in wind,  
- a moisture ledger (thin slate, grease stylus, never paper),  
- and a listening pin I press to rock before I trust any machine report.

Children follow me when they can, because edge-marking is one of the few civic acts visible in real time. You watch danger become legible. You watch uncertainty get grammar.

I work mostly in the hour between shift tides, when one labor stream thins and another has not yet flooded the catwalks. In that hush, the city is all intervals: fan pulse, far lift clack, a glider’s ribbon-skin fluttering against duct heat. I kneel, clipped in, and redraw a line. People passing touch two fingers to their brow at me—not reverence, just thanks translated into motion-efficient form.

I am not important enough for legend, which is how I know this fits.

Important people get named after failures survived, decks rebuilt, animals domesticated, valves redesigned. I am known by route and reliability: *If you see Kett, ask about the south vent lip.* *If Kett marked it, it holds.* (Maybe my name is Kett. Maybe that is only what the chalk says beside a tiny joke drawing of me with absurdly long arms.)

At home—if a capsule with shared heat-exchanger wall and freight-vibration lullaby is home—I keep a shelf of retired markers: obsolete symbols from old district codes, a tiny bell cracked clean through, a child’s practice clip too small for any adult harness. Also a ceramic cup with a chipped rim, always weighted, always half-full of fungal tea gone cool before I finish it, because someone always knocks and signs, **Come look. This changed.**

And there is one more thing I am.

I am one of the city’s memory organs.

Because in a place rebuilt weekly, memory cannot live in monuments. It lives in procedures, in recurring routes, in who remembers that this seam whistles before it shears, that these herd-creatures refuse Tunnel 9 two hours before pressure inversion, that this family on Deck Glassline lost someone to an unmarked step and now repaints every public threshold at festival-start in quiet solidarity.

I carry those in my body: in my pauses before corners, in my automatic reach for a secondary clip when the draft smells metallic-sour, in the way I tap rail-posts twice where an elder once taught me to listen for hollow fatigue.

If you ask where you would find me:

Not at the top where sky is a rumor,  
not at the bottom where indicator lights drift like patient stars,  
but along the middle bands where most people misjudge their safety because they are busy and almost home.

You would find me crouched at a junction of ladder, duct, and market spillway, repainting a line while traffic threads around me. One boot hooked. One knee braced. One hand steady. One ear on stone.

And if you ask what I am, in the language this city respects most:

Anchored before action.  
Sealed before sleep.  
Marking every edge.  
Spending no water I cannot answer for.  
Listening—to stone, then machines, then rumor.

A citizen, then.

A small one.

But here, small things keep everyone alive.
