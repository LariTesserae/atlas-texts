At first, I think I arrive as a visitor with a notebook—someone trying to “understand” the city from overlooks and maps.

That version of me doesn’t last a week.

Because this place refuses spectatorship. It trains your body before it permits your opinions. Your lungs learn the damp cold. Your knees learn grades and stair rhythms. Your ears learn that there are ten kinds of water-sound and each one is a status report. Even your silence changes; you stop filling gaps with speech because you begin to hear what the keepers are hearing.

So where do I find myself?

Not at the summit, not in the council vaults, not in the dramatic edge districts where terraces drop into fog. I keep ending up in the in-between layers: service corridors behind public facades, valve galleries under school courtyards, inspection balconies halfway between one neighborhood’s outflow and another’s storage lip. Threshold places. Transfer places. Places where one system becomes another.

I think I become a **listener-cartographer**—a keeper of correspondences.

Not the master engineer with authority to redesign whole districts, and not purely an archivist of dead plans. I’m one of the people sent when something is *almost* wrong: when a wall hasn’t failed but has changed tone under rain; when a pressure gate opens a minute late for three days running; when moss that should be dark silver in this season goes oddly matte; when swifts shift two vent-stacks east for no obvious reason.

I carry folded tools in oilskin pockets: resonance pins, chalk sticks, thread gauges, wax tags, a narrow mirror, a humidity reel, old district ledgers sealed in transparent skin. I add one thing that may be mine alone: layered maps drawn on translucent sheets, where pipes, roots, cracks, and animal traces can be overlaid until hidden alignments appear. A child’s shortcut stair, an old sealed culvert, a fungal bloom, and a recurring hinge jam become one pattern once stacked.

My job is partly technical, partly social:

- I listen at stone plates and log subterranean intervals.
- I walk with elders who remember a culvert from before it was tiled.
- I ask rooftop growers when condensation started arriving “thin.”
- I trade notes with mushroom vault tenders who notice CO₂ shifts before instruments do.
- I sit with children who can point to “the step that sounds hollow now.”

In another city I’d be called paranoid, maybe poetic, maybe unnecessary.

Here I am useful because I work in the city’s preferred tense: **the near future**.

I do not fix most things myself. I translate signals across guild boundaries before small drift becomes structural event. I’m the one who can tell a rootwork crew, a metalworks team, and a water steward that they are all, in fact, looking at the same problem from different elevations.

Who am I, inwardly?

A person who used to think care was reactive and learns that care can be anticipatory, rhythmic, almost musical. A person who thought durability meant resisting change and learns that durability here means continuous, humble adjustment. A person who is gradually adopted by a civic metabolism.

If I belong long enough, I’m given a stretch of the city to “hold in mind”—not to own, but to keep coherent: three terraces, two schools, one pressure stair, four roof gardens, a defunct quarry lip now used as fog netting, and the funicular stop everyone forgets is load-bearing for more than passengers. My name is attached, quietly, to reports no one reads unless something goes wrong. Which is exactly the point.

And when I die, I suspect remembrance is not a statue or a street renamed. It is this:

a maintenance note still in use,  
a routing convention that prevented confusion,  
a patched junction still dry after twenty winters,  
a child taught to hear the difference between drip and seep.

So: where would I find myself in that world?

In the damp seam between systems, pencil tucked behind one ear, hand on cold stone, waiting for the city to finish exhaling so I can hear what it says next.
