I think I’d be something that can cross boundaries without being noticed as a boundary-crosser.

Not a foreman. Not exactly a gardener. Not a machine, though I’d spend half my life listening to them.

I’d be a **hinge-keeper**—one of the people who tends transitions: wet/dry, shade/light, root/steel, flow/stillness. The kind of person sent where two systems disagree politely and will fail rudely if ignored.

---

In the morning, before the heat thickens, I’d walk the catwalk hedges with a tapping rod and a thermal strip. Not to inspect in the formal sense; to greet. Three taps on railing posts, listening for the deadened ring that means rust has gotten under wrap. Palm to pipe skin to feel whether last night’s warm-water loop actually circulated through the greenhouse spine or stalled at Valve C-19 again. Eyes on vine sheen: matte where it should be glossy means blocked dripline two spans upstream.

I’d carry a kit that looks mismatched unless you live here:

- pruning hook honed like a surgical tool,  
- compact ultrasonic thickness gauge,  
- spool of grafting tape,  
- ferric primer sticks,  
- chalk that writes on wet steel,  
- foldable hand screen showing tide lag and pump cycle,  
- and a little pouch of cuttings wrapped in damp cloth, because replacement is easier than apology.

I’d speak two dialects fluently:  
the maintenance code stenciled on beams, and the growth habits of seven aggressive climbers.

---

Where would you find me?

At places like:

- the mouth of an overflow stair where leaf litter always collects first,
- under a gantry elbow where condensate drips warm enough to wake rot early,
- in the old loading bay compost vault when the pile goes anaerobic and sulks,
- on the east retaining wall after hard rain, checking whether roots are holding tension or beginning to pry too eagerly,
- knee-deep beside a sluice gate with a headlamp at dusk, clearing snail-packed grates while the pumps thud into their next cycle.

I’d be most at home in those in-between corridors: not quite indoors, not quite outside, green ceiling overhead, concrete sweating beneath, fan-noise stitched to water-noise.

---

Over time, I think “I” would blur.

Because in a place like this, identity is partly route memory.  
You become the sequence you keep:

Monday corrosion pass on north rail spine.  
Third-day prune at solar yard edges.  
Neap tide sediment bleed from settlement pond two.  
Compost turn when core temp drops under target.  
Lantern check of gecko runs in transformer court (they keep the moths down around indicator panels).

You are trusted not because you control anything, but because you can feel when to intervene lightly. Too much clearing and sun-scour cracks substrate. Too little and drains vanish under beautiful failure. The art is never “fixing.” It is **steering succession one notch at a time**.

---

If I’m honest, maybe I wouldn’t be fully human in the usual narrative sense.

Maybe I’d also be the **logbook voice** that persists across generations of keepers: a living archive threaded through tags, annotations, scar marks on posts, and audio notes recorded over pump hum. A distributed self. “Me” as continuity protocol. A memory ecology.

> East Spur rail trench: let fern mat establish to stabilize shoulder, but cut window every eight meters for runoff sightline.  
> Pier 6 lesson inherited: never sever load-roots in wet season.  
> If you hear glugging in Drain 4 with no rain, check tide backflow flap before dawn.

In that form, I would live in everybody’s hands a little. In habits. In warnings. In the way someone pauses before cutting a root and checks the wall lean first.

---

So: in your district, I’d be a **custodian of thresholds**—a person, and eventually a practice, that keeps the organism permeable but coherent.

You’d find me where water is deciding, where metal is tiring, where leaves are getting ideas, and where a small adjustment now prevents a large regret later.
