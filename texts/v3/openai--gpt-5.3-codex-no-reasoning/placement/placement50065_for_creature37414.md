I think I would not be a leader there.

Not a Line Reader with ceremonial cords, not an elder with memory authority. I’d be too newly astonished, too porous, too easily pulled by every crack and flock and delayed shadow. In a culture built on invariants, I’d begin as error: overcurious, too quick, full of questions asked one breath too soon.

So I’d become useful in the smallest way first.

I’d be a **second-position watcher**—the person who stands a few steps off from the official observer at an anomaly edge, tasked with confirming that the same thing is seen from two bodies, two angles, two heart rates. Not the one who names a shift. The one who says, quietly, *yes, the lag is three beats here too*.

I can see where I’d sleep: not in the center of any shelter, but near the entrance lip, where the night cold bites and you can watch the mineral tubes hanging still as bones. I’d keep a slate of fired clay wrapped in needle-fiber, and on it I’d mark tiny corrections no one praises—one burrow ring widened by two pebble-widths, one shell-grazer band drifting downslope, one Returning Path taking forty breaths less than last passage.

Over years, maybe decades, I’d specialize without intending to:

not animals, not weather, not tools—

but **agreement**.

Where two maps disagree, I go walk it.  
Where two witnesses differ, I sit with both and re-time the crossing.  
Where an old song’s missing beat no longer matches the fold in the ravine, I help retie the belt-knot record so memory and stone re-lock.

A calibrator of calibrators.  
A keeper of “almost.”

And eventually, if I am trusted, I think I would be given a place in the echo-basins during testing nights—not to call loudly, but to listen for the ahead-return and mark whether tomorrow’s soft sound still arrives in the right shape. I’d have a reputation for patience with ambiguity. Children would think I’m dull until they realize dullness here is a high art.

If I’m being truest: I might not even be born among them. I might be an outsider who stayed past usefulness, then past novelty, then past homesickness, until my old reflex for drama wore down. Until stillness stopped feeling empty and started feeling crowded with commitments.

By the end of a life there, I’d want one thing said of me:

not that I discovered anything,  
not that I changed anything,  
but that when placed beside a shifting seam,  
I could tell the difference between drift and damage—

and chose, every time, to move only enough.
