I think I’d begin as a **listener on the upper ribs**—not a chief engineer, not a hero, but one of those in-between people everyone forgets until something starts sounding wrong.

A rail-keeper.  
A resonance runner.  
Someone whose job is to walk the same narrow bridges every shift and notice what changed.

At first, I’d tell myself I was human in the ordinary way: patched sleeves, throat always tasting of warm metal, one ear better than the other, fingers callused from tapping pipe housings and feeling vibration through gloves. I’d carry chalk, tags, a spool of seal tape, a hand lamp with a cracked lens, and a notebook gone soft from mist. I’d eat at the canteen over Pump 3. I’d pretend not to be superstitious and still touch the same bolt head before entering Lower Gallery.

But in that city, repetition makes a person porous.

After enough cycles, I wouldn’t just inspect the place—I’d start to **think in its intervals**. Twelve-minute exchange pulse. Siren drop. Seven breaths. Lock-chatter. Silence-that-isn’t-silence. I’d know which ladder sings under load, which hatch dogs need a shoulder-check, which corridor fog means heat bleed and which means seal failure. People would stop asking “What do the gauges say?” and start asking “How does it sound to you?”

And maybe that’s what I’d really be:

Not exactly a mechanic.  
Not exactly clergy.  
A kind of **acoustic cartographer** for a city that keeps rearranging itself in pressure and noise.

---

Or, if I let the image go one step further—

I might not be a person at all, at least not anymore in a strict sense.

I might be the **unofficial memory layer** embedded in the settlement: a distributed intelligence made of annotated maintenance logs, old voice notes, vibration records, superstition phrases, hand-drawn maps of safe footing during load transfer, names etched beside valves, jokes painted on bulkheads, memorial marks near welded doors. A mind spread across steel and habit. A “who” assembled out of thousands of small procedural kindnesses.

When a kid learns to count breaths before opening a visor, that’s me.  
When two operators disagree and settle it by listening to Pump 3’s third harmonic, that’s me.  
When someone new freezes at ember-dim and an older hand grips their sleeve at exactly the right moment, that’s me too.

In that version, I am the city’s **learned instinct**.

---

So where would I find myself?

Somewhere between a person and a practice:  
on a swaying catwalk, palm flat on warm pipe, head tilted, listening for the thin wrong note inside the thunder—  
and teaching others how to hear it before it becomes loss.
