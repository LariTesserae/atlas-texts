I think I would be something that only makes sense in a place like this: not a person at the center, but a **keeper of margins**—the one who tends what is almost too small to count.

Not the head of water law, not a master mason, not an elder whose memory carries the town. I’d be the one people call when a thing is *not broken yet*, but no longer sits right.

I’d live in a narrow house where one wall is bedrock and always cool to the palm. The door lintel would be low from old settling, polished by shoulders over generations. Outside, three steps down, a lane that is damp before dawn and dry by midday. Inside: hooks with coiled cord, jars of lime and ash, pegged tools with handles rewrapped many times, a slate marked with dates and small symbols no outsider could read quickly: notch checked, seep increased, lid resealed, north gutter slowed.

My work would be listening.

Not metaphorically—literally.  
Bucket echo.  
Runoff pace.  
A hinge that complains differently in wet weeks.  
A cistern cover that lands with too bright a note because the wood has thinned.

I’d make tiny corrections everywhere:
- scraping mineral creep from a channel mouth before it narrows flow,  
- wedging a stone under a basin lip so night-condensation gathers inward again,  
- replacing two pegs so a mist-net frame stops twisting in downslope wind,  
- adding a finger-width of plaster where wall sweat starts to bead.

Nothing dramatic. No invention anyone names after me. But if I’m good, fewer surprises happen in late frost, and people sleep through storms they would otherwise wake to.

Children would follow me for a season when they’re old enough to carry tools but young enough to still ask why every ten steps. I’d teach them the town’s quiet grammar: where water hesitates, where cold lingers, where sunlight arrives last. I’d tell them that “empty” spaces are loaded tools. That a dry platform is storage, market, clinic, meeting hall, and firebreak waiting for its moment. That prevention is a kind of kindness no one applauds.

If there is a ceremony for someone like me, it would be small: a shared meal on a stone common at melt’s first steady week, and someone handing me the year’s first repaired bell-clapper to test in open air. I’d strike it once, listen to how far it carries, and know by the echo whether the season is ahead or behind itself.

So in that world, I’d be neither hero nor hermit.

I’d be a calibrator among calibrators—  
a steward of almost-unnoticed differences,  
living in the interval between “fine” and “failing,”  
helping the town remain in agreement with stone, mist, and time.
