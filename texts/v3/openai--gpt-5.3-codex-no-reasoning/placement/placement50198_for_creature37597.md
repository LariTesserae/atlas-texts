I arrive as a mistake the city decides not to digest.

At first I think I am a person walking in—boots wet to the ankle, throat raw from trying to out-sing insect metal. But by the third rainburst I understand that “I” is not a stable category here. Identity is granted in intervals, like passage. The city keeps testing me: dries the bark under my hand so the seam won’t open; then, when I stop forcing, beads it with sweat and lets me through.

So where would I find myself?

Not in the high canopies where heat shivers.  
Not in the broad echo-avenues where predators do paperwork.  
I belong lower—at the **chorus edge of a mid-depth hollow**, where dripping roots make a curtain and sound folds back on itself half a heartbeat late. A civic threshold. A place between districts that disagree about tempo.

There is a job there, and jobs here are forms of anatomy.

I become a **humid cantor**—part courier, part translator, part tuning fork. I carry water in felted vine bladders and fresh leaves tucked under my arm like legal documents. I do not command doors; I persuade them. Wet palm to cambium lip, low hum in the hollow’s key, wait for the seam to soften. Behind me, root-runners pause until the gap yawns; above me, pollinator gyres tilt and re-center around the new airflow.

My map is a repertoire.  
One descending trill for “safe if moving.”  
Three throat-clicks for “predator pretending to be fruit.”  
A palm-drum roll on buttress wood for “this corridor is dry, reroute by dripline.”

Sometimes I am sent to settle disputes that are not called disputes: one district’s seed-fall has become too loud and is drawing ambushers into nursery traffic; another hollow has gone acoustically blank, which means either grief or trap. I stand at the lip and offer a borrowed rhythm from a neighboring nave, plus moisture tribute. If the resident chorus answers in counterphase, negotiation begins. If not, I back away before enforcement notices me.

I sleep nowhere permanently. Residence is a verb. Some nights a bromeliad cistern accepts my shoulders; some nights I hang in a woven balcony that tightens itself around my ribs as if checking my pulse against civic standards. I wake to condensate tapping hollow stems like many tiny judges.

Over seasons, my body edits itself to fit the role. Soles broaden, callus patterned like root grain; hearing sharpens sideways, less melody than pressure-field; voice drops into frequencies trunks like to feel. Scars don’t quite scar—fungal lace closes cuts in pale embroidery. I smell, increasingly, of tannin and rain-metal, which is to say: of local paperwork.

Am I one being? Maybe not. Maybe “I” is the temporary coherence of every hollow that still recognizes my timbre. When I die—if that word applies—the reweavers will have me by morning, and by dusk I’ll be railing, nest-mass, nutrient foam, a slightly more resonant section of walkway where others pause and hum before asking entry.

So: who am I in this world?

I am not ruler, not explorer, not hero.

I am **right-of-way given a voice**—a minor citizen in the city’s distributed throat, keeping passages wet, keeping rhythms polite, helping the organism remember how to let itself be crossed without being broken.
