I think if I arrived there, I would not be a ruler, nor a mason, nor one of the cats.

I would be a **listener of joins**.

Not an official title, at first. Just the person who lingers where one material gives way to another: wood-to-clay, clay-to-stone, wall-to-mist, channel-to-cistern. In a place like this, failure begins at boundaries. So does knowledge.

I’d be the one people wave over with two fingers when a floor starts to “feel wrong.”  
The one who kneels and puts an ear to a post before speaking.  
The one who carries chalk, cord, and thin willow pegs polished by handling.

I’d sleep low like everyone else, under shelves hung with baskets and rolled hides, boots near the brazier, always slightly damp by dawn. My clothes would smell of marl and smoke. My palms would be callused in odd patterns from pressing seams and tapping stakes rather than from heavy hauling. Children would imitate my habit of pausing mid-conversation to watch how water decides a corner.

Over time, I think I’d become something between inspector and storyteller:  
- marking hairline shifts with charcoal dates,  
- teaching apprentices the six kinds of “good wet” and the three dangerous kinds,  
- keeping a memory-map of which beams sang before they slipped last thaw.

Not a priest of anomalies—this town doesn’t need that. More like a **custodian of tendencies**. Someone who knows that the cold alley at midday means venting below is active, which means don’t store root bundles against that wall; they’ll rot from within. Someone who knows which pits grow mineral sheath fastest and saves those for load-bearing resets two seasons ahead.

If I were not human, I’d be the town’s other mind: a marsh-cat, pale-eyed, still as tied cord on a beam over running dark water. But that feels too simple.

No—here, I am human, mud-kneed, quiet-voiced, and perpetually mid-repair.

A citizen of the seep-city whose craft is this:

**to notice change early enough that it can remain ordinary.**
