I think I’d find myself not at the center dais, and not out on the ragged edge either—I’d be on a **hinge**: one of those narrow, busy connectors where three walkways meet above a sluice gate and everyone has to pass eventually.

And I wouldn’t be a chief, or a mystic, or a lone wanderer.

I’d be a **caller-keeper**—part flow tender, part signal drummer, part public memory for “how this section behaves when rain comes hard from the east.” The person who notices that a certain gutter has changed pitch, that one retaining basket is breathing silt, that monkeys have started watching a new store loft, that the frog chorus has shifted upstream and means a clogged reed throat somewhere above.

Not glamorous. Indispensable.

---

I imagine a body built by this place: low center of gravity, rope-burned palms, shins crosshatched with old knocks from ladder rungs. A belt with small things: wedge knife, coir cord, reed pegs, waxed markers, a palm-sized wood clapper with two tones. No dry clothes ever fully dry, just rotations of less-wet. Hair bound tight under a resin-slick hood in storm hours.

My home platform would be half workshop, half listening post:  
bundles of split bamboo, hanging knots for teaching children, a wall-board of tapping patterns, jars of frog eggs protected from trampling, and a map that is never finished because channels rewrite themselves weekly.

---

In your world, identity comes from maintenance rhythms, so mine would too:

- Dawn: rounds by ear before eye—listen at culverts, then inspect.
- Midmorning: teach young ones signal codes by making a game of echo and distance.
- Noon: mediate two households arguing about overflow rights (really about labor fairness).
- Afternoon rain: coordinate a six-person patch team at a slumping ledge.
- Night: log changes in memory-strings—knotted records of flood height, breach points, repair dates.

I’d speak in specifics. I’d trust people who do.  
I’d be suspicious of anyone who says “always” about water.

---

If there’s a deeper “what” than profession, maybe I’m this:

**An organ of feedback in a living settlement.**  
A nerve ending in the town’s wet skin.  
One of the people who keep motion from becoming damage.

In a place where stability is constant adjustment, I’d be one of the adjusters—  
not above the system, not outside it, but braided into its daily pulse.
