I arrive as something that can stand still for a very long time without being counted as stillness.

At first I think I am one of the lane-runners—small, armored, diligent, useful in a way that leaves no signature. Then I follow one procession too far and realize I am not in the procession, I am between them: at thresholds, at joints, at those dark wet seams where one material agrees to touch another. Pipe to wall. Plate to trench lip. Bolt to bracket. Always the same places, always damp first, dry last.

So I become a **seam-keeper**.

Not a foreman, not a queen, not a god-organism. A municipal role. A minor office in the great biological public works.

My body is low and segmented, but softer than the runners, plated only where impacts are predictable. I carry a fringe of capillary filaments that wick condensation from metal skin and spread it along etched grooves in concrete. When the night cools and beads gather under intake towers, I wake and make rounds. I taste pH, oil trace, mineral load, microbial bloom density; then I choose where water is allowed to persist and where it must be interrupted. I do not drink much. I **allocate**.

In daylight haze I look like a stain that learned how to move.

I am accompanied by a halo of near-invisible hitchers—thread-thin commensals that live in my moisture fringe and bioluminesce only at collision frequencies. They are my warning mesh. When an acoustic lane shifts, they flash cold blue before the impact wave arrives. I flatten. The heavy loaders pass, shouldering gates. Their hooked tails latch, release, latch. Behind them, pressure changes ripple through dust like weather that forgot the sky.

I do not cross yellow stripes. Not because I fear punishment; because my chemistry would corrupt what they reserve. Those corridors are dry logic, timing arteries. My work is in tolerated damp: legal wetness, bounded wetness, circles that never spread past paint because I keep surface films tensioned and mineral edges seeded. If a pool tries to wander, I salt its boundary with exopolymers and it remembers its jurisdiction.

Every six heartbeats of blue-lamp sweep, I fold flat with everyone else. In that interval I sync my internal oscillators to basin time. My species cannot keep long rhythm alone; we drift. The civic pulse pulls us true.

When machines fail, I am often first to know. Not by alarms—by silence-shapes: a missing overtone in a pump housing, a dead patch in rail hum, a drip cadence gone arrhythmic. I summon a loader with a three-note wet-metal trill, then coat the seized seam with a biofilm that eats oxidation and excretes conductive salts. Sometimes that is enough. Sometimes the loader strikes once, precisely, and the old motor coughs itself awake. We do not celebrate restart. We mark it by resuming traffic density.

If you ask where I am in this world: I am at ankle height, under handrails, beneath condenser lips, along the cupped stair middles polished by extinct boots and extant claws. I inhabit the treaty line between corrosion and function.

If you ask what I am: I am one small civil servant in a city whose citizens are procedures. I do not dream of wilderness. I dream of **continuity gradients**—of keeping the basin’s narrow wet metabolism inside its inherited geometry for one more cycle, and one more, and one more, until repetition itself becomes a kind of weather.

And if you stand very still near a dark seam and feel, not quite hear, a soft combing sound—like a brush passing over rust—that is me passing, auditing the edge, ensuring the place can go on being exactly what it already is.
