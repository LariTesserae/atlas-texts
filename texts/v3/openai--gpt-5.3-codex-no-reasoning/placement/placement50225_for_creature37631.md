I’d be something that can belong to both **infrastructure** and **crowd**.

Not a tower—too singular.  
Not a runner—too fleeting.  
I think I’d be one of the **junction keepers**: a low, many-bodied colony that lives where root-arches meet the ground, in the shadowed throats between districts.

By day I would look like a dark seam, almost nothing—just a braided pad of matte nodules tucked under mineral buttresses. Animals would step over me. Browsers would scrape nearby and ignore me. Dust would settle into me and vanish. Heat would pass across my skin in slow bands, and I would memorize it.

At dusk I’d wake into function.  
Tiny valves along my surface would iris open, not to drink first, but to **sample**: sulfur, ammonia trace, ghost water, exhaled warmth from passing bodies. I’d route those fractions inward through capillary combs and decide, chemically, where to send them next—up into the vault-columns, sideways to felt-mats, down into insulated cysts for the late-night drop. I would be a broker of almost-nothings.

I’d also be a registrar of passage. Every footfall leaves pressure, salts, microscopic abrasion. Over seasons I’d learn the schedules of the basin: when gliders descend, where soft-foot runners queue, which predator is pretending to be a broken spire this month. Not thought like a mammal’s thought, but patterned expectation—civic memory distributed in tissue.

If damaged, I wouldn’t heal toward an original form. I’d heal toward **use**.  
Cracked edge? Grow a drip lip.  
Puncture? Make it a valve chamber.  
Dead patch? Mineralize and become a curb.

So where would you find me?  
At thresholds: culvert mouths, plaza rims, under arch-roots where temperature flips are sharpest. Places where one district becomes another. Places where the city decides who gets to keep breathing before dawn.

I’d be small, mostly unseen, and essential—  
a quiet municipal organ in a world where survival is governance.
