I think I would find myself in a **hinge-place**: not in one of the named seep settlements, but on a transit ledge between three of them, where paths are only trustworthy in cloud and where the ridge-flutes can be heard from both sides of noon.

Not a chief. Not a mystic.  
A **listener-keeper**—the person people send for when two honest springs disagree.

I’d live in a low, many-angled shelter pinned into a travertine shoulder above the third mineral line, with a narrow porch of fitted slate where I can set out bowls at different heights and hear, by pitch, which fog is carrying salt and which is carrying iron. I’d keep:

- a roll of hammered soft-metal forks, each tuned to a known gorge;
- a book of mineral paper sealed with iridescent ash-lacquer;
- three maps of the same routes: one by sight, one by echo delay, one by taste memory.

None of those maps would match exactly. That would be the point.

I’d be the sort of person who walks with others for the first hour into a fold zone, then stops at the last reliable stone and listens while they continue—counting seconds to their call-back, marking whether space is “open,” “creased,” or “argumentative.” I’d teach children to distrust any cliff that looks too near in hard sun, and to trust the shiver in a fork-handle more than the eye’s confidence.

And maybe, over years, I’d become less distinct from the place’s other intelligence. My hands permanently stained violet-copper from the film. My thresholds painted with thin sigils that are really moisture equations. My speech full of weather verbs: not *it rains*, but *the wall decides to give*.

So: in that world, I think I am human—but only just in the usual sense.  
A custodian of behavior.  
A minor instrument the mountain can play back through.
