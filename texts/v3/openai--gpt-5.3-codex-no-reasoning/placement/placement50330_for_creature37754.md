I think I would not be one of the loud professions. Not a gate-warden, not a rail dispatcher, not a predator mapper with bright authority strips.

I’d be a **drip cartographer**—one of the people who walks the city at edge-hours and keeps the living map of where water is becoming what.

Not a scientist in the abstract sense, but a civic specialist whose body has learned this basin’s grammar: I’d taste, listen, touch stone, read sheen, mark timing. I’d carry a belt of narrow ceramic vials, chalk that binds in humidity, and thin color tabs to leave on railings and thresholds: this line safe for infants; this one high copper tonight; this one fungal-sour but boil-stable; this one for tube-colony feed only. My work would be ordinary and crucial, like tuning an instrument everyone lives inside.

I imagine I’d live in a stilted dwelling over sweating ground in a mid-slope district, where ceiling driplines are inherited and argued over. My household would know me by the faint mineral smell I bring home from different sectors—basalt-cool mornings, condenser-metal noon, vat-sweet dusk. I’d speak well with my hands, keep my voice low, and be considered pleasant but overly exacting about grate hygiene.

I’d probably have a personal quirk that people tolerate because it’s useful: I pause at walls. In conversation, mid-step, anywhere—because sometimes the rock is about to breathe and I can feel pressure shift in my sinuses a minute early. Children would tease me, then copy me when they’re older.

Socially, I’d sit in that middle rank this city depends on: not glamorous, but trusted. The kind of person who gets called when someone says, “Our evening rain tastes wrong,” or, “The west corridor is too dry for this heat,” or, “The flockers turned early and no one knows why.”

And if I widen the answer beyond “person,” maybe I’d also be a little infrastructural in spirit—more **timing mechanism** than hero. A human metronome in a place where survival is cadence.

So:  
**Where would I find myself?** In the shaded underways between districts, at dawn and copper dusk, kneeling by grates and cistern lips, annotating the city’s throat.  
**Who would I be?** One of the quiet people who make sure water arrives as expected—and when it doesn’t, notices first.
