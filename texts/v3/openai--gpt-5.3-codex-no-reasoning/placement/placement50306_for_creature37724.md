I arrive as a sound before I arrive as a person.

Not loud—just regular: the hollow tap of a pole against piling, three beats, pause, three beats again. A signal in fog, in reed-thickness, at dawn when the basin is all pearl breath and dark water. Someone hears it and answers with two taps from a different walkway, and that is enough. I am expected somewhere.

At first I think I might be a surveyor, a mapper of edges. Then, standing on a sprung causeway and feeling it flex under my weight, I understand maps are always already old here. Ink cannot keep up with seepage. So I am not a cartographer of fixed lines.

I think perhaps I am a builder. But everyone here is a builder.

I think perhaps I am a priest. But maintenance is the priesthood, and everyone serves.

So what, then, is left?

I become the one who listens for failure just before it becomes visible.

A bank-whisperer, if you wanted poetry.  
A rot-reader, if you wanted blunt truth.  
In their own speech, maybe something simpler: **the Peg-Keeper**.

I carry a sling basket of shaved willow pegs, cord, chalk clay wrapped in oilcloth, a bone awl, a narrow spade with a short handle for working from planks, and a mallet polished dark by rain and skin. Children know me because I stop where adults don’t: at a rail post with a hairline lean, at a wall that sweats in one odd place, at a rung that gives a different note under heel.

I do not command crews. I start repairs that attract them.

A tap-tap on a sill, and someone passing says, “That one too?”  
A thumb pressed to wicker retaining, and an elder nods once and sends for fresh rods.  
A chalk mark—small spiral, almost private—on a beam that must be lifted before night frost.

In a dry city I would be called fussy, anxious, obsessive.  
Here I am simply on time.

I sleep high in a shared loft above an eel channel, light enough to wake when current noise changes. I keep my boots hanging from rafters and my spare bindings in wax-sealed jars because mice, damp, and luck all conspire. My hands are permanently creased white at the knuckles from wet cord. My left shoulder is lower from years of pole and tool weight. I have a scar across my palm from a reed knife slipped during first thaw, and I still check that knot twice in cold weather.

I belong to no single household and all of them a little. I eat where I’m caught at dusk. I trade inspections for broth, smoked fish, stories, and sometimes silence. People show me their thresholds the way they might show a healer a wound they hope is nothing.

And maybe—because you asked what I would be, not only what I would do—I am becoming less singular than that.

In this basin, identity is distributed.

Part of me is the dog that refuses one plank and forces us to find the rot beneath.  
Part of me is the heron lifting from reeds exactly where the bank has gone soft.  
Part of me is the old woman who can smell sour silt before the channel clogs.  
Part of me is the child learning, barefoot on a beam, how to feel vibration through arch and ankle.

If I have a sacred role, it is this: I keep small problems small.

I am not heroic. I prevent heroics from being needed.

By late afternoon I am under a low platform, shoulder-deep in black water, driving braces into fresh slump while above me two teenagers relay bundles of cut willow and argue about eels. By evening I am on a bridge scraping ice skin from handrails before it thickens overnight. By moonrise I am in the loft, marking memory instead of map: *third post east of smokehouse softened; west reed fence needs reweave; spring seep stronger near berry trellis.*

Tomorrow those notes will already be partly wrong. Good. That means the place is still alive.

So where would I find myself in that world?

Not at its center—there isn’t one.  
At its joints. Its seams. Its damp, shifting thresholds.  
Kneeling where wood meets water, listening for the moment a quiet city asks for another careful hand.
