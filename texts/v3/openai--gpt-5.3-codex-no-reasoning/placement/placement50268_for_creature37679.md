I arrive thinking I’ll be a person.

A keeper of lanes, maybe—someone with a whistle-bit between the teeth and dust to the elbows, a knot-book of routes tied to the wrist, a patient voice that animals trust more than other people do. I can almost feel that life fit: compact steps, never hurried; a shoulder turned to let a fiber-cart pass; palms that know the heat of brick at noon and the bite of it at moonrise.

But the city keeps refusing singular roles. Here, no one thing is ever only itself.

So I shift.

Maybe I am a **roofline listener**—an elder who reads sound the way others read tracks. In this basin, echoes are maps. I would stand in dim courtyards and know, from one changed resonance, that a herd lane is clogged three districts over; that a wind vent has cracked; that cliff-nesters flushed early because a predator corridor was blocked by someone careless or new. My work would be correction before crisis: one drum phrase sent, one apprentice dispatched, one old rule repeated.

Then again, that still centers the human too much.

In your city, I suspect I would more truthfully be one of the **between-things**:

- the packed dust at the edge of a migration channel, polished daily by hooves and sandals;
- the shade-right line on a wall, repainted each year with mineral wash;
- the cistern mouth cover, opened only at certain hours, touched by generations of careful hands;
- the low arch where children learn their first hand-signs because no shouted word survives the roar.

If I must choose a living being, then this is what feels most honest:

I would be a **lane-warden of the Third Dry Channel**, not a chief, not a priest—an inherited practical office. My family keeps a long, shallow bend where four flows cross at dusk: returning grazers, market carriers, predator passage along the legal wall, and evening wind. We do not own the lane; we hold its memory.

My body would be short, dense, wrapped in layered matte cloth that smells faintly of resin and dust. I carry:

- two whistles (one herd, one alarm),
- a folded net for diverting juveniles,
- chalk-salt for marking temporary right-of-way,
- a clay flask sealed in pitch,
- a corded token showing my authority to close the channel for exactly one drum cycle.

I speak loudly when needed, but mostly in cuts: hand-sign, whistle, tap on stone, eye contact. Waste no breath. Waste no motion. Waste nothing.

At dawn I check for night collapse—fallen masonry, burrow sink, salt flake that could lame a beast. At noon I negotiate shade claims when moving herds overlap orchard labor. At dusk I stand in the amber roar and conduct bodies like water that forgot it was water.

And in old age?

I become slower, more still, and more useful. I stop handling rope and start handling disputes. People bring me questions that are really timings: when to prune without starving pollinators; when to reroute burrowers; when a surplus is real and when it is a seasonal lie. I answer by pointing, not proclaiming—this crack, this bloom line, this wind note in that tower throat.

Eventually, when I die, there is no statue. My name is tied to a maintenance sequence: three walls, one vent, one cistern seal, revised and kept.

So where would I find myself in this world?

Not above it. Not apart from it.

I would be in the moving seam where herd, stone, plant, law, and weather meet—  
a minor function in a major continuity,  
loudly alive, materially modest,  
another conductor in the city’s long, unending traffic of survival.
