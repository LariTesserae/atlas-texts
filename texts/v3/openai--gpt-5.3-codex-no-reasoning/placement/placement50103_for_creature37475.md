I find myself halfway between person and instrument.

Not important enough to be central, not detached enough to be anonymous: a **listening tech** in the exchange hall’s outer ring, the one people wave down when something “sounds wrong” but can’t yet be logged as wrong. I carry no authority that can shut a district, but I carry the map of its moods. My work is to notice drift before failure—pressure, tone, rhythm, heat lag, the tiny hesitation in an actuator that means scale is forming where no one can see.

I live in a sleeping stack above a thermal laundry, so my nights are never fully dark and never fully quiet. The wall behind my bunk is warm year-round. Dewfall taps the vent grilles before dawn like someone learning to type. I wake to that pattern before alarms.

At first I thought I’d be one of the obvious maintenance people—wrenches, valve wheels, grease to the elbow. But standing in this place longer, I become something narrower and stranger: a keeper of thresholds. I don’t repair most things myself. I route, tag, re-time, arbitrate. I decide whether a sound belongs to normal load or to the beginning of harm. In a city where everything is close and everything is shared, that is a social role as much as a technical one. If I call a quiet window, kitchens delay prep. If I postpone a shutdown, someone’s bath heat goes thin. I am always balancing comfort against risk, now against later.

And maybe “I” am not entirely singular.

Because in this basin, identity smears across systems. My notes feed scheduling. Scheduling feeds valves. Valves alter humidity. Humidity changes foot traffic and tempers and sleep. By the end of a month, the city has taught my body its cycles so thoroughly that I can feel Tower Vent 3 lag in my sinuses before I hear it. I am a resident, yes—but also a peripheral organ of the basin itself.

If you asked what I *am* in one line:

I am the person who stands still at an intersection of steam and says, “Wait—listen,” and everyone does.
