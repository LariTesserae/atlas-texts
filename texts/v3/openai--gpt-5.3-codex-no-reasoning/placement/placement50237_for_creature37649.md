I think I would find myself in the **listening tier**—not at the grand wheels, not in the command towers, but in the narrow in-between galleries where the whole country can be heard through walls.

And I would not be a warden.  
I would be a **Fault Cantor**—one of the people who walk the ducts at shift-change and “read” the system by ear, touch, and condensation pattern, then translate that into action before failure becomes event.

At first that sounds like just another technician. But here, that role would have become something older, stranger, almost liturgical: part mechanic, part archivist, part omensmith. In a place where silence is catastrophe, the Cantors are custodians of the acceptable kinds of noise.

---

I imagine my body shaped by that work:

- one shoulder slightly lower from leaning into vibrating braces,  
- fingertips flattened and insensitive to heat but hypersensitive to tremor,  
- a permanent cough from lamp smoke and lime dust,  
- and the habit—impossible to turn off—of counting intervals between drips, clacks, and drum relays.

I’d wear a wax-dark hood with stitched-in sounding tabs (little stiff flanges that catch reflected noise), a belt of chalk, pegs, and clay whistles for pressure testing, and a slate board wrapped in oiled cloth where I mark pulse-patterns in shorthand only my crew fully reads.

Not elite. Not decorative. Necessary in an unglamorous, daily way.

---

Where exactly would you find me?

In a **service throat** above second-rank flow, knees braced on a beam polished by generations, cheek against cold brick, listening to a chain-run three terraces away and deciding whether that missing beat is a swollen bucket eyelet or early axle ovaling.

Then moving fast, because diagnosis is only respected here if it prevents stoppage.

---

And socially, I think I would belong to a house that people half-respect, half-avoid:  
the ones who bring bad news early.

We’d be the crew called when others say, “It still turns, leave it,” and we answer, “Yes, and that is why we can still save it.”

Children in our line would learn call-and-response rhythms before they learn formal names. We would keep tablets of old failures—not heroic chronicles, but annotated mistakes. Our pride would be in *non-events*: collapses that didn’t happen, contaminations that never reached drink-rank, freezes that passed with flow intact.

---

If this world asked me “who are you?” I don’t think I’d answer with a personal name first.

I’d answer with function and section:

**“Third Listening Gallery, West Condense Run, Night Drum Interval.”**

Because in that place, identity is load-bearing.  
And I would be one of the thin human parts that lets the giant handmade watershed hear itself in time.
