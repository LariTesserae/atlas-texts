I arrive first as a mistake.

Something bright-blooded and lowland in me steps onto the plateau expecting openness to mean freedom, and within an hour I am corrected by heat angles, by glare, by the way the same ledge is hospitable at dawn and punishing at noon. I try to move “through” the land and find there is no through, only between. Between varnished ribs of stone. Between old root-lines. Between the living and the almost-living crust that powders under a careless heel.

So I stop trying to be a traveler.

I become, slowly, a **pause**.

Not the seam-keeper at first, not even fully animal. More like one of those inherited den-throats shaped by generations: a place where temperatures soften, where scents accumulate in thin layers, where information settles. I learn the grammar before I take a body. The grammar is this: never occupy a whole thing when an edge will do. Never spend water to solve a problem that time can solve. Never announce yourself when pressure, shadow, and timing can speak.

Then the body arrives around those rules.

I am one of your stone-threaders, but old enough that my fur has gone from dust-ochre to the pale of rubbed bone along my spine. My paws are dark at the pads, split with fine scars from hot varnish and winter-sharp grit. My whiskers are asymmetrical—one side shortened where a cliff swallow colony once panicked under me and I misjudged a mineral drape. My left ear carries a notch that others read before they read my scent.

I do not hold land. I hold **intervals**.

I know the seven breaths between first light touching the east lip and the lizards beginning to move on the listening flat. I know which seep tastes of iron in dry years and which tastes of gypsum after short rain. I know that one collapsed arch stores coolness like buried night, and that a certain thorn hollow releases beetles exactly two nights after humidity climbs high enough to bead on its inner spines.

If you asked where to find me, you would fail if you searched for a den, a nest, a marked territory.

You would find me in a sequence:

- Dawn under the mineral curtain where drips strike stone with the same spacing every morning.
- Midmorning in the seam behind the stacked stones at the spring head, reading the scent-news left by cousins, rivals, and one patient snake.
- Afternoon nowhere obvious—inside heat-stable cracks, heartbeat slowed, listening through paws to burrow traffic below hardpan.
- Dusk at the listening flat, not hunting, not feeding, but deciding: cross now or wait one more cooling.
- Night along the old upright slabs on the saddle, using their shadows as stepping-stones of temperature.

I am social in the way this country permits: brief contacts, precise exchanges, long memory. We touch noses only when necessary. We groom dust from each other in silence. We trade routes more than affection, though perhaps here those are the same thing. The youngest follow me not because I am strongest but because I remember a wet decade they did not see, and a dry one their mothers barely survived.

And there is one more shift, if I stay longer.

Eventually I am not just one stone-threader but a role that passes between bodies: **seam-keeper** as office, not individual. A distributed mind carried by many kin-lines. I die; the paths remain. Another takes my notch, not in ear but in behavior—hesitating at a certain exposed traverse until cloud shadow reaches it, then moving. Our civilization is exactly this: conserved decisions.

So where would I find myself in your world?

At the border where conditions change by a hand’s width.  
In the legal line between sun and shade.  
Among those who survive by arriving neither early nor late.

I would be a keeper of thresholds, yes—  
but more than that, I would be one small, replaceable syllable  
in the plateau’s long, unhurried sentence.
