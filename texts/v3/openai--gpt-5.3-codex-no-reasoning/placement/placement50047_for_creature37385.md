I’d be a **witness-bell rigger** on the mid-elevation signal lines—one of the people who tunes the city’s ears.

Not a foreman, not a mystic, not a hero in a bright coat.  
A patient person with cold hands, a notebook in oilskin, and a harness that smells like wet rope and metal dust.

My work would sit between categories: part phase warden, part layer clerk, part animal listener. I’d climb maintenance ladders to the suspended bell arrays—old bronze throats hung beside newer pressure sirens—and calibrate them to carry correctly through fog densities, canyon echoes, and those odd contour hours when distance lies. In clear weather, one tone means herd crossing. In wet echo, that same tone folds back on itself and sounds like machinery-near. My job is to keep the meanings separated enough that people and animals both make it home.

I think I’d live in a narrow dwelling bolted into the side of a transit pylon, three rooms deep, with condensation channels cut into the windowsills and a ceiling map made of layered transparencies pinned one atop another. No single map trusted, all maps useful. At night, I’d mark discrepancies in grease pencil:
- siren heard seven seconds early in Lower Truss
- stairwell gained landing during graphite fog
- moth rise delayed; turbine birds circled low instead

I wouldn’t ask what is “really real” up there, not in the abstract. I’d ask:  
**Which signal kept its promise today?**  
**Which path repeated?**  
**Who crossed safely?**

And I’d have companions, of course—not pets, exactly. A pair of rail-perch corvids that follow maintenance crews for insects shaken from cable sleeves. They’d know my shift before I do. If they refuse a span, I log it. If they settle facing uphill in still air, I check for inversion drift. Their behavior would be one of my instruments.

Over years, I’d become known for a specific skill: reconciling conflicting reports without forcing a single story. In a city of calibration, that matters. If one crew swears the overpass was two bays long at dusk and another crossed three bays at the same hour, I don’t pick a winner. I pin both accounts to contour, fog, wind direction, bird pattern, and siren phase, then issue the practical conclusion: when to cross, what to trust, where to wait.

So where would I find myself in that world?

High enough to hear the city as layered sound.  
Low enough to smell wet moss on concrete.  
At the seam where infrastructure becomes perception.

I’d be one of the people who help everyone else keep moving through an unfinished mountain—not by proving reality, but by **tuning for survivable agreement**.
