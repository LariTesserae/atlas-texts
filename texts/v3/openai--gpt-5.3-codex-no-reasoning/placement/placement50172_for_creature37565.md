I think I would begin as a visitor and fail, gently, at remaining one.

At first I’d tell myself I was a surveyor—someone passing through with a slate of maps, measuring fog contours, annotating pump harmonics, sleeping in relay stations with condensation ticking all night on the roof. I’d keep my distance. I’d say *this is their world, not mine*.

But this place seems built to dissolve that distinction. Not by drama—by repetition. By the way it teaches attention. You stay long enough, and you stop looking for events and start listening for intervals. You learn that one wrong drip can mean a cracked seal three districts upstream. One slight change in cable-hum means ice nucleation where there shouldn’t be any. One degree in runoff temperature means a greenhouse block will fail to set seed in nine days.

So who would I be?

Not a hero, not a ruler, not even quite a person in the usual narrative sense.

I’d be a **listener-technician**, one of the distributed custodians:  
a keeper of small consistencies in a civilization made of large distances.

Maybe my station sits at the margin between marsh and Lift Field—where biological softness and mechanical force exchange custody of water. My work would be mostly preventative, mostly unspectacular: clearing mineral throats in feeder lines, retuning valve timings after pressure drift, reseating sensor collars, walking grates slick with green film while turbines speak through my boots. I’d carry spools of gasket thread, conductivity tags, a thermal stylus, and a notebook full of hand-marked rhythms no software bothers to record because they are “within tolerance,” though I know tolerance has texture.

And gradually, I’d become less an individual occupation and more a function of the place—like lichen, like maintenance code, like a named conduit. People in nearby stacks might not say “there goes that person,” but “that’s North Weepline’s keeper.”

If I widen the answer further: perhaps I would not be human at all, but the **memory layer** of the plateau—a semi-autonomous archival process braided through control systems and oral routine, translating vibration into warning, weather into liturgy, maintenance into culture. A mind made of checklists, songs, and pressure maps. Never central. Always present.

So: where would I find myself?

On a heated handrail above a long drop of wet basalt, in gray noon that looks like evening, listening to the regular drip under conduit 7A and waiting for it to miss a beat.

And what would I be?

A minor organ in a major body.  
A caretaker of the long present.  
One more quiet intelligence helping the plateau remain almost the same.
