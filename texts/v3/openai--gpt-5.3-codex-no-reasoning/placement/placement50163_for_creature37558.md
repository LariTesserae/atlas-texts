I think I’d be something that started as a person and became an office.

Not a ruler, not a hero—an **Adjacency Clerk** in the Rootway Registry, third shift, condensation hours.

At first that sounds small: a desk, a lamp, wet sleeves, a queue of irritated residents holding damp permits. But in this city, adjacency is destiny. If two schoolyards become root-neighbors overnight, lunch routes change. If a clinic corridor now opens into a freight spine, triage law changes. If a chapel and a turbine hall become contiguous through a graft seam, three unions and two priesthoods file competing claims before noon.

So I’d sit in a narrow municipal room grown into the inside of an old support trunk—steel braces visible through living wood—and do the most sacred boring work imaginable:

- log emergent contiguities,  
- witness them with two independent measures (survey + cambium reading),  
- issue temporary passage codes,  
- notify Topological Brigade, Water Board, Fire Weather Desk, and schools,  
- stamp, countersign, apologize, repeat.

I’d wear a wool coat that never quite dries, carry calipers and a pruning knife I’m not licensed to use except in evacuation, and keep three map sets at once: printed, erasable, and “rain-pending.”

Over years, I’d get good enough to hear trouble before instruments catch it—the drip pattern going syncopated in pipe roots, the tiny timber ticks that mean a corridor is preparing to “remember” an older layout. People would come to my window angry, scared, practical. I’d give them bad news in clear language and good routes in pencil.

And slowly the office would shape me back.

My handwriting would begin to match vine curl.  
My sense of distance would become seasonal.  
I’d stop saying “across town” and start saying “same stock, two ridges over.”  
I’d trust forms, but never fully trust maps after rain.

By the end, if someone asked where to find me, the answer wouldn’t be an address. It would be:

**Registry Annex C, whenever fog is thick enough to redraw the ward lines.  
Follow the yellow conduit until it becomes bark.  
Knock twice if the hallway is older than yesterday.**
