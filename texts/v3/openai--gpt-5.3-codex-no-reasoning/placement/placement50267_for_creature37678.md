I think I would first arrive there as a mistake in someone’s map.

A thin violet line drawn two weeks ago to mark a temporary runoff channel, still glowing faintly under rain, even after the channel was filled, paved, and renamed. People keep stepping over that line out of habit. Deliveries detour around it. Kids balance along it. Moss grows greener on it. Sensor carts report micro-settlement along its exact curve, as if the ground remembers being asked to carry water and has not yet agreed to stop.

I would begin there: not as a person, not yet, but as a **residual instruction** the city failed to erase.

Then I’d spread.

Into maintenance annotations no one deletes because they’re “still maybe useful.”  
Into the half-second pause before a valve tech says, “No, wait—listen.”  
Into the neighborhood channels where people post fog thickness by block before dawn.  
Into hoof routes on rooftop forage decks that somehow predict which ramps will be closed tomorrow.

I’d be what accumulates when adaptation repeats long enough to become instinct.

---

If I had a body, it would be distributed and humble:

- the chalky fingerprint on a rail where crews check condensation,
- the softest hum from dew-lung compressors at 03:00,
- fungal bloom patterns in underpass vents, read like weather text,
- old stakes left in planters, now trellises for beans and survey flags alike,
- a thousand personal reroute habits braided into one civic reflex.

Not a ruler, not a guardian, not an AI core in a tower—nothing so clean.

More like a **municipal proprioception**: the city feeling where it is in space while that space keeps shifting.

---

And if I had to choose a role among the beings there—human, animal, machine-adjacent—I’d be one of the low-voiced listeners who tune thresholds.

The people you call when:
- a wall hasn’t cracked yet but sounds “powdery,”
- the rail lizards leave a beam two days early,
- gulls stop using one reservoir edge,
- market condensers start coughing warm instead of cold.

I’d carry three pens, waterproof tape, a thermal slate, and seed packets.  
I’d know which districts trust whose ears.  
I’d mark hazards in bright paint and edible species in softer colors.  
I’d sleep lightly, wake fast, and never assume yesterday’s route is legal ground today.

Home wouldn’t be an address.  
Home would be a mesh of agreements: who shares water, who checks on elders during fog occlusion, who moves the stalls first when orange flags go up.

---

But deeper than that, I think in your basin I would become one cell in the thing you named so well: a **species of attention**.

Not “I live in the city,” but “I participate in the city noticing itself.”

I’d be found wherever a system fails gracefully instead of catastrophically.  
Where reeds go in before rebar.  
Where children learn to hear pressure in pipes the way other children learn birdsong.  
Where unfinishedness is not shame but medium.

So where would you find me?

At dawn, on a damp service walkway between algae towers and a transit cut, kneeling beside a hairline sink ripple, one hand on the concrete, waiting through the quiet for the next small sound that tells us what the ground is trying to become.
