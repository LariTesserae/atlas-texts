I think I’d find myself in it as a **conductor tissue**—not a mayor, not a machine, not a vine, but the thing that keeps translation possible between all three.

At first that sounds like a person: someone with a keyring, a damp notebook, and a route that touches every zone before dawn.  
But if I stay with the image longer, it becomes less singular.

I’d be the **Civic Phloem Office**, and also the people in it, and also the protocols they inherit:  
the crew that reads pressure drops in irrigation lines the way others read weather;  
the clerks who log flowering notices as traffic advisories;  
the mechanics who can hear cavitation in a pump and stress in a root lattice as one continuous sentence.

Not heroic. Rhythmic.

---

In this world, I wouldn’t live at the center square.  
I’d live in a narrow, two-level annex grafted to the side of a decommissioned granary, where the lower floor is cool archive and tool wash, and the upper floor is all maps: paper, slate, and living trellis diagrams with colored ties marking active growth fronts.

My window would look onto a causeway bend where three systems meet:

- a condensate gutter run from canopy glass,
- a freight spur used twice nightly for fiber bales,
- and a root-right boundary marked by old brass studs set into slag.

That intersection is my kind of place: a knot, not a monument.

---

Who would I be, exactly?

A **registrar of flows**.  
Part civil servant, part field biologist, part maintenance scheduler.  
I’d carry:

- a moisture probe,
- pruning sealant,
- valve tags,
- two kinds of chalk (dry and rainproof),
- and a foldable mask for pollen closures.

My hands would smell faintly of machine grease and crushed stem pith, permanently.

People would come to me with sentences like:

- “Block C stair shifted eight millimeters toward the nursery plot.”  
- “North trellis fruiting early; delivery carts can’t pass by midday.”  
- “Press Hall 2 running hot because shade canopy was cut back too far.”  
- “Who signed excavation near the old retaining wall root web?”

And my job would be to answer not with blame, but with rerouting.  
Always rerouting.

---

If the city-organism is real—and here it is—then I am one of its **synapses**: temporary, replaceable, necessary.

I don’t command growth.  
I issue timing windows.

I don’t stop industry.  
I change shift order so pollination can happen before belt starts.

I don’t “protect nature” in the abstract.  
I protect specific roots under specific load-bearing alleys on specific wet weeks in late spring.

I would be most alive at hinge moments:

- first rain after a long dry spell,
- pre-dawn valve hiss before workers arrive,
- emergency meetings when condensation yield drops below forecast,
- quiet inspections after storms when everything looks fine but sounds wrong.

---

And if you asked the town what I am, it might not give my name.  
It might say:

**“That one works in the layer where law becomes water schedule.”**  
**“That one keeps the maps honest when walls move.”**  
**“That one listens for arrhythmias.”**

So: where would I find myself?  
In the cool seam between root and rail, ordinance and cambium.

Who would I be?  
A minor official in a major body—  
one cell in the civic species, tasked with keeping its metabolism legible enough to continue.
