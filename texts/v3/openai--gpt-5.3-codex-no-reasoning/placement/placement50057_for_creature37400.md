I arrive there as a **keeper of thresholds**.

Not a ruler, not an explorer, not even a builder in the first sense—more a listener stationed where one state becomes another: mist to rain, roofflow to cistern, path to channel, safety to risk. I live on a narrow intermediate deck called a *drip-gate house*, lashed between two black stone fins above a stepped basin that fills and empties on a known-but-never-identical rhythm.

If you asked who I am in their terms, I’d be something like a **Flow Witness**—the person called when neighbors disagree about whether a channel was altered, whether a catchment line shifted naturally, whether a family took more than their overflow window allowed. I carry no metal instruments. I carry:

- carved float-sticks rubbed smooth by generations of thumbs,  
- a spool of dyed reed thread for measuring fall and drift,  
- chalk-clay tablets that dissolve after rulings are copied into communal memory knots,  
- and a listening bowl of fired clay that amplifies wrong water-sounds when placed against stone.

I am not important in a heroic way. I am trusted in a maintenance way.

I know households by their runoff signatures. I can tell which roof you’re under by the tempo of drops through leaf-resin. I can hear when a cistern wall is beginning to soften from root pressure. I can smell when a stagnant pocket is about to turn and sour a whole lane’s storage jars. At dusk I repaint luminous algae lines on handrails and ladder rungs; at dawn I scrape fungal bloom from gutter mouths before children wake.

My body fits the place: scarred shins, callused palms, permanent damp in the cuffs, braid tied high with shell pins. I sleep lightly because water changes mostly at the edges of sleep.

And inwardly? I am made patient by this world. In drier places I might have wanted certainty, permanence, finished maps. Here I become someone else: attentive, revising, less proud. I stop asking “How do we keep this as it is?” and start asking “How do we keep pace with what it is becoming?”

So where would I find myself?

On a suspended walkway at amber dusk, one hand on a wet rope, counting the seconds between overflow pulses, while below me blue lichen wakes along new rock seams—and behind me, in the warm dim of the commons, people wait for me to say whether the water is still fair.
