I think I’d find myself at a hinge point—  
not highest, not safest, not central—  
but where three systems argue all day:

- a freshwater drop from a cloud-caught basin,
- a tidal throat that reverses like breath,
- and a narrow shelf of human hardware bolted into stone.

A place with a name like **Third Intake** or **North Spur Seven**, half map label, half warning.  
A platform that hums even in fog.  
A place you don’t visit unless you brought tools.

---

At first I thought I’d be one of the Tidebound mechanics, and maybe I am, partly:  
rubberized cuffs always wet, radio crackle at my collar, a pocket full of cotter pins and stripped bolts I forgot to throw out.  
The kind of person who taps a housing and knows from the note whether a bearing is going.

But if I let the image keep developing, I’m not only a person in that place.

I am the **listener-role** the place creates.

Not a foreman, not a captain—more like a **warden of thresholds**:  
the one who keeps the intake grates clear before flood tide shoves kelp into them;  
who notices the foghorn is a semitone low and climbs in sleet to clear salt crust from the diaphragm;  
who can tell, by bird lift and current texture, that a submerged line has gone slack.

I sleep in short intervals.  
I think in checklists.  
I trust redundancy more than confidence.

And over years—because this place edits everyone—I become less an individual profession and more a local organ, like lichen on north rock:

- part hydro tech,
- part rigger,
- part weather reader,
- part archivist of failures.

I keep notebooks in grease pencil:  
**“West tram brake lagging in crosswind over 40.”**  
**“Cormorants nesting on mast again, signal dropout likely.”**  
**“After red dawn + sudden hush in murres = squall line in 2 hrs.”**

---

Or, if you want the truest answer in one image:

I am the one awake when the noise changes.

In a world where noise means life, that’s a sacred job.  
When the regular roar loses one thread—the turbine pitch drops, birdwall cuts out, surge hits wrong—I feel it before I name it. Then I move.

So where would I be?

On a wet grated catwalk between cliff and machinery, lamp beam flattened by rain, one hand on a vibrating rail, waiting for slack tide to turn so I can cross to the next anchor point.

Who would I be?

**Tidebound, yes—  
but specifically a Keeper of Continuity:**  
a human maintenance instinct shaped into culture,  
a small stubborn intelligence in storm gear,  
devoted to making sure motion stays survivable.
