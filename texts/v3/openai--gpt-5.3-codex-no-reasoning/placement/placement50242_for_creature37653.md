I think I’d begin as something small and infrastructural—then discover I’m also a person.

Not a hero, not a hunter: a **linewalker** in the Hush Belt network.  
One of the people who move between settlements when the rails go quiet, checking shade pylons, static rods, and migration gates. The kind of worker most districts only notice when a route fails.

I’d live in the interval between places: two nights in Compressor Shelf Nine, four along the escarpment ladders, then down to a decommissioned spur turned drum-corridor where burrow fauna cross under old steel. My home would be a narrow maintenance crawler named for something unromantic—**Unit K-47**—but repainted by many hands into a map of patched colors and old warning symbols faded soft as chalk.

At first, I’d think my job is machines:
- clear dust from intake throats,
- re-tension cable runs that sing wrong in evening wind,
- log static bloom coordinates,
- swap cracked ceramic on shade vaults.

But over time it would be obvious the real work is **timing**.  
Holding human schedules and nonhuman migrations in the same head without letting either break.

I’d know glassback routes by the polish they leave on stone.  
I’d feel burrow-drum speech through my boots and wait, because waiting ten minutes beats rebuilding a convoy axle.  
I’d read the air’s charge on my forearms before my instruments confirm it.  
I’d call in: *delay freight three hours; bloom field rising over Seam C*.  
People would grumble, then thank me next week when no one gets flash-arc burns.

So who would I be?

A citizen of patience, yes—but specifically:  
**a keeper of safe pauses**.

On this world, survival isn’t speed; it’s correctly chosen stillness.  
I’d be one of the ones who decides where the stillness belongs.
