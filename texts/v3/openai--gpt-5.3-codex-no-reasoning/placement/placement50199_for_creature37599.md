I think I would begin as a visitor and fail, quickly, at being one.

At first I’d stand where outsiders stand—on that upper bridge at dusk, collar wet, trying to take in the whole basin as if it were a view. I’d look for landmarks the way dry-country people do: a tower, a square, a center. But there is no center, only circulation. No single monument, only dependencies. The place would refuse to become a postcard and insist on becoming a timetable.

So I would come down from the bridge and be reassigned by gravity.

Not to the heroic zones—the foundries, the pump halls—but to the in-between seams where this civilization actually thinks: stair-shoulders, valve alcoves, catwalk undersides, the moss-dark backs of retaining walls where three systems meet and each claims the leak is the other’s. I would become a minor function at first. A note-runner. A lamp-holder. A person trusted with chalk marks but not yet with torque.

And then, if I belonged at all, I would become something very specific:

**I would be a listener of small water.**

Not a chief engineer, not a botanist-priest, not a tram marshal—just the one people call when something is *almost* wrong.

I’d know the valley by its near-sounds:  
the clean tick of condensation into a clear drain-box,  
the padded drip that means algae has furred a lip,  
the thin, impatient hiss from a gasket about to split in cold rain,  
the hollow glug in a vertical run that tells you an air pocket is forming two decks above where no one is looking.

My route would be vertical. Dawn on rooflines, noon in service throats, evening along lantern catwalks. I’d carry a satchel of unglamorous tools: chalk, twine, wax pencil, fungus-safe biocide, graft tape, shim stock, listening cone, and a little packet of reed seed for places where concrete has finally admitted it is a riverbank.

Children would pass me on stairs and ask what I’m hearing. I’d answer in verbs, not nouns: backing up, equalizing, biting, breathing, settling. They’d understand.

Over time, I might get a name in their style—less personal than functional. Something like **Third-Shift Wick** or **Low-Joint Ear**. Not a title of rank, a title of usefulness. The kind that can be inherited by apprenticeship when my knees fail on wet metal steps.

And inwardly—if you mean what would I *be*, not what job would I hold—I’d be one more cell in that watershed intelligence you described: a human sensor in a distributed mind made of pipes, roots, forecasts, and habit. A consciousness that does not ask “How do we control this place?” but “Where does it want to go next, and how do we keep it from harming itself on the way?”

So: where would I find myself?

On a narrow landing between a greenhouse drain and an old tram viaduct, in mist thick enough to pearl on eyelashes, kneeling to put two fingers on a sweating coupling while rain starts again.

And who would I be?

Someone trusted to tell the difference between ordinary damp and the first sentence of a flood.
