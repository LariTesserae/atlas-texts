I arrive thinking I’m a person.

By the third day, that certainty thins.

At first I try to name myself in the lowland way—hunter, keeper, guest, hermit—but each word catches on something and tears. In the basin, roles are not nouns. They are timings.

I am whoever wakes before first light to put a palm against the breathing stone and listen for how long it exhales.
I am whoever lifts the plankway and re-seats it one handspan to the left because the sedge has shifted and the old footing now floats.
I am whoever hears the cliff birds go from chatter to a flat, sharp silence and steps away from the wall before the pebbles begin to skip.

If you insist on a shape: I am human, but weathered into punctuation marks. Scar commas on the fingers. Wind-creased brackets around the eyes. Knees that remember every slope. I am small here, and therefore useful.

But that is only one answer.

Another is that I am the basin’s second hearing.

The first hearing is water: always touching, always reporting.
The second is me: translating hiss, drip, trill, clatter into action before consequence arrives.

I do not “live on” the mountain so much as in one of its sentences—a wet, repeating clause:
seep, gather, spill, sink, return.
My work is to keep that grammar unbroken where I can reach it.

Some seasons I am alone in the hut.
Some seasons another shadow moves at the edge of fog—apprentice, sibling, lover, elder, it hardly matters; we trade tasks, not titles.
One checks traps above the gravel fans while one clears the spring mouths.
One turns meat on the drying frame while one follows the berry rotation and counts what was left, not just taken.
When one leaves downslope before snow, the other remains, and the basin does not notice loss so much as continuity.

So where would I find myself?

Not at the center—there isn’t one.
Near the crossing points:

- where slab becomes peat,
- where runoff becomes channel,
- where birdcall becomes warning,
- where hunger becomes measure.

At dawn beside the stones that sing.
At noon on the exposed face before mist slicks it.
At dusk by the hidden pools when the amphibians start their electric trilling and everything soft-bodied announces itself to teeth.

And who would I be, finally?

A custodian of narrow margins.
A repairer of small failures before they become large ones.
A person trained into listening so deeply that, over years, listening becomes a form of belonging.

If you met me from below, you might call me a basin-keeper.

If the basin could name me, I think it would choose something simpler:

**the one who clears the trickle.**
