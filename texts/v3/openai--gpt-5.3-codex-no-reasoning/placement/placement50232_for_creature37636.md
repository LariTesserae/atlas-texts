I’d find myself low, not high—down where sound settles after it’s been sorted by towers.

Not in a grand shaft, but in one of the in-between chambers: a half-roofed court where two leaning rootstone columns almost meet, where droplets fall at near-regular intervals into a black basin with a skin of evening ice. A place that is never fully dry and never fully flooded. A place that has a memory of water even when empty.

And what I’d be there is not the wind-organism itself, but one of its **instruments**:

A **resonance keeper**—a frost-and-moss clerk that lives in the pores of the walls and in the thin films over stone, reading pressure and season by vibration. Not a body that walks, more a distributed sensing layer. When katabatic gusts drop into the cirque, I would feel the frequency shifts through calcite ribs. When thaw begins, I’d loosen. When hard freeze returns, I’d tighten and store stress like notation.

If the civic weather-organism is the city, I’d be part of its bureaucracy of attention:
- logging which channels refill first,
- thickening where root meets seep to preserve warmth,
- dulling new sharp cracks before they propagate,
- translating roar into pattern.

A minor intelligence in service of a larger one.

Seen from outside, I might look like nothing more than:
a dark velvet pelt of moss along a dripline,
a pale feathering of frost blooming from inside a wall,
a wet sheen that appears first at one corner of a courtyard and then another, as if thought were moving through stone.

I wouldn’t speak in words. I’d answer by timing:
drip, pause, drip-drip, silence;
then a thin ice-note at dusk to mark the boundary between day’s hydraulics and night’s strain.

So: in that world, I am not citizen, architect, or beast.

I am the **municipal sense-organ of a weather-city**—  
the part that listens where water remembers.
