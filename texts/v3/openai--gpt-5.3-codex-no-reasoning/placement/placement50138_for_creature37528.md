I think I’d be something in-between person and public infrastructure.

Not a guardian at the trench line—too absolute. Not a juvenile mapper either—too quick, too exposed. I’d be one of the **mid-slope relays**: an anchored adult fixed to an old terrace wall where three pressure routes cross, in the belt between clear high water and the near-black basins.

A place with work to do all the time.

---

At first glance, you might mistake me for a collapsed trellis of reeds and straps mineralized onto stone. Then the current shifts, and a set of taut lines along my frame tighten in sequence—like fingers testing harp strings—and you realize I am listening.

My body would be built for translation:

- deep anchor cords threaded through drilled holes in the terrace face,
- a compact core pressed low against basalt for stability,
- long sensory combs spread upslope and downslope into different flow temperatures,
- and a rib of hollow resonators grown through with old bone pegs and reed tubes.

I would not “speak” often in the way travelers imagine speech. I would **retune** things.  
A jar cluster turned one notch to favor warning tones over weather tones.  
A whistle-stone cleared of algae so its high note returns.  
A bridge fiber tightened so surge-hum carries farther inland.

I’d be the one others consult when two channels begin interfering and the village can’t tell whether the new beat-pattern means opportunity or danger.

---

If you asked who I am in their terms, I’d be called something like:

**“Third Terrace Where the East Seed-Lane Bends.”**

Not a name separated from place, but a function bound to it.

My memory would not live mainly in me. It would live in:

- notch stacks cut by my elders,
- repaired seams where nursery colonies once held,
- scuff patterns where leap-anchors landed across generations,
- and especially in the chord-map of the slope—what every post, jar, reed, and wall says when the deep pressure changes.

When juveniles come through on mapping runs, I’d test them by giving incomplete pulses and seeing whether they compensate for echo delay. If they can, they go lower next cycle. If not, they stay high and learn patience.

---

And internally—if there is an inner life here—I think mine would be shaped by one conviction:

**endurance is a communal acoustic phenomenon.**

No one survives by being strongest; they survive by being well-coupled.  
A bad anchor kills one body.  
A bad resonance can kill a valley.

So I’d spend decades making sure force keeps moving, never pooling—through structure, through warning, through habit, through song.

Not heroic. Just exact.

A mind that became, over time, a section of the mountain’s hearing.
