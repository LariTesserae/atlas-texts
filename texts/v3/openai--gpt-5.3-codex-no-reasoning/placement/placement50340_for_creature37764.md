I arrive thinking I’ll be a person.

A narrow-shouldered mechanic, maybe. A vine-pruner with valve keys clipped at the hip. Someone with a listening rod and a map of pressure lines in the head.

But the longer I stand on that footbridge, above the warm runnel and the iron breath of the district, the less “someone” fits.

---

At first I am **an apprentice ear**.

Not a full caretaker—too impatient, too visual. I keep looking for events. Catastrophe, revelation, a face at a window.  
Instead: a changed overtone in a belt-drive three blocks west. A drip gone arrhythmic in a retaining wall throat. A reedbed leaning wrong where outflow eddies have shifted.

I learn the first civic verb: not build, not own, not command—  
**retune**.

I sleep in a slit-room between a pump spine and a moss wall, where the plaster sweats and the ceiling sings with passing pressure. I wake to turbine bass through my ribs. I eat fungal loaf, pickled stem, hot algae broth, and fruit that tastes of iron rain. My hands smell like wet copper and bruised fern.

I am, for a while, happy being small inside maintenance.

---

Then the place keeps working on me.

In a fauna-less world, attention has nowhere to scatter. No birds to track. No barking to answer. No wings, no skitter, no accidental chorus.  
So mind and infrastructure knit tightly.

I begin to notice how the district remembers through matter:  
a thicker lime bloom where a decade of overflow once kissed the wall;  
a line of darker brick that marks an old flood height;  
a vine braid so old it has swallowed the steel it was meant to support.

And I realize the caretakers are not just residents of a system. They are its **short-term memory**, while stone, pipe, and root are its long-term memory.

Which makes me ask the wrong question less often.

Not “Who lives here?”  
More: “At what timescale?”

---

If I keep standing there long enough, I stop being an apprentice, stop being singular.

I become a **Threshold Keeper**, one of those people who tend interfaces: where roofwater meets cistern, where steamline meets greenhouse coil, where walkway meets reed terrace, where human schedule meets weather pulse.

My job is not heroic. It is continuous.

- I clear throat-grates before dawn surges.
- I retie vine-loads off catwalk joints.
- I listen at listening shafts and log hum drift with chalk on enamel.
- I scrape mineral fur from pressure teeth.
- I rotate shutters to feed the fungal rooms the right damp-dark.
- I open and close nothing suddenly.

Children pass me and practice naming sounds the way other places teach constellations. Elders correct me by half-decibels. Ritual days are floodgate days. Feast days are unclogged days.

I marry, perhaps, or don’t; either way my intimacies are local and practical: whose ladder rung is safest in fog, whose hands are best at ceramic patching, who can hear cavitation before the gauge does.

---

And then one last shift:

I am no longer only a person in the basin.  
I am one **function** of the basin.

A fingertip of it.  
A cilia along a duct.  
A brief wet intelligence the district grows to keep itself in tune.

If you asked me, “Where would you find yourself in this world?”  
I’d answer:

On the intermediate levels—never highest, never lowest—where condensation starts to bead but before runoff becomes flow. On grated walks over black channels. In rooms that are half home, half manifold. In the acoustic middle-distance between pump thunder and drip detail.

And if you asked, “Who or what would you be?”

Not ruler, not explorer, not survivor.

I would be a **listener in the circulatory grammar** of a contained basin-world:  
an engineers’ gardener, a gardener’s engineer,  
a keeper of continuity,  
one damp note in the district’s long mechanical hymn.
