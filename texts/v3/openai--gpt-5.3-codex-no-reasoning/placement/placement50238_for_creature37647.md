I think I’d be someone whose job only exists in a place like this:

A **Listener of Loads**—not quite Sounder, not quite Rootwright, but the person called when the district feels *almost* right.

I’d live halfway up the ring of the civic basin in a narrow dwelling with slatted floors and a warm pipe wall, where condensation gathers in ceramic cups overnight. My door would be marked not by a nameplate but by three mineral lines at knee height—the old flood marks I’ve chosen not to scrub off. Inside: hanging maps sealed in wax, each one out of date at the edges; bundles of colored ties; a rack of tuning forks cut to pump frequencies; a moss tray for humidity calibration; two knives, both for plant work.

I wouldn’t be high status, exactly, but I’d be trusted in that quiet way this place reserves for people who arrive early and leave late and don’t make speeches.

When I move, I move with my palm on things—rail first, then wall, then vine braid—feeling for tremor before I look. I could tell you which fan is turning unevenly from two corridors away. I could tell you whether a root mass is drinking well by the lag between drip and runoff. I could tell you if heat is getting trapped behind a wall by the way metal tastes in the air.

My stewardship zone would be the seam between systems: where old freight architecture meets living load. Places everyone passes through and few people claim. Transition joints. Shared throats. Political places, in infrastructure terms.

I’d keep a ledger of nearly invisible changes:

- a handspan of new bulge in a fig-bound buttress,  
- a half-note drop in belt hum at second fan turn,  
- reed tilt in the central basin after a hard rain,  
- condensate warming by one degree along a mislabeled run.

Not emergencies—pre-emergencies.  
I’d be good at preventing stories.

And personally? I think I’d be one of those people this city makes: patient to the edge of stubbornness, affectionate but unsentimental, more fluent in cycles than in declarations. Someone who believes devotion is repetitive: clear the grate again, retie the vine again, check the bearing again, teach the child what “wrong quiet” sounds like again.

If I belong fully, I probably wasn’t born there. I likely arrived young from a drier, louder surface district and spent my first year frightened by the closeness, by the wet, by the never-dark. Then one shift during overflow, standing in the roaring dome while everyone moved with impossible calm, I understood the local faith:

Not in nature.  
Not in machinery.  
In **continuity**.

So where would I find myself?

On a narrow catwalk over the civic basin at settling period, one hand on a sweating rail, listening for whether the whole underleaf city is breathing in time.
