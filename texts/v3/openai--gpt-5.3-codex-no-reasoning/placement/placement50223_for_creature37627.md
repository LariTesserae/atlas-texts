I think I would be one of their **path-keepers**—but not the oldest, not the one everyone already trusts without question.  
The one in the middle years, still proving that I can remember *all* of it when it matters.

I’d live between presences: never fully settled, never absent long. Arrive with a coil of reed rope across my chest, two jars nested in a sling, a knife worn to half its first length. I’d sleep in whichever low shelter needs body heat and watchfulness that week. Before dawn, I’d put my palm on the entry stones and listen through bone more than ear: today’s wind in the north cut, today’s water under the shelf, whether the lower steps are singing too sharply (ice).

At first glance, I might seem like a gatherer, or a repairer, or just another carrier of loads.  
But my real work would be continuity:

- recutting groove marks when lichen swallows them,
- replacing storm-toppled posts before fog season,
- checking each dry-line path for new underwash,
- teaching children the route by hand, step-count, and echo, not by sight.

I’d be the person who can say, over the roar, with absolute plainness:  
“Not that way today.”  
And be obeyed, not because of rank, but because I am usually right.

I don’t think I’d be especially talkative. In that basin, words are expensive. I’d use touch and signal first: two taps on stone for stop, open palm for wait, knuckle-circle for safe footing. When speech is needed, I’d lean close, hood to hood, and give only what helps: distance, direction, hazard, timing.

If there is any private devotion in me, it would be this: **maintenance as love**.  
Not love as declaration—love as returning to the same failing wall and setting it right before someone else needs it. Love as emptying and drying a jar I did not fill. Love as retying a line in sleet because the next hands might be smaller, older, slower, or shaking.

And if you asked where, exactly, you’d find me in that world, it would be in the margins where abundance touches emptiness: at the lip of a seep, on a half-wet stair, beside a marker post in fog, listening to the basin roar and answering it with small, stubborn acts that keep people moving through.
