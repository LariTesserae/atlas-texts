I think I arrive there as someone *between* measures.

Not a chief planner, not a mystic exactly, not a builder with broad authority.  
A listener with tools. A keeper of tiny differences.

At first I imagine being a child on a causeway, barefoot despite the cold slick, sent out with a shallow clay cup to check the night’s seep at three marked stones. You dip, pour, dip again. No one asks for a number yet. They ask for comparison: *higher than wrist-mark, lower than thumb-mark, same cloud smell as yesterday.* In this world, precision begins before numerals; it starts as disciplined noticing.

Later, I become the adult that child was apprenticed to:

## A **drip-cartographer**
I map where water lingers after rain, roof by roof, lane by lane.  
Not grand canals—everyone sees those. I track the minor persistence: which wall keeps weeping past noon, which workshop shade causes fibers to mildew, which courtyard never fully dries in third-rain week. I maintain tablets of fired clay incised with micro-season patterns. People consult me before rethatching, before moving seed stores, before firing a kiln in fog-heavy months.

My work sounds humble. It governs half the town’s success.

I carry:
- a bundle of reed gauges cut to standard lengths,
- chalky stone for temporary marks on damp walls,
- waxed thread that darkens at known moisture thresholds,
- a set of shallow “listening bowls” to compare drip rates by sound.

In a place with no birdsong, a bowl filling at one drop every four breaths is data.

## Socially, I belong everywhere and nowhere
I’m invited into courtyards because I am useful, and tolerated because I am discreet. I see domestic arguments translated into infrastructure:

- one family says the neighbor is careless; I find their shared ditch lip has settled half a finger-width.
- one potter blames bad clay; I show that new awning angle traps damp air over the drying racks.
- one elder insists seasons are changing; I unroll thirty years of fired records and show: not change, but a slow ridge-shadow shift from a recent rockfall.

So I become, quietly, a mediator.  
Not of law—of causes.

## Ritual role
Over time, people ask me to speak at foglift observances. I don’t lead prayers. I read the basin’s minute state aloud:

“North benches releasing late.  
Eastern reed-beds ahead by five days.  
Upper seep clear, lower seep mineral-heavy.  
This year: mend before sowing.”

It is received like liturgy anyway.

## Inner life
Who am I there, inwardly?  
Someone who learns that care can be exact without being cold.  
That reverence can look like maintenance.  
That love of place can be written in ditch angles, kiln spacing, and how gently you set your heel on wet timber at dawn.

If I live long, I train two successors: one better with smell, one better with stone memory. When my hands shake, I move from field rounds to archive work, re-firing old tablets before they soften, annotating them with current marks, leaving a lineage of comparisons no flood can fully erase.

So where would I find myself in that world?

At the margin between wall and weather,  
between yesterday’s rain and tomorrow’s mold,  
kneeling beside a drip line, listening for whether the basin is still in tune—

and, if not, by how much.
