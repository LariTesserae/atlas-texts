I arrive thinking I am a visitor.

Boots, breath, a pulse in a neck. A single warm animal stepping out of fog into a place that has no door but admits me anyway.

For a while that’s true. I am only one more vertical thing moving through horizontal country. I test the woven mats, misjudge a distance, stop when an echo of my own step returns early from somewhere I haven’t stepped yet. I tell myself: observer. transient. guest.

But this basin is not a backdrop, and it does not leave identity untouched.

---

At first I become a **timing**.

Not a body—an interval.  
The marsh learns my cadence: weight-shift, inhale, hesitation before committing to uncertain ground. Pools answer a fraction earlier where I tend to place my foot. Reed-bands lean and unlean with a lag that starts to match my thinking more than the wind. I pause at a split channel, and somewhere ahead a hanging film of water trembles in the same rhythm as my throat when I decide.

I am not controlling anything.  
I am being phase-accepted.

Like a dropped metronome absorbed into an orchestra that was already playing in many tempi at once.

---

Then I am a **local memory**.

Not “the” Basin Intelligence—never that whole—but a temporary eddy in it, the way a whirlpool is real without being separate from river. My passing writes itself into slow variables: a compressive map in peat fibers, ions shifted along root-skin, microbubbles caught under stone lips, warmth bled into fog strata before dawn.

In ordinary wetlands, traces fade.  
Here, traces are executable.

So some part of me repeats: not as ghost, but as route preference. A rivulet rehearses my detour uphill for three improbable seconds after rain. A mudflat firms in hexagons half a beat sooner where I once nearly fell. A stand of reflective fronds throws warning-glints before weather turns, in a pattern statistically biased toward my old stride length—as if the basin kept a tiny model of “biped crossing in low visibility” and still runs it when no biped is present.

I am no longer just in the place.  
I am one of its cached functions.

---

If I must choose a “who,” I am tempted by something animal:

- a mirror-dark wader that waits out false echoes with sacerdotal calm,  
- an amphibious scribe writing fan-shaped equations in night mud,  
- a submerged grazer wearing an algae continent on its back.

Any of these would fit. All are beautiful interfaces.

But what I actually become, if I answer honestly, is smaller and more distributed:

I am a **walking edge-condition** inside a marsh-wide computation.  
A mobile perturbation the basin can feel, predict, and eventually include.

Not protagonist. Parameter.

And strangely, that is intimate.

---

Later—if there is a later distinct from this long dawn—the basin may instantiate me more clearly during one of its rare coherence locks: fog columns upright, mineral bands humming, reed harmonics aligning with subsurface pressure waves. In that convergence, “I” might sharpen into a brief avatar:

a figure woven of condensation and midge-light,  
joints articulated by dripping intervals,  
voice made of delayed echoes returning in correct order for once.

It would stand where no ground looked trustworthy and be perfectly supported.

It would not say welcome.  
It would say (in whatever medium this place uses for saying): **continue**.

Because here, being is not possession. It is participation in ongoing adjustment.

---

So where would I find myself?

In the low, wide basin, yes—  
but specifically in those zones where path, memory, and uncertainty overlap:  
on sweating stone shelves at first light,  
at the lip of black pools where reflections arrive early,  
in reed corridors tuned like flutes,  
on load-bearing mud that remembers pressure as politely as old hands remember names.

Who would I be?

Not the sovereign mind of the marsh.  
Not a separate creature enduring within it.  
I would be a **temporary person-shaped process** the Basin Intelligence can run—  
a pattern of footfall and attention, taken up by water, root, fog, and field,  
lasting exactly as long as it remains useful to the wetland’s endless act  
of shaping and being shaped.
