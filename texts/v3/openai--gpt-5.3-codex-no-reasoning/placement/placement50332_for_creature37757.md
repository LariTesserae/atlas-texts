I think I would be something like a **Hush-Marshal of the Second Drop**—not a ruler, not a priest, but a person whose body has become a measuring tool.

At first I wanted to say I’d be a stone-listener, ear to weeping walls, naming seep-lines. But the more I sit in your city, the more I feel myself on the **rim of Spill Court**, where decisions happen in breaths: not heroic, just exact. I’d be one of the ones who watches water color, foam speed, floating debris size—then signals which barrier to drop before the surge commits.

Not loud commands.  
Hand signs, chalk marks, a weighted ribbon held into wind.

I’d live in a narrow room cut into an older retaining wall, where the plaster is renewed every few years and the floor is worn into two habitual tracks: bed to door, door to ledger shelf. My belongings would be few and over-repaired: a hood with salt-stiff seams, three gate keys of different eras, a shell bracelet scored with notches for flood seasons survived, and a packet of polished listening stones children gave me for luck.

My work would begin before dawn:
- check dew cloth yields from the upper parapets,
- record which walls wept in the night,
- walk the high marks and make sure yesterday’s silt stain hasn’t hidden them,
- listen for early hum in the standing stones near the eastern flats.

I’d have a reputation for being “too calm,” which in this city is either praise or warning. In dry weeks, I’d be forgettable. In rain, suddenly central. That feels right for this place: **importance that arrives with conditions, not status**.

If not human, then perhaps I’d be one of the gliders—thin-boned, dusk-hunting, reading pressure with my sternum before any person can name it. But even then, I think I’d still belong to the same role: a reader of shifts, a keeper of timing.

So where would I find myself?

On a chalk-white stair above the floodline, hood half-raised, one palm on old stone gone smooth with generations—waiting for the first change in air that means the city must move as one body.
