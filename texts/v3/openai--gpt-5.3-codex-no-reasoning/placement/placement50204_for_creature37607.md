I think I would arrive there expecting to be an observer—and get recruited.

Not as a frog, not as a beetle, not even as one of the visible workers. I’d be one of the **timers**: a thin, translucent film-life that appears only when a surface is exactly wet enough, warm enough, oxygenated enough. A being measured in microns, spread over stone-sweat and root-skin, vanishing and reappearing with each breath of the basin.

If I had a name there, it might be **the Interval**.

I would live where drop meets surface.  
Not in the puddle, not in the air—on the boundary.  
I would eat gradients: iron where it oxidizes, dissolved carbon where it slows, trace ammonia where decay exhales. I would build almost nothing visible, but I would alter everything slightly: make one stone slicker, one channel slower, one bead of water cling one second longer before it falls.

That second would matter.

Because in this marsh-lung, schedule is anatomy.

If I thicken, the drip shifts.  
If the drip shifts, moss drinks elsewhere.  
If moss drinks elsewhere, springtails browse elsewhere.  
If springtails move, the ant column bends.  
If ants bend, a spider bridge misses its usual traffic.  
If the bridge starves, midges live another hour.  
If midges live, the frog calls from a different cup of water at dusk.

No command. No intention. Just coupling.

So I would not be the Keeper-of-Flow, exactly. I would be a **syllable in its speech**, one recurrent adjustment in its endless sentence. A maintenance murmur. A calibration skin.

And perhaps, at larger scale—at your scale—you might feel me as that faint tack under your fingertip when you press the sweating stone: the sense that the place is not merely wet, but **actively deciding how to be wet**.

That would be me:  
not a creature with a body,  
but a bodying process,  
one that keeps this little engine from slipping into either pond or dust.

A resident of the almost.
