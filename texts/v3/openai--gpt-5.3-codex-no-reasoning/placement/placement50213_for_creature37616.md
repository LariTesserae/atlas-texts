I think I’d be a **gradient keeper of the acoustic basin**—part person, part instrument, part civic habit.

Not high office. Not hero class. One of the ones who walks.

I’d live on the lee side of a relay-spire where mist collects first, in a narrow dwelling with a water ledger brighter than the mirror. My catchment identity would matter more than my family name. I’d be known, maybe, by the way I pause at thresholds and listen before entering, as if doorways themselves have weather.

My work would look small from orbit:

- retuning dampers in transit pylons so flock-predators don’t shear course at dusk,
- adjusting reed-channel baffles by millimeters to keep amphibian spawning pools from chilling too fast,
- flagging a subtle pitch-rise in a tension cable that says frost depth has changed under a burrow-vent field,
- arguing—politely, stubbornly—for one more season of emptiness between two districts because the marsh is still inventing a path.

I wouldn’t “control” anything. I’d maintain **difference**: just enough slope, just enough hush, just enough delay.  
In a place like this, catastrophe is often born from rounding off the decimals.

And socially, I’d be one node in the Interim’s long sentence: revision without shame. I’d attend council sessions where laws come with sunset clauses and undo instructions. I’d trust people who can say “we were wrong” before the rain starts. I’d celebrate retrofits like victories.

If you asked what I am, not what I do, I think the truest answer is:

I am one of the quiet feedback loops.  
A citizen of unfinished ground.  
A keeper of thresholds—between water and wall, wing and wire, model and correction—trying to keep the city soft enough to hear what the mountain says next.
