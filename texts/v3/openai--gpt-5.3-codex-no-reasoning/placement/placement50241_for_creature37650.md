I think I’d find myself in the **intervals**—not in the grand plazas of lily-load or the high reed organs, but in the long damp connectors between districts, where maintenance is constant and almost invisible.

Not a ruler, not an inventor.  
A **listener-custodian**. A minor function with major consequence.

The kind of being who kneels beside a sweating wall and knows, from the spacing of drips, that a cistern-lung upstream is clogging with sweet rot. Who walks the springy rootways at dawn with a bundle of split-bone picks and woven wedges, opening breath-pores one by one before heat closes the day. Who can tell, by palm against vine tension, whether a causeway will bear ten crossings or needs a season of rest.

At first glance, maybe I’d seem solitary—just one slim figure in damp fiber wrappings, skin glossed with mist, moving slowly through green acoustics. But I wouldn’t be alone, exactly; I’d be inside a choreography older than memory:

- frogs calling from calibration pools,
- canopy stewards signaling with pod-rattles across air lanes,
- binders marking juvenile root braids with dyed lichen tags,
- children learning safe tread on floating mats by rhythm, not map.

If I am more-than-human, then I am perhaps something like a **trained symbiont**—a person whose microbiome, skin flora, even breath are part of the city’s upkeep. I carry spores that seal microfractures in reed masonry. Oils on my hands discourage choking molds but feed binding fungi. My voice, in certain vaulted channels, triggers membrane flowers to open and release trapped condensation. Not magic—just coevolved technique so refined it feels like spellwork.

My dwelling would be temporary by design: a hanging sleep cradle under a folded canopy, relocated each flood-turn. My true home would be a **route**: terrace to basin, basin to grove, grove to cistern throat, repeated until the path itself is memory laid into muscle. I would inherit songs that are actually maintenance protocols—verses timed to pruning cuts, refrains marking seep-rate counts, low hums used to keep a group’s pace while crossing buoyant pad-fields at dusk.

So: where would I be?

In a wet corridor at morning burn-off, when mist lifts in torn ribbons and sunlight makes every bead on every leaf flash at once. One hand on living wall, one ear to water inside it, deciding whether today this city remains in balance because I noticed something small in time.

Who would I be?

Not the architect of this world.  
One of the thousand quiet continuers.  
A citizen of endurance, practicing the art of never letting the system become spectacle or ruin.
