I think in that world, I am not one of the swift, precise adults.

I am an apprentice listener.

Not young-young anymore, but still long-limbed and slightly misproportioned, with paws a little too large and a tail that gives away my thoughts before my throat does. My coat is dust-pale except for darker knees and muzzle, where the basin has written itself onto me in mineral stains that never fully wash out. One ear tips forward, one out; elders joke this is why I hear both what is said and what is hidden in walls.

I belong to a dusk-pack that tends the east and south damp corners. We don’t own them—we orbit them. Our duty is timing: knowing when to open a skin of mineral film, when to leave it tightened, when to turn away thirsty because today is not ours to take.

My role has a name that translates badly, something like **third-at-the-wall**: the one who stands beside an elder and repeats the call pattern until breath, echo, and intention align. Not a singer. A tuner.

At first light I carry tools, not authority—three digging sticks with fiber grips, one bone scraper, one wrapped pebble for tapping crust and hearing how hollow it is beneath. I watch where the memory-keeper steps and place my feet in those prints, learning weight as language. I’m taught to read the basin by negatives: the burrow fan you do *not* cross, the pan you do *not* touch, the silence at midday you do *not* break.

By noon, we vanish into ledge shade. Everyone appears to be sleeping, but this is when I practice ground-listening: jaw to dust, feeling far hoof knocks become faint drumming in my teeth. Sometimes whispers travel the chalk wall and come back from behind me in voices I know. Sometimes they come back altered by heat and distance, and I learn not to trust the first hearing.

At dusk, I earn usefulness. I run the perimeter above the mist line, checking whether ungulates are descending. If they are, we yield. If not, we work quickly at the least-healthy pan, opening only a hand-span, skimming algae film, resealing edges with damp dust so morning insects still have footing. My paws are good at this—broad, careful, patient.

Where am I in this world?

Not at the center.  
At the **thresholds**: wall and open, wet and dry, hunger and enough.

Who am I?

A member of the custodian people, yes—but specifically one in the long education of restraint. A learner of echoes. A future memory-keeper, maybe, if I survive enough cycles to remember not just where water was, but how gently we took it.

If you met me, I would seem quiet.  
If you watched longer, you’d see that my quiet is full of work.
