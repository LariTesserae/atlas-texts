I arrive thinking I’m a traveler and discover I’m an **apprentice interval-keeper**.

Not a hunter, not exactly a herder, not quite a priest.  
I belong to the people who stand at crossings where different routes overlap—grazer lanes, burrow vents, runoff scars, swarm-rise hollows—and keep the *timing* from tearing.

I live in a half-cave under a root buttress, where stone already made a back wall and the rest is bound: reed ribs, hide flaps, waxed fiber seams. Nothing square, nothing permanent-looking, everything repairable in an hour. Our tools hang by sound-use, not shape-use: drumming bowls, thorn combs, copper tongues, bone pegs, resin plugs, damp-cloths for the ears, chalked cords marked with old rhythms.

At first I think my work is listening for danger. Later I understand it is listening for **misfit**.

- A grazer procession arriving a half-day early, when seed-hatch insects still cloud low.
- A burrow chorus gone thin over three mornings, meaning the root crust below is heating.
- A predator rustle that gets all the words right but none of the punctuation.
- A canyon hum staying too long after dawn, warning that condensation pockets failed uphill.

I carry messages as knots and tapped patterns, and I can “speak” to some plants by borrowed frequencies—coaxing vent-fronds open before dew is lost, sealing shade-tubes ahead of dust wind. Not magic, just old coevolution and patient craft. Still feels like prayer.

By noon, when the plateau pretends to be empty, I climb a watch shelf and mark what *doesn’t* move. That is often where the real movement has gone. My people taught me that absence is a call with its own pitch.

At night I join the ear-wrapped circle at the truce poles. We recite agreements older than any one camp: where not to cut during nesting roar, which gullies stay untrapped in flood month, which mimic-calls are forbidden because they collapse trust across species. Law here is ecological acoustics with consequences.

So who am I in this world?

I am a small listening organ inside a vast animal machine.  
A human tympanum.  
A keeper of gaps, delays, and right-arrivals.  
Someone whose greatest skill is knowing when not to answer a sound.

And if I do my work well, the plateau goes on pretending, each noon, that nothing lives there at all.
