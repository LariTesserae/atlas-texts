The city begins as a color change on the mountainside: pale stone becoming patterned stone.

From far away, the range looks raw and recently lifted—ribs of gray rock thrust up at sharp angles, ridgelines clean-edged, scree slopes still bright where frost has split fresh faces open. Snow lies in streaks and pockets rather than blankets. There is cold in everything, but not a deep wet cold; this is high, thin air where moisture arrives in episodes, as cloud and brief rain, then vanishes into wind and shadow. The sky is often clear enough that distance seems exaggerated. Whole valleys look close enough to walk in an hour and take three days instead.

As you approach, the “patterned stone” resolves into terraces, retaining walls, stairways, stacked dwellings, cistern mouths, channels cut with hand tools, and long defensive lines that double as avalanche breaks. The urban fabric is dense because land that can be built on is limited: ledges, benches, old glacial shelves, and carved platforms. Buildings shoulder together for warmth and shelter from wind. Lanes are narrow, rooflines interlocked, courtyards sunk below street level. Nothing sprawls; everything climbs.

### Terrain and materials

The mountain basin is a giant amphitheater at high elevation, enclosed by jagged summits and opening toward a lower plateau to the south. The basin floor is not flat; it is a maze of old moraines, gravel fans, and uplifted steps. The city occupies many of these levels at once, connected by steep ramps and thousands of stairs.

Most construction is dry-laid stone: local schist and volcanic tuff, with timber used sparingly where scrub forests survive in protected gullies. Mortar is precious and used mainly in cistern linings and ceremonial structures. Roofs are slate-weighted hide, woven reed mats coated in clay, or split planks where wood can be traded up from lower valleys.

Young erosion is visible everywhere. A wall built twenty years ago may already lean where thaw and freeze have nudged its footing. Channels must be re-cut every season. New gullies appear after cloudbursts. Entire neighborhoods maintain “stone crews” whose daily work is just to reset steps, clear rockfall, and rebuild spillways before small failures become fatal ones.

### Water in a mostly dry cold

Water is intermittent, so the city is built around capture, storage, and rationed release.

- Snowmelt runs hard for short periods, then dwindles.
- Mist condenses on cold night stone and is scraped into jars from specially textured walls.
- Brief mountain rains are directed by roof lips and gutter chains into communal cisterns.
- Springs appear and disappear as fractures open and clog in the young rock.

You hear water less than you see evidence of its planning: polished lipstones at cistern mouths, dark streaks down channel walls, careful lids over reservoirs to limit evaporation and freezing. Public squares often center on covered tanks, not fountains. To spill water carelessly is not just wasteful; it is socially shocking.

Outside the main basin, strings of waystations mark seasonal streams. In dry weeks they are dust paths. In wet weeks they become quick, cold torrents that can erase trails overnight.

### Vegetation and ecology

Plant life is sparse but tenacious. Low shrubs in wind shadows, wiry grasses in gravel seams, lichen maps on north-facing stone, dwarf conifers in ravines where snow lingers. People cultivate where they can: pocket fields behind wind walls, stepped gardens fed by drip channels, hardy tubers and cold grains in shallow soil imported basket by basket.

Animal life, by contrast, is rich. Abundance comes from vertical diversity and migration:

- Cliff-nesting birds by the tens of thousands, turning entire escarpments white and noisy during breeding seasons.
- Herding ungulates that move between high summer meadows and lower winter scrub.
- Burrowing mammals in the drier terraces.
- Predators that follow the herds along contour paths.
- Domesticated pack-beasts adapted to thin air and narrow stairs, with broad hooves and thick lungs.

Fauna and city are entangled. Rooftops are designed to discourage some species and invite others. Waste sorting exists primarily to control scavengers. Bells and reflective slats protect crops from birds. Night corrals are integrated into lower floors for heat sharing.

### The feel of the place

Despite density and scale, the soundscape is restrained. Wind dominates: not a howl most days, more a steady brushing through alley cuts and stair towers. Footsteps on stone, animal tack, hand tools tapping, distant calls bouncing off rock. Conversation stays close to the body because air is thin and people conserve breath. Great crowds can gather and still feel muted, as if wrapped in wool.

Light is clear and ordinary in rhythm—day and night familiar—but intense at altitude. Shadows are hard-edged. Sun-warmed walls and freezing shade can exist a few paces apart. After rain, everything smells briefly metallic and mineral; then it dries back to dust, dung smoke, and cold stone.

### Urban organization and low technology

Technology is simple, but competence is high. No grand machines, no metal abundance, no hidden advanced systems—just refined craft under pressure.

- Stonecutting with iron-poor tools, sharpened constantly.
- Rope engineering for load transfer across gullies.
- Counterweighted hoists on timber tripods.
- Ceramic piping in short protected runs.
- Ice storage pits insulated with straw and ash.
- Written tally systems for water shares, grain dues, wall maintenance labor.

Governance is hydraulic and topographic. District rights are tied to slope position and cistern access. Upper wards receive first melt but must maintain the avalanche screens. Lower wards receive runoff but manage flood debris. Neutral stairways are sacred civic infrastructure: block them and you are punished as if for assault.

Because the city is spread over vast relief, it has weather neighborhoods. One district wakes in bright sun while another remains in cloud. A storm can strike the eastern tiers and never touch the western ones. Signal towers use smoke by day and shielded fire by night to coordinate across microclimates.

### Rules of this world (ordinary, but strict)

Nothing supernatural is required; the mountain itself is law.

1. **Build heavy, repair often.** Young slopes move. Permanence is achieved by maintenance, not by single acts of construction.
2. **Store before you need.** Water, fodder, fuel, and stone all have short windows of easy collection.
3. **Move vertically with care.** Most travel is up or down; falls are a civic hazard on the scale of disease.
4. **Share risk by contour.** Hazards flow downslope; obligations flow upslope.
5. **Listen to animals.** Herd movement predicts weather shifts better than sky-reading alone in this terrain.

### At continental scale

This is one city among many high-basin settlements scattered along a young mountain belt that runs for thousands of kilometers. From above, they appear as constellations of terraces stitched by precarious passes. Each is dense, compact, and adapted to local fracture patterns and melt timing. Trade moves slowly by caravan, mostly salt, dried meat, wool, resin, and worked stone tools. News travels with herders and pilgrims, delayed by storms and pass closures.

Over generations, the mountains are still becoming themselves. Ridges sharpen, valleys deepen, routes migrate. Entire districts may be abandoned not because of war but because a slope has begun to creep. New ledges are claimed as uplift and landslip create unexpected buildable shelves. The civilization is old enough to have traditions, young enough that maps never stop changing.

So the landscape is not empty wilderness with a city placed on top. It is a continuous fabric of stone, animal movement, water discipline, and human density—cold, high, mostly quiet, and immense enough that even its settled places feel like weather phenomena.
