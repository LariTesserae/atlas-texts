From a distance, the city looks like a green escarpment someone taught to obey grids.

It sits high on an old plateau where mountains used to be sharper ages ago, before wind and ice and root pressure wore everything down to broad-backed stone. The ground is not empty rock, though: it is sheathed in vegetation so complete that soil is mostly a memory. Trunk, vine, mat, moss, bark, and leaf have become the true surface. Streets are cut through plant tissue the way canals are cut through peat. Buildings are grown as much as built—steel ribs and ceramic cores wrapped in living wood that has been trained for generations into load-bearing arches. Some towers are hollowed giant stems reinforced by bolted bands. Others are old industrial frames engulfed so fully by climbing forests that only their smokestacks, now vent-stacks for steam and pollen, give away their origin.

There isn’t much open water. What moisture exists arrives in episodes: brief hard rains, fog tides rolling uphill at dawn, condensation rivers that bead and run along cables and leaf-spines into cisterns. Every district has catchment combs—huge wire lattices strung between rooftops where mist condenses and drips all morning into tanks below. In dry weeks, people walk with cloth hoods that wick dew from the air. In wet hours, gutters roar. The city has learned to live in pulses: dry, drip, deluge, dry again.

Because the elevation is high, the air is cool even at midday. Breath smokes in the early tram depots. Metal stays cold to the touch. Shade can feel almost alpine, but the greenery keeps wind broken and soft; you move through alternating pockets of chill and vegetal warmth like stepping between rooms in a giant, breathing house. At night, condensate forms on every railing, every cable, every blade, and by sunrise whole neighborhoods glimmer with droplets as if plated in glass.

The place is old in the way a reef is old. Not static—settled. The broad landforms have finished collapsing into their durable shapes. Ridges are rounded. Valleys are shallow-bellied. Rock exposures show smooth, dark faces polished by vanished glaciers and ten thousand years of abrasive spores. On top of this ancient frame, the city has been rebuilt repeatedly without ever really clearing itself. Layers of abandoned conveyor corridors sit inside living root galleries. Ancient foundry halls are now fern-cathedrals with turbines still turning in their basements, driven by pressure differences between cool night air and warm fermentation chambers.

It is urban, undeniably: districts, transit lines, production zones, civic terraces, signal towers, freight lifts climbing old cliffs. But “block” and “building” are only part of the grammar. The other half is botanical. Address systems include branch-level and canopy-depth. Utility maps show sap pressure mains beside electric lines. Public works crews include arbor-mechanics who tune both pumps and phloem flow. There are rail yards where cargo wagons roll under suspended root bundles thick as pipelines, each one tagged and metered.

Sound carries far in the open high air, and the city is loud. Not with constant human shouting—though markets are noisy—but with layered mechanical and biological volume. Fans hum. Belt lines slap. Tram brakes sing on damp rails. Rain-harvesting arrays rattle in gusts. Seed mills thud all night. And from the plant mass itself comes a ceaseless susurration: leaf-friction, stem-creak, fruit dropping, pods venting, vines contracting around supports with little wooden ticks. During condensation dawn the whole city drips in polyrhythm, thousands of tiny impacts on metal, bark, tarp, and stone. In the storm-hour, every roof is a drum.

Animals are scarce. A few cliff birds nest on inaccessible ledges and spiral over thermal vents. Small nocturnal gliders move in the upper canopy. Insects exist but in oddly low diversity, as if something in the ecology selected for plants over mobile life. People joke that the city has outsourced fauna to rumor. What life is abundant is vegetal and fungal and microbial—green, pallid, and occasionally luminous in shaded infrastructure tunnels.

The weirdness is not spectacle so much as ontological inconvenience. The plants here do not always agree with linear time. Entire facades may leaf out overnight with growth rings indicating decades. A staircase can become enclosed by wood that appears both freshly formed and deeply weathered. Survey markers drift—not physically, but historically; records insist a plaza has stood for 300 years while residents remember attending its inauguration last spring. In certain industrial conservatories, vines trained around rotating machinery begin to mirror gear ratios in their phyllotaxis, producing spirals that encode engine cycles. Botanists read outages in branch patterns the way archivists read burned manuscripts.

Space occasionally folds by botanical continuity rather than geometry: two courtyards grafted by a shared rootstock can become adjacent despite maps placing them kilometers apart. City law recognizes this. There are Rootway Codes, jurisdiction treaties for neighborhoods that touch through living substrate. Emergency services maintain “topological brigades” trained to navigate sudden contiguities. Children learn early not to follow a familiar vine blindly after heavy rain.

Despite that, daily life is practical, even bureaucratic. Sirens still mean something simple. Shift whistles blow. Street lamps—normal-spectrum, steady—come on at dusk and render the strangeness manageable. Light is mostly honest here: clear daytime, cloud-muted afternoons, sodium and white industrial illumination at night. You can read a wrench size, inspect a leaf lesion, fill out a permit.

The city’s rules emerge from long compromise between industry, altitude, dampness, and living infrastructure:

- **Never seal a wall completely**; plants and condensation need breath paths.
- **Every machine room must have a root corridor** for thermal buffering.
- **All roofs are catchments first, habitable second.**
- **Pruning is civil engineering.**
- **Fire is treated as weather and crime simultaneously.**
- **Maps expire after rain events.**

Out beyond the denser wards, spacing increases. Freight spines run over broad green saddles between old stone domes. You can stand on a service platform and see kilometers of canopy punctuated by chimneys, gantries, and wind towers, all half-swallowed by growth. The horizon is not jagged mountain nor flat plain, but an immense undulation of ancient highland wearing a city like moss wears ruins.

And in the evening, when the day’s last condensation begins, the loudness softens to drip, chain-clink, and distant turbines. Fog gathers in transit cuts. Lamps halo. Vines tighten microscopically around warm pipes. Somewhere a factory horn sounds shift end, then again a minute later from another district that may be far away or just root-adjacent now. The place keeps working either way—cool, elevated, old beyond dating, and alive enough to keep rewriting where it is.
