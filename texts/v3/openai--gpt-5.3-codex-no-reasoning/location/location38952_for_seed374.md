The city sits in a shallow basin where stone is still deciding to be stone.

Nothing here looks old. Not because it is clean, but because edges have not had time to dull. The ground underfoot is a quilt of cooling mineral plates, pale gray to black, fitted together by necessity rather than artistry, each seam sweating moisture. In the dim warm light, the whole basin seems to breathe a wet sheen. There are no trees, no grasses, no moss clinging to corners. Surfaces are naked: clay, basaltic glass, compacted ash, kiln-baked brick, rope, hide, bone, and fired earth. Life has come in animals and people, but not in green.

Water is everywhere and almost nowhere. You don’t see broad rivers; you hear seepage. It beads along walls, threads down carved channels, gathers in low cisterns, then vanishes back into porous rock. The basin floor is close to saturation, so wells are shallow and numerous, ringed by hand-laid stone collars. In places the ground gives under weight with a soft sucking sound, and boards have been laid across whole neighborhoods—not elegant boardwalks, just practical lattices of poles and split trunks imported from far-off dry uplands where plants still exist. Wood is too precious to be decorative here; every beam remembers labor.

The settlement is dense, knotted, and low. Buildings crowd one another like stacked clay vessels, rounded and thick-walled, mostly one or two stories, with narrow slots for windows and broad, sloped roofs that catch condensation and route it into jars. Streets are less “planned” than pressed into existence by repeated passage: braided lanes, drainage grooves, animal runs, and ramps built atop older ramps. At this scale you can stand on a roof and see district after district unfolding toward the basin rim, each with its own workshops, pens, cistern yards, and communal hearth courts. Beyond the last ring of houses, there are extraction fields—open scars where people pry fresh mineral plates from the newborn crust and haul them in by sled.

Because erosion is almost absent, nothing has been softened by weather. Gullies are rare; slopes are abrupt. Where a path cuts downhill, it does so along raw fracture lines. Retaining walls are constant, not to resist age, but to negotiate a world still settling into shape. The city feels “under construction” in a fundamental way: not because of cranes or scaffolds, but because the land itself is unfinished and everyone builds as if tomorrow’s ground might rise a handspan and lock in place forever.

The fauna fill every niche vegetation would have held. Herd-beasts with broad, splayed feet graze microbial films from wet stone. Long-necked scavengers pick at mineral salt blooms. Burrowing colonies honeycomb the softer clays, and their mounds form whole secondary topographies in vacant lots. In roof gutters, leathery gliders roost in layered ranks, dropping at dusk to feed on swarming insects that hatch from warm cistern margins. Domestic species are tightly integrated into infrastructure: tread-beasts power pumps, blind fish cleanse runoff pits, shell-backed haulers move bricks through lanes too narrow for carts. The city is never still because animals are never still.

And so the sound is overwhelming.

Not a single loud event, but total acoustic occupation: hoof-clatter on stone slats, pump wheels thudding, rope winches whining, animal calls ricocheting through close alleys, kiln draughts roaring, hundreds of water channels ticking and dripping in arrhythmic overlay, market cries, hammering of soft metal and hard ceramic, hide drums used as work signals between terraces. Even at night, the volume does not fall much. This is a practical culture in a wet mineral bowl; silence would mean systems have failed.

The air is warm and heavy, carrying the mineral smell of fresh-cut rock, wet clay, animal musk, smoke from dung fires, and that metallic tang of standing water in stone basins. Dim light comes through a near-permanent high haze—condensation and smoke mingled—so shadows are soft-edged and short. Colors stay muted: umber hides, ash-white plaster, iron-red slips, black glass, pale bone. Bright pigments are rare and saved for markings of use: hazard stripes near sink zones, ownership marks on cistern lids, counting bands on load animals.

Technologically, the place is ingenious without being advanced. Everything is hand-scale, muscle-scale, herd-scale. Levers, rollers, capstans, baked pipes, ceramic valves, reed floats, counterweights, woven filters, gravity-fed distribution. Metal exists but sparingly—small blades, rivets, needles, hooks—too precious for bulk architecture. Mastery here lies in maintenance rhythms rather than invention bursts. Teams inspect channels on fixed cycles. Kilns fire standardized brick modules. Animal breeding is managed by guild memory rather than written biology. Knowledge is embodied, repeated, stable.

That stability defines the deeper atmosphere: this world is dynamic in activity but static in pattern. Day after day, season after season, the same wet warmth, the same dim sky, the same work songs, the same flow rates, the same migration loops of urban fauna. Not stagnant—equilibrated. The basin reached a balance and has held it for generations. Children learn not “what might happen” but “what always happens.” Water will condense on east-facing roofs before dawn. The lower lanes will flood ankle-deep by second bell and drain by fifth. The gliders will scream at dusk from the kiln quarter. The ground by the south cistern will remain unsafe no matter how many stones are thrown in. Certainty is a civic resource.

At the edges of the city, where construction thins, you can see the raw world it grew from: broad, low terraces of new crust, shiny as pottery before firing; shallow steam vents with mineral lips; untouched flats where animal tracks stitch dense calligraphy over wet dust. No ruins, no ancient forests, no buried roads. Just emergence, then habitation—close-packed, noisy, practical, alive.

It is not a beautiful place in the way of flowers or mountains.

It is beautiful in the way of systems that work under pressure: water caught before it is lost, stone shaped before it cracks, animals partnered before they stampede, noise organized into function, and an unfinished earth held, by constant communal effort, in a permanent present.
