From above, it looks like a moss-dark bowl cut into a high shoulder of land: not a valley exactly, not a city exactly, but something between marsh, forest, and settlement that has learned to share one skin.

The ground is never simply ground. Every step lands on roots, peat, woven branch mats, or old planks gone soft and green with lichen. Water sits just below everything. Press a heel and clear beads rise through the mat. After rain—and it rains often—mist hangs waist-high, and the whole place steams faintly in the cool air, as if breathing through a million leaf-pores.

The terrain is terraced without ever having been fully carved. Long ages of runoff and patient handwork have shaped shelves along the slope: narrow ledges planted with reed-beds and medicinal herbs, broader platforms carrying clustered dwellings, then steep drop-offs where vine curtains hide trickling stone faces. Rock appears in slick ribs, black and iron-stained, weeping continuously. People have learned to direct these drips with split bamboo and clay lips into cistern pots, troughs, and tiny rills that feed hanging gardens below.

It is dense in every sense. Green overlays brown overlays gray. Ferns root in roof-thatch; roofs sag onto trellises; trellises become living beams. Trees are not around the settlement—they are inside it, through it, load-bearing. Great pollarded trunks rise through floor openings and spread branchwork that supports ropeways, drying racks, and suspended walkpaths. Homes are wattle, daub, timber, and packed reed, but no wall stays bare long. Creepers lace everything, and edible leaves are harvested daily from doorframes, fence lines, and even public railings.

Because space is tight and wet, everything is vertical. Stairs, ladders, slatted ramps, branch bridges. Platforms over channels. Fish pens below kitchens. Aviaries above smoke vents. Between structures run narrow, slick alleys where water chatters down gutters lined with rounded stones polished by generations of flow. You can cross from one end of this place to the other without touching open soil if you know the raised route; strangers usually don’t and end up ankle-deep in cool brown water full of darting life.

And life is everywhere, insistently. Frogs in dozens of sizes. Snails with lacquered spiral shells. Fat black beetles under bark tiles. Bright dragonflies stitching the damp air. Eels in the channels. Marsh birds nesting in rafters and shouting at dawn loud enough to rattle sleep from everyone at once. Small hoofed grazers are kept in woven pens on drier shelves; their dung is composted into heat-mounds for seedlings. Dogs bark at monkeys. Monkeys steal fruit and occasionally tools. At night, the insect chorus swells until conversation becomes gesture unless you lean close.

Loudness is constant but varied: drip and pour, croak and trill, wingbeat, distant hammering on wood pegs, the slap of wet rope against poles in wind, children calling over water noise, market arguments, pestles in mortars, the hollow knocking used as a signal when a walkway floods or a retaining wall needs hands. Nothing stays still long here. Channels overtop, then recede. A tree drops a branch and by evening it has become a bridge patch. Wind rips vine screens loose and by morning they’re rewoven. Turbulence is normal, not disaster—maintenance is a daily rhythm as fundamental as cooking.

Despite all this motion, the place feels old and settled. Edges are rounded. Paths follow habits older than memory. Water has chosen its routes and people mostly agree with it. Where erosion once bit hard, stone baskets filled with cobbles now hold slopes in place, their gaps planted with rooting grasses. The oldest retaining walls are half-swallowed by fig roots, and no one can say where masonry ends and tree begins. Tools are simple—wooden spades tipped with bone or iron scraps, broad knives, hand augers, woven lifts run by people and counterweights—but used with exact skill.

Light arrives filtered. Mornings are silver through mist. By midday, normal daylight breaks in shafts through layered canopy and smoke, turning every droplet into a brief spark. Evenings come quickly under leaves; lamps are resin and wick, hung under eaves where moths gather in pale swarms. Nothing supernatural intrudes. The strangeness is only abundance and interdependence pushed near saturation.

Rules of the place are practical and communal:
- Keep moving water moving; blocked flow is everyone’s problem.
- Harvest by rotation; walls and trellises are also fields.
- Never cut a structural tree without council agreement.
- Loud signals have meanings; learn them or cause trouble.
- Leave niches for nesting creatures, because pest control is ecological, not individual.
- Store food high, dry, and tied, or the night population will claim it.

At its center there is usually a public wet court: a broad timber platform over shallow pooled water, market by day, repair yard in storms, meeting ground whenever siren-drums call. Around it, this compact high marsh-town vibrates—cool, saturated, crowded, alive—less built on the landscape than braided into it, one more organism in a roaring, dripping web.
