At first you think it’s a vacant lot at the edge of an old town—flat, cool ground, low sky, almost no horizon because the dark is so complete that distance folds in on itself. Then your eyes adjust and the place keeps unfolding, not outward so much as inward, layer after layer, like a city block grown from moss and patience.

It is small, bounded—no bigger than a district you could cross in ten minutes if the paths stayed still. They do not.

---

The “streets” are braided roots the width of tram lines, elevated a little above black soil that is never quite mud, never quite solid. Moisture gathers here by habit, not weather: condensation beads on leaf undersides, drips down cable-vines, disappears. Rain comes in short, practical bursts, as if delivered by schedule. There are no puddles that last; water enters, announces itself in scent, and gets taken up by everything green almost immediately.

And nearly everything is green.

Not bright meadow green—factory green, cellar green, sleeping-machine green. The architecture is alive: wall-ferns knit into retaining faces, lichen cements seams between bark-columns, huge folded leaves serve as awnings over transit cuttings where small freight crawlers pass. Buildings exist, yes, but they are scaffolds and pressure frames, secondary to the vegetal mass that has occupied them, learned their shapes, and overwritten them. An apartment block becomes a vertical peat organ. A warehouse becomes a seed vault with breathing doors.

There is plenty of structure, but very little clutter. Density is strangely low for an urban place. Volumes are hollow. Courtyards are vast bowls of dim air. Atriums open like lungs with only one bridge crossing each. You can stand in the center of a plant-built plaza and feel alone, profoundly, while hearing an entire biome at work somewhere above you.

---

The ground itself feels unfinished, as if geology has only recently agreed to hold still. Kerbs rise from soil and stop mid-curve. A slab road extends thirty meters then dissolves into fibrous mat. Stone appears in soft, infant ridges under the roots, not worn by ages but newly exposed, chalky and uncertain. Erosion has barely started; nothing has had time to be old in the usual way. Instead of ruins, there are interruptions.

It gives the district a prototype quality—an industrial pilot city handed over to plants before commissioning.

That industrial layer is still functioning in parts. In the dark you catch the glint of enamel piping threaded through banyan trunks. Condenser towers wrapped in ivy breathe out cool vapor every hour. Silent conveyor belts move seedpods, fungal bricks, mineral paste. Dynamos turn slowly in underground chambers, powered by root pressure and weight-shift from the canopy above, producing a low current for lamps that almost never turn on. Electricity exists mostly as potential here: a held breath in copper veins.

---

Light is the rarest resource. Day is only a suggestion, a thinning of black into deep blue-gray. The canopy and built overhangs trap what little arrives. Most illumination is biological and selective: thin fungal script glowing along handrails; pulse-lamps in nectar wells that flash only when pollinators approach; pale moth-swarm halos that drift down alleys and vanish around corners they haven’t reached yet.

In several plazas, darkness behaves incorrectly. It pools in geometric shapes independent of objects, like cast shadows from absent towers. Step into one and sound dampens further, your exhale hangs visibly, and for a moment you hear yesterday’s rain. Step out and time resumes its ordinary slowness.

Reality is porous here in small, repeatable ways. Staircases descend into the same courtyard from three nonmatching directions. Drain grates open onto canopies. If you mark a wall, the mark migrates overnight to a different wall made from a related species. Nobody treats this as uncanny; it is zoning.

---

Fauna are everywhere despite the emptiness.

You rarely see them in crowds. Instead, each large volume contains one or two presences moving with total confidence: antlered herons stepping roof-gutters for snails; six-winged gliders roosting in ventilation throats; fox-sized beetles polishing lichen off load-bearing vines; schools of translucent eels threading through suspended water tubes like living current indicators. In the root streets, soft-footed grazers crop moss from moving belts and follow maintenance lights as if herded by stars.

Predators exist, but predation is quiet and administrative. A vine contracts. A shadow folds. A flock abruptly reorganizes from spiral to absence. Then the district continues.

Insects are the true citizens: pollinators, decomposers, signalers. Their abundance drives the city’s timing. Whole intersections open and close based on migration pulses. Some doors are keyed to wingbeat frequency. There are posted advisories—grown as color bands in bark—warning of larval hatches that can strip an avenue overnight and regrow it by dawn.

---

Sound sits just above silence.

You hear drip, leaf-friction, far machinery turning under load, occasional cable twangs when growth tension releases. Animal voices are brief and directional: one bark, one click train, one descending whistle. No continuous roar, no market hum. Even active processes seem to prefer hush.

The loudest regular event is the condensation harvest: at certain cool hours, membranes unfurl from high frames and shake themselves, sending silver rain into collection troughs. For sixty seconds the whole district sounds like applause in another room.

Afterward, the quiet returns, denser than before.

---

There are rules, if you stay long enough to notice them:

- **Nothing inert remains inert.** If left in place, tools sprout symbiotic sheaths, rails root, cables branch.
- **Paths negotiate daily.** Footways are recommendations; root growth has right-of-way unless cut by authorized grazers.
- **Darkness has legal districts.** Some shadow pools are protected habitats for nocturnal pollinators and memory phenomena.
- **Water is temporary but never wasted.** Every drop is claimed by plant, animal, machine, or stone-film within minutes.
- **Maps are seasonal organisms.** Printed maps fail. Living maps are cultivated in moss trays and must be watered to stay accurate.
- **Empty space is intentional infrastructure.** Hollows provide flight lanes, thermal sinks, hunting corridors, and places for the unreal to vent harmlessly.

---

If you climb one of the old signal towers—now a trellis wrapped in fruiting vines—you can finally read the whole landscape at once. A low, cool basin. A compact urban organism in near-dark. Vast internal rooms with almost nothing in them except life, moving. Industry slowed to accompaniment. Geology still deciding its final grammar. Water arriving in whispers. Animals threading every level. And reality, under all of it, flexing like a muscle that has not yet learned it is bone.
