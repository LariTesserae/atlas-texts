At first it seems like a cliffside settlement emptied of people, but the longer you stand in it, the clearer it becomes: this is not a settlement *on* a mountain. The mountain is gone. Only the making remains.

The whole place occupies roughly the footprint of a small fortress court and its surrounding galleries—something you could cross in minutes if crossing were easy. It isn’t easy. Every span is occupied. Every wall is doubled by scaffolds, braces, buttresses, ladders, pegged shelves, hanging racks, stacked bins, cords, lattices, and wind-frames. There are no open plazas, no clean sightlines, no spare ledges. The eye keeps searching for bare stone and finds none. Even what looks like cliff is fitted masonry: old blocks, older than memory, worn to a soft, almost geologic smoothness by heat and grit and touch.

Everything is dry. Not just “no river” dry, but chemically dry—the kind of dryness that has moved beyond thirst into a law of existence. Joints are packed with powdery minerals instead of mortar in some places, in others with resin that has long ago cured to amber-black ceramic. Bowls stand out to catch nothing. Channels run underfoot with no stain of flow. Cistern mouths are sealed by fitted lids and ringed with warning marks so old their meaning has become pattern. Even the air feels scraped clean of moisture, thin and warm, carrying a taste like ground pottery.

The elevation makes itself known in the lungs: breaths come quick until your body accepts the terms. The warmth is constant but never lush—sun-baked masonry by day, retained heat by night, always within a narrow band that supports labor and little else. There is no shade in the soft sense, only dimness made by overlap: roof slats, suspended screens, stacked platforms. Light arrives as diluted bronze through dust and woven barriers. It does not illuminate so much as settle.

And the sound—this is the defining weather. Deafening, perpetual, and strangely uniform. Hundreds of suspended clappers strike stone tongues in the wind. Taut cords hum. Loose metal rings chatter against pegs. Hollow columns resonate like flutes with only one note each. Somewhere beneath all that, treadle hammers cycle, ratchets click, counterweights knock. None of it implies active workers; it all runs from fixed forces: thermal expansion, slight pressure differences, gravity-fed drop systems wound long ago. The mechanisms are simple, visible, repairable with rope, wood, fired clay, and soft hammered metals. Primitive in method, absolute in coverage. The din is not an event but a condition, as steady as gravity.

Because it never changes, the ear stops parsing detail. After an hour the noise becomes a solid wall, and individual impacts only flash out when you are very near. Communication here was never spoken across distance; everywhere there are tactile codes carved at hand height, knot sequences on hanging cords, and directional tapping rails that carry vibration through the structure better than voice could. In a world of permanent thunder, touch became language.

Age is written into every surface. Not ruin—settling. This place has passed through collapse and repair so many times that collapse has been designed out of it. Fragile forms disappeared ages ago. What remains is overbuilt, recursively reinforced, and rounded by abrasion into an architecture that looks almost eroded despite being entirely made. Corners are gone. Edges are blunted. Steps are cupped by countless footfalls that are no longer happening. Ancient, yes, but not dead: stable in the way a fossil reef is stable, all niches filled, no room for novelty.

There is no vegetation, no lichen even, no green dust in cracks. There are no animals in rafters, no insects in bins, no droppings, no nests. Ecologically, the place is occupied by process rather than organisms. Heat gradients move air; air moves vanes; vanes move weights; weights strike, lift, release, reset. Mineral dust migrates, settles, is shaken loose by vibration, settles again. Salt-like crusts bloom on warmer surfaces and are scraped into troughs by fixed brushes mounted to swinging arms. If there is “life” here, it is pattern persistence: maintenance loops that outlasted maintainers.

The rules of the world are plain once you notice them:

- **Nothing organic takes hold.** Not because it is hunted, but because there is no moisture budget for growth and no quiet refuge from abrasion.
- **Empty space is temporary.** If a gap opens, something swings, drops, or is pulled into it; the system abhors voids.
- **Silence is impossible.** Remove one sounding element and three others amplify to fill its absence.
- **Change is local and cyclical, not historical.** A pendulum wears through a pin; a spare pin, pre-hung on a cord beside it for centuries, drops into place; motion resumes exactly as before.

From above—if you climb the outer bracing and look down through the dim—you see a dense, terraced basin of contraptions packed so tightly they resemble a woven textile more than a town. Warm dust rises in slow sheets. Bronze light catches on polished strike points and nowhere else. The whole structure vibrates at the edge of visibility, as if drawn in lines too close together.

It is an old high place built by simple hands into total environment, then kept by inertia, then by design, then by neither—only by the fact that every part depends on every other and all of them are still, somehow, exactly in balance.
