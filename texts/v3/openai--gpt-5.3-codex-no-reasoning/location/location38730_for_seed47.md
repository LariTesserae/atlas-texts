From orbit it looks almost finished.

Not dead—finished, in the way a language can be finished: every rough edge worn down by use, every excess removed. A high world, all uplift and exposure, where the skin of the planet has been sanded for ages under a thin, clear atmosphere. Continents are mostly tableland and escarpment, vast pale shields of mineral crust broken by trenches so deep they hold their own weather. There is almost no open water. What moisture exists lives as transient films in shadow or as cold vapor combed by wind into streamers over the heights, then gone again.

At ground level, the first thing is distance. Not emptiness in a romantic sense—emptiness as a physical law. Features are far apart, life is far apart, structures are far apart. A person could walk half a day toward a dark shape on the horizon and discover it was only a standing rib of black stone, five stories high and no wider than a road lane, polished by ten million years of grit.

The terrain is old beyond easy intuition. Slopes are not jagged unless freshly broken by recent instability; most ridges are blunted and mathematically smooth, curved like pressure waves frozen into rock. Valleys are broad and overdeep, their floors paved with mineral flats: sodium glass, iron oxides, matte silica dust, fields of hexagonal fracture plates that clatter only when struck. In some basins the crust has settled into honeycomb voids—kilometers of shallow collapse pits linked by narrow necks, as if the land exhaled and held its breath forever.

Yet this “final form” is continuously being disrupted.

The atmosphere is in near-constant motion. Because elevation is extreme and thermal gradients run hard along cliff systems, the winds never settle into one prevailing direction. Instead they shear, plunge, rebound. Invisible rivers of cold air spill from plateau lips while warmer layers rise from dark mineral pans; where they meet, columns form—dustless vortices at first, then suddenly loaded with metallic flakes and brittle salts. Storms are frequent but often nearly silent at a distance, seen as moving distortions, walls of refracted light carrying static charge. Lightning is rare in the usual sense; electrical discharge here prefers surface-skimming filaments that run along ridge tops and antenna-like spires, crawling blue-white and gone.

Sound is mostly absent, and that absence has texture. Wind often moves too high above the ground to hear. Sand grains are too coarse and sparse for a steady hiss. You hear your own equipment, your breath, the minute tick of cooling stone at dusk. Occasionally a deep, single report rolls across hundreds of kilometers when a thermal slab detaches from a cliff and fails. Then quiet again.

Vegetation exists, but grudgingly. Not forests—never anything like that. Instead: low mats in fault shadows where vapor condenses each dawn, black-green and metallic sheened; upright “wire shrubs” on sheltered ledges, each a bundle of conductive filaments that harvest electrostatic charge and fold shut during storms; pale lichen fans that grow directly into porous basalt, extracting trace volatiles and rare earths. Their growth rings measure decades. Their blooms, when they happen, are brief and chemical: a sharp iodine smell, a matte violet dust.

Animal life is rare enough that tracks feel ceremonial. Most fauna are small and intermittent: glider-forms that launch from scarp to scarp on thermal lift, then clamp motionless for hours; burrowing hexapods with ceramic shells that can survive charge events by grounding into mineral veins; translucent scavenger kites that feed on microbial films after condensation mornings. There are larger signatures—occasional long, sinuous trench marks where something heavy crossed a dust pan weeks ago—but sightings are uncommon, debated, half-mythic.

Scattered across this austere geology are the built things.

They are never clustered into cities. One tower on a ridge. Three low vaults in a basin. A ring of waist-high pylons around a sinkhole. A suspended lattice stretched between two buttes, thin as spider silk at this scale, still intact. The materials do not weather like local stone: matte black composites that shed grit, translucent ceramics with internal moving light, metal-foam blocks whose edges remain exact after millennia of abrasion. Some ruins are plainly dead. Others are dormant. A few are active in ways that make ordinary categories—machine, building, organism—feel inadequate.

In storm hours, certain installations wake. Surfaces reorient to windborne charge. Needle arrays extend, gather, and retract. Buried systems vent brief plumes of noble gas that glow in the slant light. Entire cliff faces sometimes reveal patterned conductivity, as if circuitry had been printed through the rock itself at continental scale. The technology is far beyond modern engineering not because it is flashy, but because it is ecologically embedded: it uses erosion as a clock, turbulence as power source, geologic strain as data channel. It does not resist this world’s violence; it is tuned to it.

There are rules here, if you stay long enough to notice:

1. **Height is hazard and resource at once.** The highest places are clearest, coldest, and most electrically active.  
2. **Shelter is geometric, not material.** A meter of overhang matters more than a meter of wall.  
3. **Stillness is temporary.** If the air is calm, something else is building—charge, temperature inversion, slope failure.  
4. **Old does not mean inert.** Ancient surfaces may be part of active systems older than any recorded culture.  
5. **Distance deceives.** The clear air compresses perspective; storms and structures are almost always farther and larger than they appear.

By evening, normal sunlight turns forgiving. Long shadows flood the troughs, and the high plateaus glow a dry copper-white. The active installations dim. The wire shrubs close. Condensation begins in microfilms where stone radiates heat into the black sky. For perhaps twenty minutes the whole landscape seems almost gentle: an enormous quiet archive, waiting.

Then, somewhere beyond the visible escarpment, turbulence lifts again—new plumes, new charges, new collapse—and the old world resumes its restless motion, endlessly reworked but never remade.
