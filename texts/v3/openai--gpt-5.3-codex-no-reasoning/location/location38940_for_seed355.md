The city sits in a shallow basin so low and flat that water never really leaves it. It only changes shape.

By day—if there is a day here—you can trace old river geometry in the streets: broad avenues bowed like meanders, plazas sagging into saucer-like pans, whole districts divided by long, patient channels where stone steps descend into dark, warm water. The ground is mineral-rich clay, black when wet, pale when it dries, but it almost never dries. Moisture lives just under every surface. Walls sweat. Pavement beads. Iron blooms with thick orange rust and then wears it smooth again under constant dripping.

There are few plants. Not none—never quite none—but sparse, stubborn growth: clumps of reed in cracked fountains, thin mats of salt-tolerant moss along the north sides of buildings, occasional ropey vines that descend from broken cornices and stop halfway, as if they forgot what soil is. Most roofs are bare. Courtyards hold mineral crusts instead of gardens. The wetness here does not become lushness; the heat takes too much too quickly, and the chemistry of the water seems to favor stone over leaf.

It is hot in the way enclosed baths are hot. Air presses down, heavy and humid, close to skin even in open squares. At ground level there is often a low haze—condensate, steam, evaporative breath from canals and cistern mouths. Above that haze: darkness. Not night exactly, but a permanent low-light condition, as if the sky is made of smoked glass and wet cloth. You can see, but mostly by gradients and gleam: wet surfaces reflecting dim pallor from nowhere in particular. Brightness appears only where liquid catches an angle and gives it back.

Because there are no animals, the silence is immediate and uncanny. No insect whine, no wing movement, no paw in runoff, no scavenger arguments in alley mouths. Sound comes from matter itself: drip, runoff, distant sluice-surges, the faint settling groan of old foundations, occasional sudden cracks when heat expands trapped air in sealed chambers. Sometimes whole building facades tick in sequence as temperature shifts pass through them. If wind comes, it comes as a low pressure-wave, not a rustle—there is too little foliage to translate it.

The built world is dense in intention, sparse in occupancy. Causeways, arcades, retaining walls, drainage towers, stepped reservoirs, bridged roofs, culverts big enough to walk through—everything suggests a large urban civilization shaped around water management over very long ages. The architecture has passed beyond pristine engineering into a mature truce with erosion. Edges are rounded. Carvings have slumped into abstract forms. Stair treads are cupped by centuries of runoff. Buttresses have grown mineral skirts from persistent seepage. Even where structures fail, they fail slowly, becoming spillways, terraces, new channels. Nothing is sudden ruin; everything is conversion.

At district scale, the city is porous. Open lots where whole blocks once stood now serve as shallow flood gardens without gardens—retention fields tiled in old masonry, filling and emptying with weather cycles. Raised walkways connect intact quarters across these basins. In low periods, the basins become flats of cracked silt and reflective puddles. In high periods, they merge into inland lagoons that mirror the dark sky, punctured by rooflines and tower stumps. Orientation depends on vertical markers: siphon spires, bellless belfries, chimney forests from ancient bathhouses.

And then the world’s rules start to slip.

Water here does not obey one level. Two canals can cross without touching, each holding its own surface tension like stacked glass. Rain can fall upward from hot stone into cloud, then return minutes later as cold needles. Condensation gathers into hanging beads beneath arches, grows to fist-size, and drifts sideways instead of dropping, as if searching for a remembered riverbed. Some alleys are wet only when no one is looking directly into them; pools appear in peripheral vision, then are dry underfoot. Reservoirs may contain rooms: standing at the parapet you see stairs descending through the surface into lit darkness, yet when entered the water is only knee-deep and warm.

The darkness participates. Shadows detach from structures and remain after collapse, geometric voids standing in humid air. Light does not spread evenly; it pools like liquid. You can walk from near-black into a pale zone with no source, where every droplet shines and every wall seems newly varnished, then step out and find that zone gone when viewed from behind.

Despite the weirdness, the place feels old, settled, in equilibrium with itself. This is not catastrophe frozen mid-event; it is a long-lived regime. The active processes are cyclical and infrastructural: pressure shifts in buried cistern networks, periodic overflow through terraced channels, thermal breaths from subterranean chambers, mineral deposition and dissolution, slow architectural migration as foundations subside and platforms are relaid above them. The city is continuously remade without builders.

There are rules, if you stay long enough to learn them:

- Vertical surfaces are never dry below shoulder height.
- Sound carries farther over warm water than over stone; whispers can cross plazas at flood stage.
- The darkest streets are often the safest to traverse; bright glints can indicate unstable depth.
- Old red masonry sweats fresh water; black stone sweats brine.
- When three drips sync into a steady triplet rhythm, a pressure release is coming somewhere nearby.
- Pools that reflect stars are not reflecting the sky.

At the basin’s edge, where elevation rises only slightly, the city thins into embanked roads and half-submerged gateworks opening onto salt flats. Beyond, darkness swallows distance quickly, but occasionally you glimpse other low silhouettes: another district, another water-field, maybe another city built to the same hydrologic logic. Between them lie broad, quiet expanses where shallow water lies over old paving, warm as skin, with no ripple unless the ground itself exhales.

It is a hot, wet, mostly silent urban marsh without marsh-life—an architecture of seepage and memory, weathered into competence, governed by fluid laws that have stopped pretending to be physical and become local instead. The place does not feel abandoned. It feels occupied by process.
