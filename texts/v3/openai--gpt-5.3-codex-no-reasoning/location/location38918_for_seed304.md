The town sits where the ground has not quite decided to be land.

It is low, nearly level from afar, but up close it breaks into knuckles and channels: dark ridges of new-thrown earth, slick clay shelves, shallow basins full of standing water. Nothing here looks ancient. Edges are still sharp. Banks collapse in fresh scars. Even the stones seem recently uncovered, their faces unweathered and pale where they’ve been split by frost and wet, and almost black where water has already learned their grain.

You enter it by causeway, because there are no true streets at ground level—only raised paths, timber-laid and mud-packed, that cross one another over drowned courtyards and reedless ponds. The settlement is dense, compressed into a small bowl of ground only a few hundred paces wide, ringed by lower marsh that gleams like hammered tin under cloud. Houses press shoulder to shoulder as if for warmth: wattle walls plastered with clay, low stone foundations, roofs shingled with bark and stitched hide where wood runs short. Between them run channels no wider than a handcart, water moving through with patient intent.

Everything is wet, but not violently so. Not a river-city. A seep-city.

Water appears from places you do not expect: from the seams between paving stones, from the underside of stairs, from ropes left overnight that drip in the morning as though they had rained upward. Clay jars sweat. Door lintels bead with moisture. In cool hours, breath hangs low in alleys and then is gone. The people here build not against floods, but against persistence—against the constant touch of damp.

Vegetation is sparse, almost reluctant. A few tough sedges at the margins. Lichen crusting the north walls. Thin willow whips in the softest spots, coppiced hard before they can drink too deep and loosen foundations. Most green is cultivated in boxes and roof trays: bitter herbs, onion shoots, medicinal mosses. Wild growth finds little room; stone, timber, cordage, and human arrangement have taken nearly every surface.

And still the place feels alive with process. Poles are always being reset where mud has shifted. New retaining wicker is woven each week along trench sides. Children tamp clay into cracks with flat stones. Potters scrape silt from settling pits at dawn. Tanners stretch skins under awnings where smoke keeps mold away. At every corner, someone is maintaining an edge between shape and slump.

The soundscape is quiet in the way of concentration. No grand market roar, no great animal calls. You hear:
- water ticking from eaves,
- soft footfalls on damp boards,
- the knock of wooden pegs driven into wet ground,
- distant mallet-on-stake,
- murmured speech kept close to the mouth.
When wind comes, it moves through rigging lines and drying cords with a low throat-note. Frogs call at night from outside the ring of houses, but even they seem muted by mist.

Fauna is limited but present in careful niches. Marsh birds visit in small numbers—long-legged, watchful, never lingering near people. Eels are trapped in woven funnels where channels narrow. Small pale fish gather in deeper cisterns. Rats exist, of course, but fewer than expected; the town keeps lean marsh-cats with tufted ears and webbed-looking paws, bred for balance on slick beams and for patience over speed.

The unusual quality of the place is not one dramatic impossibility but a stack of small disobediences. Water in shallow basins sometimes forms a skin thick enough to hold ash patterns for days without wind disturbing them. Certain alleys exhale chill at midday, as if connected to subterranean voids. Fresh-cut posts, set into specific pits, begin to calcify over a season, mineral sheaths climbing wood grain like bone. No one calls these miracles. They are treated as local rules, learned young, practical as knotwork.

So the world here has rules like these:

1. **Nothing dry is permanent.**  
   Anything that must stay true—doorframes, walkways, storage bins—must breathe, flex, and be reset.

2. **Weight must be spread.**  
   Broad feet on posts, lattice foundations, layered mats beneath stone. Point-loads sink; distributed loads endure.

3. **Flow is safer than stillness.**  
   Stagnant pools sour and undermine. Every household keeps a channel clear, however small, so water has somewhere to go.

4. **High storage, low sleeping.**  
   Goods are kept overhead away from damp rise; people sleep near ground where air is less cutting and heat from clay braziers pools.

5. **No deep digging after rain.**  
   New voids fill fast and unpredictably; the earth can liquefy in pockets. Many stories begin with ignored timing.

At town-center scale—no grand plaza, just a widened platform over converging drains—stands a tower of lashed poles and rammed marl, perhaps four stories, leaning slightly but intentionally so. It serves as watchpoint, weather post, and calendar. Not for stars (cloud takes those often), but for water behavior: marked sticks in gauge wells, chalk lines where condensation reaches at dawn, wind-vane rattles that predict how the marsh will breathe that day.

The climate stays cool most of the year. Not freezing often, but cold enough that fingers redden in morning work and everyone values smoke, wool, and shared walls. Rain comes frequently, sometimes as fine drift for days, sometimes in sudden sheets that drum roofs flat and silver every surface. After rain, the whole settlement seems to inhale: channels quicken, pits brim, ropes darken, and the smell rises—clay, tannin, peat, ash.

Beyond the packed habitations, the boundary is abrupt. Ten steps from the last retaining fence and you are ankle-deep in hummocked mire, then shin-deep, then lost among mirror-pools and raw earth islands newly born and not yet named. The town is small because it must be. It occupies the one coherent knot of ground in a landscape still forming itself.

At dusk, light is ordinary and therefore beautiful: gray-gold on wet plaster, dull gleam on pooled water, soft shadow under bridges. Lamps are few and low-tech—fat in shell cups, wick in tallow, shielded from damp air. Reflections do half the work of illumination. The city doubles itself in every channel, wavering.

It feels less like a place built once and more like a conversation that never ends: between hand and mud, post and seep, plan and settling ground. A crowded, quiet, cool, wet young town, holding together not by resisting change, but by making change part of its architecture.
