The city sits in a basin that was already old when its first walls were laid.

It is below the level of the surrounding land, not as a sudden pit but as a broad, sunken plain of stone and pale dust, ringed by worn escarpments whose edges have been sanded down by ages of dry wind. Nothing here is sharp unless recently broken. Corners slump. Ridges sag. Even rubble looks tired. The whole place has the posture of something that has finished moving.

There is almost no free water. No rivers, no ponds, no dew brightening the mornings. Moisture exists only as a trace, locked deep in porous rock, in waxy tissues, in the breath of animals, in cisterns sealed with clay and pitch. The air is cool but dry enough to pull at your throat. Salt blooms in white fans along old masonry. Metal is rare; it would pit and flake in this mineral air anyway. Most structures are built from compacted earth, fired brick, and blocks of soft sedimentary stone, all in muted creams and umbers that dim light further.

And yet it is lush.

Not lush in the rain-forest sense, but in volume of life—dense canopies of drought-grown forms spread over courtyards and avenues: tall candelabra succulents, rope-vines with leathery leaves, low thickets of silver shrubs, mats of mosslike crust that wake at night from absorbed vapor. The city has learned to build around root systems. Buildings are buttressed by living columns; roofs are latticed for shade plants; old plazas are now orchards of barrel trunks and fan-crowns. Green appears in dusty blue, iron-red, gray-violet, and deep matte jade. Every plant looks armored, hoarding, patient.

From a distance the settlement and the ecology are inseparable: long, low districts draped in vegetation, separated by broad dry channels that once carried flash floods centuries apart. Those channels are now roads, market grounds, migration routes, and wind corridors. The urban plan follows topography and ancient drainage logic, because in a place this dry, even hypothetical water still rules design.

The light is thin. The basin walls shorten dawn and steal evening; suspended dust softens noon to an amber haze. Shadows are present all day, and people favor overhangs, covered walks, and deep-set windows. At night, cold settles quickly. Pale lichen on stone and reflective salts make darkness readable without ever becoming bright.

What overwhelms a newcomer is the sound.

It is deafening, constant, layered: the roaring passage of animal herds through stone streets; the percussive clatter of hooves and chitin on paving; calls, bellows, wingbeats under vaulted arcades; market shouting; drumming used for distance communication; wind bellowing through tuned vents and hollow towers. The city’s architecture amplifies noise. Many buildings are old resonant shells, and whole neighborhoods hum when gusts align. Quiet exists only in sealed storerooms and burial chambers.

Fauna is abundant and embedded in daily life. Herding species graze scrub terraces beyond the ring walls, then are brought through city funnels at dusk. Burrowing grazers tunnel beneath orchards, turning soils. Cliff-nesters pack the basin walls in colonies. Insects and analogues—thick-shelled, dry-adapted, immensely numerous—work every scrap of organic matter before decay can fail. Predators are visible but tolerated, even useful; city law reserves certain corridors for their movement so they keep rodent populations in check. Domestication is broad but low-tech: fiber harnesses, woven muzzles, wooden yokes, whistles, hand signals. No engines, no circuitry, no synthetic hum—only muscle, habit, and architecture.

Because change is slow here, systems are conservative. District boundaries follow lines older than memory. Compost pits are inherited. Shade rights are codified by custom. Seed varieties are named like family members. Repairs happen constantly but incrementally: one brick, one beam, one woven screen at a time. Nothing is demolished quickly; it is pared down, repurposed, absorbed. A collapsed hall becomes a pen, then a garden wall, then a terrace.

The rules of the place are simple and strict:

- **Capture what passes** (dust, dung, seed, wind, fleeting humidity).
- **Lose nothing volatile** (water, coolness, fertile soil, breeding stock).
- **Build thick, build low, build to shade.**
- **Leave movement lanes open** for herds, wind, and people.
- **Never trust abundance**; any surplus is seasonal illusion.

So the landscape feels paradoxical but coherent: a cool dry underland, ancient and mostly stable, urban yet pre-industrial, sparse in spacing but rich in living mass, dim in light but loud beyond comfort. It is a place where civilization did not conquer nature or retreat from it. It interlocked with it, slowly, over so long a time that stone, plant, animal, and street now seem to have been planned together from the start.
