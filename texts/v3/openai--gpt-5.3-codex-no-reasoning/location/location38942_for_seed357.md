From a distance, the place looks like a city that a rainforest dreamed, then forgot how to stop growing.

It sits high—on a lifted table of dark stone and young, raw ridges—where the air should be thin and dry, but isn’t. Heat rises from every surface. Clouds move low, snagging on sharp escarpments and unfinished cliffs, and then simply unravel into mist. Water is never absent. It clings to edges, beads on vertical faces, leaks from seams in walls, gathers in long reflective gutters that look engineered until you notice they are lined with root tissue, not brick.

Everything is built. Nothing is mechanical.

At first that sounds contradictory, but here “built” means assembled by growth: buttressed corridors braided from living trunks; plazas paved in interlocked bark-plates; retaining walls made of fused reed-columns, still green and pulsing with sap. Entire districts rise in terraces of vegetation shaped into intent. There are arches that have never been cut, only trained. Towers of vine-wrapped lattice stand with the confidence of architecture and the metabolism of orchards.

Because density is low, these structures do not crowd one another. Instead, they are separated by broad wet expanses—warm marsh courts, shallow mirror-basins, fields of giant horsetail-like stems spaced so far apart each casts its own distinct shadow. You can see far: across kilometers of living construction, interrupted by open voids full of steam and reflected sky. It feels both abundant and empty.

The ground is young and still deciding what it is. Edges are sharp, slopes abrupt, channels recently carved. Fresh collapses expose bright mineral layers beneath the plant skin. In some places the terrain looks like it lifted last season, still angular, with root-mats racing to bind it before the next rainburst. Erosion has started but not matured—water cuts quick, narrow runnels; mud shelves shear off whole and slide into lower pools, where new growth immediately stitches over them.

The atmosphere is loud. Not with creatures—there are none—but with process:
- continual sheet-drip from leaf-eaves thirty meters up,
- sudden trunk-cracks as water-swollen fibers shift,
- the slap of broad fronds in gust fronts,
- rushing overflow through vascular canals in the walls,
- low thunder rolling along the plateau rim,
- and, underneath everything, a steady interior gurgle from living infrastructure moving water uphill and down again.

In heat-heavy afternoons, condensation forms visibly. Cool fog fingers spill from high galleries into sunlit courtyards and vanish in minutes. Then rain returns in violent, localized bursts: one district hammered white with water while another, a kilometer away, steams quietly under clear light. The place is in constant hydraulic argument with itself.

Without animals, pollinators, or grazers, plant forms have taken over every ecological role through strange strategies. Reproduction happens by pressure-release pods that burst in humidity spikes, by floating rafts of spores riding flood channels, by root grafting across entire neighborhoods. Competition is architectural: whichever species can span widest, anchor deepest, and redirect runoff wins territory. Some groves build literal overpasses to steal sunlight from rival canopies. Some mat-plants exude slick films that redirect foot-deep water away from competitors and into their own cistern tissues.

And there are rules—silent but strict, legible once you watch awhile:

1. **Water must move.**  
   Any basin that stagnates is invaded and transformed into channel, sponge, or lift-column within days.

2. **Nothing remains singular.**  
   A wall becomes trellis, becomes reservoir, becomes seedbed. Every structure is temporary in function, even if permanent in mass.

3. **Height invites weather.**  
   The tallest living towers are constantly reshaped by wind-tear and regrowth; stability here is rhythmic, not static.

4. **Open space is defended.**  
   The vast gaps are not neglect—they are active floodplains, pressure-release zones, and light wells. Growth approaches them, then halts at invisible hydrological boundaries.

The strangeness is strongest at twilight, when normal light softens and the wet surfaces start returning the day in fragments. Whole avenues of trunk-columns glow with reflected sky from below because their roots stand in shallow water. Mist catches between elevated walkways of woven cane and turns them into suspended, fading lines. You realize the “city” has no center. It is a regional organism: scattered precincts of intention linked by runoff, heat, and growth memory.

If you stand still long enough, you can watch change occur at human timescale—tendrils tightening around a support, a drainage lip buckling and redirecting a stream, a fresh green seam sealing a crack in a load-bearing stem. Turbulence here does not mean random destruction. It means relentless revision. The landscape is always under construction by life, always partly flooded, always warm, always loud, and never, even briefly, finished.
