The high basin does not look old enough to be called a valley.

From a distance, it appears as a tilted green plain laid over unfinished stone, lifted almost to cloud height. The contours are broad and open—no tight canyons, no dramatic teeth of mountain—just long, patient swells and shallow troughs where water gathers and never quite leaves. Bright light floods everything. Even when clouds pass, they are pale and thin, and the landscape keeps shining through them: wet leaf-gloss, silver films over rock, white sky reflected in every standing pool.

Underfoot, the ground is more plant than soil. Mats of sedge, moss, and low, ropey vine-roots knit the surface into a springy skin. In places it holds your weight; in places a boot heel breaks through and dark water wells up from inches below. The vegetation has not arranged itself into forest or meadow in the usual way. It is continuous but not crowded—an enormous living fabric stretched across open space, with individual shrubs or stunted trees standing far apart like punctuation marks on a page already full of green script.

Because erosion is still slight, the geology feels raw, as if recently exposed. Slabs of pale igneous rock jut out smooth and uncarved, their edges blunt rather than worn. There are no deep gullies yet, only tentative channels where rainwater has begun to choose routes downhill. You can watch landscape in the act of deciding itself: a rill that this season cuts three new centimeters into peat, a seep that stains stone orange with mineral runoff, a new pond where last year there was only saturated turf. Nothing happens quickly, but everything is underway.

Water is everywhere and always moving, though often by seep, drip, and ooze rather than torrent. It threads through roots, gathers in clear lenses atop clay, condenses on cold metal, and hangs from every blade at dawn. Large boulders weep from hairline cracks. In the mornings, low vapor lifts off the marsh flats and then thins into the bright air. The basin smells of wet chlorophyll, iron, and cold mud.

Scattered across this living wetland are industrial interventions, sparse but unmistakable:  
- a pumping station on pilings, painted once in warning yellow, now lichen-flecked;  
- narrow-gauge service tracks raised on gravel ribs, vanishing into reeds;  
- a line of power poles stepping across the open ground, wires humming in damp weather;  
- intake towers at two reservoir lips where water is measured, diverted, and sent downslope through buried mains.

None of it forms a city. Each structure stands largely alone, with a maintenance shack or tool shed nearby, then kilometers of open vegetated ground before the next sign of human intention. The built world here is intermittent, functional, and weather-exposed, as if occupation is temporary but recurring.

The sound is astonishingly loud for such an apparently empty place. The constant deafening layer comes from overlap: wind combing through dense wet grasses; pump turbines throbbing at low frequency; pressure valves releasing in long, breathy roars; guy-wires singing; sheet runoff hissing over broad rock faces; and, after rain, millions of droplets falling from leaf to leaf in endless staggered percussion. You don’t hear discrete events so much as a sustained environmental engine. Silence never fully arrives.

Fauna is present but sparse. A few long-legged marsh birds cross the flats in ones and twos. Insects appear in sudden local swarms over warm pools, then vanish with the wind. Occasionally small amphibians pulse in ditches near outflow grates. Large animals are mostly absent—perhaps displaced by exposure, altitude, and noise, perhaps simply rare at this stage of ecological settling.

Light defines the mood. At this elevation the sky is close and hard-edged; brightness can be almost architectural. Shadows are short and clean. Water doubles the luminance. Even rusted machinery seems outlined in glare. At midday, the whole basin feels overdeveloped, every wet surface a mirror. Toward evening, the same brightness softens into a cool, blue-white sheen, and the plants seem to absorb it rather than reflect it.

The rules of the place are simple and strict:

1. **Anything unrooted drifts.**  
   Tools, planks, even gravel migrate slowly downslope in storm months.

2. **Anything untreated is overgrown.**  
   Vegetation does not rush, but it does not stop. Catwalk mesh, valve housings, warning signs—all become substrate.

3. **Water is shallow until suddenly deep.**  
   Most surfaces are passable until one step finds an old settling pocket, a collapsed root chamber, a hidden cistern edge.

4. **Infrastructure must flex.**  
   Foundations are broad, joints oversized, pipes looped; rigid construction fails as the wet ground subtly shifts.

Over decades, this high wet plain will likely become more carved, more sorted: channels deepening, peat thickening, industrial lines either consolidated or abandoned. Right now it sits in a transitional calm—bright, loud, green, and unfinished—an elevated marshland where biology is abundant, machinery is sparse, and geology is still in first draft.
