At first it reads as a forest at night—until you realize the “trees” are load-bearing columns, bolted in gridded rows, and every leaf is stamped from metal foil thin as onion skin.

The place sits high, but not as a mountain sits high. It is a raised continent of fabrication: a plateau of black alloy and kiln-baked ceramic, uplifted above a deeper dark where old basins once were. Wind climbs to it, hot and mineral, and loses whatever moisture it had long before reaching the upper shelves. Nothing drips here. Nothing softens. Even rot is a dry process: flaking, powdering, exfoliating into glittering dust.

And yet it is completely overgrown.

Not overgrown by vines in the old sense—overgrown by engineered botany that has replaced architecture and become architecture. Trunkworks, people call it in old warning plaques: vascular pylons as broad as chimneys, bark like layered slate, roots braided through cable conduits and vent mouths. Canopies spread in overlapping planes from gantry to gantry, leaf-panels tuned to absorb dim frequencies of heat rather than visible light. In the near-dark they drink infrared glow from furnaces and buried lines. Photosynthesis here is almost metaphor; this is thermosynthesis, electrosynthesis, chemistry running through cellulose laced with copper salts.

Because the light is so low, the whole landscape is legible by edge and ember. You see horizons in negative: serrations of frond arrays against a dull, rust-red haze. Here and there industrial towers breathe a deep orange through slatted throats, then shutter closed. Between them, miles of vegetal mass fill every gap—mats, fans, thorn-lattices, scaffold reeds, bladder pods full of volatile gas. Packed so tightly that “ground” is rarely visible. When a seam opens, you glimpse substrate: tiled maintenance decks worn smooth by ages, rivets swallowed by root, old warning stripes fossilized into varnish.

Age is everywhere. Not ruin—settling. This world has had so long to continue that change and permanence have merged. Erosion did not carve rivers; there are none. It carved by heat pulse, abrasive dust, cyclic expansion. Corners became rounds, rounds became planes. Entire factory facades have weathered into soft geometries like ancient cliff dwellings, except every surface is alive with leaf-skin and slow pumping veins. The oldest growth forms buttress-walls thick as fortifications. You can read centuries in their banding: dark resin years, pale ash years, years of copper bloom.

It is quiet in the way a machine room can be quiet when everything is functioning perfectly at scale. No birds, no insects, no mammal movement—no quick noises at all. Sound comes as long events. A distant conveyor starts with a basso shudder and runs for ten minutes beneath hearing, felt in the ribs more than the ear. Pressure valves sigh. Seed-hoppers click open in synchronized chains like muted castanets. Somewhere out in the dark, a whole stand of hollow stalks turns as one to face a heat bloom, and their dry leaves rub together with the whisper of many pages being turned.

The turbulence is not loud; it is structural. Growth fronts migrate overnight, sealing passages, overloading catwalks, forcing reroutes of steam and current. Thermal storms roll across the plateau in invisible walls—air suddenly too hot, then merely hot, then charged with metallic grit that makes every frond bristle and discharge blue pinpricks. Spore-fogs ignite without flame, brief ghostly glows that travel through canopy layers like thought through a brain. Industrial systems respond: shutters slam, louvers pivot, coolant salts are blown through ducts, pruning arms descend and shear back meters of growth in showers of dry blades. By next cycle it has grown back denser.

Ecology here has no fauna because it has no empty trophic niche for motion-flesh. The plants do everything. They mine, refine, scaffold, store, signal, defend, recycle. Some crack bed-ceramic with acid roots and draw out trace metals. Some extrude hard shells that become bearings and insulators for neighboring colonies. Some are sacrificial heat sinks, black velvet fields that char and slough, protecting the slower vascular cores beneath. Reproduction is infrastructural: cut one conduit and a hundred runners invade the wound, weaving a new pipe-organ of xylem and wire.

The rules of the world feel old and binding:

- **Water is memory, not medium.** Life runs on oils, molten salts, and pressurized gases. Any true liquid water flashed away ages ago.
- **Darkness is resource distribution.** Light is too scarce to waste; heat differentials and electrical leakage are the primary currencies.
- **Space does not stay empty.** Any volume unoccupied will be filled—by growth, by scaffolding, by condensers, by dust-cemented husks.
- **Nothing is wild, nothing is fully controlled.** Every organism is built; every built thing is naturalized; intention has aged into ecosystem.

If you travel far enough across this open, crowded highland, you find districts without ever finding a border. In one region, fan-palms of carbon felt rise above smelter orchards, their undersides glowing dimly where thermocouples nest. In another, kilometer-long trellis valleys hold hanging pod-lathes that spin seed-capsules from recycled lignin and slag. There are old transit trenches roofed entirely by root-glass, where darkness becomes nearly absolute and navigation depends on faint inductive hums in the handrails. There are uplift ridges where ancient wind has polished every exposed stem to a satin sheen, and the whole slope clicks in tiny thermal contractions at dawnless “morning.”

And still the place keeps becoming itself. Every cycle, a little drier, a little denser, a little more integrated: factory into forest, forest into factory, until the distinction is ceremonial at best. A civilization might once have built it. Now it persists by vegetative industry alone—a hot, high, black-green expanse in near-night, breathing quietly, changing violently, older than intention.
