It is a **garden under stone**, and the first wrong impression is that it should be dead.

You enter through a split in the bedrock no wider than shoulders, a crack that descends only a little before opening into a pocketed chamber the size of a village square. The ceiling hangs low in places, high in others, all of it raw and young—jagged lips of dark mineral, sharp as broken pottery, with none of the rounded softness of old caves. This place was not worn into being by rivers. It was opened violently, then stopped. A wound that healed only enough to hold shape.

And yet it is lush.

Not with leaves, not with anything wet. The growth here is mineral-botanical, dry-bodied and thriving in warm air rich with dust and chemical breath. From the floor rise clustered **glass-reed thickets**, translucent stalks jointed like bamboo, each segment filled not with sap but drifting metallic pollen that flashes when disturbed. Between them spread mats of **felt lichen**—thick, velvety black-green sheets that drink vapor and heat, not liquid, gripping the stone with fine hooks. Along the walls climb fans of **salt fern**, blades white and brittle-looking, but alive, flexing to minute air-currents from vents in the rock. At a touch they recoil and ring faintly, like struck porcelain.

There is almost no open water anywhere; only, in a few shaded hollows, a skin of viscous amber solvent no deeper than a fingernail. Life here does not rely on flowing liquid. It runs on films, mists, exhalations, and mineral exchange. Roots are more like antennae. Flowers, where they exist, are vents.

The warmth is constant—body-warm, oven-soft, unchanging. No dawn, no cooling night, no weather. The air tastes of copper, gypsum, and something sweetly acrid, like bruised resin. You can spend hours here and never feel a shift in temperature or humidity. Dust motes hover in suspended patterns as if the room has rehearsed its own stillness.

Despite the stillness, it is loud.

Every surface participates in a continuous, layered noise:  
- the dry clatter of reed-stems tapping one another,  
- the hard wing-buzz of thick-bodied cave skippers,  
- throat-drumming from unseen burrows,  
- periodic crack reports as salt growth sheds and falls,  
- and a low architectural hum where wind threads narrow fissures above.

It is not chaotic sound. It is repetitive, cyclical, exact. Like a machine made from ecology.

Animals are everywhere once your eyes tune in. **Shard-mice** dart between roots on six splayed limbs, their fur replaced by overlapping mica scales. **Blind nectar-bats** hang from ceiling hooks, long tongues probing mineral blossoms that open on clocklike intervals. **Carapace grazers**, each no larger than a dinner plate, rasp the lichen mats in slow radial paths, leaving precise spiral clearings that are refilled by growth at the exact same rate. Predators exist too—thin ribbon-snakes that sense vibration through their ribs and strike at sound rather than movement. Population never booms, never crashes. Every mouth has a matched source, every hunter a fixed corridor, every nest a remembered cavity.

The place feels strange not because it is alien alone, but because it is **finished** in a way living places usually are not. Nothing is becoming. Everything is maintaining.

Scattered through this chamber are signs of a people with simple tools and patient habits. Low stone circles mortared with clay. Bone pegs driven into cracks. Ladder rungs carved directly into a wall-face. A few woven screens made from shed reed-skin, hardened with resin, dividing sleeping hollows from work areas. No metal gleam; at most, hammered native copper beads tied as counters or charms. One squat kiln, now cold but intact, lined with salt brick. Grinding basins. Flint blades re-sharpened down to thumbnails.

No settlement sprawls here—just **placements**, each structure far from the next, as if built to avoid interrupting ecological lanes. The inhabitants, if present, would move softly and predictably, harvesting on fixed turns, never taking seed nodes, never blocking vent mouths, never widening paths beyond old marks. In a world this stable, survival is less about innovation than obedience to proven rhythm. Rules become environmental law:

- Don’t shadow the west wall during pollination hour.  
- Don’t kill a singer-bat in nesting month.  
- Don’t scrape lichen below the third notch on the harvest stone.  
- Don’t sleep under the ringing ferns when the ceiling hum deepens.

These are practical rules first, sacred second.

Light is dim but sufficient, generated by colonies of **ember mold** in ceiling seams: not bright points, more a deep red-brown exhalation, as if the rock itself is banked with coals. Some plants reflect this with pale iridescence, so distances are deceptive; a nearby stalk can seem far, and a far wall can feel close. Shadows are soft-edged and almost stationary.

If you stay long enough, the final strangeness settles in: there is no narrative arc here. No storm comes. No bloom season arrives. No ruin advances. No spring refreshes. The same sounds cycle, the same wings beat, the same fragments fall from the same ledges, and new growth replaces old in exact measure. Even the sharpness of the rock remains, because breakage and deposition are balanced to the grain.

A hidden, warm chamber below the surface, nearly dry, loud with life, lush without water, primitive in craft, sparse in construction, and profoundly—almost unnervingly—at equilibrium.
