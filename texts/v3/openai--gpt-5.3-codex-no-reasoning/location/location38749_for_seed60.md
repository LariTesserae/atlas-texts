The place is about the size of a village commons, if a commons had forgotten grass and become a single, breathing machine-garden.

At first glance it reads as a **thicketed basin**—low ground, warm air, everything damp in a way that never quite becomes standing flood. Water is close, always. You can press a heel into the dark floor and watch clear moisture gather around it. The soil is less soil than layered rootfelt: old stems mulched into sponge, vine fibers braided into mats, fungal lace stitching it all together. Every surface seems alive or recently alive. Even the stones are mantled in leaf-thin liverworts and glossy films that bead condensation.

Nothing stands alone. Plants have packed the space to near-solidness, rising in columns, arches, drapes, and shelves. Trunks twist together into load-bearing bundles. Reeds emerge through bromeliad cups. Ferns root in the pockets of bigger ferns. Flowering vines run from one support to another until the supports vanish, and what remains is a green architecture with no obvious beginning. You move through **rooms made by growth**: a vaulted lane where broad leaves overlap like shingles; a chamber where hanging roots make a bead curtain; a low tunnel kept open only because warm pipework runs beneath it.

Because there is pipework.

Scattered through the vegetation are industrial relics still functioning, though none of them dominate. A rusted pump tower leans among fig-like buttresses, its flywheel turning at a patient pace. Ceramic-insulated cables run between iron posts, half-swallowed by creepers, carrying enough current to keep small motors humming. There are pressure gauges under glass fogged from inside. A belt drive loops from an electric motor to a chain of paddles that lift water from one shallow cistern to another, each cistern edged with roots that have grown into the brick joints and made them watertight again. It is unclear whether the machinery supports the plants or the plants now support the machinery’s continued motion. Likely both.

The **soundscape** is fuller than the place looks from outside. Loud, but not abrupt: a constant body of noise.  
- Rotary hum from old motors.  
- Rhythmic clack of an offset valve arm.  
- Dripfalls from leaf tips, multiplied into thousands of small taps.  
- Slow gurgle in buried culverts.  
- Periodic hiss as steam vents from a moss-rimmed manifold.  

Wind barely reaches the floor, so sounds don’t disperse; they stack. You feel them in your sternum more than your ears after a while.

Light is ordinary daylight, yet filtered and sorted by leaf density. The upper canopy catches the white of noon; below, illumination falls in green-gold slabs and moving cutouts. Nothing is truly dark by day, but contrast is high: bright planes on waxy leaves, then pockets where detail dissolves into layered silhouettes. After rain—which is frequent enough that the place smells perpetually post-rain—sunlight pulls vapor from every surface. Mist forms in low hollows and around warm housings, so that machinery appears and disappears in its own breath.

Ecology here is overwhelmingly vegetal, with fauna sparse and specialized. You rarely see large animals. Instead:  
- Thin amphibians tucked in valve shadows, skin matching oxidized metal.  
- A few nectar bats at dusk, threading precise routes through vine corridors.  
- Beetles that bore only dead cane, creating tiny flute-holes that whistle when pressure changes.  
- Occasional pale eels in sumps, survivors from older flood channels.  

The true movers are plants and water. Vines tighten over months and slowly bend railings. Root masses redirect trickles into new runnels. Floating mats in settling ponds accrete, sink, and rise again with trapped gases. Change is slow enough to feel stable day to day, fast enough that a path left untended for a season is gone.

There are signs of people, but lightly applied: catwalk fragments, a corrugated shed converted into a seed-drying loft, hand-painted depth marks on cistern walls, a tool rack under an awning of giant leaves. Someone still visits to grease bearings, clear intake screens, maybe harvest medicinal cuttings. Yet the governing rule of the place is not human scheduling. The rule is **mutual encroachment without collapse**. Every structure is being digested, and in being digested it is preserved. Every plant competes for light, and in competing it creates support and shade and channels for moisture that keep the whole system buffered.

The weirdness lives in that balance. Metal blooms with living veneers that conduct tiny currents. Some vines have learned the warmth pattern of live cables and coil along them like script. Pressure changes in the old network trigger synchronized opening of certain flowers—white throats unfurling whenever the main pump shifts load at dawn. In one corner, a bank of gauge faces has become a pollinator station: each cracked dial holds rain, each rain pocket hosts a miniature wetland, each wetland feeds roots that now pin the entire panel upright.

If you stood here long enough, you’d stop dividing things into natural and built. You’d track instead gradients: wetter to drier, warmer pipe-runs to cool basins, loud heart of machinery to muffled perimeter thickets. You’d learn that this is a low, established world with boundaries, densely occupied down to the centimeter, where almost nothing dramatic happens quickly—and where, year by year, everything changes shape together.
