At first glance it looks impossible: a **high hanging basin** of stone, warm as skin, full of green life, and almost no water anywhere.

The basin sits near a ridgeline, cupped between black volcanic shoulders. Its floor is not soil in the usual sense but a quilt of **porous mineral shelves**—pale pumice, iron-red crust, bands of glassy slag—broken by fissures that breathe. Warm gases rise from those cracks in irregular pulses, and the whole place seems to inhale and exhale underfoot. You can stand still and feel the ground’s rhythm through your soles.

There are no streams, no pools, no wet mud. Yet vegetation thrives in profusion by learning a different chemistry. The “plants” are mostly **silicate fronds, wax-limned vines, and bladder-thickets** that drink vapor directly from the air and from the mineral breath of the ground. Their tissues are oily, resinous, and springy; if cut, they release fragrant fogs instead of sap. Many are a deep matte green, but in dim light you catch copper, violet, and soot-blue sheens where they’ve grown metallic skins against desiccation.

Because light is muted—sun filtered through high dust and constant geothermal haze—the vegetation grows broad and thin, maximizing every photon. Leaves are like banners. Moss-like films coat north-facing stone in velvet sheets. Tall, jointed stalks bend and ring softly as hot gusts move through them.

The basin is small enough to cross in an hour, maybe less, bounded by broken walls of uplifted rock. But it feels larger because of motion. The air is never still. **Thermal squalls** chase each other from vent to vent, lifting grit, seeds, ash, and insects into sudden spirals. Whole patches of light and shadow migrate over the terrain as vapor plumes thicken and thin. Every few minutes some part of the place rearranges itself: a crust collapses, a gas pocket erupts with a booming cough, a leaning mineral arch finally sloughs off in a clatter of shards.

It is loud—astonishingly loud for somewhere so contained.  
Not one noise, but many layered systems:

- the low organ-note of wind through vent chimneys,  
- insect choirs clicking in synchronized bursts,  
- hollow knocking from seedpods striking stone,  
- reptilian bark-calls ricocheting off basin walls,  
- abrupt detonations when volatile pockets ignite and snuff out,  
- a constant papery hiss as dry leaves scrape and rattle.

Fauna is everywhere, though rarely clustered. Life here is abundant but **spaced**: each species keeps territory around the precious vent-zones and shade-lines. You see six-legged gliders with translucent sail-skins that harvest heat gradients; squat grazers with ceramic-looking plates that rasp mineral lichen from rocks; ribbon-bodied hunters that move by contracting like bellows, tasting the air with fan-shaped tongues. Burrowing things drum on the undersides of crust shelves, making the ground answer in percussion.

Many creatures are adapted to dryness in uncanny ways. They store not water but **solvent oils** in abdominal sacs. Some exhale cooling mists from side vents to regulate body temperature. Others farm symbiotic films on their backs—microbial mats that fix trace compounds out of volcanic gases.

Scattered signs of people—or people-like tool users—appear between thickets and vents. Primitive, careful construction: stacked-stone windbreaks, bone-and-reed ladders, clay chimneys fitted over fumaroles, sling-nets for catching airborne seed bladders and glider fauna. No roads, only braided footpaths marked by polished stepping stones where crust won’t crack. A few half-shelters cling to cliff alcoves, roofed in layered hide and lacquered leaves, weighted with slag blocks. Metal exists but is rare and simple: hammered copper hooks, tin bells, patched knives. Most tools are stone, shell, fired clay, and woven fiber.

Their rules seem shaped by turbulence. You infer taboos from arrangement:

- No dwelling is built directly over a breathing fissure.  
- Vent-crowns are ringed with carved warning stakes.  
- Seed-harvest platforms are temporary, rebuilt often.  
- Sound signals matter—hanging chimes and drum posts at intervals, likely to communicate over the constant noise and shifting visibility.

Erosion has done its long work here. Nothing feels rawly new. Even active vents wear collars of old deposits; even shattered slopes have lichen-maps and established burrows. It is a mature instability, a place that has been turbulent for so long that turbulence itself is the settled order.

Toward evening—if “evening” can be called in this dimness—the basin glows from below. Vents shine ember-orange. Leaves pick up that underlight and become lantern-thin. Fauna activity peaks: mating calls, territorial drumming, cloudbursts of winged scavengers. Predators move in silhouettes along ridge lines while the basin floor flickers with heat mirage and floating spores.

The strangest part is how alive it feels without water’s softness. This is lushness without rivers, fertility without rain: an ecology built on breath, heat, and mineral appetite. A dry garden in a roaring bowl of stone, always on the edge of rearrangement, never collapsing entirely, never calming, endlessly becoming itself again.
