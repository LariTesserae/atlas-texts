The continent is a high, cold table of stone so old it has forgotten the drama of mountains.

From a distance it looks level, but as you cross it you find that “flat” means only that nothing is sudden. The ground rises in miles-long swells, then settles into basins broad as inland seas, then rises again in peeled layers of slate, basalt, and pale sediment polished by eras of wind. Every edge has been softened, not by gentleness but by duration. Ridges have been worn into ribs. Cliffs have slumped into stepped shelves. Even broken rock has had time to become orderly.

There is water here, but it never commits to being a river for long. Moisture appears in strips and pockets: black seep lines under overhangs, veils of condensation at dawn on the lee side of old walls, mirror-thin pools in depressions where stone salts hold dampness for weeks after rain. Rains come as passing events—cold fronts pushing over the highlands, dropping needles of water that rattle like grain on slate and vanish into fissures. Some basins hold tarns, quiet and opaque, rimmed with mineral crusts that grow and flake with each freeze-thaw cycle. In winter, fog forms without warning in low ground and hangs at knee height, as if the land is exhaling.

Vegetation should not be this abundant in so much cold and stone, but it is—just not in the way a forest is abundant. It comes in networks. Carpets of dark moss fill every long crack. Ropey lichens drape boulders in gray-green beards. Dwarf reedfields gather around seepage trenches, then abruptly stop at dry stone. Whole districts are crosshatched by low, woody shrubs no higher than the shin, their branches lacquered by ice, roots spread wide through gravel skin. In sheltered cuts between ancient terraces, you find dense pockets: thickets of broad-leafed coldplants with waxy black leaves, steam fern in geothermal breath zones, mats of red cushion growth that feel warm from slow metabolism. Green is everywhere, yet never crowded; life occupies what little it needs and leaves great distances bare.

There are no animals. No tracks, no calls, no wing-shadows, no burrows. Pollination is done by wind pulses and electrostatic dust attraction. Seed dispersal is mostly mechanical: pods that crack in frost and fling grains downslope, buoyant fronds that tumble for miles, spores that wake only after being abraded by airborne grit. Decay is fungal, patient, and nearly silent. The ecology turns without teeth or eyes.

And still—there are cities.

They are widely spaced, each one sitting where geology and moisture cooperate: at fault-warm basins, along perennial condensate escarpments, around old crater-lakes now mostly sedge and stone. Built at this tech level means stone, fired clay, timber carried from lower valleys, ropes braided from tough hill plants, hammered soft metals where ores permit. No glass towers, no engines. But urban, unmistakably urban: terraces of dwellings cut directly into slopes; causeways laid across frost heave zones; cistern fields; stair networks; dry plazas with windbreak walls; marker monoliths aligned to seasonal light. Their planning is grand, their means simple.

Infrastructure is the art here. Rain-catch combs combing fog from passing fronts. Condensation cloisters: arcades of cold stone where nightly droplets gather and run into channels. Gravity aqueducts built with astonishing precision over tens of kilometers. Heat courts above geothermal seams where food-plants are coaxed through freeze seasons. Signal lines of suspended chimes that can carry coded knocks across still air for leagues without needing fire.

At this scale, weather belongs to the plateau itself. You can stand in clear daylight and watch a snow line moving across a basin two hundred kilometers away. Dust devils made of mica flakes travel like silver columns. Cold katabatic winds pour nightly from higher shelves, then reverse by noon when dark stone warms just enough to lift air. Storms often arrive as horizontal weather: bands of sleet marching at uniform speed, clipping hilltops while valleys remain calm. The sky is normal brightness, ordinary sun and moon—but perspective behaves badly.

That is where the weirdness enters: not spectacle first, but geometry.

Distances dilate. A tower seen on the horizon may take three days to reach, or an hour, and both outcomes are consistent with local maps. Straight roads occasionally return to their starting cistern from the opposite direction without obvious turn. Reflections in shallow pools show cloud layers that are not above the viewer, and sometimes show old versions of nearby ruins still intact. Frost patterns grow in script-like branches that mirror city plans. Echoes answer before the sound that caused them. In some districts, shadows detach at sunrise and lag behind their objects by several minutes, then catch up violently at noon.

The people—few relative to the land—treat this not as anomaly but climate. Their rules of movement are practical metaphysics:

- Never navigate by a single landmark; use triads.
- Cross basin floors only in wind hours, when distances are “tight.”
- Build doorways in pairs facing nonparallel lines, to discourage interior drift.
- Keep water in textured vessels; flat surfaces invite false reflections.
- In mapping, record travel by effort and song-count, not by measured length.

Because sound is scarce, every noise is precise. Wind through reedfields: a dry hiss. Ice slivering off ledges: brittle ticks. Drips in a condensation hall: ceremonial, metronomic. Far quarry hammers: irregular, human. During storms, rooftops murmur as sleet skates over tile. Most of the time, silence stretches so wide that you hear your own clothing stiffen in cold.

At night, light remains ordinary, but the land does not. City lamps—oil, resin, sheltered coals—sit in long rows along terraces, and each flame seems to occupy two positions at once in the peripheral eye. Stars are bright in the thin air. Some constellations appear doubled near the horizon, like old alignments still persisting in worn spacetime. In the high basins, auroral veils sometimes thread low, pale green, silent, snagging on standing stones and making them seem momentarily soft.

Nothing here is young except weather and construction repairs. Everything else has reached a late, stable phase: rock old enough to be patient, plants adapted to intervals, settlements built for recurrence rather than expansion. It is an active world made of slow cycles and quick adjustments. A place vast enough to have its own habits of reality, and old enough that its inhabitants have stopped asking why those habits exist and focused, with great elegance, on living well inside them.
