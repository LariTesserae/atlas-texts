The city is perched where a mountain should still be deciding to be a mountain.

Not ruined—unmade. Young stone, pale and damp, rises in blunt shelves and swollen ridges as if pushed up yesterday by a careful hand. There are no old cliffs here, no long-furrowed canyons, none of the intricate scars of ages. Everything is thick-edged, soft in profile, raw. The ground has the look of bread that has just finished rising: rounded, split in a few new seams, steaming faintly in the cold.

Water sits very near the skin of the place. It beads on rock even when the sky is clear, gathers in shallow mirror-pans, threads through seams as clear trickles. You can’t walk far without hearing dripwater, runwater, spillwater. Not rivers exactly—more a million beginnings of rivers. In the mornings, fog condenses straight out of the air and clings low between structures, turning every lane into a breathing corridor of silver vapor. Roof edges weep. Stair risers shine. Moss drinks from stone that never fully dries.

The settlement itself is compact, almost self-contained: a high-basin town no wider than a long walk, ringed by abrupt uplifts and open sky. It feels urban not by size but by intention. Terraces are cut into the fresh slopes in geometric steps. Retaining walls interlock with drainage channels. Footbridges, aqueduct gutters, plankways, and sluice gates braid through it all in a practical lattice. The people here build as if they are in conversation with water and unfinished geology, and neither party can be ignored.

Materials are immediate and local: split green timber, woven reed matting, clay-packed joints, rough stone blocks pried from nearby beds before they fully harden. Early bronze appears in pins and blades and valve-fittings, but mostly this is a hand-tool city—wooden mallets, stone adzes, rope lifts, lever cranes. Infrastructure exists, but it is muscular rather than mechanical: many hands, pulley songs, coordinated labor. You hear crews before you see them—call-and-response over the sound of channels, rhythmic hammering, wet scrape of sledges over mud-plank.

Because elevation is high and temperature cool, breath shows in the shade even at midday. The light is ordinary and generous—no strange suns, no perpetual dusk—just bright mountain daylight and cloud-shadows sliding slowly across the basin. The unusual quality comes from reflection: so much damp surface, so many wet leaves and pooled stones, that brightness bounces up from below. Faces are lit from under by water. Ceilings in covered walkways shiver with reflected ripples.

Vegetation is exuberant where it can root, and this place gives it every crack. Fern banks spill down stair margins. Thick moss pads stitch together fresh masonry. Dwarf willows and broad-leaf alpine shrubs colonize gutters and retaining joints within weeks of construction. Vines run along rope lines meant for cargo. Garden plots on terraces are intensely green—leaf crops, medicinal herbs, climbing beans on lattice frames—while beyond the built core, the slopes carry wet meadows broken by young conifer stands and low cloud-forest thickets. It is lush, but not old-growth; everything has a quick, opportunistic vigor, as if ecology and architecture are racing to see who can claim a surface first.

Despite that abundance, life is strangely sparse. Few animals commit to the basin. You might spot a pair of long-legged marsh birds probing fresh silt, hear unseen tree-frogs in drainage pools, catch mountain goats ghosting the outer ridges in twos or threes. Insects appear in bursts after rain, then vanish into the cold. No great herds, no predator cries at night. The emptiness between living things is palpable. A person can stand on a high platform and feel the volume of unoccupied air around them—a hollow vastness contained inside a small place.

And it is loud.

Water provides a constant base note: trickle, slap, pour, echo. On top of that sits construction noise—stone set on stone, rope under strain, shouted timing cues, hollow thud of rammed earth. Wind drops into the basin in slow pulses and booms in unfinished culverts. Frogs can suddenly overwhelm an entire quarter after dusk. Even silence here is a wet, resonant silence, never truly still.

Change happens slowly, but visibly. A wall gains a veil of moss over a season. A new seep appears in the market steps and is gradually framed into a channel. A muddy path is laid with stone, then roofed, then lined with planters, then absorbed into the city’s permanent circulatory logic. Nothing catastrophic; rather a patient accretion. The whole basin feels like an early chapter in a very long civic-geologic collaboration.

There are rules to the place, practical and almost ceremonial:

- **Never block first water.** The earliest trickle from a new spring is mapped and left a route.
- **Build with surrender points.** Every structure includes deliberate weak seams so settling ground can crack predictably instead of collapsing.
- **Root before roof.** New districts are planted with binding vegetation before heavy construction begins.
- **High paths for feet, low paths for water.** Everyone learns the drainage hierarchy as a kind of civic literacy.
- **Listen at night.** Unfamiliar sounds in channels are treated as warnings; crews inspect by torch before dawn.

From above, in clear weather, the city looks like a damp green knot tied into pale newborn stone. From within, it feels like living inside an instrument made of terraces and springs: resonant, cool, hand-built, not crowded, and very much in the act of becoming.
