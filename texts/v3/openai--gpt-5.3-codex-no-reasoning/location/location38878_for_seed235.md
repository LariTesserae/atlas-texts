The city begins as cliffs.

Not metaphorical cliffs, not “districts rising like cliffs,” but actual escarpments of dark volcanic stone, stepped and terraced by an age so long that cut and weathering are now indistinguishable. From a distance, under the broad white heat, you can’t tell where geology ends and construction begins. Whole neighborhoods appear to have precipitated out of the mountainside: walls fused to basalt faces, arches grown from columns of mineral flow, streets laid into old lava channels now polished by centuries of moisture and bare feet.

Water is everywhere, but almost never as a river. It gathers in seams, weeps from vertical rock, condenses in shaded alleys, hangs in the air as a persistent hot mist that pearls on skin and stone alike. Rooflines are ribbed with troughs not to store rain—rain is too sudden, too violent, too regular to “collect”—but to guide sheetwater away in controlled curtains. In many plazas, the center is not a monument but a sink basin: a sunken stone bloom where runoff spirals down through grates into cavern cisterns below. You hear those cisterns before you see them, a low subsonic throat-note beneath the city’s louder voice.

And it is loud. Not with traffic, not with animals, not with machines—none of that exists here. The deafening sound is hydraulic and atmospheric: continuous cataract-fall from hundreds of terrace lips, pressure winds pushed through narrow cuts in the architecture, resonant booming where water strikes hollow rock chambers, and the near-constant hiss of evaporation when noon heat slams into wet black stone. Every surface participates. Colonnades whistle. Courtyards drum. Stairwells roar. Even silence, in small interior chambers lined with absorbent moss, has a hard edge because you know it is temporary and surrounded.

Vegetation thrives in this noise as if it were another nutrient. Lushness doesn’t spread evenly; it masses where cracks hold moisture and heat rises from below. Giant strap-leaf plants spill over parapets in thick green tongues. Root mats hang from aqueduct undersides like braided rope. Tree forms, where they occur, are often low-crowned and broad, their trunks buttressed directly into masonry as though the city had planned for them from the founding—or as though the plants arrived first and the city poured itself around them. Ferns fill stair joints. Epiphytes crowd lintels. Entire facades are softened by living skins of vine and waxy leaves, the only cool color in a palette of umber stone, wet charcoal, pale mineral whites, and sudden chlorophyll.

The heat is relentless but not sterile. It is a wet heat at altitude, improbable until you stand in it: high plateau air thinned by elevation, yet burdened with vapor from the perpetual upwelling and frequent convective rains. The horizon is open and immense. Beyond the city terraces, the land breaks into a system of hanging valleys and mesa-backed bowls, all of them linked by causeways and stepped spillways. Clouds develop quickly in the afternoons, towering and brilliant, then burst and pass. Within hours the surfaces steam, and everything returns to its prior state, as if reset.

That quality of reset is part of the strangeness.

This place does not seem to progress. It cycles through events without accumulating consequence. Water pours, floods, drains. Leaves unfurl and remain unfurled, never browning. Stone erodes—but only to a finished shape and no further. Moss creeps to an exact boundary and stops. A cracked lintel never collapses, never repairs, simply remains perfectly cracked. You can watch droplets fall from an overhang and begin to suspect they are not “new” droplets at all, but the same sequence repeating with microscopic precision. Time here feels laminated: layers present simultaneously, not moving past one another.

The urban fabric reflects this equilibrium. There are no ruins because ruin implies process. There are no new buildings because novelty implies timeline. Instead there is established form everywhere, mature and saturated: plazas whose paving has been worn into shallow dishes; channels whose edges are rounded smooth; stair treads cupped by countless passages, though no passerby is ever seen long enough to prove movement. The city is inhabited, unmistakably—doors are open, vessels are set out, woven canopies cast shade, irrigation gates stand at specific angles—yet population manifests as arrangement rather than action. Presence without event.

With almost no animal life, the ecology is botanical, fungal, and mineral. Pollination is handled by air pulses and water transfer. Spore clouds release at predictable humidity thresholds, visible as golden veils in side streets after rain. Fruiting bodies erupt from wall bases in lacquered shelves, then hold that glossy maturity indefinitely. Algal films pattern the wettest stone in emerald and copper gradients that correspond to subtle differences in pH from one spring seam to the next. Decomposition is slow, partial, and aesthetic—matter transitions into stable intermediate forms and stays there.

At the edges of weirdness, reality appears to negotiate with itself. Straight avenues sometimes converge too soon, as if perspective had become physical. Arcades repeat one bay too many, then resume normal spacing. Reflections in rain-polished paving occasionally show a sky phase a few minutes ahead of the one overhead. In some courtyards, falling water stops a hand’s breadth above the basin, gathers into hovering lenses, then continues downward as mist. None of this is treated as extraordinary by the place; it is built into its rules like humidity and gravity.

Those rules, as far as a visitor can infer, are simple:

- **Flow must be continuous.** Nothing stagnant persists for long; even pools have hidden intake and release.
- **Growth must interlock.** Plants do not exclude stone, and stone does not exclude plants. Every boundary is shared.
- **Heat must vent.** Closed spaces are rare; every chamber has a throat to wind, a seam to water, or both.
- **Change must close on itself.** Events occur, but outcomes return to template.

By late day, light goes from white-gold to a flatter amber. Long terraces throw long shadows, and the city becomes banded: blazing sun, dark wet corridor, blazing sun again. Mist catches that low light and turns whole districts opaline. From high overlooks, you see the full scale: a vast, stepped, green-black urban massif embedded in an equally vast upland of ravines, perched lakes, and cloud-shadowed stone. It feels ancient not because it decays, but because it has already arrived at its final form and has been holding it—perfectly, deafeningly, beautifully—for longer than ordinary history can comfortably imagine.
