From a distance, it looks like a continent of pale antlers.

Not trees exactly—more like towers of fibrous, calcified stalk, branching and rebranching into lattices that rise out of a dry basin and climb into high, thin air. The whole world is vegetal, but not green in the way forests are green. It is silver-sage, chalk, sulfur-yellow at the tips, and bruised violet where tissues are old and pressure-hardened. Every surface is plant tissue in some phase: living rind, dead scaffold, powdery shed skin, resin-glass, spore felt. You can walk for days and never place a foot on stone that has not been wrapped, penetrated, or replaced by roots.

And yet it is sparse.

The growth does not make a dense jungle; it makes distance. Giant colonies stand kilometers apart, each a city-sized thicket with open mineral flats between them—dry, glittering pans of borate dust and salt-ash where wind skates freely. In the bright light, these intervals look empty enough to be ocean. Mirage bands shimmer low to the ground, and the horizon keeps retreating, interrupted only by far-off vertical bloomfields like frozen explosions.

There is almost no free water. Moisture exists as chemistry rather than liquid: bound in gels, trapped in waxy nodules, inhaled directly from cold night fogs that never become rain. The plants feed on atmospheric solvents and deep mineral brines locked far below, pumping ions up through capillary trunks taller than cliffs. If you cut one open, the sap is not wet so much as syrup-metallic, stringing in threads that crystallize in minutes in this dry, cold-bright air.

The high elevation gives everything hard edges. Sky is an intense cobalt by day, almost black at zenith, with fierce white sun and knife-clean shadows. Nights plunge into cold quickly. Frost forms not as soft ice but as brittle combs on leaf-spines, then sublimates at dawn with little pings like tiny bells. The temperature hovers in that austere zone where breath smokes but soil can still steam where exothermic roots are active.

The land is old in its forms. Wind has had ages to work: ridges are fluted, trunks are undercut, dead groves lie collapsed into smooth drifts of cellulose shale. Entire escarpments are made of compacted generations of fallen growth, eroded into amphitheaters and slot canyons whose walls show growth rings meters thick. This is erosion and ecology braided together—weathering not as destruction of life, but pruning at continental scale.

What makes it industrial is not machinery built from outside materials. It is the machinery of the plants themselves—and of what lives among them.

Each great colony behaves like a factory district. There are intake fields: low fans of black, velvet leaves angled to capture dawn condensation and charged dust. There are cracking towers: hollow stems that resonate in wind, shaking mineral grains loose from trapped aerosols. There are smelter organs: bulbous nodules where microbial consortia run hot chemo-reactions, venting bitter gases through chimney flowers. At their bases, conveyor-roots creep and pulse, carrying slurry to extrusion groves where new structural stalks are printed in spirals. It is production, throughput, waste heat, supply chain—just biological and geologic, not built.

The fauna make it louder still.

Herds of six-limbed grazers move between colonies, harvesting shed pith and fungal mats. Their teeth are ceramic; their lungs are vast bellows. Flocks of membrane-winged siphoners nest in high branch lattices and launch in coordinated bursts that sound like sailcloth torn by hurricanes. Burrow swarms churn the flats, grinding plant husk into fine dust, releasing nutrient plumes visible from kilometers away.

Predators hunt by vibration in this visibility-blasted world. Some stand motionless like broken stalks until a footfall triggers them. Others ride the updrafts around tall growths, dropping with an impact crack that echoes for seconds.

And over all of it: the sound.

The place is deafening even when it seems still. Wind through millions of hollow stems makes organ tones, shrieks, flutes, and infrasonic moans you feel in your ribs. Seed-pods detonate in chain reactions like distant artillery. Gas vents roar. Frond-fields clatter like endless bone chimes. Migratory swarms pass overhead in droning bands that blot the sun and leave your ears ringing. During storm periods—frequent, violent, mostly dry—the entire biome becomes a single percussive instrument: dust thunder, electrostatic snapping, branchfall crashes, and the deep, continuous freight-train bellow of air descending through canyon-groves.

Weather is local and self-made at this scale. The giant colonies seed turbulence: they heat air at their cores with metabolic reactions, spawn convective plumes, and draw in cold downslope winds. Dust cyclones wander between them, picking up spores until they become moving walls of gold-brown haze. Lightning is common but odd—branching sideways through charged spore clouds, igniting resin films in brief blue fires that race along dead bark then extinguish for lack of volatile fuel.

No human-built structures stand here. Nothing straight, nothing riveted, nothing quarried into intended shape. But processes have rules.

Paths form anyway: broad game corridors where hooves and root-secreted slicks polish the ground to a dark sheen. Some groves are taboo to most animals because they emit narcotic terpenes at noon. Other zones are nurseries where even apex hunters tread lightly because defensive vines can launch needle sprays with ballistic precision. Entire migration calendars are keyed to flowering sirens—tower blooms that emit low-frequency calls audible over dozens of kilometers, summoning pollinators and scavengers alike into temporary, chaotic markets of life and death.

The weirdness is subtle until you stay long enough. Plants here communicate mechanically as much as chemically; a snapped branch in one district can trigger pod release in another via pressure waves through root-web strata. Some towering stalks slowly rotate over months, tracking not the sun but prevailing mineral winds. In certain flats, mats of lichen-like tissue lift and fold at dusk, exposing warm sublayers where small fauna gather to survive the cold—then flatten again at dawn, erasing all sign.

Seen from above, the continent resembles circuitry drawn by a patient hand: branching conductive lines, nodal factories, empty insulating gaps. Seen from within, it is a dry forest-ocean in perpetual labor, old yet never finished, bright as a forge, loud as an airport, alive in every direction, and always on the verge of rearranging itself in the next gust.
