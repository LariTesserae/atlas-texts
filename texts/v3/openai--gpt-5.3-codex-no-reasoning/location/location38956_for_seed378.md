The city sits on the shoulder of a high volcanic basin, where the ground is never dry and almost nothing green has ever grown.

From a distance it looks pale and metallic under a hard white sun: terraces of concrete-ceramic blocks, cooling towers, rail gantries, and broad flood channels laid out in practical grids that have slowly bent to the land’s old fractures. Between districts, open flats of black glassy stone stretch for kilometers, fractured like old ice. The basin is elevated enough that the horizon feels close and immense at once—air thin enough to sharpen edges, heat intense enough to make those same edges shimmer.

Water is everywhere, but never as a friendly river. It seeps, condenses, sweats from rock. In shaded cuts and under retaining walls, droplets form in endless rows and fall with metronomic patience into mineral pans. After the daily thermal storms, whole streets gleam ankle-deep, then drain into stepped reservoirs lined with salt-resistant brick. Along the basin margins, marsh-like fields spread—not with reeds, but with chimneys of mineral accretion and mats of rubbery, fungus-like films that drink heat and exhale faint metallic vapor. Puddles skin over with iridescent bacterial lacquer, break, and reform by afternoon.

There are no trees, no grasses, no flowers—no familiar plant geometry at all. The vertical life here belongs to towers, vents, and animal bodies. Ecology took another route: colonies of pale tube-creatures in drainage culverts filtering warm runoff; broad-backed grazers with enamel plates licking mineral crust from wet stone; needle-legged flockers that move in synchronized bands across the flats at dawn to harvest the brief bloom of airborne spores. Predators glide more than run, using thermal updrafts from factory stacks and sunstruck basalt. Many animals are bright-eyed and heat-adapted, with reflective skin, oversized ears or gill-frills, and behavior tuned to water pulses and steam cycles.

Despite abundance of life, the basin is quiet.

Sound gets swallowed by scale and habit. Machinery is heavily baffled; engines hum inside insulated housings; conveyor lines ride magnetic beds with barely a scrape. The loudest noises are soft ones: distant dripping under viaducts, the hiss of condensing pipes, a dry clicking chorus from crustacean-like scavengers in storm gutters, the occasional low call of a hunting pair echoing off cistern walls. Wind crosses open plazas and empty freight yards with a thin, flute-like tone, then disappears. Even markets operate in near-murmur; people signal with shutters, lights, hand signs, and coded color strips rather than shouting.

The built world is mature, repaired, and adapted rather than new. Streets are crowned for runoff; every threshold has a grate; every roof is a collector. Buildings stand on mineral-composite stilts where the ground sweats too much, or sink deep pilings where old lava tubes run underneath. Rail lines and pipeways are elevated, creating long shaded corridors where cool-loving species cluster at midday. Floodgates are civic monuments here—massive, ornamented, and maintained like temples. Industry and hydrology are the same system: heat exchangers in foundries feed desalination towers; condensate from turbines feeds animal cisterns; biowaste from slaughter halls feeds fungal vats that produce insulation, medicine, and feed-gel.

The strangeness is not spectacle but rule.

Water falls upward in certain noon hours, drawn as mist toward charged mesh spires. Some neighborhoods “rain” indoors each evening from ceiling condensers, and families set bowls to different corners because each dripline carries a different mineral profile. In low plazas, thin membranes grow overnight across standing pools, taut as drumhead, and local children skate them at dawn before they dissolve. On the high rim roads, you can sometimes see columns of animals simply stop, turn their heads to bare rock, and wait—minutes later, that wall begins to bead with fresh water as if the stone itself has decided to breathe.

Nothing here is accidental anymore. Long residence has made customs out of physics. Travel happens in two daily waves: pre-heat and post-flashstorm. Predation seasons are mapped onto industrial maintenance windows so that vulnerable herds can pass under lit viaducts while heavy machinery is paused. Quiet hours are strict, not moral—acoustic disturbance changes hunting success and can trigger dangerous mass movement in flocking species. Waste heat quotas are enforced district by district because too much local warming shifts condensation zones and can starve entire blocks of water.

In bright afternoon, the basin is all glare and geometry: white sky, black stone, silver rails, moving dots of animals crossing wet flats. At evening the light turns copper, and steam hangs in low sheets between structures. Night never gets cold, only less punishing. Lamps are hooded and amber, attracting fewer winged scavengers. Above, stars are sharp in the thin air; below, reservoirs hold their own constellations, trembling with drip-rings.

It is a hot, wet, nearly plantless highland city that learned to live inside its own weather—industrial, inhabited, and alive in ways that are patient, mineral, and quietly in motion.
