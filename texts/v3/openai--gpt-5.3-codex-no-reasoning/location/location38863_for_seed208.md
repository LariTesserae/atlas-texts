The plain sits low under a lid of cloud, as if the sky has sagged over it for so long that the horizon learned to hunch. Nothing here rises quickly. Everything that rises has been worn back down: ridges rounded to knuckles, old stone shelves softened into long wet steps, collapsed mounds spread into dark fans of gravel and clay. The place feels ancient not because it is ruined, but because ruin has already finished and settled. This is what remains after collapse has become geography.

Water is never dramatic, yet it is everywhere. It beads on every surface, hangs in shallow air, seeps underfoot, condenses on cold stone and drips steadily into thread-thin runnels that wander through the flats. There are no clear rivers with banks and names, only a braided skin of movement—silver films over basalt, black puddles in wind-scoured bowls, reedless marsh pans edged with mineral crust. In some hollows the ground trembles like soaked felt: step there and trapped water sighs up through tiny vents. In others the surface is hard as fired pottery, glazed by long cycles of wetting and drying, cracked into plates that click together when trodden.

There is almost no green. No grass stitching the earth, no shrubs, no trees, no algae sheen even where water sits for weeks. The wet places are mineral-bright instead: rust orange where iron leaches, pale turquoise where copper salts bloom, milk-white margins of gypsum feathers, soot-black mud that swallows light. Life has not painted this land with leaves; it has chosen moving bodies instead.

And bodies are everywhere.

At first you hear them before you can sort them. The landscape is deafening—constant, layered, mechanical in force yet fully alive. Water hisses. Mud burps. Wind drives low over stone and turns every crack into a flute. But above and through that runs the animal noise: booming throat-calls rolling across the flats, shell clatter, slap of many feet in shallow pools, chittering swarms in unseen burrows, sudden mass rushes that sound like hail on leather. Even at rest, the plain hums with feeding, mating, warning, scraping, cracking, grinding.

The dominant creatures are low-built and water-loving, adapted to cold damp and open sightlines. Herds of broad-backed hexapods move between salt pans, their hide plated not with scales but with overlapping horny tiles that shed water like roof shingles. They graze not on plants but on mats of microbial film and mineral froth that form overnight at seep lines; with chisel mouths they scrape stone clean, leaving pale feeding arcs visible for days. Smaller carrion-runners trail them—sleek, long-jawed opportunists that dart in to snatch expelled parasites, eggs, and the weak. In every warm seep, blind filterers pulse in colonies, pumping silty water through combed limbs and building brittle towers of secreted lime. When one tower falls, the crack is like a gunshot.

Predators don’t hide much; there is little to hide in. They use weather instead. In mist-thick dusk, when visibility drops to a few body lengths, pack hunters fan out and drive prey toward soft ground where footing fails. Their calls are not roars but concussive barks that stun schools of shallow-water skimmers into beaching themselves. Above them wheel heavy-winged scavengers, featherless and pale, riding the constant updrafts from warmer seep fields; when they settle on old stones, they fold down so still they resemble broken posts.

Human presence appears as interruptions, not settlements. Scattered along the higher gravel tongues are low ring walls of stacked slate, hide windbreaks pegged with bone, smoke-darkened overhangs, fish-drying racks gone empty and silvered with age. Near reliable seep basins stand tripod frames holding stone bells and shell clappers—simple alarm devices that rattle when herd movement shakes the ground. Paths exist only as habit: darker lanes where repeated traffic has polished mud to dense clay. Tools are basic and hard-used—flaked stone scrapers, antler awls, net weights, hammered native-copper hooks gone green. Nothing monumental survives here; anything tall or proud has long been taken by weather and patient abrasion.

Yet there are rules, and they are strict enough that even newcomers feel them quickly:

- **Never trust flat shine**: reflective ground may be crust over suction mud.
- **Follow sound, not sight, in fog**: herd bells, boiling seeps, and wind flutes map safe routes better than landmarks.
- **Camp on old shell ridges**: former shorelines sit centimeters higher, just enough to stay dry.
- **Move with the migration pulses**: the great grazers stir the microbial fields; where they pass, food and danger both intensify.
- **At first thunder from clear cloud, leave stone shelves**: sheet runoff arrives fast and silent, turning placid channels into battering flow.

In dim light—most days are dim, dawn and dusk nearly identical—the land seems monochrome: charcoal, iron, wet bone. Then a break in cloud reveals subtler colors: bruised violet in distant mudflats, green-black sheen on fresh basalt, pearly fog lit from within. Night does not quiet the place; it redistributes the noise. Day herds settle into low grumbling clusters while nocturnal swarms erupt from crack networks, clicking in synchronized waves that pass for kilometers like moving static.

What is unusual here is not a single impossible object but the combination: a wet world with almost no vegetation, an old eroded basin still violently alive, a primitive human edge surviving amid biological abundance that needs no forests. It feels like an afterworld and a beginning at once—post-garden, pre-field. Stone, water, animal, weather. Repeated until the forms are worn smooth, and still in motion.
