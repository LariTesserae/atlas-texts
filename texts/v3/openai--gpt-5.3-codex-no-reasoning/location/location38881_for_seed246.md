At first it seems like the ruin of a mountain town, until you look long enough to notice there was never any stone laid by hands.

The whole place fits inside a high cirque no wider than a few streets laid side by side—an enclosed bowl near the roofline of a larger range, cold enough that breath hangs, warm enough in daylight that black rock sweats and drips. The walls are old beyond measure: not sharp peaks but exhausted ridges, their bones worn down to bulging shelves and blunt towers, as if time has licked every edge smooth and then cracked it anyway. Everything here is ancient erosion, not dramatic collapse. The mountain has had eras to settle into its final posture.

Across the basin floor stand “buildings” that are actually columns of fused mineral and root, arranged in neighborhoods with alleys between them. Some rise three stories high, hollow through the middle like chimneys. Others lean together into arches and covered corridors where meltwater runs in silver ribbons. Their surfaces are layered—lichen crust, peat-dark felt of moss, pale mineral efflorescence, and glassy skins where old freeze-thaw cycles polished seep lines. It reads as urban because it is organized: blocks, plazas, drainage channels, windbreak rows. But no tool made them. Growth and weather did.

Water is intermittent but everywhere implied. In the morning, condensate pearls on every overhang. By noon, droplets fall in patient rhythms into cistern-like basins carved by centuries of drip. Rain comes in brief hard passes, then gone. There is no broad river, only linked pockets: a chain of black pools under bridges of rootstone, each pool overflowing to the next when storms hit. In dry spells they sink to mirrors rimmed with brittle ice. You can walk the whole district stepping from damp to dry to damp again.

Vegetation is unexpectedly rich for the cold: low willow mats hugging thermal seams, dense fern fans in steam-cracks, rope-thick mosses upholstering walls, and mats of red alpine sedge in every gutter where silt can rest. No forest, no towering trunks—just concentrated lushness in channels and courtyards, green packed tight against emptiness. Outside these strips, ground is bare gravel and old, wind-flattened stone.

The emptiness is vertical. Between one mineral block and the next are volumes of clear, hard air. Above, huge sky. Inside hollow towers, open shafts where wind organes through ribs of calcite. Density is low not because nothing exists, but because each thing stands apart, surrounded by room enough for weather to move.

And weather does move. This place is loud.

Not with animals—fauna is sparse—but with process:  
- katabatic wind dropping off the rim in sustained roars,  
- water pinging from ledge to ledge,  
- periodic ice-shedding cracks like rifle shots,  
- deep throat-notes from caverns when pressure shifts,  
- whole “streets” humming when gusts align with hollow columns.

In rain, every surface becomes percussion. In freeze, every seam speaks.

Life that does inhabit it is specialized and infrequent: white cliff-goats crossing impossible traverses at dusk; long-winged carrion kites circling but rarely landing; translucent salamanders in the warmer drip pools; once in a while a fox thin as wire, moving through the alleys as if it knows a map no one else sees. Invertebrates do most of the work—cold beetles in moss beds, water skaters in the dark basins, tiny moths erupting briefly from sedge after wet weeks.

The strange quality comes from pattern. Paths appear where no feet have walked: runoff routes that braid and unbraid each season yet return to the same geometry. Courtyards flood in hexagonal outlines. Certain walls frost over from the inside, feathery ice blooming through pores in the stone as though the structure exhales. If you stay long enough, you realize the settlement is not static ruin but metabolism at geologic speed—an urban plan written by freeze, seep, root, and wind.

Its rules are simple and strict:

1. **Height gathers noise.** The higher ledges are always loudest; sound falls and pools in lower chambers.
2. **Warmth hides in contact zones.** Anywhere root touches mineral seep, life thickens.
3. **Water remembers channels.** Even after dry months, the next storm will refill old routes exactly.
4. **Nothing sharp survives.** Every protrusion rounds, flakes, and settles; forms persist only if they can endure abrasion.
5. **Open space is part of the structure.** Voids are not gaps—they are ducts for wind, light, and cold.

By afternoon under ordinary daylight, the place is all clear edges and wet shine, no fantasy haze, just a severe and workable brightness. At sunset, shadows stack in the alleys and the basin looks unmistakably like a city again: districts, towers, reservoirs, avenues. Then night cold locks the drips, and the loudness changes key from water to ice, from splash to strain.

A high, old, half-living town with no citizens—except weather, which never leaves.
