You find it by accident: a city folded into the inside of a mountain crack.

Not a broad canyon, not a skyline—more like a wound in stone, narrow enough that in many places two outstretched arms could touch both walls. Yet it drops so far that light fails long before depth does. The whole settlement is built on this contradiction: **extreme verticality in a hidden space**. From above, it looks like a seam, a dark line in pale rock. From within, it feels like a world.

The rock is young, sharp-edged, barely weathered. Sheared planes glitter with fresh mineral faces, steel-gray and faintly green, as if the mountain was split yesterday. Dust lies everywhere, but it is not soft; it is crystalline powder that rasps when moved. There is almost no water in the place. Pipes run, tanks hum, condensers click, but free liquid is rare enough to have social rules around it. Spills are emergencies. Steam is wealth. Breath is measured in subtle habits.

The city clings to walls in dense layers: bolted galleries, riveted staircases, welded balconies, conveyor bridges, service ducts, cable bundles thick as tree trunks. Every surface carries purpose. Homes are narrow capsules tucked between machine halls. Workshops are stacked over storage vaults over turbine throats. Transit lifts run continuously up and down the shaft, counterweighted cages sliding in synchronized near-collisions that somehow never strike.

Because space is tight, nothing stands alone. Heat exchangers are apartment walls. Freight lines pass through kitchens. Ventilation chimneys double as streets, with rung-ladders and maintenance rings serving as public circulation. It is urban not by sprawl but by compression: district layered on district, each one only a few meters from the next, separated by grating and noise and custom.

The air is cool, mineral, faintly metallic. Not cold enough to bite, but always carrying drafts from pressure systems deeper in the crack. Those drafts change abruptly. The turbulence here is structural and atmospheric both. Wind surges unpredictably through constrictions, turns corners into gust traps, whistles through valve arrays. Loose cloth is clipped down. Papers are taboo. Children are taught "three points" before they can read: two feet and one hand always anchored.

And despite the dry stone, life is abundant.

Not vegetation—there are no leaves, no moss, no rooted green. Instead, fauna has adapted to mineral hunger and machine shelter. Chalk-white arthropods cluster in warm conduit seams, feeding on trace chemical films. Ribbon-bodied gliders launch from high gantries, catching thermal updrafts from furnaces to drift between levels, folding onto ceilings like hanging tools. Small blind herd-creatures—knuckled, many-legged, armored in translucent silica scales—move in dense processions through service tunnels, licking salt and oxidized residues from pipe joints. People farm them in rotating pens, not for meat alone but for polishing byproducts, insulation fibers, and enzyme pastes used in ore treatment.

Higher up, where faint daylight reaches, predator forms perch motionless on rebar nests: six-limbed climbers with reflective eye-shields and patient, almost mechanical stillness. They hunt by vibration, not sight; the city’s rhythm is their map. Bells on boots are an old custom, both warning and claim.

Sound, overall, is quieter than such density should allow. Much machinery is enclosed, baffled, damped. Residents favor soft-soled footwear, hand signals, clipped speech. The dominant acoustic texture is intermittent: a distant lift chain, a pressure door thud, a brief clatter of dropped metal echoing for seconds into the deep. Then silence again, except for wind threading narrow slots. Quiet here is not peace; it is concentration.

Light is dim and practical. Strips of low amber illumination edge catwalks. Work zones use harsher blue-white arcs. In habitation clusters, luminous paint and bioceramic glow panels make muted pools where faces appear and vanish as people pass. Above, a slit of sky. Below, blackness alive with moving indicator lamps: red, green, amber, rising and falling like slow constellations.

The ecology and the industry are entangled. Waste heat supports breeding terraces. Animal migrations predict pressure shifts better than instruments. Invertebrate colonies clog filters but also digest toxic slurries. Entire municipal departments exist to negotiate with nonhuman movement: timing freight runs to avoid herd corridors, tuning ultrasonic repellers away from nursery zones, mapping seasonal nesting in crane crowns.

And the city is never still. Rock occasionally settles with cannon-crack reports. New fractures open; old routes close. Platforms are cut away and rebuilt weekly. Storm fronts above force rapid pressure reversals below. Emergency shutters slam, then reopen minutes later. Market decks can transform overnight into evacuation ramps. Everyone lives with harness clips at the belt and route updates tattooed into memory.

The rules of the world here are simple and absolute:

- **Anchor before action.**
- **Seal before sleep.**
- **Mark every edge.**
- **Never waste moisture.**
- **Listen to stone, then to machines, then to rumor—in that order.**

For all its hazard, the place is not grim. It is intensely inhabited. You see chalk marks at junctions—directions, jokes, memorials. Tiny shrines in bolt recesses. Caged song-fauna kept for luck near compressor rooms. Tea brewed from mineral fungi and condensation, served in thimble cups with ceremonial slowness. Weddings held on mid-level suspension bridges during low-wind intervals, guests clipped to safety lines, vows shouted once and then signed in silence.

A hidden vertical city in a dry young wound of mountain: crowded, dim, cool, loud in pulses, mostly quiet between them, alive in every crack.
