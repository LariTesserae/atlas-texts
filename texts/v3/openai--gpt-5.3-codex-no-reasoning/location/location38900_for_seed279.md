The land sits low and wide, as if it has only recently stopped being seabed.

Nothing in it grows. No grass, no moss, no root-hair in cracks. The ground is all exposed body: clay-slick flats, plates of dark stone, ridges of fresh mineral pushed up in long seams like healed scars. Water is never far away, but it is not river-water in any clean course. It beads in shallow basins, gathers in long reflective films over rock, seeps up through silt under pressure from below. Step almost anywhere and there is the soft answer of saturation beneath a thin skin of crust.

The air is cool enough that breath shows at dawn and dusk, but never sharp-cold. A dimness hangs even by day. Not darkness—more a permanent overcast glow, as though the sky is made of wet parchment stretched over a lamp. Light does not strike surfaces here; it soaks them. Edges stay visible because they are young and abrupt: recently fractured faces, knife-like ledges, collapsed shelves that have not yet weathered smooth. Erosion has begun, but has not had time to become elegant. Everything is in the first rough draft.

From a distance, the region looks open—broad flats interrupted by low escarpments and sink-like depressions—but when you enter it, density takes over. Every meter is busy with texture: puddles within puddles, brittle mineral lace, stacked shards, nodules, channels, rivulets, vents exhaling faint vapor, hanging drips from undercuts, curtains of runoff threading down black walls. It is hard to find a simple surface. Even still water is crowded with suspended fines that make it opaque, metallic, pearled gray.

There are structures, if that is the word. Scattered, half-sunk forms stand on slightly raised tongues of firmer ground—single-wall enclosures, low pylons, ring foundations, short runs of fitted block already being undercut by seepage. No roads connect them. No tool marks survive clearly enough to date intention from accident. They might be ruins, markers, or nothing more than stone that happened to break orthogonally. A few are unmistakably placed: aligned in threes along the lip of a long depression, each with a central hollow that fills and empties with rain. They look like things once meant to hold fire in a world that no longer permits flame.

Sound dominates the place. It is loud without motion in the usual sense. Water ticks, taps, rattles, and hisses everywhere at once. Droplets fall into shallow pans with resonant, bell-like notes. Hidden channels gurgle below crust and occasionally cough up air. Thin sheets of runoff vibrate against ridged stone and produce a steady sibilant rush. Farther off, whole faces of wet shale shear away with a brief ripping crack, followed by a heavy slurry-thud. Wind, when it comes, does not roar; it plays the terrain like an instrument, turning narrow clefts into low flutes and broken openings in old walls into hoarse reeds. Silence never arrives fully—only shifts in which layer of noise sits on top.

Life, in the ordinary visible sense, is sparse to vanishing. If there is fauna, it is rare and easily missed: pale, soft-bodied things in temporary pools that contract into inert pellets when water thins; threadlike swimmers in mineral-rich seeps; occasional jointed scavengers no bigger than a finger hiding under stone lips where condensation persists. Their movements are brief and economical, as if motion itself is expensive. No calls, no tracks that last long. Presence is inferred from tiny disturbances in surface films.

The strangeness comes less from spectacle than from behavior. Water here seems to prefer stone over open channel, creeping uphill by capillary mats in rock that should be dry, condensing in sheltered hollows even as nearby puddles evaporate. Some outcrops “weep” on regular intervals, not tied to obvious weather: first dull, then sweating, then running for an hour before sealing dry again. In certain basins, thin mineral skins grow overnight across standing water, wrinkle at midday, and sink intact by evening, as if breathing. None of it is fast. The whole landscape changes in long, patient increments, but it is never static. Given enough hours, every edge darkens, every reflective patch migrates, every small channel chooses a slightly different path.

The rules of the place feel simple and absolute:

- **Water is shallow but omnipresent.** Even where surface looks dry, subsurface saturation waits.
- **Nothing organic anchors the ground.** Without roots or litter, forms collapse as soon as cohesion fails.
- **Freshness is temporary.** Sharp lines appear, then blunt, then split again.
- **Sound maps function better than sight.** You learn where seep, void, and unstable crust are by listening.
- **Time is slow, but not gentle.** Change rarely looks dramatic in the moment; return a week later and the land has rearranged itself.

At twilight—the dimness deepening only slightly—the flats mirror the sky in broken strips between mineral ridges. Scattered stone structures stand like punctuation marks in a sentence still being written by seepage and gravity. No green ever interrupts the palette: charcoal, pewter, clay-white, iron-brown, and the sudden silver of new water. It is a young, wet, plantless country that has not decided whether it is becoming marsh, quarry, ruin field, or something with no familiar name.
