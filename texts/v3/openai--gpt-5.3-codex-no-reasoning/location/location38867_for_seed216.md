From a distance it looks like a mountain range draped in dark velvet.

Closer, you realize the “mountains” are not stone at all, and not exactly alive in any way a forest is alive. They are **woven**—continent-long embankments, terraces, and hollow towers made of braided trunks, packed rootfelt, layered reeds, stitched vine-cables, all blackened by age and rain until they resemble basalt. Every ridge is built, but built by hands that never wanted straight lines. Everything sags a little, swells a little, drinks, exhales. The world is architecture performed in plant tissue.

The highlands are warm despite altitude. Mist rises from everywhere: from cut channels, from breathing mat-soil, from the seams in the great woven retaining walls where water beads and drips steadily. Rain is common but not dramatic—long, patient rain that never quite becomes storm, then stops without clearing. The air afterward is heavy and dim, as if light has to push through leaf-oil and vapor to reach you. Even at midday there is a dusk quality. Colors sit low: green-black, peat-red, fungus-white, the amber of old cane, wet bronze where old sap hardens.

There is very little “stuff,” in a way. Vast spans of open air between giant structures. Long bridges of living lattice crossing chasms with nothing below but mist and far-off canopy. Empty galleries the size of valleys, their walls made from piled root columns, their floors mostly bare except for thin slicks of water and a few patient moss islands. Density is low not because life is absent, but because it is concentrated into colossal singular forms and then gives way to space. One monumental tree-city node, then kilometers of fog and silence before the next.

The ground—where there is ground—is a membrane over depth. You can step on a plaza made of tightly intergrown rhizomes and feel it flex, hear water move under it. There is no naked geology exposed except in rare cuts: pockets of old rock peering through like forgotten bones. Most surfaces are fabricated substrate: woven, grafted, tamped, trained over centuries. Primitive technology, yes, but ancient and complete. Stone knives, shell adzes, hardwood wedges, fire-hollowing, cordage, hand pressure, repetition over generations. No steel, no concrete, yet entire escarpments have been “made” by guiding roots into load-bearing buttresses and flooding terraces in timed sequences until they set into permanent form.

It is an old world in equilibrium with its own labor. Erosion has not defeated it; erosion has been recruited. Water is sent down sacrificial spillways lined with fast-growing reedwood. Wind is broken by curtain-vines planted at exact intervals. Slumps are expected, redirected, composted into new buttresses. Nothing is finished, but nothing is temporary either.

Fauna thrives in the seams.

You rarely see crowds of animals, because the scale swallows them, but life is everywhere if you wait. Herds of six-legged grazers move silently along high suspended meadows, clipping epiphyte mats. Broad-backed amphibians bask in warm seepage basins carved into living floors. In the dim rafters beneath overhang-bridges hang colonies of membrane-winged pollinators, folding and unfolding like wet fans. Predators are mostly patient ambushers camouflaged as knots of bark or flower-clots; prey species know every vibration in the woven ground. There are fish in aerial cisterns, trapped and transferred by flood pulses through siphon-vines no thicker than a wrist but kilometers long.

Sound is sparse, delicate, and far-traveling. Drip. Leaf-rub. The soft cable-creak of loaded vine spans. A single animal call can arrive from impossible distance, flattened by mist. Now and then a deep internal thud rolls through the substrate when a major root-column shifts and seats itself more firmly. People who live here (small, scattered settlements tucked into warm cavities of the megastructure) speak softly because loudness feels wrong, like shouting in a temple that is listening.

And then there is the world’s break from ordinary reality.

At this altitude, amid this engineered biomass, condensation does strange work. Fog does not only obscure; it **records**. Last week’s movement can replay in vapor as pale afterimages: a line of animals walking where no ledge exists now, workers hauling fiber along vanished scaffolds, rain falling upward for a minute in a cold pocket. Some valleys hold “borrowed gravity,” where seeds drift sideways and water gathers in vertical sheets along woven walls before deciding which way down is. In certain dim courts the plant-architecture grows around absence—empty geometric voids that roots will not cross, as if an invisible stone sits there. Fauna route around these too. Nobody knows whether these are sacred defects, old tools still functioning, or scars where the rules wore thin.

Night does come, but day is never bright enough for a true contrast. Bioluminescent lichens stipple the undersides of arches, making floating constellations below your feet when you cross high spans. Warm rain continues. The vast structures drink and swell. Far away, in darkness and mist, another terrace-city exhales pollen like smoke.

So the landscape feels less like wilderness and more like a single, distributed organism-civilization: mostly quiet, mostly empty at human scale, yet gigantic and constantly active in slow pulses—wet, warm, dim, and old beyond counting, held together by rope, root, memory, and patient hands.
