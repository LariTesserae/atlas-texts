It sits high enough that clouds don’t always pass overhead—they sometimes pass *through*.

Not a mountain range, not a valley system: one compact high basin, maybe a kilometer across, like a broken bowl perched on the spine of a peak. The rim is jagged and young, dark volcanic teeth with little soil on their faces. You can still read the violence that made it: fresh fracture lines, splintered shelves, boulders that look dropped rather than worn. Erosion hasn’t had the long argument with stone yet. Everything is edged, immediate, recently exposed.

And yet it is wet. Constantly.

The basin floor is a suspended marsh-garden fed by seepage from the walls. Water gathers in shallow channels, then disappears beneath mats of roots and pumice gravel, then reappears as mirror-bright pools no larger than a room. Every surface sweats. The air condenses against metal, leaf, skin. In the mornings, fog climbs out of cracks and sits waist-high over the reeds. By noon the mist thins into a bright, ordinary daylight, but nothing ever dries completely.

Because it is so high, vegetation doesn’t spread as forest; it concentrates. Dense clusters. Tangled, glossy-leaf shrubs shoulder into each other; ropey vines tie boulders to scaffolds; mosses pad every shaded edge. Ferns grow from seams in raw basalt, and pale green algae films the slower water. Plants occupy volume here, not just ground—layer over layer, nearly touching, making short passages feel like interior corridors.

The industrial presence appears in fragments, as if a much larger operation once intended to happen and then stalled at first deployment.

There are three main structures:

- A narrow-gauge rail spur that enters through a blasted notch in the rim, then ends abruptly over the marsh on a half-finished trestle.
- A pumping house of riveted steel plates, no bigger than a chapel, its roof furred with lichens and dripping condensate.
- A vertical condenser tower anchored to the inner cliff, with finned copper vanes and slow-turning intake fans.

Pipes run between them on stilted supports. Some still hum. Some are split open with roots threaded through. Valves the size of steering wheels protrude from fern thickets. Ceramic insulators line a cable route that still carries intermittent power; on damp days the lines crackle faintly blue. Nothing is pristine, nothing is fully ruined. It feels in use by process, if not by people.

Sound defines the place before sight does.

You hear water everywhere: drips from overhead girders, rivulets under plankways, sudden gulps where marsh drains through hidden stone throats. Add machinery to that—belt slaps, fan drone, the low cyclical thump of an old piston pump that catches, releases, catches again. Metal pops as it warms in sunlight. Steam vents in short sighs. Wind comes in bursts over the rim and turns cables into long, humming strings. Loud, but not frantic. A working loudness with pauses long enough to notice individual noises and learn their rhythm.

Fauna is sparse at this altitude, but not absent. A few narrow-winged birds nest in inaccessible ledges and skim the basin only at dusk. Amphibians—small, dark, almost stone-colored—occupy the warmer outflow channels near machine housings. Insects gather around standing water and around warm motor casings, making two separate constellations of motion. Larger animals seem to avoid the noise and unstable footing.

The strangeness is mild, cumulative, believable until it isn’t.

Water here behaves with preference. Condensate forms fastest on worked metal, slowest on untouched rock, as if the air “chooses” engineered surfaces. Certain mosses grow only where current passes through buried cables, tracing unseen electrical paths in glowing green filigree at twilight. The condenser tower sometimes exhales cold mist uphill, against gravity, for minutes at a time. Local workers—if they still come—have painted level marks not on tanks, but on boulders, tracking seasonal “breathing” of groundwater that rises and falls in stone pores rather than open pools.

The rules of the basin seem to be:

1. **Height captures weather.** Clouds snag here and pay in water.
2. **Fresh stone redirects everything.** Channels change subtly each season because the substrate is still settling.
3. **Machines are part of hydrology now.** Even broken pumps create warm pockets, altered flows, and niches where plants anchor.
4. **Nothing moves quickly except sound.** Physical change is slow: rust thickens, roots pry joints apart, silt lifts plankways millimeter by millimeter.
5. **Boundaries are real.** Outside the rim: exposed alpine barrens. Inside: enclosed humidity, dense green, resonant metal.

Walking through it feels like moving inside a mechanism that was designed to harvest cloudwater and accidentally grew a wet hanging garden around itself. Every few meters, your boot steps from grating to peat to stone to plank. Your hand reaches for railings and comes away beaded with moisture. The light is straightforward daylight, but the density of surfaces—leaf, pipe, cable, fog—breaks it into near-field layers. Nothing is far away. Even the sky appears framed by steel and cliff.

By evening, the pump cadence slows, or seems to. Fog returns from the basin floor upward, swallowing lower structures first. The condenser tower remains visible longest: a dark, vertical comb against amber cloud, still turning, still drawing breath from the mountain.
