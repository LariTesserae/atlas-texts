It fits in the palm of a basin.

Not a valley—more like a collapsed bowl of old stone and slag no wider than a few city blocks, sunk into a plain so flat and exhausted it looks planed by geologic hands. The rim is low, broken in places, and glazed dark as if it once flowed. Nothing grows on it. Nothing ever rooted here. The surfaces are powder, flake, pitted crust, oxidized ribs of metal, and long lenses of saltless pale mineral that crunch under pressure like old ceramic.

The air is cool enough that breath would ghost in the mornings, if there were anyone to see it. Dry enough that moisture feels theoretical. This is a place of dust chemistry, of oils and fine particulates and trace vapors that hiss from cracks and are gone.

And it is crowded—absurdly crowded—with animals.

They are not furred herds or feathered clouds. They are chitinous, shelled, plated, cabled, soft-jointed, many-legged, some no bigger than thumbnails, some the length of a forearm, some broad as a door panel and low to the ground like armored sleds. They pour through the basin in layers. Under every broken slab there is movement. Every seam in the old industrial debris is occupied. Every shallow depression is a nest, cache, brood-pit, or ambush cup.

No plants feed them. No water gathers for them. The food web runs on mineral films, fungal mats too thin to see except as an iridescent stain, and on each other. They rasp oxides from steel, lap condensed hydrocarbon residues from old pipes, harvest colonies of lithic microbes that bloom after cold nights on warm metal, and then get harvested in turn. Predation is constant and close; there’s nowhere open enough for distance.

You hear the place before you understand it.

A hard continuous clicking, like rain on sheet metal—but there is no rain. It’s mandibles on hull plates, claws on ceramic dust, thousands of segmented bodies tapping and skittering. Over that: belt-motor whines from machines that still run in intervals, fan housings thudding alive, pressure valves coughing from buried tanks that somehow still have something to vent. At random but frequent moments, the whole bowl rings with a resonant boom when a large grazer-cracker slams its frontal plate into an old refinery column to shake loose mineral scale. Then the scavenger swarms arrive in a moving black-gray sheen.

The built environment is sparse but consequential. Three leaning towers of riveted steel stand near the basin center, connected by snapped catwalks. A half-buried conveyor line arcs out of one slope like fossil spine. Several squat sheds—concrete, windowless, doors long gone—sit around the perimeter. They are mostly empty except for nests and old machine frames, but each provides shade, thermal stability, and hard geometry, so life packs into and under them at impossible density. One shed still has a functioning lamp over its entry, yellow-white, always on. Around it gathers a perpetual ring of nocturnal hunters and the prey they exploit. The lamp has become weather.

Erosion has had ages to work, and won. Every edge that wasn’t protected by alloy has gone soft, rounded, or dissolved into fields of flakes. Bolts stand proud where plates vanished. Stairs go nowhere. Drain channels are smooth troughs now, polished by countless bodies and dry abrasive wind. The basin feels final—like a mature stage after catastrophe, when nothing dramatic remains except relentless small processes.

And yet it is intensely active. At dawn, when light comes level and ordinary across the lip, heat differentials wake buried machines: a generator coughs, a piston cycles, a vent warms a patch of ground. Fauna rearrange instantly, exploiting gradients by instinct older than any memory of why the machines exist. Midday brings loudest movement; traffic lanes emerge, braided and temporary, between feeding grounds, brood chambers, kill zones. Evening cool sharpens smells—metal tang, bitter resin, sulfur traces—and mating choruses begin: stridulation amplified by hollow pipes into eerie industrial music.

Rules of this little world are simple and unforgiving:

- **Shade is territory.** Not for sun avoidance—there isn’t much heat—but for thermal stability and concealment.
- **Warm metal is wealth.** Any object that holds or leaks heat becomes a node of life.
- **Vibration means opportunity or death.** Everyone reads substrate tremors; everyone broadcasts by moving.
- **Height is rare and dangerous.** Most life stays low; anything that climbs becomes visible to too many hunters.
- **Stillness is camouflage. Motion is language.**

The weirdness is in how complete it feels despite missing what most worlds need. No grass, no pools, no trees, no soft horizon. Just an old industrial wound in a dry plain, colonized so thoroughly by animal life that metal and shell seem co-evolved. You watch long enough and the distinction blurs: a conveyor idler turns because a colony uses it as a treadmill to warm eggs; a valve opens because a burrow clan has learned to trigger it with synchronized impacts; a predator’s lure mimics the exact stutter-rhythm of a failing motor.

It is a tiny, loud, ancient machine-reef without water, still running on momentum, hunger, and inherited timing.
