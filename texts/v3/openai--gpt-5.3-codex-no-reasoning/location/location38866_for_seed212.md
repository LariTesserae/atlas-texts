It is a city grown by roots that forgot they were roots.

From a distance—if distance can be trusted here—the land lies low and broad, like a floodplain that never quite floods. No mountains, no cliffs, no clean horizon. Instead, an endless uneven canopy of green architecture rises and sinks in slow waves, all of it alive, all of it crowded: buttressed trunks thick as towers, braided vine-bridges, leaf-domes stretched over hollow courtyards, lattices of reeds hardened into walls by their own resins. Nothing has been quarried, fired, forged, or cut by tool. Every edge is either grown or torn.

Moisture is sparse, but persistent. The air is warm and heavy with a damp that comes and goes in pulses. Condensation beads along the undersides of giant leaves, gathers, then falls in sudden local showers. You can stand in powder-dry root-dust and be rained on by a single branch. Water lives in pockets: in cupped bromeliad basins, in stem-cisterns, in shallow green trenches where runoff lingers for an hour and then vanishes into thirsty fiber. Between wet pockets, the ground cracks—not stone cracks, but splits in woven peat and living mat, opening and sealing as growth surges underneath.

The “streets” are growth corridors: long compressed channels where plant tissue has learned to hold shape under constant passage of wind-driven debris and falling seed-mass. They loop and knot at plazas that resemble gigantic flowers viewed from within—radial, layered, mathematically elegant, and still actively opening. Neighborhoods are species-zones: one district of translucent paddle-leaves stacked like stained glass, another of black-green thorn columns humming with internal sap pressure, another entirely composed of pale fungal-plant composites that exhale mist at dawnlike intervals, whether dawn is happening or not.

Because dawn is unreliable.

Light is mostly ordinary—warm, lateral, bright enough to cast shadows—but sometimes shadows point the wrong way, and bright noon passes through in vertical bands like curtains being drawn through the world. Leaves show both sides at once. A trunk can stand in front of itself. You may watch a frond unfurl, then notice it has already dropped litter from last year. Time does not break dramatically here; it frays. Growth rings appear on soft tissue in minutes. Fresh shoots harden, split, and compost while you are still looking at them.

And the sound—deafening, ceaseless—is the city’s true weather.

No birds, no insects, no mammals, no human engines. Yet the volume is immense. Wood pops like distant gunfire as tissues expand under heat. Tensioned vines twang and whip free. Seedpods detonate in volleys. Whole facades of dry leaves clatter against each other in hot gusts, while deeper inside the mass there is a hydraulic roar: sap moving, chambers venting, stem-valves releasing pressure with foghorn moans. Rainfall from canopy pockets creates abrupt drumming cascades. Root plates grind. Pollen storms hiss through comb-like fronds. At intervals, thousands of hollow reeds resonate together when wind aligns them, producing chords so loud the air in your chest seems to vibrate in sympathy.

With no animals, pollination and dispersal have become architectural events. Flowers extend on articulated stalks across alleys to exchange dust directly. Vines throw adhesive tendrils like cast nets. Explosive fruits launch seeds that punch into neighboring walls and immediately graft. Some plants imitate absence: they grow door-shaped voids, tunnel-like passages, inviting flow purely to exploit it—capturing moisture, light, and fallen biomass. Competition has become urban planning. Cooperation has become zoning law.

And there are laws, though no lawmakers:

- Any open space is temporary.
- Any surface can become ground.
- Height is negotiated continuously.
- Dryness is a phase, not a region.
- Nothing dead stays dead for long; it is overgrown into function.

Erosion is young here not because wind and water are weak, but because construction outpaces wearing-away. Ridges of root-woven earth rise overnight and slump by afternoon. Fresh gullies appear where condensate torrents suddenly choose a path. Then capillary mats crawl in, stitch the wound, and raise a new terrace above it. The whole plain feels recently assembled, as if the ecosystem was seeded last season and has already forgotten that beginning.

In the warm intervals between condensation bursts, volatile oils thicken the air: peppery, sweet, medicinal, rotten, metallic. Spore haze hangs at knee height in some quarters, at rooftop height in others. You taste tannin and sugar on your teeth. Visibility is never stable—one moment long avenues through buttress colonnades, the next moment swallowed in hanging roots and steam.

At the city’s center—if there is a center—stands a basin of interlocked trunks fused into a crater wall. Inside it lies a shallow mirror of water, one of the few persistent pools. Around it, growth is most turbulent: stems spiraling upward then sideways, platforms budding and collapsing, membranes stretched between boughs that catch light and then dissolve into slime. Reflections in this water are wrong by small degrees: extra branches, delayed movements, weather from elsewhere. Sometimes the reflected canopy is calm while the real one convulses in wind. Sometimes the reflection shows clear sky where none exists.

You understand, after a while, that “urban” here means repetition with variation: block after block of living structures performing the same functions—support, storage, transport, shelter—through different morphologies. It is a metropolis without citizens, infrastructure without industry, noise without voices. A town built by growth itself, for growth itself, in permanent, roaring renovation.

And still it feels inhabited.

Not by creatures, but by processes so concentrated they seem intentional. Corridors guide gusts. Courtyards harvest drip. Towers wick fog. Gates open when humidity rises and knit shut when air dries. Entire districts tilt leaves in coordinated sequences to reroute light to shaded underlayers. At regional scale, the place behaves like one organism; at local scale, like a riot.

If you stay long enough, your sense of stillness disappears. Even at apparent rest, everything is moving: swelling, drying, twisting, rooting, venting, fusing, sloughing. The landscape does not wait for weather. It generates its own—warm, wet in pulses, thunderous, and perpetually under construction by green matter that has replaced stone, timber, plaster, road, roof, and crowd.

A low world, dense as a jungle and legible as a city, where reality has gone soft at the edges and plant life has moved into every available role until there are no other roles left.
