From orbit it looks like a cracked bronze lung.

Not blue, not green—mostly iron ochre, ash-gray, and pale mineral whites where ancient basins evaporated down to hard chemical crusts. The planet’s skin is folded upward into mile-high escarpments and long-backed ridges, so the continents are less “flat land” than staircases of stone climbing into thin warm air. The elevation is not dramatic in sharp peaks so much as relentless altitude: whole provinces sit at heights where clouds should form, but there is almost no free water to make them. The sky is broad and clean, an ordinary-colored daylight faded slightly by dust, with sunsets that go copper, then violet.

Down on the surface, the first thing you notice is space. Distances are huge. Things exist in clusters: a rail line, a processing stack, a town of pressure-sealed blocks, then fifty kilometers of empty alluvial plain littered with black wind-polished rocks. The emptiness is not lifeless, though. It is inhabited the way a dry reef is inhabited—pockets, burrows, thermal shadows, migration corridors invisible until you stand still long enough.

### Ground and Stone

The ground is old. Not ancient-and-untouched, but ancient-and-worked-on. Wind has combed the same cliffs for ages, carving fluted pillars and undercut shelves. Temperature swings open hairline fractures; dust settles into them; crystal growth pries them wider. Slopes are armored in scree that has found its angle and stayed there for centuries. Dry riverbeds—if you can call them rivers—are channels where dense brine fogs once crawled or where seasonal solvent runoff happened in earlier epochs. They are now pale, braided scars packed with salts and metallic sediments.

In the lowlands are flats of evaporite and clinker, hard enough to ring under boots. In the uplands: basalt plateaus punctured by fumarole fields, not violently active, just quietly venting warm gas. The whole landscape gives off the feeling of equilibrium: harsh, but settled into itself.

### Air and Weather

Warm, thin, and mostly still. Wind exists, but in long moods rather than gusty tempers. It can spend a week barely moving, then spend three days sweeping grit along the ground in low rust-colored veils. Real storms are rare; atmospheric drama tends to be electrical rather than wet—sheet lightning high in dry clouds, static hum through exposed cable runs, faint corona glows on antenna spines at dusk.

Because there’s so little moisture, sound carries oddly far in some conditions and dies quickly in others. A freight crawler grinding twenty kilometers away might be clearly audible at dawn; by noon, the same machine vanishes into heat shimmer silence.

### Built Presence

Civilization here is industrial but distributed. No single megacity dominates; instead, there are chains of engineered settlements pinned to resource seams and elevation shelves. Think: refinery towns, compressor fields, foundry terraces, logistics hubs, each with their own atmosphere control yards and heat-radiator forests.

Architecture is pragmatic and layered. Lower levels are old—ceramic brick, riveted steel, mineral concrete darkened by dust varnish. Newer additions perch above in modular frames, composite skins, insulated ducting, and bright hazard striping that has long since bleached to pastel. Buildings squat low against wind except for towers that must rise: stacks, cranes, relay masts, and elevator pylons bridging cliff levels. Between districts, enclosed conveyor tubes and elevated rail lines stitch the emptiness together.

At night, the settlements don’t glow continuously; they pulse. Power is budgeted. Districts cycle from full industrial illumination to maintenance mode. Seen from a ridge, the basin below looks like a constellation learning to breathe.

### Soundscape

Mostly quiet, yes—but not silent.

- A constant sub-audible throb from turbines and compressor stations.
- Periodic hammer-clangs from ore processing.
- Cable singing in wind.
- The dry skitter of small animals under slag plates.
- Far-off warning horns that mark shift changes or pressure events.
- In open country: your own movement, startlingly loud.

There are places called Hush Belts, usually between industrial zones, where regulation forbids heavy traffic during breeding migrations of native fauna. In those belts, the loudest thing at midday may be the ticking of cooling metal.

### Ecology Without Green

There are no plants in any familiar sense: no leaves, no grass, no trunks, no photosynthetic canopies. The base of the food web is chemoautotrophic and lithotrophic life—films and crusts of mineral-metabolizing organisms that colonize rock pores, vent margins, and subsurface fractures. You rarely *see* them directly except as subtle sheens: charcoal iridescence on basalt, pale sulfur lace in fumarole mouths, rust-black mats under shelter stones.

From that foundation, fauna flourishes in strange abundance.

Not lush abundance—distributed abundance. Creatures are adapted to distance, scarcity, and dry chemistry:

- **Glassbacks**: six-limbed grazers the size of deer, with translucent silica scales that refract sunlight and reduce heating. They rasp microbial crust from stone using vibrating jaw plates.
- **Column mites**: swarming arthropod analogs that build meter-tall porous chimneys over warm vents; entire colonies migrate once every few years, leaving fossil-like spires behind.
- **Kite predators**: wide, membrane-winged hunters that ride thermal gradients along escarpments. They hardly flap; they pivot and descend with eerie precision.
- **Burrow drums**: stocky omnivores that communicate through low-frequency ground knocks, felt through boots before heard.
- **Rail scavengers**: opportunists trailing freight corridors, feeding on heat-loving microbial blooms around leaking couplings and discarded industrial salts.

Most animals are quiet, energy-efficient, and patient. Activity spikes at dawn and dusk. Midday is stillness.

### Rules of Living Here

The world teaches specific habits:

1. **Shade is infrastructure.** In open terrain, shelters are mapped like wells on old Earth maps.
2. **Altitude is economy.** High shelves offer cooler equipment operation and clearer transmission; low basins offer easier extraction and transport.
3. **Dust is an adversary and a resource.** It abrades seals, fouls joints, and also carries useful fines for processing.
4. **Water is strategic, not ambient.** What little exists is reclaimed, cycled, guarded, metered. Cultural taboos form around waste.
5. **Movement is planned.** Vastness punishes spontaneity; routes are chosen by thermal windows, predator migrations, and maintenance schedules.

### The Strange Part

The weirdness is not flamboyant; it is systemic.

Certain salt flats “sing” at sunset as crystal lattices contract and release, producing harmonic tones that travel for kilometers. Some cliff faces display slow-moving electrical auroras—surface discharges crawling like pale insects over mineral veins after dry lightning events. In deep quarries, workers report hearing synchronized knocks from untouched rock, as if burrow drums are using the excavated voids as instruments.

There are also the **static blooms**: temporary geometries of charged dust that rise from the ground in calm weather, forming rotating columns or branching fans, holding shape for minutes, then collapsing. Small fauna gather around them, apparently using the fields for navigation or parasite control. Industrial crews sometimes delay operations to watch where blooms appear; they correlate with sub-surface conductive seams.

### Time and Change

Nothing here rushes unless machinery forces it.

Mountain faces wear down grain by grain. Animal migrations are tied to decadal thermal drifts. Settlements accrete rather than reinvent: a new smelter bolted onto an old yard, an abandoned rail spur repurposed as habitat corridor, a decommissioned stack converted into a nesting tower. Even accidents become geology quickly—spilled slag fields weather into black terraces, vehicle graveyards into artificial reefs for scavengers.

This is a warm high world of dry breath and patient industry, where life learned to eat stone, cities learned to pulse instead of blaze, and silence is not emptiness but interval: the long held note between machines, wind, and the careful footsteps of things that survive by spending little and enduring long.
