At first glance it looks like an abandoned district of a low, old city—streets, courtyards, stair-stepped walls, bridges between blocks—but then your eye adjusts and you realize almost none of it is mineral.

The “buildings” are trunks.

Not tree trunks in the usual sense, but columns of braided stems, cables of vine thick as masonry, buttressed roots that have learned right angles through centuries of leaning against one another. Whole facades are leaf-skin stretched over ribwork of living wood. Balconies are mats of interlocked branches that flex slightly under weight and slowly re-knit after damage. Doorways open and close by growth, not hinges: lips of cambium swelling apart in warm damp hours, sealing to a seam in dry heat.

It is lowland country, nearly flat, a basin that once held slow rivers and now holds a settlement-forest grown over their memory. Elevation barely changes except where old root-mounds lift plazas by a few body-heights. Water is never dominant, but always present in ambush: sweating from bark at dawn, collecting in cupped leaves under eaves, threading narrow black runnels along the old street channels when rain comes. The rain is episodic and theatrical—hard drumming bursts, then long warm steam. Puddles persist in shade and vanish in sun, leaving mineral ghosts and pollen scum.

Despite the thick biomass, the place feels empty.

Density is low because volume is huge and occupancy sparse. The plant-city rises into vaulted green chambers, each the size of a cathedral nave, and between structural growths are startling hollows: courtyards with nothing in them but moving light; avenues as wide as dry canals where only wind and spores travel; chambers under tangled canopies where sound arrives long before any body does. You can walk for minutes hearing life all around you and see almost no creature at all—until suddenly a flock detonates from a wall of fern, or a six-legged grazer unfolds itself from what you thought was a mossed parapet.

Fauna is excessive, but evasive. Pollinators in dense rotating clouds. Ground-runners that navigate by vibration through root lattices. Wet-skinned climbers sleeping inside bromeliad cisterns. Predators that mimic hanging seed pods until prey passes beneath. Everything breeds quickly after rain pulses; everything migrates vertically between canopy heat and root-cool when the day turns.

And the sound—this is what defines the place more than shape.

It is deafening in layers:

- constant stridulation, a metallic insect sheen;
- deep woody groans as load-bearing trunks flex and redistribute weight;
- percussive seed-fall like distant carpentry;
- sheet-rain impacts amplified by leaf architecture into drum-roll thunder;
- animal choruses that phase in and out as if districts are taking turns speaking;
- wet snaps, tears, and reknits of growth under tension.

No single source dominates for long. The whole biome is an orchestra tuning, performing, and collapsing into noise at once. Silence exists only as a remembered concept.

The temperature holds warm but not brutal. Heat pools in enclosed courts, then releases in moist drafts down root-canals. At night, condensate gathers and drips in syncopated rhythms from high leaves into hollow stems, making the dark sound like crowded workshops. Air smells of green tannin, bruised sap, fungal sweetness, and the faint iron of wet soil that rarely sees open sky.

Erosion has done its long work. Nothing here is new, yet nothing is fixed. Edges are rounded by time: old growth polished by generations of claws and rain. Paths persist because feet, hooves, and flowing water agree on them, not because stone was laid. Where once there may have been hard infrastructure, it has been absorbed—ceramic becoming lichen-host, metal becoming root-anchor, geometry becoming habit in living tissue. The settlement has stabilized into a negotiated form: not ruin, not wilderness, but an established civic ecology.

The weirdness appears when you try to map it.

Distances are inconsistent. A corridor between two vine-towers may take thirty steps in morning and seventy at dusk. Courtyards recur in different neighborhoods with identical branch scars and the same leaning fig, as if one place is being reused. Rain sometimes falls upward in narrow shafts, drawn from puddles into thirsty canopy. Certain archways return you to where you started unless you pass while singing or carrying fresh leaves. Local creatures ignore these violations completely, moving along routes that are topologically sensible to them but impossible in Euclidean terms.

There are rules, but biological rather than mechanical:

1. **Growth has right-of-way.** Anything static is overgrown, incorporated, or excluded.
2. **Moisture grants passage.** Damp surfaces open—dry ones resist.
3. **Sound is territorial.** Each major hollow has an acoustic signature; mimic it and residents tolerate you, disrupt it and predation increases.
4. **Nothing dead stays inert.** Fallen matter is quickly rewoven into flooring, nest-mass, or nutrient foam.
5. **Orientation is seasonal.** “North” is less useful than “sporeward,” “dripline,” or “chorus edge.”

In bright normal daylight it can seem almost understandable: a warm, overgrown, living town in a shallow basin. But stay longer and the place asserts its deeper nature—an urbanism without tools, architecture without builders, order without stillness. A plant-made city that is mostly empty space and almost no silence, where reality is flexible but ecology is strict, and where every wall, bridge, and plaza is alive enough to be listening.
