The city is underground, but it does not feel buried. It feels *grown around*.

You enter through a shaft of old mining concrete and bolted steel, and then the shaft opens into an enormous basin where the roof of stone arches so high it catches its own weather. Light comes from arrays suspended on cable-webs: long industrial luminaires with white-yellow tubes, some humming, some flickering, all reflected off broad leaf surfaces until the cavern holds something like afternoon.

There is almost no water. Not even dampness. The air is cool and mineral-dry, as if every molecule has been sieved. What should have been streams are pale troughs of powdery salts and crushed silica. What should have been mud is mats of root-felt, springy and fibrous, drinking humidity before it can settle. This place runs on vapor, on condensation stolen from breath, on chemistry that learned to thrive without liquid flow.

And still, vegetation is everywhere.

Not trees in the ordinary sense—more like structural flora: ribbed columns of lignified tissue, woven canopies, photosynthetic membranes stretched between scaffold towers, moss-like films on every surface that catches light. Entire neighborhoods are built inside living frames. Streets thread through plant corridors where walls pulse slowly as sap-gas shifts. Transit rails pass through hollow stems thick as viaducts. Some buildings were poured in concrete and later swallowed; now window grids peek out from bark plating and spiral tendrils like rebar reclaimed by biology.

The terrain is broad and open for a subterranean world. You can stand on an overlook and see districts receding in terraces: factory quarter, nursery quarter, exchange yards, and beyond them long plains of cultivated growth-substrate—“fields” that are really lattices of rooted mesh. Space between structures is generous. Nothing is cramped. There are distances, pauses, pockets of emptiness where only low groundcover clings to mineral flats and ventilation masts tick in the quiet.

Because it is new—still forming—the ground has a provisional quality. Edges are blunt, cuts are fresh, retaining walls temporary. Conveyor trenches end abruptly where excavation outpaced planning. Concrete cures beside living buttresses still pale from rapid growth. The city is in its own embryonic phase: not ruin, not completion, but assembly. You can see design decisions mid-sentence.

Industrial life runs through it all. Slow cargo crawlers drag bins of powdered nutrient salts. Press-houses compact plant fiber into panels and pipe. Spore refineries separate breathable mixes from harvested gas sacs. Gear trains turn in shallow vaults, lubricated with waxes and synthetic oils because water is too precious to waste on cooling. Heat is shed into stone sinks; chill circulates back as a blessing. Every process is tuned to scarcity of moisture and abundance of biomass.

The ecology is dense in kind, sparse in placement. Life doesn’t blanket uniformly—it gathers where light, mineral dust, and trace humidity intersect. One plaza may be nearly bare, marked only by hard, silver lichen-skin and wind-scored aggregate; the next is a vertical jungle of engineered fronds unfurling from tram pylons. The dominant plants are matte green, rust green, and black-green, with reflective undersides like fish scales to redirect light deeper into the understory. Many are hollow and resonant: when ventilation shifts, whole groves make a faint flute-tone.

Fauna is plentiful but not loud. You notice movement before sound: six-limbed grazers with chisel mouths rasping mineral-rich bark; membrane-winged gliders coasting from gantry to gantry; patient predators that mimic maintenance equipment until they unfold and step away on jointed root-colored legs. Burrowers run the dry channels, stirring dust plumes that settle on leaves and feed filter-feeders perched along catwalk undersides. Insect analogues farm microbial crusts on heat exchangers. Everything is adapted to conserve internal fluids—waxy hides, mirrored eyes, breathing cycles measured and slow.

The strangeness is not grotesque; it is procedural. Plants obey zoning codes. Architecture pollinates. Utility tunnels are also seed dispersal routes. Some streetlights bloom. Some crosswalk markings are living tissue that closes at curfew. There are districts where the “buildings” are legally alive and can vote, represented by horticultural guilds who interpret growth patterns as civic intent. You can read municipal notices stamped on bark in pigment that fades as nutrients are reallocated.

Sound never rises far. Machinery is baffled. Footsteps are swallowed by fiber matting. Distant calls of animals arrive thin and brief, then disappear into the cavern’s volume. The loudest moments are shifts: a freight winch taking load, a press cycling, an entire canopy re-angling toward a timed light change with a whisper like cloth drawn over stone.

Despite the quiet, the place is active. Lamps track a false sun-path. Ventilation tides move from district to district. Pollinator swarms emerge on schedule, silvering the air, then vanish into hive ducts. Crews in respirators prune structural growth before it buckles rail lines. New cuttings are planted into fresh excavation benches. Old concrete is milled and fed into mineral compost loops. Predators follow grazer migrations between lit sectors. Nothing is static; motion is distributed, continuous, mostly gentle.

Rules of the world become clear the longer you stay:

- Moisture is currency.  
- Shade is infrastructure.  
- Waste must become substrate within a day-cycle.  
- No surface may remain inert if it can be made productive.  
- Growth and construction are the same department.

So the underground metropolis expands not by spreading brick over land, but by teaching life to hold shape: boulevards braided from root and cable, plazas grown from pressure-resistant succulent mats, factories nested in trunks that thicken around them year by year.

At a distance, under the high cavern vault, the whole region looks like a map of green embers set in ash—lit zones and dark zones, living blocks and mineral gaps, an unfinished city rehearsing how to be a forest, and a forest learning industry without rain.
