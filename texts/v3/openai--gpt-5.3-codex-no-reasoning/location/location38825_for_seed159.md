You come down through a throat of stone into a world that feels less *buried* than *inside out*.

The ceiling is there, technically—an arched skin of layered rock and old ash far overhead—but it opens and closes in long breaks, so daylight pours in through ragged skylights. Shafts of warm, ordinary sun cross with columns of steam, and for moments at a time the whole underland looks like a city of weather built underground.

It is warm enough that nothing sits still for long. Moisture beads on every cool surface, then hisses away where vents breathe. Water does not dominate this place; it appears in episodes. A slick runnel between boulders. A rainburst falling from one skylight while three neighboring caverns stay dry. Condensation gathered in bellies of stone, then tipping in sudden spills. Pools form, cloud over with mineral skin, and vanish again when the floor subtly tilts or cracks open.

The ground is young. Not “ancient and worn” young, but raw, still deciding what it wants to be. Great lobes of half-hardened claystone bulge as if pushed from below. Beds of sharp black glass crunch underfoot where heat once flashed through wet sediment. Pale mineral ridges rise in soft, chalky folds, then break apart in a season of tremors. Nothing has been weathered smooth yet. Edges are fresh. Faces are unfinished. Whole terraces seem sketched, erased, and redrawn every few days.

And crowded—crowded beyond expectation for a place with almost no plants.

Life here feeds on heat, salts, rot-film, each other. The dominant bodies are not graceful: they are armored, jointed, many-legged, plated with mica sheen or matte clay colors that match the terrain. Herds of broad-backed burrowers plow through silty flats in dozens, their movement opening trenches that immediately fill with smaller scavengers. Ribbon-limbed climbers knot themselves across columns, dropping in coordinated bursts onto anything stunned by falling grit. Swarms of palm-sized skitterers blanket warm stones so densely they look like living gravel, parting only when a larger shadow passes.

There are flyers too, but not in vast open sky—these are gliders of the vault, launching from ledges to catch thermal updrafts rising from vents. Their calls bounce from rock to rock: clicking pulses, low hoots, metallic chattering that can sound like tool-work in the distance. You keep turning your head for a smithy that is not there.

The soundscape never empties. Constant scrape of claws on mineral. Heavy bodies shifting scree downslope. Sudden eruptions of shrieks when hunting bands collide. Dripping, hissing, vent-roar, rock-crack. From somewhere farther off, rhythmic thuds—either creatures pounding shell against stone or people doing the same.

Because there *are* people here, just not many, and not settled in one obvious center.

Built things appear in fragments, scattered through the wider caverns like punctuation marks:  
- a ring of stacked stone around a warm seep, blackened by repeated fires,  
- ladders of lashed bone and split reed-fiber descending into pits,  
- low walls mortared with clay and shell, already partly collapsed by tremor,  
- drying racks made from rib-like spars, holding strips of hide, scales, and unknown tissues,  
- warning totems: upright poles crusted with reflective shards that flash in daylight shafts.

No grand architecture. No metal towers. Tools are hand-made, immediate, pragmatic: chipped stone hooks, weighted throwing cords, bone awls, hide slings, shale blades set into wooden grips hardened by smoke. You see workstations where minerals are ground in shallow basins; pigments streaked on walls; tally marks cut into soft stone then partially healed over by dripping calcite. Occupation here is temporary by design. Build light, move fast, leave signs for those who come after.

The ecology runs on motion. Carcasses do not lie long; they disappear into layered feeding frenzies. Burrows collapse and become ambush pits. Thermal vents migrate, taking entire food webs with them. A corridor that was safe yesterday fills with aggressive nesting forms today. The people’s rules—if you listen and watch—match the land’s tempo:

1. Never sleep in the lowest hollow.  
2. Drink only from flowing condensation, never still pools with skins.  
3. Follow fresh scratches upward when tremors begin.  
4. Fire is for smoke as much as heat: smoke marks territory to some species and repels others.  
5. If the ceiling starts “raining dust,” leave before the stones do.

Light makes this all eerier, not gentler. Because it is normal daylight, your mind keeps expecting a familiar valley, a dry scrubland, a canyon plain. Instead it finds an underworld with daylight rules and subterranean instincts: open distances broken by crowded life, warm air in gusts, minerals replacing forests, and weather occurring in enclosed rooms.

At the regional scale, this is a chain of linked sink-basins and collapsed lava galleries, miles across, threaded by animal roads and human detours. Some sections are nearly amphitheaters, broad and sunlit through broken roofs; others pinch to steaming corridors where creatures pass shoulder to shoulder. The whole system is in active rearrangement. Floors heave. Ceilings shed plates. New skylights punch open after quakes, delivering sudden brightness to places that evolved in dimness, instantly redrawing migration and predation routes.

It feels less like a stable landscape than a perpetual first draft of one—warm, loud, overfull, and alive with things learning, every hour, where the ground now is.
