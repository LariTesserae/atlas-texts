At first glance it seems impossible: a **high place that is mostly water**, and yet not a sea in any familiar sense. It is a basin suspended near a mountain’s crown, a bowl of black stone still being born, its rim sharp and unfinished, as if the world only recently remembered it needed this feature and pushed it up in haste.

The basin is not large. You can cross it in an hour by raft, less if the storm-current takes you. Its scale is intimate, almost secret—like a chamber hidden inside a cliff. But within that chamber the weather behaves with oceanic force.

A permanent cloud hangs just above the waterline, dense and low enough to touch. Rain does not fall from it in drops. It descends in braided sheets, almost vertical rivers, loud enough to erase speech. The sound is total—an unbroken drumming roar that fills bone and teeth. Sometimes it deepens into a bass throb when wind presses the rain against the basin walls; sometimes it rises into a metallic hiss when the sheets shred into mist. Silence here is folklore.

The water itself is cold, green-gray, and always moving. Not waves exactly—more like muscle contractions. Long swells lift from nowhere, cross at odd angles, and cancel each other in sudden pits. The surface opens and closes in rotating eyes where springwater erupts from below, because this high bowl is fed from inside the mountain: meltwater veins, geothermal breaths, pressure channels still carving their routes. That is the youngness of the place—**erosion barely begun**. The rock is fresh, raw, scarcely weathered, full of knife edges and brittle shelves that calve without warning. You can watch geology deciding what shape it wants.

There is little life, but not none. Vegetation clings where spray cannot strip it: thin mats of iron-colored moss, jointed reeds in two protected cracks, a few pale rope-vines trailing into the water as if testing it. No trees. No soft meadows. Every green thing looks provisional.

Fauna appears in flashes. Needlefish with translucent spines schooling beneath the rain-dark surface. White cave-swallows that nest in dry overhangs and dart out only during brief calmer intervals. Once in a while, something larger rolls beneath the raft—broad-backed, slow, blind-eyed—feeding at the upwelling mouths where mineral warmth gathers plankton in brief blooms. It leaves no wake, just a rearrangement of the chop.

Scattered around the rim are signs of people who came before or come seasonally:  
- low stone windbreaks stacked by hand, no mortar  
- hide lashings blackened by constant wet  
- fish traps woven from split root  
- a few narrow platforms pegged into rock, used for watching currents or lowering baskets into the springs  
- carved runnels that divert rainwater into cistern hollows (though “divert” is optimistic; in heavy surge, everything overflows)

Technology is simple, almost stubbornly so—bone hooks, stone weights, cordage, seal-skin floats, hammered soft-metal needles traded from lower valleys but rare here. Nothing fragile survives long. Anything built must bend, drain, or be replaced.

The rules of the place are learned bodily:

1. **Never trust still water**; it means a down-pull opening nearby.  
2. **Anchor in three directions**; one line always snaps.  
3. **Sleep above the fifth stain on the wall**; that marks ordinary surge height.  
4. **Listen with your feet**; tremors in planks arrive before surface changes.  
5. **Do not name tomorrow’s shape**; the basin revises itself overnight.

And that last rule is true in more than practical terms. The strangeness here is subtle but undeniable. In the heaviest rain, the impact patterns on the surface form temporary geometries—hexagonal fields, branching scripts, rings within rings—that persist too long to be random. The locals say the mountain is “writing drainage plans” through water, testing future channels. Others say the basin is a lung, and each storm is an inhale from somewhere deeper in the earth.

By noon the light is surprisingly clear. Despite cloud and rain, this is not a dark place. Light enters sideways, reflected off wet stone and the pale undersides of cloud, so everything is visible in a flattened silver. Depth perception becomes unreliable; distances feel closer than they are. The far rim seems within reach, until a rain curtain slides between and turns it into rumor.

At night there is no real darkness either—just dim, diffuse glow through vapor, enough to see ropes, edges, moving water. The roar continues. Sleep becomes adaptation, not rest.

So this hidden highwater world feels both miniature and immense: a pocket-sized inland storm-ocean, raw and under construction, sparsely alive, violently active, with just enough human presence to prove it can be endured—but not controlled.
