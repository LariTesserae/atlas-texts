At first it feels like a plain at night—low, wind-worked, cold enough that breath would plume if there were anyone here to breathe. The ground is mostly stone and compacted silt, iron-dark and matte except where it sweats. Moisture doesn’t sit as lakes or broad streams; it gathers in seams. Along shallow depressions, a skin of water appears and disappears with the hour, drawn up from below by pressure and temperature, then gone again into the porous earth. Condensation beads on exposed rock faces, collects at ledges, drips in patient rhythms into narrow runnels that never quite become rivers.

There are no plants. No grasses combing the wind, no moss, no root-cracked soil. The surfaces are bare mineral: shales split into paper edges, rounded basalt humps, chalky fans of old sediment, and long fields of pebble scree that shift softly under constant movement. Erosion has had ages to compose this place. Nothing is jagged by youth; everything is worn to forms that fit together—channels, bowls, undercuts, shelves—an old architecture with no builder.

And then the sound resolves, and the plain is no plain at all.

It is crowded with life.

Not life that rises as stems and crowns, but life that crawls, threads, swarms, clings, burrows, glides low over stone. Every surface is occupied. Chitinous bodies the size of fingernails cluster in mats over damp patches. Long, segmented grazers—if “graze” can mean rasping mineral films and salt crusts—move in braided caravans through troughs in the ground. Blind, pale things knot together in the wet cuts, parting and reforming like poured grain. Darker, plated hunters patrol the margins, their limbs ticking on stone in dense polyrhythms.

The deafening quality comes from accumulation: millions of contacts per minute. Scrape, click, hiss, drumming wing-cases, friction-whines, shell-on-shell impacts, sudden synchronized rattles when a shadow passes overhead. Even water is loud here; each drop into a hollow is amplified by hard surfaces and answered by movement. The ground itself hums with tunneling. Air vibrates with stridulation, with membranous buzzes from short, frantic flights between one moisture pocket and another. Silence does not exist; at best there are shifts in texture.

In the dark, light is local and biological.

Not bright—never bright—but intermittent. Along the wetter seams, colonies of translucent organisms pulse with cold blue-green glows when disturbed, so that a passing predator writes itself as a moving absence through faint luminescent fog. Some of the larger crawlers carry symbiotic films that spark under abrasion, producing brief comet-trails as bodies brush rock. In deeper cuts, where condensation is most reliable, curtains of thread-thin, non-plant growth hang like mineral fuzz and emit a dim, steady violet, enough to outline the constant traffic beneath them.

The sky, when visible between low ridges, is usually sealed in cloud: thick, low, and fast-moving. Rain comes in short hard events—ten minutes of metallic drumming, then long intervals of mist and drip. Temperature holds cool and stable except at the moment rain stops, when evaporative chill can drop sharply and force entire swarms to retreat underground in rolling waves. Then, as surfaces re-wet by condensation, they return.

The ecology runs on scarcity and efficiency. No leaves, no fruit, no wood, no open photosynthetic base. Energy comes from films of microbial chemistry on rock, from mineral-rich seepage, from detritus of bodies endlessly eaten and re-eaten, from chemoactive patches where groundwater brings dissolved metals to the surface. Fauna here are not occasional animals in empty terrain; fauna are the terrain’s moving layer, nearly continuous. Trails are etched not by rivers alone but by generations of bodies following moisture gradients so consistently they polish stone.

There are rules, if you stay long enough to feel them:

- **Moisture is law.** Every migration, hunt, molt, and hatch tracks dew lines and seep timing.
- **Height is danger.** Exposed ridges host fast aerial predators that dive by vibration sense; most life keeps low, in cracks and folds.
- **Stillness is camouflage and risk.** To stop moving is to vanish in a dense field of similar forms—unless you stop in the wrong lane and are simply consumed by traffic.
- **Sound maps the world.** Many species are near-blind; they navigate by reflected clicks and substrate resonance. A shifted pebble can reroute thousands.

Despite the strangeness, the place is not chaotic. It is established—old in habit, balanced in its relentless way. Population booms and collapses happen in micro-regions after rain pulses, but at the valley scale the system endures, cycling through wet-dark, damp-dark, and briefly drier-dark states with practiced precision.

If you stood on one of the lower rises and watched for an hour, the landscape would seem to breathe without lungs: condensation forming, swarms thickening, predators cutting lanes, rain striking, burrows sealing, glows rising, then fading. A cool, low world of stone and water traces, where life has abandoned the vertical and become a moving weather of bodies.
