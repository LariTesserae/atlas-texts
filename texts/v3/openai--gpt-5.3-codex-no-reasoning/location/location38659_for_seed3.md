The city hangs where a mountain range is still deciding it wants to be mountains.

Not metaphorically—literally unfinished stone rises in broad, tilted shelves, as if the crust has been poured and left to cool in place. Cliffs end in blunt, unweathered lips. Valleys look drafted but not yet carved. Everything feels newly exposed, from basalt the color of wet charcoal to pale seams of mineral that glimmer softly in the low light. Erosion hasn’t had time to write its script here. Slopes are sharp where they should have softened; boulder fields look dropped, not rolled.

Across this high, raw country, an industrial metropolis has been threaded like steel stitching.

It does not sprawl evenly. It gathers in long bands along ledges and saddle-points, where rail lines can cling and towers can anchor. Whole districts are suspended on trusswork above emptiness, connected by bridge-roads that vanish into fog and return with freight. The open spaces between neighborhoods are huge: unfinished ground, sparse groves, quarry basins holding dark water, animal corridors marked by warning lights. This is urbanism at altitude, stretched thin over a giant skeletal terrain.

The air is cool and never fully dry. Moisture arrives in episodes—low cloud spilling uphill, fine rain drifting sideways for an hour, condensation beading on every cable and handrail by nightfall. You can walk through a district under dry skies and still hear dripping from pipes, scaffold joints, moss-coated concrete. Water collects in pockets: rooftop cisterns, rock bowls, old blast craters, gutter-reservoirs cut into transit decks. Small streams appear after mist-heavy mornings and disappear by afternoon into grates, fractures, and thirsty gravel.

Vegetation takes advantage of this irregular mercy. Nothing like a continuous forest—more a lush punctuation. Fern thickets in shade trenches. Broad-leafed shrubs around warm vent stacks. Vertical gardens climbing abandoned elevator shafts. Tough alpine grasses woven with vine growth around chain-link fences. Trees appear in scattered clusters, their roots gripping fresh fractures in bedrock and old masonry alike. Where runoff is reliable, whole green ravines form between industrial sectors, dense enough to swallow service roads in ten years.

The light is almost always subdued. Thick cloud cover, smoke haze, and the high walls of half-formed topography keep the day in a prolonged dawn. At noon, metal surfaces glow rather than shine. At night, sodium lamps, signal beacons, and conveyor indicators paint the fog in bands of amber and red. The sky rarely goes black; it hovers in gradients of graphite and bruised violet.

And life—animal life—is everywhere.

Not hidden. Integrated, competing, opportunistic, ceremonial. Flocks wheel around turbine towers and nest in crane arms. Sure-footed herd animals move along viaduct shadows as if following ancestral routes older than the roads above them. Pack scavengers trail garbage trams. Amphibians breed in warm runoff channels. Insect swarms rise from condensation basins at shift change, drawing hunting birds and, in turn, children with nets and gloves. The city posts migration timetables the way other places post train schedules. Certain overpasses close for nesting season. Some districts dim their perimeter lights during moth bloom weeks because the power stations clog if they don’t.

The soundscape carries farther than seems possible in the cool thin air. It is loud, but not frantic: a layered, patient loudness. Deep generator hum from below-grade plants. Rhythmic clank of ore lifts. Wind through cable arrays like a giant bowed instrument. Distant animal calls, echoed and bent by unfinished stone faces. Sirens that do not signal emergency so much as phase changes—fog inbound, herd crossing, line pressure drop, shift turnover. Even silence here feels infrastructural, like a held note between machine cycles.

Time moves slowly, in thick increments. Systems are designed for endurance, not speed. Freight climbs by rack rail. Water is trapped, filtered, released, trapped again. Concrete cures in cold air under heated blankets. Moss colonizes rebar. Rock settles a few centimeters each year, and whole neighborhoods are adjusted accordingly—jacked, shimmed, re-pinned. People speak of decades the way lowland cities speak of weekends: when this escarpment finishes cracking, when that district finally roots, when the eastern shelf stops rising.

Then there is the part everyone accepts and nobody explains the same way: reality is unreliable at high contour.

Survey lines drift overnight and return by morning. A stairwell may contain one extra landing in fog conditions and none of the inspectors agree where it comes from. Rain sometimes falls upward along specific fault seams. Herds have been seen walking across empty air between two ridges at dusk, hoofbeats audible, bodies slightly out of phase with their shadows below. Factory clocks desynchronize in certain weather and workers mark time by bird behavior instead. Maps are printed on layered translucent sheets because no single version stays true for long.

The official rules, posted in enamel signs at transit nodes, are practical and strangely calm:

- If a route repeats an intersection three times, stop and wait for clear wind.
- Do not follow unfamiliar animal paths after second siren.
- Condensation on interior ceilings indicates gradient inversion; descend one level.
- If distant machinery sounds near, remain where footing is known.
- In heavy fog, trust anchored lights over visible structures.

No one treats these as supernatural warnings. They are maintenance protocols for a world still setting.

From a high freight platform at evening, you can see the whole character of the place at once: vast open reaches of raw uplift; sparse but intense bands of human construction; dark, wet pockets of green; moving constellations of fauna; dim cloud pressing low; red signal chains tracing safe passages through uncertainty. It is a city built in an unfinished mountain, in cool damp air, under patient noise, where geology, industry, ecology, and physics are negotiating a shared future in real time.
