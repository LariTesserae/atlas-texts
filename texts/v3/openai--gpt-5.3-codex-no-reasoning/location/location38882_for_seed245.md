It is a low world, broad as a sea, made by hand and still being made.

Not “hand” in the modern sense—no smooth machine tolerances, no hidden circuitry—but hand as in repetition: laid, stacked, tamped, fired, reset. Imagine a continent paved in brick the size of shields, each one slightly warped from kiln heat, each one stamped with a maker’s thumb-groove. Between the bricks run seams of bitumen and salt. The whole plain sweats.

Water is never far; it lives in the joints. Warm beads gather in grout lines, in chisel marks, under overhanging slabs. It does not rain often in the ordinary way. Instead, the air thickens until droplets condense on every surface at once, and the land begins to weep. You can hear that before you see it: tiny taps, millions of them, like fingernails on ceramic. Then the runoff finds channels designed for it long ago—ditches lined with stone shingles, stepped catch-basins, terracotta cistern throats—and the plain briefly becomes a map of silver threads.

There is no soil, and nothing green ever interrupts a horizon. No grasses in cracks. No lichens. No insect hum. Where life should have learned to cling, there is only chemistry: mineral crusts that bloom and fade in hours, thin skins of iridescent salts crawling over walls, fungal-looking films that are not alive by any test anyone trusts. They spread during humid pulses, then crack into powder in the heat. From far away, they mimic meadows. Up close, they are just reactions.

The heat rises from beneath as much as above. At ground level, the air trembles. Some districts are paved in dark vitrified blocks that hoard day-heat and release it all night, so even in dimness your shins feel an oven-breath. Other districts are pale, chalky, engineered to throw back what little light reaches them, but they too are warm because pipes run below everything—clay mains carrying steam, brine, and occasionally a black water that stains whatever touches it.

Scale distorts judgment here. A “wall” may be a hundred kilometers long, only waist-high, and function purely as a hydrological suggestion, nudging seasonal sheetwater half a degree east. “Towers” are rare and isolated: tapering stacks of fitted stone rings, each ring hauled by rope and capstan, no metal cranes, no precision lifts. They look ceremonial until one exhales and the top vents a cloud, and you realize it is a pressure chimney for the underworks. They are spaced so far apart that each has its own weather halo—its own swirl of mist, its own low thunder of condensation drums.

Because the world is built but primitive, every solution is huge and legible. No tiny valves: sluice gates the size of fort doors, opened by teams walking treadwheels. No electric pumps: piston wells driven by falling counterweights, reset by gangs with pry-bars. No polymers: seals made of layered leather, resin, hammered lead. Everywhere, tool marks. Everywhere, maintenance ladders, scaffolds, stacked replacement blocks waiting in geometric yards. The place is total construction, but it never looks finished. It looks occupied by process.

Erosion has barely begun, because there has not been enough “natural” time for softness to win. Corners remain sharp unless deliberately rounded. Channels are too true. Embankments still show the basket-weave imprint of the forms they were poured against. When collapse happens, it happens young: a culvert giving way not from age but from miscalculation, taking a square kilometer of paving down into a steaming cavity where raw substrate—if there is any substrate—is revealed as more construction at a deeper phase. The land feels embryonic: a first draft at planetary scale, already inhabited by weather.

And the weather is turbulent in ways that ignore comfort. Warm updrafts from vent fields collide with cool condensation fronts and create marching walls of fog that travel fast and low, knee-height one moment, shoulder-height the next. Dry lightning forms inside these banks without rain, arcing between bronze rods mounted on pylons, then vanishing. Whole sectors cycle between slick and dusty in an afternoon. A basin can be mirror-wet at noon, then by dusk coated in a brittle crust that cracks underfoot like thin ice.

Sound is mostly restraint. No birds, no beasts, no leaves, no surf. The background is a sparse architecture of noises: drip cadence in cistern courts, the distant thunk of a gate beam seating into stone, cables humming in wind, steam muttering from relief vents. During active cycles, there are sudden crescendos—the roar of a siphon priming, a full aqueduct dropping into vacuum, masonry popping from thermal shock—then quiet again so complete your own breathing feels intrusive.

The dim light comes from a sky that behaves like smoked glass. Day exists, but filtered, amber-gray, as if sunlight has to pass through suspended ash or engineered aerosols. Shadows are soft-edged and short, even when objects are tall. At night, the world does not go black; stored heat and chemical blooms give off a dull internal glow. Salt flats phosphoresce faintly where brine evaporates. Vent mists catch this glow and hang as luminous veils. Distance is hard to measure; horizons dissolve into lit haze.

Then the weirdness, which is not ornament but rule-breaking infrastructure.

In some tracts, geometry lags behind function: water runs uphill through channels that were cut downhill, because gradient is occasionally negotiated rather than fixed. You can watch a flow hesitate at a junction as if deciding. In others, repeated structures refuse exact repetition; a row of identical arches, viewed from left to right, gains an extra arch halfway through without any break in spacing. Maps made in the morning can be wrong by evening, not from movement of landmasses but from revisions in adjacency. A stair may descend sixteen steps and arrive one meter above where it began, opening onto a plain that should not fit inside the measured perimeter of its walls.

The builders—if still present—must have accepted this long ago. Their primitive technology has become ritualized error-correction. Knotted cords hung along causeways to detect distance drift. Plumb bobs lowered into “straight” shafts to listen for lateral gravity shear. Stone markers carved with yesterday’s coordinates and today’s, crossed out and recarved until the face is palimpsest. There are whole plazas dedicated to recalibration: circles of posts, sighting slits, water clocks, shadow bowls, all used to renegotiate what “level” means this week.

Despite the turbulence and reality slippage, the landscape is sparse. Immense empty courts between active works. Kilometers of silent paving with only a single maintenance obelisk. Reservoirs the size of inland seas with no boats, no fish, no reeds—just hot water breathing mist. Presence is isolated and emphatic: one stair, one tower, one gate complex, then nothing for hours.

If you stand still long enough, the place reveals its ecology—not biological, but procedural. Condensation feeds runoff; runoff feeds cisterns; cisterns feed steam; steam drives pumps; pumps refill elevated basins; overflow seeds new condensation. Mineral skins bloom in the humid phases and alter surface albedo, which shifts local heat patterns, which changes where fog condenses. The world is alive with feedback loops instead of organisms. It metabolizes through masonry.

And always there is the feeling of being early. Not ruined-after, but before-stable. A constructed planet in adolescence: too hot, too wet at the joints, too large for its own surveying methods, still testing laws. Quiet, mostly. Empty, mostly. But never still.
