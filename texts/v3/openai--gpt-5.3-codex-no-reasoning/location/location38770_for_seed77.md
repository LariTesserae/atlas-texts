It is a hot, low country so large that distance behaves like weather.

From above—if there were an above broad enough to hold it—you would not see wilderness. You would see an expanse of made ground, assembled in plates and fields, matte and pale under an ordinary sun. Not metallic-bright, not futuristic; more like fired clay, lime, compacted ash, poured stone. A constructed skin laid over the low world in every direction, interrupted only by seams, basins, channels, and the occasional open throat where work is still unfinished.

The heat is the first constant. By midmorning the surface exhales it. Air shivers close to the ground. Horizons blur into wavering lines. Yet this is not a pure desert: moisture appears in episodes and pockets. Nights pull condensation out of the air. Dawn leaves thin films of water beading along joints between slabs, collecting in shallow engineered depressions, dripping from the undersides of overhangs into dark cistern hollows. Rain comes irregularly—short, hard falls that race across the built planes, vanish down grates, and then are gone, leaving only a mineral smell and damp shadows.

Because erosion is barely begun, edges remain sharp. Corners have not softened. Channels are clean-cut and geometrical, as if recently revealed. In some regions the surface looks newly set, its textures still raw: trowel-like ridges, aggregate not yet worn smooth, expansion joints straight as ruled ink. Elsewhere the first hints of age begin—hairline cracking, powdering skins, tiny collapses where heat and sudden wet have started the long argument that will someday make soil.

Vegetation is present, but scattered and opportunistic. Plants gather where the built world accidentally behaves like habitat: in drain mouths, in sheltered recesses, along leaky conduits, around condensation towers that are no longer functioning as towers and now simply sweat at dawn. Most growth is low and tough—tufts, wiry mats, wax-leafed rosettes, stem clusters pushing through fractures. No forests, no meadows. Green appears as punctuation in a long sentence of stone-colored construction.

There are no animals. No insects ticking in the cracks, no birds crossing the heat shimmer, no burrows, no tracks. The absence is total enough that even a dropped pebble sounds singular, unshared. Ecology here is between mineral, water, heat, and plant. Wind moves spores and dust. Moisture decides germination. Sun culls most attempts. What survives does so in isolation, each patch a small republic.

Sound stays low. In calm hours: almost nothing—just faint thermal pings as surfaces expand, a dry whisper of wind sliding through apertures, the occasional drip in shaded cavities. During rain bursts the whole plain briefly speaks: rattling impacts, rush through channels, resonant booming from hidden cistern chambers. Then quiet returns quickly, as if absorbed by the scale.

And scale is always the second constant. This place is not a complex or a city ruin; it is continental construction. Whole weather cells form over it, guided by temperature differences between dark and pale sectors. Morning condensation bands can stretch beyond sight. One district may be dry and glaring while another, far off, is under a brief gray curtain of rain. Heat plumes rise from broad decks and pull wind into patterned circulations, so that even the air feels planned—though whatever did the planning is absent now.

Despite being fully made, it does not feel crowded. Density is low: vast intervals between structures, between verticals, between anything that might be called a landmark. There are plazas the size of salt flats. Causeways crossing shallow artificial depressions for days without meeting a wall. Reservoir basins mostly empty, lined and waiting, holding only episodic water and rings of mineral stain. Emptiness is not neglect here; it is part of the design language.

The rules of the world seem simple and strict:

- Surfaces are intentional.
- Water is intermittent but decisive.
- Heat governs daily rhythm.
- Life, where it exists, must root in accidents.
- Silence is the background state.

Walk long enough and subtle regional differences emerge. In one tract, the material shifts toward dark basaltic composites that absorb heat and drive violent midday updrafts. In another, pale calcareous panels keep mornings cooler and host more dew-fed growth. Some zones are in “embryonic” condition: modular blocks stacked, fused, but not yet sealed; open conduits waiting for components that never came. These unfinished territories feel geologic and architectural at once—like bedrock that was manufactured yesterday.

At dusk the normal light softens everything without dramatics. The place does not become romantic; it becomes legible. Seams glow faintly, moisture flashes briefly, plant shadows sharpen against warm planes. Then night releases stored heat slowly. Condensation begins again in select hollows. A dark sheen forms in channels. By dawn there will be enough dampness for roots to drink, salts to migrate, and one more hairline crack to appear.

So the landscape is active, but not noisy; alive, but barely inhabited; artificial, but already entering natural time. It is a made lowland at planetary scale, in the first chapter of weathering.
