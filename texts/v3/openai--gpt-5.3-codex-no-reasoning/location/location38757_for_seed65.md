At first it looks like pure stone and sky.

You stand on a **high plateau broken into basins and knife-edged ridges**, so elevated the air feels thin and rinsed clean. The light is intense—hard, white-gold, almost metallic—not because it is hot, but because there is so little haze between sun and ground. Every fracture line in the rock is visible. Every pebble throws a shadow.

From a distance the land seems empty, almost abstract: long shelves of pale volcanic stone, old glacially-scoured faces, fans of shattered scree settled into place ages ago. Nothing appears to move. Nothing appears to grow. The terrain has the confidence of something that has already finished changing.

Then the sound reaches you.

Not weather—**life**.

The whole region roars with wings, throats, claws on stone, chittering nests in cliff hollows, deep booming calls rolling from escarpment to escarpment. In the dry bright air, sound travels monstrously well. A flock crossing one canyon can fill ten miles with noise. Colonies of animals occupy ledges by the thousands, and their constant calling creates a pressure in the ears like standing near surf or machinery. Even silence here is only a thinner layer of noise.

---

Water is scarce in the way breath is scarce at altitude: always relevant, never generous.

You don’t find broad rivers. You find **seep-lines**, condensation gutters under overhangs, shallow tarns in stone bowls, thin ribbons of meltwater that appear at the same hour each day and vanish again. In shadowed cuts, water beads and drips with patient regularity, feeding mats of dark lichen and low, stubborn cushion plants no higher than a hand. Elsewhere, broad expanses remain dry for weeks, then catch a brief cold rain that darkens the rock, fills every depression, and is gone by morning into cracks, frost, and air.

Because the place has been like this for a very long time, the ecology is precise. Nothing is accidental.

- Nesting colonies align with moisture pockets.
- Grazers track mineral salts and seasonal seepage by memory.
- Predators patrol fixed aerial corridors along thermal edges.
- Scavengers keep ledges clean so thoroughly that almost nothing rots in place.

It is an abundant world built on tiny budgets.

---

Vegetation stays low, sparse, and close to stone: silver moss in cracks, wiry sedge near damp shelves, flattened shrubs in wind shadows. No forests, no meadows, no soft cover. Plants are punctuation marks, not text.

Fauna, by contrast, write the whole page.

There are cliff-birds in impossible numbers, broad-winged and narrow-winged, some riding updrafts all day without landing. There are horned runners moving in file across high saddles, their hooves worn to the same paths generation after generation. There are burrowing colonies in pockets of thin soil where warmth lingers under sunstruck rock. There are pale amphibious things in the cold basins—more skin than scale—that surface only at dawn to feed in temporary pools before retreating into damp crevices.

And overhead, always circles of carrion fliers, so high they look like drifting soot until they fold and drop.

---

Evidence of intelligence exists, but lightly.

Not buildings—never cities, never walls. Instead: stacked hunting blinds of loose stone used for a season and abandoned; bone awls; sling-stones sorted by size in shallow pits; hide windbreaks pinned under rocks near migration chokepoints. Primitive technology adapted to a place that does not reward permanence. Anything too elaborate would be torn by wind, cracked by frost, or simply unnecessary in a land whose patterns rarely change.

People—if you encounter them—move like another animal species here: migratory, observant, opportunistic, deeply literate in tracks, echoes, and cloud shadows. They know which ledge drips at noon in late cold-season, which canyon amplifies warning calls, which nesting cliffs can be harvested without collapsing next year’s population. Their craft is less invention than timing.

---

The weirdness is subtle, never flamboyant.

Distances misbehave in the crystal air; near things look far and far things look touchable. Sound bends around buttresses and arrives before the source is visible. At certain hours, sun and wind together produce standing tones in narrow chimneys—long, organ-like notes that seem geological but are made by nothing more than moving air and old rock. Animals use these tones as cues, and whole flocks may lift at once when a particular resonance begins.

There are also basins where fog forms upside-down: low cold vapor hugging the stone while the air above remains brilliantly clear. Creatures move through these fog sheets like shadows under glass.

---

For all its noise, this is a stable landscape.

Not static in the sense of lifeless—static in the sense of **equilibrium**. The erosion has already done its dramatic work. Valleys are cut, talus settled, drainage patterns ancient and dependable. Populations surge and thin within known limits. Wetness comes and goes in repeating rhythms. Even predation feels cyclical rather than chaotic.

If you returned after ten years, you would find the same ridgelines, the same nesting walls white-marked and clamorous, the same seep under the same overhang, the same wind writing the same dust trails at dusk.

A bright, cold, high country of stone and echo:
almost empty to the eye,
overfull to the ear,
and alive in every gap where water briefly appears.
