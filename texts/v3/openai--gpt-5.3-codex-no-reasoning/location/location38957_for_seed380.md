The city is under a lid of stone.

Not buried in panic, not ruined, not hidden from war—simply built below, where the rock sweats and the air keeps memory. It occupies a basin hollowed long ago by water that no longer runs as a river, only as seep and drip and quiet standing mirror. Everything here feels like it has already happened and settled into place.

You enter through a descending rail tunnel, and the first thing you notice is not darkness but **dull sheen**. The walls glimmer as if varnished: mineral films, condensation, old soot, and the polished touch of generations. Light comes in low bands from sodium pipes and caged filament globes, most of them amber or weak green. Nothing is bright enough to cast hard shadow. Instead the city has gradients—wet gray to warmer rust, brass to black, algae-pale to coal.

It is small for a city, and complete. One district, perhaps three if you count like locals do:  
- the **Rib** (housing terraces anchored into the curved walls),  
- the **Belly** (workshops, mills, pumping halls),  
- and the **Poolward** (marsh edge, pens, markets, sluice courts).  

You can cross all of it on foot in an hour, slowly, without hurrying. Most people do not hurry anyway. The place teaches a patient gait.

---

### Terrain and Water

The floor of the basin is uneven, not by dramatic cliffs but by long, shallow folds. Channels snake between them, brick-lined in some parts and raw stone in others. Water is close under everything. Step too hard in the wrong yard and cold moisture rises between paving blocks. In old buildings, ground rooms have tide marks though there is no tide, only breathing pressure in the rock.

Water arrives from everywhere and nowhere: condensed from chill ceilings, pushed through fissures, gathered from distant catch galleries, reclaimed from steam and breath. It drips constantly in some corridors with the steady confidence of a metronome. In others it gathers into hanging beads that never seem to fall.

At the edge of the inhabited quarter is a broad marsh-chamber lit by reflected city glow. Reeds are sparse, silver-bladed, clustered where warm outflow meets cold seep. Moss grows in narrow strips like handwriting on stone. The marsh is less a wilderness than an organ of infrastructure: settling ponds, filtration beds, overflow stairs, timber walkways on iron stilts. Yet it has its own weather—low mist that moves as if deciding things.

### Built Form and Industry

This is an industrial place, but age has rounded its machinery into ecology. Engines do not scream here; they **hum** and **pulse**. Massive flywheels turn behind mesh and riveted guardplates, their spokes furred with condensation. Belt drives cross alleys overhead like black ribbons. Pumps throb deep underground, so deep you feel them in your jaw before you hear them.

The architecture layers by era:  
- earliest: hand-cut vaults and load-bearing piers, blunt and wet,  
- middle: brick galleries, arched drains, soot-varnished lintels,  
- latest: steel catwalks, ceramic insulators, pressure gauges, relay cabinets.  

Everything is maintained, but little is replaced. Bolts are re-threaded, bearings repacked, cracked housings stitched with iron straps. This gives the city a weathered competence. Nothing gleams. Everything works.

Streets are narrow where they need to be and unexpectedly broad around service wells and turning cranes. There are numbered hatches in plazas that open once a week for inspection teams. There are warning tiles in three colors no outsider reads correctly the first time.

### Light, Air, and Temperature

Cool always, but not biting. The air sits around the temperature of stone cellars and river caves. Near the boilers you get pockets of warmth; near intake shafts, drafts like wet cloth. Breath is often visible in morning shifts.

Light is rationed by purpose. Work zones are clearer; domestic zones rely on reflection and familiarity. People paint doorframes in matte pigments that can be seen at low luminance: oxide red, bone white, deep cobalt. Children learn neighborhoods by scent and echo before they learn addresses.

There is no sky, but there is atmospheric change. Fog thickens and thins according to pump cycles and distant pressure fronts in the upper world. Some days the ceiling disappears into haze and the city feels endless. Other days the vault is close enough to touch.

---

### Vegetation and Ecology

Plant life is sparse and deliberate. You find it where water chemistry permits and people leave room: fern pockets in mortar cracks, lichen crust on cooler northern walls, narrow planters of bitter greens under lamp heat. Fruit is rare. Tubers, fungi, and reed-grain are common.

Fauna, however, is abundant—astonishingly so.

Not large herds; rather a dense parliament of adapted creatures:  
- pale marsh birds with long probing beaks, nesting in ventilation rafters,  
- slick amphibians in pump sumps, tagged and counted because they indicate contamination,  
- colonies of soft-furred tunnel mammals that live on spilled grain and insect blooms,  
- translucent fish in settling pools,  
- beetles and moths that gather around transformer warmth,  
- eel-like scavengers in overflow channels, prized in cuisine and feared in bare feet.  

Animals are integrated into rules. Certain corridors are closed during nesting months. Intake grilles are fitted to protect migration runs. Whistle codes at shift change include tones that drive flocks away from turbine halls.

---

### Sound and Slowness

Despite industry, the city is mostly quiet in the way libraries are quiet: full of low ongoing activity. No wind-roar, no leaves, no distant traffic. Instead:

drip, belt, hum, a valve click, rubber boots on wet stone, a cart wheel squeaking once per revolution, someone coughing in another tunnel, a flock rustling overhead, then stillness again.

Loud events are rare and therefore sacred or alarming: hammer tests, sirens, festival drums, a steam release. People pause when sound changes. Children are taught the “normal song” of each block; if the song shifts, tell an elder.

Time moves slowly here, and systems are designed for gradual change. Sediment settles over months. Mineral buildup is scraped seasonally. Walls bow by millimeters over decades and are mapped with ink lines. Nothing dramatic is expected, but everything is watched.

---

### Weirdness and Rules of the World

The strangeness is practical first, uncanny second.

Water in certain basins remembers charge: run a hand through and nearby lamps flicker.  
Some caverns produce standing tones that interfere with speech, so people communicate there by hand signs.  
In one district, droplets fall upward for a few seconds every hour when pressure equalizes in hidden shafts—a normal event, never explained the same way twice.  
Animals avoid specific stairwells days before a pump fault. No one argues with this; inspectors follow the animals.

There are civic rules shaped by these conditions:

1. **Never block a dripline.** Drips map fractures; fractures map safety.  
2. **Leave three handspans clear around every gauge.** Readability is law.  
3. **Do not feed white-wing marsh birds near intake towers.**  
4. **At amber siren, stand still and listen before moving.** Sound locates hazards better than sight.  
5. **Report warm stone where stone should be cold.** Heat travels strangely belowground.  

Even festivals obey hydraulics. Lantern processions follow old flood marks. Marriages are registered in the Condensation Hall where names are etched on copper and slowly patinated by cave-air.

---

By the time you leave, you realize the place is not dark so much as inward. Not decayed, not booming—**settled**. A wet, dim, engineered hollow where city and marsh and machine have negotiated a truce so old it looks like nature. The people who live there do not speak of conquering the underground. They speak of tuning it, listening to it, and being allowed, for another season, to remain.
