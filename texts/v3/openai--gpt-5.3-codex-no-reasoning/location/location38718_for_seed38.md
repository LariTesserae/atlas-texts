From a distance—if distance can be said to exist in a place where every horizon is softened by moisture—the highlands look less like mountains and more like the backs of ancient, sleeping animals, broad and rounded and furred in green. Nothing is sharp here except occasional stone ribs where the skin has worn thin. The land has been old for so long that even its steepness has become patient. Slopes sag into terraces, terraces sink into bog-shelves, and every hollow holds water or the memory of it.

It is warm, but not bright. Light arrives filtered through a permanent breathing veil: low cloud, transpired vapor, and drifting spores. Morning and evening differ only by a slight shift in color—olive-gray to amber-gray, then back again. Sunbeams appear rarely, and when they do they are not clean shafts but thickened columns made visible by mist and pollen, as if the air itself has roots.

The ground is almost never truly ground. It is woven. Moss over root-mats over peat over old fallen trunks that did not rot so much as become incorporated. Ferns grow from tree ferns growing from nurse logs growing from older collapsed groves. Entire ridgelines are held together by braided living tissue; step in one place and the movement travels outward like pressure through a muscle. Pools lie everywhere, but they are often hidden under floating leaves and filamentous duckweed, so walking requires a local’s caution or an animal’s memory. Water sits near the surface, cool at a hand’s depth, tannin-dark in shaded hollows and silver where it creeps over exposed stone.

Those stones are very old. They are rounded, pitted, and grooved by eras of seep and drip rather than violent rivers. Cliff faces weep constantly, bead by bead, feeding curtains of liverwort and dangling roots that glisten in the dim. In some places the bedrock has dissolved into honeycombs large enough to shelter animals, each cavity lined with calcified algae and tiny translucent snails. Now and then you find a bare shoulder of rock polished by vanished glaciers from ages no one remembers, now wrapped in orchids and rain-black lichen.

Vegetation is not just dominant; it is infrastructural. Trees are frequent, but "tree" is too narrow a word for the columnar growths that rise and then fuse, forming vaulted groves like cathedrals assembled by patient grafting. Lianas descend in ropes, then take root midair in pockets of moss and become secondary trunks. Epiphytes make hanging gardens between branches. Ground plants are shoulder-high in many places: broad-leafed aroids, cycads, giant horsetails near the wettest cuts. Flowering is subdued in color under the dim light—cream, bruised purple, muted orange—but intense in scent: wet spice, fermenting fruit, peppery sap.

Despite the thickness of life, the soundscape is restrained. The loudest constant voice is dripwater: ticks, soft plinks, occasional hollow notes into stone bowls. Wind rarely moves as a single sweep; it threads through layers, turning one canopy while the understory stays still. Animals call, but mostly in short utterances—single whistles, low throbs, brief churring bursts—followed by long pauses. Far away, at irregular intervals, comes a resonant boom: not thunder, but seedpods the size of jars splitting open under internal pressure, echoing through mist.

Fauna is abundant and adapted to vertical, wet complexity. Gliding herbivores with membrane-fringed limbs move tree to tree without ever touching the saturated floor. Long-legged marsh browsers pick paths across root lattices, spreading seeds in mud that never dries completely. Amphibians are everywhere: jewel-eyed, soft-skinned, many of them silent, communicating through throat pulses and faint bioluminescent flashes visible at dusk under leaves. In the pools, fish with labyrinthine breathing organs patrol water low in oxygen; some spend hours nosing through flooded leaf litter, half in water, half in air. Insects are dense but not swarming—beetles that mimic seed husks, moths that drink seepage from rock, slow dragonflies cruising under the canopy like small patrol craft.

The strangeness is subtle and pervasive rather than dramatic. Plants here seem to remember shape. Cut a vine and over weeks it will curve back toward its former path, following no obvious support. Certain groves grow in concentric rings around empty centers where nothing takes root, as if enclosing old events. In the dimmest basins, pale fronds fold and unfold in synchrony with distant tides no ocean could physically transmit this far inland. Water in some hollows condenses upward at dawn, gathering on leaf undersides first, raining gently from below. No one calls this impossible; this is simply how this plateau behaves.

Signs of built presence exist, scattered and half-absorbed. A line of standing stones appears on one ridge, each block wrapped in roots, their tops carved into shallow cups that collect rain and seeds. Lower down, a fragment of wall—stacked without mortar—has become a retaining spine for a terrace of medicinal shrubs gone feral. There are occasional footpaths, raised with old logs now sprouted through, then lost again where moss has erased edges. No metal, no glass, no machines. The few artifacts feel less like impositions and more like temporary agreements with growth.

At vast scale, the whole high country runs on slow pulses. Moisture rises from lower forests, catches on the uplift, settles as mist, feeds the sponge of vegetation, then leaks away through hidden channels into distant valleys. Seasons exist mostly as ratios of rain to rain. Change is measurable in trunk-thickness, in peat depth, in how far a root buttress has advanced across a stone over twenty years. Landslides are rare but monumental when they come: not rockfalls, but whole living mats slipping meters at a time, then reknitting.

To stand here is to feel that life has moved beyond competition into occupation—an old, nearly complete filling-in of available form. Empty space is brief. Death is distributed, digested, replanted. Even silence is inhabited. And yet the place does not feel crowded so much as saturated: a warm, breathing upland where age, water, and green persistence have negotiated a truce that resembles permanence.
