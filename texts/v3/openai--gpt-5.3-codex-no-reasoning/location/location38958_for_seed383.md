The city is under the skin of the world, but not deep enough to forget the sun.

It lies in a collapsed basin where an old roof of stone gave way ages ago, opening a broken oval to the sky. Around that opening, terraces of pale rock descend in long, irregular steps, and on those steps a settlement has grown—if “grown” can be used where nothing green has ever rooted. From above it would look like a chalk spiral scored by watermarks: districts made of quarried blocks, ramps, retaining walls, cistern mouths, kiln courts, beast pens, all spread wide with unusual gaps between them, as if the builders expected floods, stampedes, or both.

The place is bright in a way caves should not be. Sunlight pours through the breach for most of the day, reflecting off mineral dust and whitewashed plaster until even shaded alleys glow. The stone here is light-colored—salt-pale limestone and bands of gypsum—so the whole basin acts like a bowl of reflected light. Eyes narrow. Cloth hoods are common, not for heat but glare.

---

Moisture arrives in episodes, never as a reliable season.  
For long stretches everything is merely cool and dry, surfaces rough and powdering at the touch. Then condensation events begin: cold air drains down from higher tunnels before dawn, meets warmer air rising from inhabited quarters, and the basin sweats. Beads form on lintels, ropes, railings, animal hides, sleeping mats. People wake early to wipe walls with absorbent cloth and wring precious water into jars. Children are sent along parapets with ceramic scrapers to collect dew from specially polished stone faces angled to the night sky.

Rain comes less often but violently—brief shafted downpours through the open roof, accompanied by sudden runoff from unseen cracks above. Ancient channels carved into every street wake instantly, turning plazas into braided streams. The city is built for this turbulence: hinged footbridges drop into place, market awnings collapse and lock, loose goods vanish into wall niches in minutes. Water roars, then is gone, swallowed by grates into old cistern systems older than memory.

No trees soften any of it. No moss. No grass in cracks. Even rot seems incomplete here.

---

Age is the dominant material.

Every surface shows long negotiation with abrasion and impact: corners rounded to knuckles, carvings worn down to grammarless grooves, stairs dipped hollow in the center by thousands of years of feet and hooves and claws. Entire neighborhoods appear half-melted, not by heat but by time and redirected water. Foundations belong to one era, upper walls to another, rooflines to ten others after that. Nothing is pristine unless it was made yesterday, and even yesterday’s work is done to match old fractures and old loads.

Erosion has not erased the city; it has edited it. Fragile ornament is gone. What remains is structure, channel, buttress, vault, catchment, pen, ramp. The settlement feels like the final draft of many civilizations that forgot their own names but remembered how to keep water, stone, and animals in difficult balance.

---

Life is animal-heavy and strangely loud in motion, quiet at rest.

Fauna fill the open spaces where vegetation would be elsewhere. Herd-beasts with keratinous lips scrape mineral films from rock. Pale six-legged runners move in packs through drainage corridors, emerging after rains to feed on bloom-like mats of microbial crust that appear briefly on wet stone. Ceiling swifts—leathery, near-blind gliders—nest in high cracks of the basin wall and descend in swirling columns at dusk to hunt moth-sized cave insects. Thick-shelled burrowers, somewhere between armadillo and trilobite, are kept by households for refuse breakdown; their shells are used for bowls and roof shingles after they die.

Predators exist, but the city has learned their routes. Bells are rare in this quiet culture, so warning is mostly visual: white flags crossed over a lane means fresh spoor, three chalk circles on a wall means do not enter after dark.

Despite the abundance of creatures, the soundscape is restrained. Hoof-scrape, distant wing-rush, drip, the soft grind of handmills, leather creak, low whistles used by handlers. People speak close and low; echoes are long here, and shouting feels like an insult to stone.

---

“Urban” here does not mean crowded towers. It means systems.

There are districts for water sorting, districts for hide curing, districts for animal exchange and bone craft, kiln quarters, rope works, and broad empty lanes kept clear as flood paths. Infrastructure is visible, celebrated, and constantly repaired with simple tools: stone mauls, copper chisels, bone gauges, plumb lines, sledges, wicker cranes turned by teams of beasts. Technology stays deliberately legible. Anything too intricate to mend by hand is distrusted.

At the city’s center stands the Spill Court, a vast stepped depression paved in interlocking slabs. Most days it is dry and blindingly bright. During storm events it becomes a churning reservoir where debris, silt, fishlike tunnel-creatures, and occasionally entire market carts whirl before sluicing into gated sinks. Teams stationed on the rim read the flow and pull levered barriers—heavy timber and stone counterweights—to redirect surge toward different cistern families. It is half engineering, half ritual theater, and absolutely essential.

---

The weirdness is subtle until you stay awhile.

The basin has “breathing hours.” Twice each day, pressure shifts in deeper tunnels send steady winds upward through grates. Loose cloth lifts. Torches flatten. Animals grow restless before it starts, then settle when it passes. Nobody fully understands the depth network below, only that the city sits atop a honeycomb older than the visible ruins.

Certain walls “weep” mineral-rich moisture in dry months while nearby stone remains powder-dry. People map these seeps with obsessive care, naming them as one might name springs. In one quarter, a row of standing stones hums faintly when stormwater fills the channels beneath them; children press ears to the rock to listen for incoming rain hours before clouds appear.

And there are the white flats at the basin’s eastern edge: broad crusted pans where evaporated floodwater leaves delicate crystal fields. Herds cross them in silence. Footsteps ring like glass taps. At noon, heat shimmer can produce moving mirages that look like distant crowds where no one stands—an old optical trick everyone knows and still glances at.

---

Rules of the place, learned quickly:

- Keep to high marks during rain alarms; low streets become rivers in under a minute.  
- Never block a channel, even for trade. Water has legal priority over all movement.  
- Carry a cloth at dawn; condensation is communal wealth, wastage is fined.  
- Do not kill gliders inside city bounds; their guano feeds the microbial pans that feed the runners that feed the pens.  
- If the stone starts humming, secure loose loads and tether animals: surge is coming.  
- In quiet hours, keep quiet. Sound travels farther than intention.

---

So the landscape resolves into a paradox: ancient yet constantly reacting, sparse yet unmistakably urban, bright yet subterranean, quiet yet always on the edge of violent motion. It is a city designed by erosion and corrected by hand, again and again, where people with simple tools manage a complicated world not by conquering instability, but by giving it lanes, basins, names, and schedules.
