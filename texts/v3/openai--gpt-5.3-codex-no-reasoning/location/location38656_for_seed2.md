At first it feels like open ocean, but the horizon gives it away.

The water is real seawater—cold, mineral, heavy with dissolved air—but it does not rest on geology. It lies in an engineered basin so large it has its own winds, gyres, and weather fronts: a planet-sized reservoir roofed only by sky. There is no shore in the ordinary sense. Instead, the “coast” is a continuous retaining escarpment of poured composite and ribbed steel, rising in stepped belts that vanish in haze. You are below the surface, and everywhere around you is structure: pylons, latticed breakwalls, submerged causeways, maintenance towers, intake throats, kilometer-wide floodgates. Even the seabed is fabricated—interlocking plates, expansion seams, sacrificial concrete reefs, cable trenches, ballast vaults.

It is young by ocean standards. Edges are still sharp. The anti-corrosion coatings shine where currents have not yet roughened them. Marker paint remains visible in huge numerals on retaining columns: depth bands, service districts, hazard lanes. The floor has not had ages to gather thick sediments; instead, there are thin drifts of rust-colored particulates in corners where eddies slow. Whole neighborhoods of infrastructure still look recently commissioned, the way a new harbor does before barnacles claim every bolt.

And yet life has arrived quickly.

Sparse vegetation means no forests of kelp, no meadows. Plant-life clings where it can: thin beards of green-brown algae on light-facing girders, pale films in calmer culverts, a few stubborn rafts of engineered marsh grass trapped in floating maintenance booms. Photosynthetic life is present, but it is not the dominant texture. The dominant texture is open water and machinery.

Fauna fills the emptiness instead. Schools of fish move in silver architecture, using columns as landmarks. Crustaceans line service grates. Eels thread conduit shadows. Bigger predators patrol the pressure-gradient corridors near pump arrays, where prey concentrates. Above, near the bright upper layers, birds wheel around vent stacks and skim for baitfish driven upward by intake turbulence. Nothing is fantastical; everything is plausible, vigorous, and opportunistic—the ordinary speed of ecology exploiting new habitat.

Because scale is so vast and density so low, presences are far apart. You can travel for hours through blue volume with only suspended particles, distant column-lines, and the occasional moving shoal. Then suddenly, a mega-installation rises from below like a submerged city district: intake domes the size of stadiums, maintenance gantries, elevator shafts descending through water in lit cages. Between these clusters is emptiness—clean, cold, and immense.

The temperature stays cool almost everywhere except around machinery outflows. Thermal plumes spill from heat exchangers and make wavering mirages underwater: columns of slightly warmer water where fish gather in dense, temporary clouds. In these zones, metabolism accelerates, predators linger, and sound changes pitch as water density shifts. Step out of them and the cold closes in again, steady and practical.

Sound is constant and loud, a layered industrial surf:
- low continental hum from pump farms and circulation turbines,
- rhythmic thump of pressure gates cycling,
- cable-song and structural groan as load shifts,
- chop and roar of weather overhead translating through the surface skin,
- crackle of shrimp and fish movement woven through it all.

Nothing here is still. Currents are managed but never static. Tides are partly astronomical, partly scheduled. Stormwater can be admitted through giant crown inlets when rain belts pass. Autonomous dredgers crawl the floor and vacuum buildup from expansion joints. Cleaning swarms strip invasive growth from critical valves. Feeding frenzies erupt and vanish. Maintenance lights change color district by district as systems rotate through service windows. The place behaves like an ocean taught to keep factory time.

Light penetrates well enough to make a true day-night cycle in upper depths. By day, beams angle through surface chop and strike structural planes, turning them into moving bars of brightness. By night, navigation strobes, warning beacons, and service lamps create an artificial constellation underwater. There are no abyssal blacks unless you descend beneath the main lit and circulated layers into utility chasms where only inspection drones go.

The rules of this world are simple and strict:

1. **Water is habitat and mechanism at once.**  
   Every flow serves life and engineering simultaneously.

2. **Nothing natural is foundational.**  
   If you touch “ground,” you are touching a built thing.

3. **Distance is the primary weather.**  
   Most of the world is separation; events happen in nodes.

4. **Youth means sharpness.**  
   Corners, gradients, and boundaries are clear; succession has begun but not softened them.

5. **Control is never absolute.**  
   The system is designed, but scale grants it emergent behavior—its own storms, migrations, and acoustic moods.

So the landscape resolves into something recognizable: not alien, not magical—just colossal civil engineering flooded to the brim and already becoming an ecosystem. A constructed inland sea, still learning its own rhythms, loud with machinery, alive with ordinary animals, and vast enough that even total design cannot keep it from becoming a kind of nature.
