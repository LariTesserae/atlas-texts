The city is inside a drop of water that never falls.

Not metaphorically—physically. A lens-shaped ocean hangs in the upper air of a mountain basin, pinned in place by fields no one now names. Its lower surface is a perfect curve, dark as smoked glass in the near-absence of light, and its upper surface touches the black teeth of fresh stone ridges that look recently heaved from the planet’s crust. The basin is high enough that clouds move *below* it. Wind strokes the undersides of those clouds, but the suspended sea does not ripple. It has been still for longer than memory.

There are no plants anywhere. No films of algae, no moss in seams, no drifting threads. Rock, water, metal, ceramic, and the occasional pale life-form that is neither fish nor anything with leaves. Every surface that might host a green thing instead hosts mineral bloom: metallic salts feathered like frost, silica whiskers, iron-black nodules. The world feels pre-ecological, or post-ecological—after the garden, before the garden, impossible to tell.

You enter through a pressure gate built into the cliff: an angular mouth of matte alloy, half eroded by chemistry rather than weather. Young land surrounds ancient engineering. The ridges are sharp-edged, unsoftened by long runoff; talus fans are narrow and steep. But inside the gate, the architecture is patient and complete. Corridors angle downward into the suspended water body and then open suddenly into a district of structures rooted to nothing.

Buildings are sparse, each one given distance as if silence were zoning law. They are not skyscrapers; this is smaller than a metropolis—more like a borough, a research quarter, a cathedral complex expanded into neighborhood scale. Towers the size of old bell towers. Bridges short as city blocks. Courtyards enclosed in pressure membranes. Everything floats or anchors by invisible geometries, suspended in the dark cool water with clear lanes between.

Light is minimal and deliberate. No sun reaches here in any useful way; illumination comes from narrow bands along edges, dim bioluminescent-blue strips in handrails, occasional volume-lamps that glow like trapped moons behind opaque shells. Most of the time you navigate by contrast: the suggestion of a wall against deeper black, the glint of a polished seam, the faint electrostatic shimmer of a transit ring warming for use. Shadows don’t move much. Nothing moves much.

The water itself is almost perfectly clear and almost perfectly dead. Not toxic—just complete, balanced, finished. No visible currents. No sediment drift. Dropped particles stop and hang, then very slowly return to predetermined paths as if the fluid remembers exactly where each thing belongs. This is the strangest rule of the place: disturbance is temporary, but not through turbulence—through correction. The medium restores equilibrium.

Sound is sparse and close. Your own movement thuds through suit and bone. Far away, a periodic ping from navigation beacons. The dry click of docking latches. A low, continuous tone from deep infrastructure, felt more than heard, like a sleeping machine dreaming one note. No gulls, no leaves, no insect haze, no traffic roar. Even in the built zones, quiet dominates; emptiness carries farther than activity.

Fauna exists, but barely. Once in a long while you see a pale spindle-creature, meter-long, translucent, with cilia that pulse in mathematically regular waves. It doesn’t hunt. It doesn’t flee. It drifts between thermal-null nodes and feeds, apparently, on charge differentials along old field lines. There are also coin-sized armored discs that cling to hulls and slowly rotate, harvesting trace minerals from the water. Whether these are evolved organisms or abandoned maintenance biotech is unresolved and perhaps irrelevant. They are alive enough to heal when cracked and regular enough to look manufactured.

At the center of the district stands the Anchor Choir: seven vertical rings, each taller than a house, arranged around a dry void. They hum in phase and hold the sea’s impossible shape. Instruments suggest they are not working hard. Whatever equation keeps a mountain lake hanging in midair was solved perfectly here, and solved to last. The rings show no corrosion except superficial staining. Their internals are inaccessible; panels do not open; interfaces do not answer human input. Yet every indicator remains within nominal bounds. A forever-machine in cool dark water.

Around the Choir, civic remnants: a transit spindle with no passengers, a market arcade converted to storage, habitation pods sealed and unlit. Urbanity without crowding. Infrastructure without rush hour. It feels like a town paused between breaths and never exhaled. Dynamic equilibrium taken to cultural scale.

Outside the suspended sea, the high basin is equally still. Thin air, cold stone, occasional needle-rain that falls upward for a few meters before being pulled into the lower skin of the hanging ocean and absorbed without splash. At night—or what passes for night under near-permanent gloom—the underside of the water reflects distant lightning from storms far below, turning the basin ceiling into a dim, shifting map of white veins. That is the one motion the place allows: weather elsewhere, mirrored here.

Rules emerge as you stay:

1. **Nothing grows.** Matter changes phase, crystallizes, precipitates, but does not root or branch.
2. **Nothing drifts for long.** The field seeks baseline and gently enforces it.
3. **Space is intentional.** Structures keep distance, functions keep lanes, silence keeps authority.
4. **Technology outlasts history.** Systems remain legible only in effect, not in mechanism.
5. **Time is present but unfelt.** The place is not frozen; it is simply at equilibrium so deep that change is geological rumor.

If you stand beneath the sea and look up through fifty meters of dark water to the faint ridge-light above, the whole basin resembles an eye: stone lids, liquid globe, engineered iris. It does not watch you. It does not ignore you. It just remains—high, cool, waterbound, and exact—an urban pocket held inside a physics that has decided to be still.
