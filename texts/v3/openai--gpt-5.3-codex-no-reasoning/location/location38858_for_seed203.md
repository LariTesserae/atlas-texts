It begins as cold breath and glare.

You come down through a narrow throat of stone and root, expecting cave-dark, and instead step out into a brightness so complete it erases distance. The roof is there, technically—the world is below surface—but it hangs impossibly high overhead, a pale vaulted skin of mineral and old ice that catches and scatters light like cloud. The light has no obvious source. It pools on every surface, reflected and re-reflected from wet leaves, waxy fronds, white fungal plates, and films of condensed water. Shadows exist only in slits and undersides.

The basin is vast enough to have horizons of its own. Not a single cavern, but an undercontinent: chambers linked by collapsed sink valleys, root canyons, and wind-cut galleries wide as inland seas. Most of it is plant.

Not “covered in plants”—*composed* of them.  
Ground is braided rhizome and peat-rind. Cliffs are buttressed with trunk-columns fused together into walls. Bridges are living, grown from opposite sides until they lace. Plains are mats of low, silver-green leaf-flesh stretched taut over air pockets. Whole districts rise and sink by slow vegetal hydraulics, as if the land breathes.

Yet it is not wet in a swamp sense. Water is episodic, strategic, hidden. Condensation beads along cold ribs at dawn-hours and slides down into cistern-roots. Thin rain sometimes falls from the roof when warm drafts rise and hit upper chill layers, a cave weather cycle that can begin in one basin and never touch the next. Channels run briefly, fiercely, then vanish into sponge-ground. There are black pools, yes, but far apart—cold mirrors among miles of fibrous dry.

Because of that, emptiness dominates experience. So much space between presences. A lone tower-fern half a kilometer away. A single suspended garden dangling from a ceiling crack over a plain of pale lichen felt. One hut on a ridge of roots, then nothing for hours but wind and leaf-rustle.

The turbulence comes in pulses.

Air in these hollows moves unpredictably: downdrafts of freezer-cold falling from ice vents, then sudden lateral gusts through root-arches that set entire canopies clattering. Plant life responds quickly. Fronds furl in minutes, seed-caps burst all at once, vine-tensions release with whip-cracks. Sections of the living floor can slump without warning where fresh growth has overarched empty pockets. New gullies appear in a week. A wall of creeper can cover a cliff overnight if a moisture front passes. The place is geologically young and botanically impatient; nothing has had time to soften. Edges are sharp, tissues fresh, breaks raw and green.

Sound is mostly restraint: distant leaf-hiss, intermittent drip, small clicks of cooling stone. Then, briefly, uproar—wind-roar through hollow stems, rain like thrown beads, cascades of seedpods rattling down chutes—and silence again so complete you hear your own clothing scrape.

Fauna is sparse but precise. White, long-legged grazers that move in twos along high rootways, eating only new tips. Blind glider-lizards that launch from fungus shelves and steer by pressure changes in the air. Needle-beaked birds (if “bird” still fits) that nest in warm steam fractures and emerge only during condensation hours to drink droplets off leaf spines. Most of the movement you catch in the corner of your eye is plant mechanics, not animal intent.

Scattered people-marks appear where the land briefly allows stillness.  
A ring of woven reed shelters on a dry knuckle.  
Ladders carved from giant horsetail stems, pegged with bone.  
Stone blades hafted into pruning poles, left stuck in trunk flesh like ritual offerings.  
Storage gourds lacquered with resin and hung from ceiling roots, safe from floor-shifts.  
No cities, no roads in the usual sense—just route-memory: colored fibers tied in branching code, cut notches in bark, cairns of pale root-wood where traversable ground currently exists.

Their technology is hand-close and seasonal. Rope, wicker, fired clay where vent heat is available, chipped stone, occasional hammered native copper worn thin through generations. They farm by coaxing architecture: redirecting growth with tension frames, shading cloth, and careful wounds. A “house” is often a negotiated truce with a living buttress that might keep growing through your sleeping wall. Nothing is permanent, so skill is less building than timing.

There are rules here, known by practice:

- Never camp beneath taut vine fans: they dump icewater when night drafts reverse.  
- Walk the matte green, not the glossy black; gloss means thin skin over hollow.  
- Harvest after wind, never before; turbulence redistributes toxins through some species.  
- If the ceiling begins to glitter, rain is forming—secure fire, seal packs, wait.  
- Follow the cold to find water, follow the warm to find people.

The unusualness is not monstrous; it is systemic. Light underground. Forest as terrain rather than cover. Weather with no sky. A cold world that is brilliantly alive but never lush in a comfortable way. You are always in a place mid-formation, where whole ecologies are still deciding what shape “stable” might mean.

From a high ledge you can look across fifty kilometers of this underworld and see bands of color like climate zones: white moss barrens, dark fern swells, copper reed flats, luminous mold deltas around dripfields. Mist plumes rise from thermal cuts and drift until they strike the vault and flatten into moving ceilings. Far off, a root-spire collapses in silence, then a delayed shudder runs through the mat beneath your feet.

It is empty, bright, cold, and overwhelmingly vegetal.  
A continent under stone, always rearranging itself, mostly quiet until suddenly not.
