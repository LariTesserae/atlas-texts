The plain is low and old enough to have forgotten cliffs.

Everything here has been worn down to intentionless shapes: long-backed swells of black stone, shallow basins glazed with thin ice, gravel fans spread like muted metal dust. Nothing rises high except weather. You can walk for hours and never climb more than the height of a house, yet the place feels enormous because it has no edges—only transitions: frozen seep into mirror-pool, mirror-pool into slush-rind, slush-rind into fields of polished cobbles that click and settle under their own weight.

Water is everywhere but rarely theatrical. It doesn’t waterfall; it persists. It beads from rock faces in strings, gathers in skin-thin sheets over stone, vanishes beneath crust, exhales again as ground-fog. After each brief sleet, the whole region seems to sweat. The water table sits almost at the surface, so every step finds saturation somewhere below. Pools lie under translucent ice membranes that flex when heavy animals pass, making low drum-notes you feel through your ankles.

There are no plants—no moss, no lichen, no root, no green interruption. The mineral palette does all the work: iron-black, lead-gray, salt-white, bruised violet where the wet stone catches dim sky. In the cold, salts feather across mud like frost-ferns, the only branching forms in sight. Life has taken different routes.

Fauna dominates the visual field. Not in herds on distant ridges, but everywhere, packed into every available niche of this flattened world. Broad-backed crawlers lie half-submerged in thermal seeps, their hides ridged like conveyor belts and ticking with internal motion. Glass-jointed stalkers pick their way across the stone pans in synchronized lines, each footfall precise, machine-like, though no machine made them. Rafts of membrane-winged skimmers flap low over open water, beating the surface into constant stipple. Burrowing things push up the sediment from below, so entire mudflats rise and subside as if breathing.

And the noise—this place never really goes quiet. Wind alone would be enough, dragging across open wet stone with a long cable-hum, but the animals add layers: piston coughs, rotary chattering, percussive clacks, steam-whistle cries, grinding throat-tones. Many species evolved resonant cavities and ribbed plates that amplify sound; calls carry for kilometers in the dim air. Territorial boundaries are acoustic, not visual. Valleys of fog belong to whichever chorus can hold them.

The industrial quality is undeniable, but it is not built. Nothing has been assembled; everything has accreted or evolved. Biological processes here converged on the logic of factories: throughput, extraction, heat exchange, pressure cycles. Whole feeding grounds operate like refineries. One species stirs anoxic mud, another filters released gases through latticed mouths, another harvests the precipitated mineral slurries. Waste heat from dense congregations keeps channels open in the ice. At night (if you can call the thicker dimness night), the plain is fretted with dull orange glows from metabolic vents and chemiluminescent exhaust plumes.

Reality is least reliable around water interfaces. Reflections in the pools are delayed by a few seconds, sometimes showing animals that have already moved on—or not yet arrived. Sounds detach from sources and migrate with the fog. Footprints fill with black water that briefly holds tiny, rotating star-fields before turning opaque. In certain basins, distance folds oddly: a creature half a kilometer away can sound like it is inside your chest, while nearby movement appears miniature, toy-like, then snaps back to scale when you blink. Local fauna seem adapted; they navigate by pressure gradients and infrasonic maps rather than line-of-sight.

The sky contributes to the breakdown. It is low, layered, and full of condensing planes, as if cloud decks were stacked sheets of smoked glass. Light leaks through in weak bands, never fully day, never truly dark. Sometimes a bright seam opens near the horizon and travels sideways for hours, painting every wet surface with a moving bar of silver. Predators time migrations to these seams because prey silhouettes sharpen for a brief window before dissolving again.

Despite the cold, the landscape is active in continuous small ways. Ice forms and fails. Mud domes inflate and collapse. Channels reroute overnight. Animal concentrations shift like weather fronts. If you marked a map in the morning, by evening the same ground would still exist but not in the same arrangement. Ancient erosion flattened the bones of the land; living density keeps rewriting the skin.

There are rules, if you stay long enough to notice:

- Never trust still water; it may be membrane, mirror, or opening.
- Follow sound harmonics, not echoes—echoes lie here.
- Warm fog means congregations; cold fog means exposed flats and sudden sink.
- Where stone is brightest, footing is worst: polished surfaces are fed by constant micro-seep.
- If all noise drops at once, something large is surfacing nearby.

What finally defines the place is this paradox: it feels like the end-state of geology and the overclocked middle of biology at the same time. The ground has aged into near-final form, but life—strange, loud, process-heavy life—has packed every hollow, channel, and vapor layer with motion. A wet, dim, industrial wilderness with no builders, only operators.
