By noon, the plateau looks almost empty.

From far away it reads as a pale, high country—sun-struck shelves of stone and baked ochre soils, broad basins, long wind-carved backs of ridge. Heat wavers above it in silver sheets. The air seems too thin for abundance. You can stand on a rise and see fifty kilometers, maybe more, and for whole minutes nothing obvious moves except a few dark threads turning in the sky.

Then your eyes adjust, and the place overturns itself.

The ground is not bare ground at all. Nearly every surface that isn’t sheer rock is skinned in plant life: low mats with waxy leaves packed like scales, thorny stem-forests no taller than your waist, rope-thick creepers threading cracks in cliff faces, bulb fields swollen with stored water, and huge raft-like root platforms spread over dry gullies waiting for runoff. Vegetation does not always rise into trees here—it lays, clings, braids, and armors. In many valleys, the plants are the soil: centuries of compacted roots, shredded stems, fungal glue, and wind dust, all alive, all slowly shifting.

Water is rare in the open and everywhere in secret. Rain comes in short, violent spells, drumming hard enough to strip dust from stone, then vanishing for long hot stretches. Moisture lingers as trapped breath: condensation beading under leaf canopies at dawn, thin films inside shade-tubes formed by curled fronds, cool seep lines under root masses, pockets of black mud in basalt hollows. Whole ecologies orbit these hidden damp places. Animals know every one.

And animals are the dominant force—visually, acoustically, physically. Not in one dramatic herd, but in layers so numerous the landscape seems furred with life. Insect clouds boil out of the plant mats at sunrise, rise in columns, and drift like smoke. Armored grazers with shovel-snouts comb the root crust in processions that stretch for kilometers, opening temporary lanes that close again in days as stems rebound. Leapers with translucent throat-sails move from shadow to shadow, snapping seed-pods and the creatures feeding there. Burrowers vent warm breath from thousands of holes. Above, broad-winged scavenger-kites wheel on thermal rivers, while needle-fast hunters stitch between them.

The sound is constant and punishing. Clicking choruses, wing-hum, bark-strikes, stridulation in metallic waves, low herd thunder, warning rattles, mating booms amplified by hollow trunks. In some basins the acoustics trap and stack calls until the air physically vibrates against your chest. People who travel here wrap braided fiber around ears and jawbones; not to block the sound, but to keep it from exhausting them.

Despite the noise and abundance, the spacing of large things remains sparse. Forests are not continuous walls. Settlements are rare. A traveler might cross two days of living plain before finding a single built structure: a stone-and-reed watch mound, a wind-cured hide shelter under an overhang, a ladder of pegged bones climbing to a honey cache, a ring of standing poles marking an old nesting truce site. Most human craft is portable, temporary, and respectful of routes—animal routes, flood routes, spore routes. Permanent construction tends to happen where rock already offers shape: cave mouths, erosion-cut alcoves, the lee of giant root buttresses.

Technology is handwork and memory. Fiber cordage, carved hardwood, fired clay, chipped volcanic glass, hammered native copper where it can be found, hide bellows, resin glues, thorn combs, water skins lined in wax. People here are less “builders” than “binders”: binding leaf to frame, frame to stone, knowledge to song. They map by behavior rather than geometry—where the dusk swarm bends, where the grazer lines split, where condensation collects longest. Their calendars are acoustic: the Week of Triple Drumming, the Quiet Moth Gap, the Long Roar before rain.

The weirdness of the plateau is subtle until it isn’t. Certain plants react to specific calls, opening vents or sealing them when particular frequencies pass. Some valley floors “breathe” as root layers lift and settle with daily humidity, enough to crack pottery left overnight. There are groves where all stems spiral in the same direction regardless of slope, and canyons where migrating flyers vanish into glare and emerge dusted in golden spores that glow briefly at dusk. Predators sometimes mimic not just calls but the rustle-pattern of familiar species moving through dry leaves; experienced hunters listen for syntax, not just tone.

Erosion has had ages to compose this place. Cliffs are scalloped, not jagged. Drainage lines are old and understood. Even destruction—flash floods, stampede shears, fire runs in dead season—fits into cycles that regenerate the plant crust and redistribute nutrients. Nothing feels young, but nothing feels static. It is a mature violence, rehearsed and integrated.

At night the heat loosens slowly. The plateau exhales plant-sour sweetness, mineral dust, musk. Dew appears in threads and beads. Sound changes register: fewer aerial screams, more ground percussion and subterranean knocks. Under ordinary moonlight, the root fields look like calm seas, but they are full of traffic—feeding, mating, hunting, pollinating, stealing. A person lying awake in a hide lean-to may feel tiny footfalls crossing the roof all night, as if rain has learned to walk.

By dawn, brief fogs gather in hollows and evaporate before the sun clears the eastern rim. The world returns to bright normal light, hard-edged and honest-looking, and once again pretends to be empty—until it moves.
