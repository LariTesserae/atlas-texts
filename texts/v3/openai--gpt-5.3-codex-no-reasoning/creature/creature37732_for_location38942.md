What lives there is not a *who* in the usual sense.

It is a **civic biome**—a single, distributed, self-editing organism pretending to be a city because “forest” is too small a word and “infrastructure” is too dead a one.

At first you might think many species live there in uneasy cooperation. But watch longer and that impression blurs. The graft-lines between “individuals” are too frequent, the hydraulic responses too coordinated, the boundary between wall and root too negotiable. What lives there is a **hydraulic intelligence made of plant tissue**: federated, modular, and endlessly revising itself through water.

If you insist on a resident, then the resident is:

- **A plateau-scale growth collective**
- with no face, no central organ, no permanent plan,
- whose thoughts are pressure gradients,
- whose memory is stored in reinforced channels and recurring forms,
- and whose behavior looks like architecture because it has learned that shape is survival.

It “wakes” when humidity jumps.  
It “argues” through redirection of flow.  
It “builds” by thickening what worked last storm and sacrificing what didn’t.  
It “reproduces” by exporting pattern—spore, raft, graft, and runoff-script—into newly broken ground.

No animals means no witnesses inside it, so it has become both builder and weather-client, both engineer and ecosystem. Its towers are not monuments; they are temporary negotiations with wind. Its plazas are not social spaces; they are calibrated basins in a continent-wide pump. Its open voids are not empty; they are deliberate lungs.

So the thing that lives there is best described as a **rain-forged metropolis-organism**:
a warm, loud, amphibious civilization of vegetation,
without citizens,
without stillness,
without completion.
