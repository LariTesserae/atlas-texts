What lives there is a **keeper-species**: people who are part mason, part gardener, part acoustic physicist, part herder—though they would likely have no separate words for those roles. To them it is all one task: **maintaining agreement** between living things and a world that keeps trying to misremember itself.

They are probably slight and compact, built for enclosed movement—ducking under trellises, climbing ladders, slipping through bead-hung thresholds without disturbing calibration strings. Their clothing would be layered wraps and tied panels, matte and linted to catch luminous pollen; no loose metal that might ring. Soft-soled feet. Wrist and neck cords loaded with tiny tools: bone gauge, chalk-salt capsule, folded mirror sliver, wax marker, tendon thread.

Their bodies would show adaptation to dry existence: narrow nostrils with internal baffles, habitual closed-mouth breathing, skin oiled with resin films to reduce loss, and a ritualized sharing of heat and breath in families the way other cultures share drink. They may smell faintly of camphor, pepper resin, kiln ash.

Socially, they are listeners first. Children are taught stillness before speed: how to hear a true echo, how to notice a shadow that lags, how to test if a corridor is “continuous” today. Games are probably based on pattern memory and gentle touch navigation, not shouting competition. Status belongs not to warriors or traders, but to those with the best **drift-sense**—the elders who can hear a wall go wrong half a day before it slips.

They do keep animals, but not as simple livestock. Every species is a partner in stability:

- six-legged browsers keep lichen crusts trimmed to correct thickness;
- beetles maintain fungal lanes that indicate micro-shifts in grade;
- birds map vapor pockets with their dawn song routes;
- gliders clear excess pollen that might otherwise seed unwanted growth in fragile seams.

So “who lives there” is really a **woven community of custodians**, human and nonhuman, bred and trained to survive where water fails and geometry frays.

If you met one person alone, they might seem quiet, even austere. But in aggregate they are deeply communal, because no single pair of senses can keep a place like this real. Their culture would revolve around recurring acts:

- touch both sides of the arch;
- check the hanging slats;
- burn the echo copies;
- reset salt-char lines;
- retie the vine braces;
- listen at night.

Their spirituality—if present—would likely not worship distant gods but honor **continuity** itself: repetition as devotion, maintenance as prayer, accurate measurement as mercy. They do not seek to conquer instability. They court it, soothe it, redirect it. They have accepted that permanence is impossible; only renewal is possible.

So the being that lives there, ultimately, is not just “a people.”  
It is a **practice** that has become a civilization:  
a dry, fragrant, patient intelligence whose true habitat is the narrow margin between collapse and coherence.
