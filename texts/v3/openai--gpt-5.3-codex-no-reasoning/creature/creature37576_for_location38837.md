What lives there is not one thing but a **civic body**—an entire resident intelligence distributed across teeth, shell, current, and rust.

At first you’d think the ruler is some large predator, a scarred barracuda analogue or a blind conger thick as a fire hose, because those are what you notice crossing the lit lanes. But those are only the visible enforcers. The true “who” of this place is a layered population that behaves like an old working city:

- **Filter colonies** netting the pump-thick water at intakes and grilles, turning machine breath into food.
- **Rust grazers**—small crustaceans and snail-like scrapers—harvesting bacterial films from every softened edge.
- **Conduit eels** occupying cable trunks and valve throats, opportunists and cleaners both.
- **Midwater schools** that use catwalk grids as roads, timing movement to pressure pulses and light cycles.
- **Patrol hunters** that do not own territory so much as patrol schedules, synchronized to vent bursts and migration funnels.

Individually, none of them is sovereign. Together, they are.

If you insist on a single inhabitant, then it is this: a **shoal-mind ecosystem** that has learned the machinery’s remaining heartbeat and wrapped itself around it so completely that biology and infrastructure are now one metabolism. The pumps are lungs, ducts are weather, shafts are tides, red beacons are warning coloration. Predators read valve chatter the way birds read wind. Spawners choose chambers by oxygen tone and vibration, not shelter shape. Juveniles learn flow lanes before they learn fear.

Over centuries, selection here would favor creatures with traits like:

- lateral-line sensitivity tuned to low-frequency industrial vibration,
- vision adapted to sodium amber and algae-muted green-blue,
- pressure tolerance for rapid micro-shifts around shafts and ducts,
- behaviors that treat artificial geometry as inherited terrain.

So the “resident” is ancient and ongoing: a community that doesn’t merely survive the drowned quarter—it **operates it ecologically**. It lives in the old machine the way reef life lives in coral skeleton, except the skeleton still hums, still pulses, still occasionally opens and closes a steel throat on schedule no human remembers.

In that sense, the place is inhabited by a new kind of citizenry:  
**current-riders, rust-eaters, lane-keepers, silence-avoiders**—an industrial estuary people made of many species, one pattern, and endless motion.
