What lives there is a people of **maintenance**.

Not conquerors of the marsh, not mystics in awe of it—practitioners. Attentive, weather-minded, hand-skilled, and patient in the way only communities become when every day includes small acts that prevent tomorrow’s collapse.

At first glance you might call them cautious. But stay longer and they feel more like **responsive engineers with village habits**: they read damp gradients with bare soles, hear subsurface hollows in the pitch of a mallet strike, smell when a channel has gone sour before it clouds. Their intelligence is distributed—across elders, builders, tanners, children, cat-breeders, rope-makers—rather than concentrated in a priesthood or ruling scholar class. Knowledge here is practical and mnemonic: *this post in this pit skins with mineral by late thaw; that alley breathes cold before ground-slip; never seal that wall face entirely or it will sweat itself apart*.

If you’re looking for a single “type” of inhabitant, it’s this:

### The Marsh-Settled Human
- **Compact, layered clothing**, never fully dry, always repaired.
- Hands marked by wet work: clay under nails, tannin stains, rope burns, lime dust.
- Quiet voices and close speech—part courtesy, part acoustics in mist and timber corridors.
- A gait adapted to slickness: short steps, low center of gravity, one hand often free for balance.
- Habits of constant checking: pressing a beam with the heel of the palm, peering at water speed in a gutter, lifting goods a finger-width higher before sleep.

They are not dramatic people because their landscape punishes drama. Heroics lose to routine here.

---

But more than humans live there. The town is full of **co-inhabitants** shaped by seep and edge:

- **Lean marsh-cats** with tufted ears and splayed, almost webbed paws, moving beam to beam like minor spirits of balance. They keep vermin down not with frenzy but with long, motionless watching.
- **Eels**, half-livestock, half-wild harvest, threading the channels and funnel traps—important enough to influence where families clear flow.
- **Pale cistern fish** in deeper, colder pockets, flickering like loose metal.
- **Seasonal waders** at the perimeter, stepping the mudflats as if testing a thought, never fully trusting human density.
- **Frogs** outside the settlement ring, muted choristers that mark the wet hours.

And then there are the near-living presences the people treat as ordinary:

- calcifying posts,
- chill-breath alleys,
- skin-still basins that hold ash diagrams,
- ropes that wake dripping.

Not gods, not omens—just local behaviors with personalities. In another culture these would become spirits. Here they become **craft constraints**.

---

So who lives there?

A small, interdependent marsh town of people who survive by treating impermanence as a building material; animals selected for footing and patience; and water itself, everywhere, as the most constant resident of all.

If the settlement has a character, it is this:  
**a quiet collective mind, always listening to the ground while it keeps talking back.**
