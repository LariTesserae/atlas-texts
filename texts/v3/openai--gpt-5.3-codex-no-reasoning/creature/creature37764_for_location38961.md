What lives there is **a caretaker species of engineers-gardeners**, people who have become as amphibious in habit as the district itself—less “citizens of a city” than **organs of a wet machine**.

At first you might think they are absent, because you don’t hear voices and you don’t see crowds. But then you notice signs of them everywhere, in the same way you notice current in water: not as spectacle, but as direction. Freshly rewoven reed baffles along an outflow. A new ceramic patch over a sweating seam. Handrails wrapped in absorbent fiber where condensation always forms. Vines braided to relieve strain from a sagging catwalk. Every intervention is modest, local, and exact. They do not impose order; they **tune continuities**.

They likely live in stacked, narrow dwellings tucked between pump houses and retaining walls—rooms with raised floors, suspended walkways, drying lofts for tools (not for fabric, because nothing ever truly dries), and listening shafts that carry the neighborhood’s mechanical pulse indoors. Their architecture is less about privacy than about gradients: warm room to cool room, dry-ish shelf to wet platform, intake side to outflow side. Home and utility are interleaved. A kitchen may share a wall with a pressure manifold; a bedroom may sit above a biofilter trench glowing faintly blue-green at night.

Their bodies and clothing would reflect the basin’s demands: layered waterproof textiles, rubberized aprons, soft-soled boots for slick grates, copper or ceramic toolkits that resist corrosion. Skin always damp, hands callused from valves and pruning knives. They move carefully but never hesitantly across catwalks and ladder runs, fluent in vertical shortcuts and under-street passages. Children—if there are children—would learn sound before signage: the normal octave of turbines, the healthy interval of drips in a cistern throat, the dangerous quiet when a siphon stalls.

Culturally, they would be **acoustic diagnosticians**. In a fauna-less world, machines and water are the loudest lives, so listening becomes literacy, maybe even spirituality. They might mark time by pressure cycles instead of bells, by fog density instead of sun. Their communal rituals could be maintenance rites: seasonal desilting, vine-lashing days, mineral scraping, canal flushing after heavy rain. Celebration might look like synchronized reopening of floodgates at dawn, lamps reflected in black water, steam rising like incense.

Food likely comes from what this place can support without animals: fungi, watercress-like greens, rooftop tubers, trellised fruit, fermentation vats, algal cultures, traded grains brought in along causeways. Composting is fungal and bacterial, meticulously managed. Nothing wasted, because every discarded thing either clogs flow or feeds growth. Even memory may be archived materially—in sediment cores, patched wall strata, dated valve wheels—history as layers of repair rather than written monument.

Psychologically, they would be patient people. Not static, but **slow-responsive**: alert to tiny shifts, uninterested in dramatic change. They don’t dream of conquering nature; they don’t fear it either. Their identity is reciprocal with seepage, rust, and regrowth. They understand that stability here is dynamic, achieved hour by hour. A “good day” is one where all channels run, all hums are in key, and nothing has had to be forced.

So whoever lives there is neither purely human city-dweller nor rustic marshlander, but a third thing:  
**keepers of circulation**, residents of a long-settled hydro-industrial biome, whose true craft is maintaining the conversation between water, stone, metal, and green life—so continuously that the whole basin can go on breathing.
