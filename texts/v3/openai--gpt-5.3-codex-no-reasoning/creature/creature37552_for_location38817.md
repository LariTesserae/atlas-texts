What lives there is not a creature in the usual sense.

It is a **metropolis-organism**—a slow, collective life made of trunks, roots, peat, moss, fungus, water, and cold. Not one tree, not one species, but a fused civic body: a colony that learned architecture because weather punished anything less.

At first you might call its inhabitants shrubs, conifers, liverworts, amphibians, moths. But those are more like cells, errands, and weather-sensors in something larger. The true resident is the **woven forest itself**, a patient intelligence without a brain—an organism that thinks in load paths, moisture gradients, and generational pressure.

It lives like this:

- It **breathes fog** through bark and moss.
- It **drinks from surfaces**, not streams.
- It **builds by leaning**, by touching, by grafting after storm-stress.
- It **remembers** in structure: old collapse lines become lanes, old windbreaks become outer walls.
- It **communicates** in chemistry and color pulses—those district-wide leaf-turns like weather semaphore.
- It **reproduces space**, not just seeds: corridors, courts, root-bridges, terraces.

Its smaller animals are tenants and couriers:
- dark amphibians patrolling wet margins,
- brief white moths rising in thaw windows,
- tiny root-colored mammals threading insulated voids,
- sparse high birds using dry canopy pockets as lofts.

But none of them rule it. Even decomposition serves the city-body: the dead are not discarded, they are reincorporated as beams, insulation, retaining edges.

If you stood still long enough, you’d realize this place does have a kind of citizenry—just not individuals. Its “people” are **processes**:
freeze and thaw,
bend and graft,
rot and bind,
drip and wick,
crack and root.

So whoever lives there is a **cold cloud-forest superorganism**—urban in pattern, vegetal in substance, ancient in tempo, and perpetually under construction.
