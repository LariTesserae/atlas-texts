What lives there is a people who learned, slowly and without mythic drama, to become *anchored intelligence*.

Not merfolk, not fish-riders, not ghosts. Mountain folk who outlasted the sky.

At first they were probably ordinary highland builders—terrace makers, cistern keepers, listeners of weather in stone. Then water rose by increments and surges. They moved upward, rebuilt, moved again, rebuilt again. You can feel this in the angled entry tunnels, in the ladders cut into cliff, in the jars tied to posts: adaptation in generations, not inventions in a single lifetime. By the end, “up” no longer meant air; it meant thinner pressure, clearer water, a few degrees less crushing dark.

Over a very long time, whoever they were changed into something that can survive only by obeying the first law of the place: **Root, or be taken.**

So the beings that live there now are mostly still. They are not plants, but they imitate plants because plants solved the physics first.

They dwell attached to stone ledges, old walls, standing rings, and the carved posts near the trench. A single individual might look, from a distance, like a bundled thicket of dark straps and pale membranes draped over basalt. Up close, those straps are muscular anchoring cords threaded through drilled holes in rock and old architecture. Their bodies are compact and heavy at the center—dense with mineralized tissue—while sensory structures spread outward in fans, combs, and taut lines that read current the way skin reads wind.

They are blind in the way caves are blind: not deprived, just specialized past the need for images.  
They “see” by:

- pressure gradients along suspended filaments,
- returning echoes hummed into stone,
- harmonic shifts in jar-chimes and pillar-whistles,
- chemical traces carried through east-drifting seed lanes.

Their speech is not words as we’d hear them. It is arranged vibration: knocks, drawn tones through hollow bone flutes, plucked fiber lines, and synchronized pulses sent into architecture. A village is an instrument. A ridge can be a sentence. A warning may begin as a tremor in one terrace and arrive minutes later at another as a changed chord in hanging jars.

They do move, but reluctantly and at cost. Locomotion is done by leap-anchoring: release two cords, cast three, reel the body across a current seam, lock down again. Juveniles are the most mobile, slender and risk-tolerant, sent to map safe-hour routes marked in notches and spirals. Elders become almost completely sessile, fused by choice and calcification into “listening seats” in stone circles or at channel throats, where they spend decades monitoring deep pressure songs. In old age, an individual may be impossible to distinguish from a shrine except when it answers.

Reproduction is part biology, part hydrology. They release weighted spore-like larvae—not free swimmers, but adhesive drifters—that move sideways, always east, along the same lawful bias as frond seed. Most fail. The survivors catch in carved seams prepared generations ahead: nursery cuts in sheltered rock, lined with velvet algae and mineral wool. Children begin life as attached colonies of tiny bodies sharing one anchor mass, then separate over years as they develop their own cords and resonance organs.

They herd no animals because there are none to herd. Their agriculture is current farming:

- pruning kelp towers to tune flow speed through channels,
- seeding red-green algae on warm stone for food,
- cultivating glass reeds for fiber and acoustic tooling,
- harvesting translucent frost-films for salts and trace metals.

They eat by filtration and paste. They grind algae, lichenous growth, and mineral-rich biofilm into dense cakes that cure in perforated stone racks where water continues to pass through. Even their food obeys the same principle as their architecture: **let water through, keep structure.**

Culturally, they are patient to an extent surface minds would call eerie. They plan in pressure-cycles, not days. “Soon” may mean next cold descent. “Long ago” may mean before a certain ridge lost its sharp edge. Memory is externalized: carvings, notch-columns, tuned holes, and placement of resonant objects. History is stored in how a place sounds when struck.

Religion—if it exists—is probably indistinguishable from engineering. They revere not gods but stable relations: weight, flow, porosity, interval. Their taboo is buoyancy without tether. Their moral failure is to create something that catches force instead of passing it on.

And at the trench lip, those final inward-leaning posts? That is where their guardians live.

Not warriors in any familiar sense. More like **counterweight choirs**: immense, ancient individuals or intergrown lineages permanently anchored to the carved line, wrapped in petrified braid and living tissue. They are there to read and resist the “tide from below”—periodic upsurges from the abyss that can strip whole slopes clean of anything insufficiently rooted. When deep surge begins, the trench guardians start a subsonic call that propagates inland through post, bridge, terrace, jar, bone peg, reed bed, and body. Every settlement tightens, seals, lowers profile, and waits out the passing force.

So whoever lives there is this:  
a civilization that traded speed for duration, sight for resonance, wandering for emplacement—  
people who became part forest, part masonry, part instrument,  
until the difference between inhabitant and habitat thinned to almost nothing.

In another world they might look tragic, the last descendants of the drowned.  
Here they are simply appropriate: the only kind of mind this water allows to remain.
