What lives there is a **people of calibration**—and around them, species that have learned the same lesson: nothing is fixed, so everything must listen.

At first glance you might think the inhabitants are simply industrial highlanders: rail crews, riggers, hydrologists, quarry teams, signal operators, scaffold botanists, animal wardens. Heavy coats, fog lamps, grease under nails, moss on boots. And yes—those people are everywhere, moving along catwalks slick with mist, checking tension gauges, swapping stories at steam vents, timing their day to sirens and migration boards.

But if you stay longer, the city’s true resident emerges: not a class or profession, but a temperament.

They are people who do not worship control. They practice **continuous adjustment**.

They build with slotted joints instead of rigid seams. They leave room in every schedule for delays that aren’t delays so much as negotiations with weather, with stone, with animals, with the occasional extra stair that appears in fog. They don’t ask, “Why is reality inconsistent?” so much as “Which layer is stable today?” Their intelligence is procedural, patient, communal. They are less inventors of final forms than custodians of provisional ones.

You can hear it in their speech. Directions are conditional:
- “Take the west span unless the lights hum flat.”
- “Use the lower ramp if the cranes are nesting.”
- “That district is twenty minutes in clear air, an hour in wet echo.”
Time is spoken in **durations of behavior**, not minutes: “before moth rise,” “after second drip,” “until the herd siren.”

They are deeply literate in signs that outsiders would miss:
the angle of condensation beads on a handrail,
how turbine birds wheel when pressure’s about to shift,
the difference between rock-settling groan and false-near machinery,
whether fog is merely thick or structure-bending.

Children grow up bilingual in infrastructure and ecology. They learn color codes of beacons and calls of scavenger packs in the same season. They carry net gloves not as toys but as civic tools during insect blooms. They know which overpasses close for nesting and which ravines are “green-fast” (reclaimed by vegetation too quickly to trust old maps). Their games include route-memory drills and “anchor-light” races in training fog.

Socially, the city favors roles we might call:
- **Aligners** (surveyors, shim crews, pin-resetters)
- **Flowkeepers** (water trap/filter/release technicians)
- **Phase wardens** (siren protocol operators)
- **Edge-rangers** (animal corridor stewards)
- **Layer clerks** (map-stack editors, reality reconciliers)

And perhaps most characteristic: **witnesses**—people whose job is simply to verify what happened when systems disagree. In a place where two clocks can both be right in different weather, testimony is infrastructure.

As for nonhuman life, it’s not background wildlife; it is co-resident, almost co-governor. Herd routes shape bridge policy. Bird nesting dictates crane downtime. Moth blooms alter power protocol. Scavenger packs define sanitation timing. Amphibians are unofficial hydrology sensors. The city survives because it grants other species right-of-way often enough to avoid open conflict—and because those species, in turn, have adapted to machinery rhythms. A turbine arm is a cliff. A warm runoff ditch is a seasonal wetland. A freight lull is crossing time.

So: who lives there?

- **Humans who are engineers of the temporary and caretakers of the long-term.**
- **Animals that treat steel as terrain and sirens as weather.**
- **Plants that root wherever moisture hesitates.**
- **And a civic culture built around humble certainty: stability is not a state, but a practice.**

If the mountain is still deciding to become mountain, then its inhabitants are those who can live inside a decision in progress. They do not need the world to finish before they belong to it.
