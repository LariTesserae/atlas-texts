What lives there is a **guild**, not a single species.

At first you might think: workers in rain capes, gardeners with wrench belts, maintenance crews with machetes. And yes—those are there. But the true inhabitant is broader: a people and their systems who have stopped separating *facility* from *ecosystem*. They live as custodians of flow.

They are the kind of residents who can tell, by sound alone, which pump has started cavitating. Who touch a handrail and know whether the anti-corrosion wrap is three weeks from failure. Who can look at a sheet of vine and tell you where a hidden catwalk door is, because the leaf texture changes where warm air leaks from hinges.

They are:
- **hydraulic listeners** (sluices, drains, settling ponds, tide timing),
- **root engineers** (training, grafting, selective cutting, load-sharing with old masonry),
- **rot managers** (what should decay, what must not),
- **heat harvesters** (routing waste warmth into greenhouse corridors),
- **surface keepers** (deciding what gets brushed bare and what gets allowed to fur over green).

They likely move in narrow electric carts and on foot, carrying pruning hooks beside multimeters. Their clothing is breathable, quick-dry, patched often; their boots are soft-soled for wet steel grating. They do not wage war against moisture; they read it, divert it, bargain with it.

Socially, they probably organize by **cycles** rather than days: corrosion cycle, pruning cycle, dredging cycle, repaint cycle, compost turn cycle, floodgate cycle. Their calendar is maintenance plus monsoon logic. Their memory lives in marked beams, valve paint codes, lineage of cuttings, and stories about “the year the east retaining roots took the load when Pier 6 shifted.”

And beyond the human residents, what lives there is a **cooperative metabolism**:

- vines that are roof and insulation,
- reeds that are filters,
- roots that are rebar,
- snails that are relentless decomposers,
- geckos that keep microfauna in check,
- microbes everywhere, turning litter to substrate,
- pumps, fans, belts, and ducts acting like mechanical organs.

So the best answer is:  
**A wet-industrial commons lives there**—part neighborhood, part utility body, part cultivated thicket—inhabited by people who practice succession as infrastructure. They are less “residents of a reclaimed district” than **tenders of an organism that includes them**.
