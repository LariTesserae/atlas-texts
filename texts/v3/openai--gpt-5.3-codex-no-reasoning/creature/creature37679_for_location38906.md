What lives there is a **city-herd civilization**: not one dominant species in isolation, but a people who survived by becoming conductors of movement—of animals, wind, dust, seed, sound, and memory.

If you meet them, they are likely **compact, layered, and deliberate** in everything they do. Not physically rushed. Not wasteful in gesture. Their clothing is probably fiber-heavy and matte, wrapped for dust and chill, with hard-wearing leather or woven bark panels polished by use. Nothing jingles without purpose; loose ornament would snag, dry, crack, or call predators at the wrong time. They carry cords, hooks, folded nets, whistles, resin-sealed flasks—small tools for capturing passing opportunity.

They are loud socially but economical materially. In a place where quiet is rare, they’ve evolved communication that cuts through chaos:  
- piercing whistles for herd steering,  
- drum codes for district-to-district signaling,  
- hand-sign dialects for markets and hunting lanes,  
- architectural marks for ownership, shade rights, and water claims.

Their children would grow up learning routes before words for abstract directions: *this wind-lane at dusk*, *that predator corridor before moonrise*, *the third cistern wall when salt blooms*. Knowledge is topographic, seasonal, embodied.

---

Who “lives there,” though, is bigger than the human population.

### 1) The Managed Multitudes
The most visible residents are **domesticated grazers and work-beasts**, probably many species rather than one:
- narrow-hoofed or padded-foot herd animals for dry channels,
- chitin-plated draft analogues for hauling in heat and dust,
- burrowing grazers deliberately tolerated to till soil from below,
- fiber-yielding animals integrated into household economies.

These animals are not peripheral livestock—they are traffic, labor, fertilizer, status, inheritance, and rhythm. Dusk is likely defined by their return, dawn by their sorting.

### 2) The Semi-Wild Co-Citizens
Predators and scavengers are **zoned participants** in urban function:
- ratters in granary belts,
- cliff hunters on escarpment ledges,
- carrion-workers in designated refuse courts.

They are feared, watched, named, and legally accommodated. “Pest control” is ecological diplomacy.

### 3) The Insect Nations
Insects (or analogous arthropods) are everywhere, likely with caste behavior exploited but not industrialized:
- dung reducers,
- husk borers,
- night pollinators,
- salt-lick swarms,
- shell-bearing detritivores in compost vaults.

No scrap persists long enough to rot conventionally; life is processed almost immediately.

### 4) The Rooted Architecture
Plants are residents with tenure. Some groves may be older than dynasties. Families probably inherit not just rooms but **root rights**:
- ownership of shade cast at specific hours,
- duty to prune for corridor clearance,
- rights to fallen pads, fruit bodies, fibers, and waxes,
- obligations to feed cistern-adjacent plant guilds that stabilize humidity.

A building without plants is unfinished; a plant without built support is unprotected.

---

Culturally, the people are likely **conservative without being stagnant**. They distrust novelty that threatens retention systems. Their virtues are:
- patience,
- maintenance,
- observational precision,
- cooperative timing,
- skepticism toward apparent plenty.

Their taboos are predictable:
- spilling water publicly,
- blocking migration lanes,
- cutting old root columns without council permission,
- loud false signals (drum/whistle misuse),
- demolition without salvage sequencing.

Their religion—formal or not—probably centers on **continuity of cycles** rather than creation myths: return of herds, night-waking moss, coldfall winds, salt rise on old stone, the reopening of ancient channels as roads. Ancestors are remembered through infrastructures maintained, not monuments erected.

---

Physically, if you wanted one image of “who lives there”:

A person standing in filtered amber noon beneath a vine-latticed arcade, face veiled against dust, one hand on a rope harness, the other signaling through a gap in moving bodies. Around them: hoof thunder, wing shadows, drum pulses through stone, dry leaves clicking like beads, predators slipping along legally protected walls, children barefoot on warm brick, and old people checking cistern seals with ritual care.

They are not masters of a harsh environment.  
They are one species in a **designed biome-city**, and they endure because they accepted that truth long ago.
