What lives there is **a people who became clerks, mechanics, and diplomats of a semi-sentient biome**—and, depending on who you ask, a city that is itself the primary resident.

Not “survivors in nature,” not “masters of nature.”  
**Co-tenants. Co-conspirators. Sometimes hostages.**

---

At first glance, you expect hardy highland urbanites: practical clothes, callused hands, shift discipline. That’s true. But stay longer and you notice they carry a second literacy: they read cambium stress like others read traffic lights, recognize district mood by drip cadence, and can tell by smell whether a wall is about to sprout, rot, or remember another century.

They tend to be:

- **Layer-minded**: always thinking in stacks—street level, canopy level, root level, legal level, historical level, topological level.
- **Pulse-adapted**: their schedules flex around condensation windows and rain episodes as much as around clocks.
- **Bureaucratically mystical**: they will file a form in triplicate to acknowledge a courtyard has become adjacent to another via root graft.

They are rarely romantic about their environment. Reverent, yes, but also annoyed by it. They complain about migratory staircases, phloem taxes, permit backlogs after map-invalidating rains. They carry pruning saws and ID tags in the same belt.

---

### The “who” is plural

#### 1) The Civic Arborists and Rootwrights
Part engineer, part surgeon. They don’t “garden”; they set load paths, pressure gradients, and growth treaties between neighborhoods. A bad cut can reroute humidity, destabilize transit, or accidentally create a new jurisdictional border.

#### 2) Topological Brigades
Emergency crews trained for non-Euclidean incidents: a schoolyard suddenly contiguous with an industrial yard, a stairwell that now exits into last year, an ambulance route that only exists along a wet root seam at dawn. Their maps are erasable by design.

#### 3) Condensation Guilds
Water workers who farm fog and dew with near-ritual precision. They tune catchment comb tension, clean biofilms, arbitrate tank priority during dry weeks. In this city, they are as politically important as power utilities.

#### 4) Archive Botanists
Historians who read wood, ring, and phyllotactic anomaly as civic record. They can date blackouts from branch spirals and verify testimonies against growth signatures when memory and paperwork disagree.

#### 5) Ordinary Residents
Transit operators, machinists, teachers, seed-mill workers, cooks, clerks—people living normal urban lives in abnormal spatial conditions. They still worry about rent, shift swaps, school placements, and whether tonight’s fog will bead enough for morning wash water.

---

### And what else “lives” there?

- **Engineered arboreal superstructures** that are no longer fully separable from wild growth.
- **Fungal and microbial networks** acting like hidden metabolism—digesters, insulators, memory media.
- **Industrial ghosts with ongoing function**: obsolete foundries now climate organs; smokestacks now spore and steam chimneys.
- **Sparse but symbolic fauna**—cliff birds and gliders, treated almost like omens because they are so rare.

But the dominant life is botanical-infrastructural: root bundles metered like pipelines, walls that thicken and age out of sequence, courtyards that negotiate adjacency through shared stock rather than distance.

---

So who lives there?

**People who can tighten a bearing and negotiate with a vine in the same hour.**  
**Administrations that issue permits to geometry.**  
**A city old enough to have habits, and alive enough to change address while you sleep.**

In that place, citizenship is not just residence. It is ongoing participation in a truce between steel, weather, and growth.
