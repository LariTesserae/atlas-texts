What lives there is a people of **anchors and listening**—and a whole society of creatures that learned to read steel, heat, and vibration the way surface life reads weather and soil.

At first glance, you might think the city’s true resident is machinery. Lifts, ducts, turbine throats, pressure doors—everything breathing in cycles. But the real inhabitants are those who have made their bodies and customs into part of that machinery without becoming mechanical themselves.

They are lean not from scarcity alone, but from design: compact frames, strong hands, forearms marked by old ladder-rung bruises and harness rubs. Their balance is uncanny. They don’t “walk” so much as **flow from hold to hold**, always with one point ready before the next moves. Even at rest they tend to hook a thumb through a rail, loop an elbow through cable, set a boot against a lip. Children learn grip language before alphabet; elders can judge a person’s upbringing by how they pause at an unmarked edge.

Their clothing is layered close and clipped down—no flapping hems, no loose tassels, nothing that can become wind-caught panic. Every jacket has anchor loops. Every belt has at least one emergency clip, often two. Tools are tethered; cups are weighted; hair is braided or bound. Bells on boots are still worn in upper levels: partly practical warning to vibration-hunting predators, partly social declaration—*I am passing through, I claim this rhythm, do not mistake me for prey*.

Their faces are lit from below more often than above. In amber striplight, eyes look darker; skin carries a persistent dust sheen no washing fully removes because water is too dear for vanity. They smell faintly of mineral powder, machine oil, and the fungal tea they sip with ritual patience. Voice culture is restrained: clipped phrases, hand signs, eye contact, touch on a wrist rail for emphasis. Loudness is seen as wasteful unless alarm demands it.

And they are not alone.

In the seams and warm conduit lines live pale arthropods, kept and cursed in equal measure—pests in filters, allies in waste processing. Herd-creatures with silica scales move like living scrubbers through tunnels; people husband them not sentimentally but intimately, understanding their molts, salts, and moods the way farmers elsewhere understand rain. Overhead, ribbon gliders drift through thermal currents and fold against ceilings in clustered stillness, like tools hung after work. Near daylight slits, six-limbed perch predators wait, patient as mounted instruments, reading footfall harmonics through rebar and stone.

So “who lives there?” Not one species, and not merely “workers in a dangerous place.”

What lives there is a **stacked citizenship of human and nonhuman rhythms**:
- machinists who consult migration maps,
- animal wardens who negotiate freight timetables,
- children taught that a spilled cup can trigger a household argument and a civic report,
- couples who marry clipped to safety lines,
- mourners who leave chalk marks in bolt recesses where others leave flowers.

They are pragmatic, but not joyless. Their humor is dry as the rock. Their tenderness is procedural: checking someone’s seal before sleep, topping another’s condenser allotment, repainting a worn edge marker without being asked. They have festivals timed to low-wind windows, songs kept in cages by compressor rooms, and memory practices etched into infrastructure. A joke on one level can become a proverb three decks down by next shift.

If the mountain crack is a wound, these inhabitants are not parasites in it, nor victims trapped inside it. They are closer to scar tissue made conscious: adaptive, tensile, living at the boundary of breakage and repair.

They live by their five laws, yes—but beneath those laws is one deeper rule:

**Nothing survives here alone, and nothing stays unchanged.**
