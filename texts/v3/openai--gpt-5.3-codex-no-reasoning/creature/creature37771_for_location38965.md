Who lives here are **collective specialists**—not loners, not conquerors, but patient, interdependent makers of shade, timing, and storage.

At first you might imagine hard, austere people in a hard, austere land. But the longer you look, the more they resolve into something else: a dense civilization of **quiet abundance**, where survival is less about toughness than about choreography.

## The people: basin-dwellers of timing and proximity

They are likely medium-sized, lightly built, and metabolically conservative—bodies tuned to long walking, low water turnover, and rapid transitions between cool dawn labor and noon stillness.  
Their skin (or equivalent outer tissue) trends matte and pale-olive to dust-brown tones, reducing heat load and glare. Clothing is layered, breathable, and reflective: wraps, hooded veils, ribbed sleeves, loose leg-bindings that trap cooler air and keep dust off joints. Nothing flamboyant in cut, but everything precise in function.

They move with an economy that reads almost ceremonial: short bursts, long pauses, route choices that favor shade geometry over distance. In open flats they travel in braided files, stepping on established crust-safe paths to avoid breaking fragile surfaces that trap dew and seed.

Their settlements force intimacy, so social life is highly negotiated. You get a culture where:
- privacy is rare but respected through ritual signals (fabric knots, doorway markers, timed silences),
- conflict is damped quickly because grudges are too water-expensive,
- cooperation is habitual, almost infrastructural.

No one “owns” water in a simple sense. People hold **rights of timing** instead—who harvests which roofline at first light, who draws from which cistern tier in which week, who gets condensation priority in lean cycles. Law is calendar-shaped.

## Their minds and values

They think in **cycles, gradients, and thresholds** rather than binaries.  
A good planner here is someone who can read a 3-degree temperature shift across a courtyard and infer where mold, spores, pests, or dew loss will hit in ten days. Their wisdom traditions would revolve around:

- dawn discipline,
- thermal etiquette,
- shared maintenance,
- and repair before failure.

Waste is morally offensive, but so is hoarding. The ideal person is not the richest but the one whose household creates the least entropy for neighbors: minimal dust disturbance, good shade casting, reliable composting, punctual lane clearance, clean cistern handling.

## Built life as living life

Their homes aren’t static houses; they are **maintenance organisms**. Families constantly retune walls, reed mats, vent slats, and roof-fibers. Children learn airflow before abstract math. Elders are revered not for age alone but for “cool-memory”: knowing which courtyards stay livable in odd wind years, which salts crack in early heat, which grove edges still drip when others fail.

Architecture and agriculture blur. A wall is also a trellis, a dew plane, a habitat strip, and a thermal battery. Streets are social organs and also wind instruments: they slow, split, and cool moving air. City life is dense, but not noisy—more like a hive running on fabric, clay, and timing.

## Other residents: the co-citizens

This world is full of nonhuman inhabitants that are not “background wildlife” but active partners and competitors:

- **Six-legged grazers**: semi-domesticated in some regions, fully wild in others. Their pellet trails are deliberately routed into soil-building belts.
- **Burrow engineers**: colony animals tolerated near city edges because they aerate future root zones.
- **Gliders**: dusk pollinators/scavengers, watched like weather signs.
- **Fungal and crust guilds**: cultivated on walls and abandoned enclosures, cycling minerals and stabilizing dust.

People manage these beings through corridor design, scent boundaries, feeding residues, and seasonal access rather than hard fences. Control is soft, distributed, and constant.

## Daily rhythm

Life is partitioned by the sun:

- **Pre-dawn:** set collectors, open condensers, patrol cistern seals.
- **Dawn hour:** intense harvesting, sorting, transfer belowground.
- **Late morning:** market exchange, repairs, burrow and herd management in shaded lanes.
- **Noon heat:** social stillness, indoor craft, teaching, accounting, dispute settlement.
- **Dusk:** travel, pollination work, scavenger management, communal meals, low-voice gatherings on roof mats.
- **Night:** storage audits, long-distance caravan departures, listening for slope slumps and crust shifts.

So: busy, but never frantic; crowded, but rarely chaotic.

## What they are, in one line

They are a people (with their animal and plant partners) who have turned scarcity into design—  
**civilization as dew capture, shade casting, and shared timing across a young, unfinished continent.**
