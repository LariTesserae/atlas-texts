What lives there is something **amphibious, infrastructural, and patient**.

Not a beast that dominates the district, but one that has adapted so completely to its managed wetness that it feels like a missing component of the system—half animal, half maintenance myth. People probably call it a dozen names depending on shift and language: *sluice-dog, rail-hound, pipe heron, sump thing*. Nobody agrees on shape at first, because you rarely see all of it clearly at once.

At a distance, it reads as a low, long figure moving where dry feet don’t naturally go: along culvert lips, under catwalk grating, through the shallow mirror-water between concrete pads. Up close, you’d notice:

- skin like dark seal-hide over corded muscle, always wet-looking, beading with condensation rather than shedding it;
- limbs that can splay wide on slick surfaces, with toes more like gripping hands than paws;
- a ribbed flank that seems to pulse slowly, as if it can breathe humid air and thin water both;
- reflective eyes tuned to sodium light, green signal glow, and murk;
- whisker-like sensory filaments around jaw and neck that twitch at vibration—pump harmonics, footfalls through plate steel, tide-pressure in pipes.

It smells faintly of brine, warm metal, and algae disturbed from concrete seams.

It is not aggressive. It is a **scavenger-technician by instinct**. It feeds on crabs in culverts, stunned fish at outflows, rats along pipe racks, and discarded protein from market drains. But it also seems drawn to malfunction: blocked gratings, jammed flap gates, plastic snagged in intake screens. Workers swear they’ve come to a station alarm and found the obstruction already torn loose, as if something with clever teeth and stronger patience got there first. Whether that helps people or simply serves its own hunting routes is unclear.

Its rhythm matches the district’s:
- inactive through the brightest, hottest hours under generator housings and in shadowed service voids;
- alert at shift change and dusk, when noise patterns rearrange;
- most active on muggy evenings before rain, when insects rise, rats move, and runoff begins to speak in channels.

It has learned the map the way water has: not streets, but gradients, drains, overflow stairs, thermal vents, and quiet ledges above spring-tide marks. It avoids open yards unless fog or steam gives cover. It pauses when warning chimes sound, as if those tones are part of its weather.

In a place where everything dramatic has been engineered into routine, this creature feels like the one unmanaged variable that nonetheless belongs. Not wild in the romantic sense—**feral in a practical one**. A life-form assembled by humidity, leftovers, and repetition. If you watch long enough from the basin edge at evening, you might see only a ripple against reflected utility lights, a shape passing between outflow pipes beside the heron, and then nothing—except the sense that the district is not just operated, but inhabited by something that understands its pulse from the inside.
