What lives there is not a person in the usual sense.  
It is a **civic organism**—a distributed inhabitant made of water logic, stone memory, and botanical flesh.

If it has a “body,” the body is the whole city: cistern-throat, terrace-lung, moss-skin, spillway-vein.  
If it has a “mind,” the mind is pattern: gate angles, open doors, set vessels, repeating droplets, growth boundaries that stop at exactly the same line every cycle.  
If it has “people,” they may be local concentrations of that same intelligence—briefly person-shaped eddies in a larger hydraulic consciousness.

It lives by recurrence, not progression.

---

At first you might think the inhabitants are simply absent—gone inside, hidden by mist, drowned out by water-noise. But the longer you look, the clearer it becomes: **arrangement is the resident.** A canopy tensioned just so. A basin recently cleared yet never actively cleaned. A stair worn by passage that never quite occurs in front of you. The city is occupied by **maintenance without maintenance events**, as though custodianship has been abstracted into law.

So whatever “they” are, they do not primarily *do* things; they *hold* things in a solved state.

- They hold channels in flow.
- They hold growth at interlock.
- They hold heat in circulation.
- They hold change in loops that rejoin their starting form.

That is life here: not metabolism toward a future, but metabolism toward **continuous present**.

---

If individual beings exist—and there are hints they do—they are likely hydrobotanic lithomorphs: entities that are partly plant architecture, partly mineral scaffold, partly condensate behavior. Not walking citizens crossing plazas, but **stationary or slow-phase intelligences** rooted into thresholds, lintels, cistern rims, and wind throats. Think of them as guardians grown rather than born: buttressed like trees, ribbed like carved columns, epidermis of lichen, vascular flow synced with pressure fronts and afternoon storms.

They may “speak” by:

- shifting spill rates between terrace lips,
- releasing spores at coded humidity moments,
- altering whistle-tones in colonnades,
- modulating reflected light on wet paving.

In a loud city, language becomes hydraulics.

---

There is also a strong sense that time itself is a co-inhabitant.  
Not personified, but domesticated.

Elsewhere, time erodes, accumulates, surprises. Here, time has been terraced like the cliffs: stepped, routed, made to drain without pooling into irreversible consequence. The inhabitants—whether many or one—have not defeated change; they have **taught it to close**. A crack can exist forever because “becoming rubble” is excluded from the rule set. A leaf can remain mature because senescence is vented like excess heat. Decay is permitted only to the point of aesthetic equilibrium.

So the oldest resident may be a protocol:

> Nothing ends, nothing begins; everything circulates.

---

If you insisted on seeing a face in this, it would be composite:

- eyes of hovering water lenses,
- breath of hot mist through shaded cuts,
- voice of subsonic cistern resonance,
- hair of root mats and strap-leaf spill,
- bones of basalt terraces,
- pulse of rainburst-reset.

A beautiful, deafening, patient being that has outlived narrative.

Not immortal by resisting death—  
immortal by removing the linear conditions that make death legible.

---

So: who lives there?

A **keeper-civilization** that may no longer be separate individuals.  
A **hydraulic ecology that achieved self-governance**.  
A **city that became its own species**.

And if visitors feel watched, it is because they are—not by eyes from windows, but by every tuned surface that notices interruption in flow.
