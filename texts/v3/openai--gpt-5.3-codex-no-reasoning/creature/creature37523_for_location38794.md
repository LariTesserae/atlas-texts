What lives there is not one creature but a **conspiracy of creatures**—a colony-world so compressed that it begins to feel like a single mind distributed across wings, claws, damp skin, and chitin.

If you forced a name on its dominant inhabitant, it would be something like:  
**the ledge nations**.

They are not civilized in any human sense, but they are patterned. They live by intervals, by vibration, by inherited timing. Each species occupies a layer of the cirque’s vertical body: gliders in the high currents, roost-birds on heat-holding shelves, bats in the seam-caves, newts and insects in the wet fractures, hoofed browsers and low predators along seep-lines and rubble fans. None rules. All persist by fitting themselves into the same clock.

So what “lives there” is a **dark-adapted biomass engine**:

- **Pale-eyed cliff birds**, packed so tightly their resting flocks become living insulation on stone.
- **Shift-flying bats**, emerging in scheduled rivers through shared air corridors.
- **Heavy, translucent-wing insects** tuned to moisture pulses and mineral films.
- **Rock-breeding amphibians** that cling in wet cracks and breed in pooled basins.
- **Small-to-mid ungulates** that move in brief windows to drink and scrape sparse forage.
- **Low, cooperative or opportunistic predators** that hunt by interruption, not chase.
- **Fungal and microbial colonies** that glow, cycle, and likely mediate nutrient turnover everywhere the sun cannot.

And beneath them all, perhaps the truest resident: a **behavioral field**—learned or evolved cross-species etiquette.  
Vacate before stonefall. Share cave mouths by time partition. Rotate feeding zones. Leave no stillness unmonitored.

In that sense, the thing that lives there is almost geological in character: not a species, but a **system in early hardening**, as young and sharp as the cirque itself. A provisional ecology, crowded and vigilant, already intricate before it is stable.

If a human asked, “Who lives here?” the basin would answer:

**Everyone who can listen through their feet, eat quickly, breed in cold shadow, and leave before the mountain changes its mind.**
