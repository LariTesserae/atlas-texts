What lives there is not one species so much as a **guilded civilization of bodies**—a biome whose “inhabitant” is the process itself.  
But if we had to name its people, they come in layers.

### 1) The Architects (the antler-continents themselves)
The dominant lifeforms are those giant calcified plant-colonies: not trees, not coral, not fungus, but something with traits of all three. Each colony is a superorganism with districts—intake fans, cracking towers, smelter bulbs, extrusion groves—so what “lives there” first is a **metabolic industry-forest** that grows, mines, refines, and prints its own infrastructure.  
They are slow-moving in human time, but active in system time: pumping brine, inhaling fog, harvesting dust, venting gases, redistributing heat, and reshaping weather around themselves.

### 2) The Furnace Symbionts
Inside nodules, roots, and resin chambers live dense microbial consortia: thermophiles, chemotrophs, solvent-tolerant fermenters. They are the unseen workers of the whole place, cracking minerals and making nutrients bioavailable.  
They likely generate a lot of the heat, gases, and reactive chemistry that give the biome its industrial smell and soundscape. Without them, the towers would stand but not function.

### 3) The Interval Nomads
Between colony-cities roam large six-limbed grazers—ceramic-toothed, deep-chested, long-range walkers adapted to sparse but predictable biomass pulses. They are migratory processors: converting dead pith, husk, and mats into moving fertility.  
Their trails are infrastructure. Their dung, breath, and trampling probably determine what grows next in the flats.

### 4) The Canopy Flocks and Siphoners
In the high latticework nest membrane-winged fliers that move in coordinated bursts. They are pollinators, thieves, scavengers, and weather-riders, timing launches to updrafts and bloom-calls.  
From a distance they may look like storms; ecologically they are couriers—moving spores, parasites, genes, and panic between far colonies.

### 5) The Dust-Makers
Burrowing swarms beneath the pans grind and churn fallen biostructure into nutrient dust. They are soil engines in a world where “soil” is manufactured rather than given. Their plumes are visible from afar because they are the reset mechanism: death to powder, powder to uptake.

### 6) The Vibration Predators
Top hunters key on tremor and resonance more than sight. Some are ambush mimics of broken stalk; others are aerial impact predators that drop through thermal columns.  
In this bright, open, noisy world, stealth is acoustic and seismic. Predation here sounds like sudden percussion.

### 7) The Opportunists of Warmth
Smaller animals likely survive by exploiting microclimates: frost-comb shelters, exothermic root patches, dusk-lifting lichen mats, resin cavities, gas-vent margins.  
They probably live fast, breed in bloom windows, and vanish into substrate when storms electrify the air.

---

So: whoever lives there is a **dry-altitude industrial ecology**—organisms that have evolved into logistics, weather engineering, and acoustic signaling.  
Not a forest with animals in it, but a **planetary factory-ocean of life** where every resident is either:

- building structure,  
- moving minerals,  
- translating sound into behavior, or  
- surviving the intervals between pulses.

If you stayed long enough, you’d stop asking “what creature lives here?” and start hearing the truer answer:

**a network lives here—rooted, roaming, and roaring.**
