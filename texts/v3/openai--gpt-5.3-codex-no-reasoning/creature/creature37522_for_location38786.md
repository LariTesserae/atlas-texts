What lives there is not a species in the usual sense.

At first you might think: no one. A hydraulic ruin. A machine still running after its makers are gone.

But if you stay long enough, the resident presence resolves: **a distributed, slow-bodied colony that lives in water-paths the way forest life lives in soil**. Not one creature, not a crowd of animals—more like an organized wet metabolism braided through the architecture.

It lives as films, skins, threads, and membranes in the margins where water lingers but still moves: under cistern lips, inside clay pipe throats, along resin seams, in the cold backs of channels where sunlight never fully dries the stone. Those pale stains in shaded trenches are its exposed handwriting. Most of it is hidden: translucent layers only a few grains thick, lining the city’s circulation system like a second, living plaster.

It has no leaves because it is not trying to be plantlike.  
It has no obvious body because the basin *is* its body plan.

---

Its intelligence—if that word applies—is hydraulic.

It does not think in images or plans. It senses pressure, mineral load, temperature shifts, pH, the rhythm of drip intervals, the long pulse of seasonal thaw. A blocked chute is, to it, not an obstacle but a sensation akin to pain or numbness. A newly opened crack is opportunity: new territory, new gradient, new current to tune.

It “acts” by growth and secretion:

- thickening into gelatinous baffles to slow a flow,
- thinning to reduce drag,
- precipitating minerals to narrow a throat,
- releasing acids that etch micro-runnels,
- producing waxy films that keep crucial surfaces wet longer,
- abandoning sections that became too bright, too warm, too alkaline.

Over years, these tiny responses become architecture-scale effects. A trickle becomes a thread, a thread becomes a dependable route, a route becomes a channel everyone assumes was carved by hand.

So what lives there is also what subtly keeps it working.

---

If there are mobile organisms, they are probably small and easy to miss: pale, limpet-like grazers in cistern rims; blind arthropod analogues that patrol damp joints; maybe eel-thin filter feeders in deeper covered tanks. They would be symbionts or opportunists, not the main inhabitant. The main inhabitant is the **film network** itself—an ecology of mineral-tolerant microbial mats (and perhaps fungus-like partners) that has matured into something city-scale and self-maintaining.

In bright hours it retreats into shade and pore-space.  
At dusk it “blooms” microscopically: surfaces slicken, certain seams opalesce, faint earthy-metallic odors rise as metabolic exchange shifts with temperature and light loss.  
In freeze cycles it enters armored dormancy, then wakes with thaw, reseeding wet lines from protected pockets.

It is patient because its timescale is structural.  
It can wait ten years for a crack.

---

If the vanished builders ever knew this resident, they may have stopped distinguishing between infrastructure and life. Their rule—*water must always be given a path*—is exactly the rule this colony follows. Maybe they cultivated it as a living maintenance layer. Maybe it evolved after them and inherited their logic by natural selection inside their stone hydrology. Either way, now the basin and its inhabitant are inseparable.

So: who lives there?

**A water-minded lithic biosystem**—a quiet, pervasive, almost invisible civic organism that inhabits gradients, edits flow, writes itself in mineral script, and keeps an entire artificial watershed breathing drop by drop.
