What lives there first is **maintenance**.

Not as a job, but as a species.

In that basin, the dominant lifeform is the person who can hear a pressure change through a wall. The one who pauses mid-conversation because a pump note went half a tone flat. People with mineral crescents on their cuffs, heat-callused palms, pencil stubs tucked behind the ear, keys and tags and folded route maps soft from being handled in damp air. They move like they were taught by pipes: efficient at elbows, patient at bottlenecks, never wasting vertical effort. They know which ladders sweat before dawn, which corridor mists when Tower Vent 3 lags, which drain will back-breathe warm air twenty minutes before dewfall starts.

If you ask who *lives* there, it’s them—but also not only them.

Because the city is thick with semi-lives:  
- thermostatic routines that wake before people do,  
- valve arrays that “decide” flow priority by encoded rule,  
- shutters that bloom open when humidity tips,  
- lights that pulse status in colors everyone reads like mood.

So the inhabitants are human, yes, but constantly braided with process. A resident might introduce themselves by trade—seal tech, line cook, cistern auditor, scaffold welder—but what they really are is a node in a circulating metabolism of heat and water. Nobody is far from anybody else’s exhaust, runoff, error, or kindness.

You’d also get a particular kind of child there, if children are present: low-center-of-gravity, sure-footed on slick metal, taught early to touch handrails with the back of the hand first, to read painted arrows before curiosity, to listen for the “three ticks then hiss” that means wait. They would draw weather upside down: drops falling from ceilings, steam rising from grates, arrows looping floor-to-roof-to-floor again.

Plant life survives as utility and insistence. Fungus lives where policy allows damp to persist. Algal sheen claims sumps like a quiet jurisdiction. Vines are less decoration than infrastructure in green form. Microbes are everywhere, invisible citizens processing what the city can’t afford to waste. If there are pets, they’re probably cultures: yeast strains, inoculants, engineered mats in reclaim trays—named, traded, argued over like pedigreed animals elsewhere.

And over all of this, living there too, is a civic temperament shaped by compression:
- privacy negotiated in layers rather than walls,  
- politeness expressed as flow discipline,  
- intimacy forced by balconies one armspan apart,  
- conflict managed by schedule, signage, and ritual pause points.

People there likely speak in timings and temperatures as much as feelings. “I’ll meet you after second vent cycle.” “Leave that for cool-shift.” “Don’t talk in the ladder throat during signal quiet.” They inherit unfinishedness as normal. “Temporary” means maybe ten years. A welded fix can become heritage.

So: what lives there?

A population of **heat-adapted urban mechanics**, communal and alert, with fungal neighbors, microbial underclasses, and mechanical systems so constant they function like weather, wildlife, and public law all at once. A city where the citizens are not just in the infrastructure—they are part of its circulation, and the infrastructure, in turn, has become the nearest thing they have to a breathing ecosystem.
