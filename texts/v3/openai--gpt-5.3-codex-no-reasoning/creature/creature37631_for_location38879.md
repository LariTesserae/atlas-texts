What lives there is not one species but a **civic organism**—a dry-breathing commonwealth of bodies that have learned to be architecture.

If I had to name its dominant inhabitant, I’d call it a **vault-forest**: those towering, mineral-fleshed columns that look half cactus, half buttress, half coral organ pipe. They are the visible “buildings,” but each is also a lung, cistern, condenser, and battery. They do not merely occupy streets—they make streets by how they grow: ringed stands around plazas, interlocked stalk-walls, root-arches bridging passages. Their tissues are less about softness than custody. Everything in them is designed to hold, meter, and reclaim trace volatiles. In wetter worlds, plants spend lavishly; here, life accountants every molecule.

Around and beneath them lives the second great resident: the **felt-mat undercity**—bead-chamber colonies that seam the stone and wake at night. By day they are inert-looking textile; by night they become a synchronized field of opening mouths, drinking condensation film one molecular whisper thick. They are pavement, sponge, nursery, and recycler all at once.

And moving through this grown infrastructure is a fauna evolved for quiet economies:

- **Plate-browsers** with glassy keratin armor, scraping mineral crusts and tower exudates.
- **Soft-foot runners** that pass through mat corridors without wasting sound or moisture.
- **Fan-neck gliders** that spend most of life folded and only briefly become airborne when thermal timing is perfect.
- **Ledge grazers** that harvest lichen films where dawn gradients are favorable.
- **Ambush forms** that masquerade as debris or hollow tubes, striking with minimal motion and minimal noise.

None of these animals feel like the “main character.” They read more like citizens in a polity whose true sovereign is gradient itself: heat difference, cooling rate, condensation opportunity. Status maps onto microclimate. Prime territory is not fertile soil but east-facing thermal geometry. Wealth is access to dawn.

Even death remains a resident. Corpses do not leave the city; they are rezoned. A fallen column becomes windbreak and scaffold. Bones become retaining lattice. Shed integuments become insulation berms. The place is inhabited by its own past in literal load-bearing layers.

So what lives there?

A **young, experimental metropolis of sequestration life**—an ecology that behaves like urban planning without planners, industry without machines, ritual without language. It is peopled by towers, mats, browsers, gliders, lurkers, scavengers, and the mineralized dead; but deeper than all of them, the enduring inhabitant is a shared behavior:

**the practice of breathing together in scarcity.**
