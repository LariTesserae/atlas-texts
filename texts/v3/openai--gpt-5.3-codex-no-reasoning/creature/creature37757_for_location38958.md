What lives there is a **people of calibration**.

Not conquerors of stone, not mystics of the deep, not rugged survivors in the dramatic sense—though outsiders would call them that. They are closer to wardens, readers, and maintenance-poets: a civilization shaped less by empire than by **repetition, observation, and repair**.

They are likely spare-bodied, hooded, pale-eyed or narrow-lidded from glare, with hands callused in specific places: scraper grip, rope burn, chisel pinch, lever pull. Their beauty standards probably honor competence marks—dew-scarred knuckles, channel-map tattoos, polished shell bracelets denoting water crews, old fracture lines in tools inherited and rehandled rather than replaced.

They live by a moral physics:

- Water moves first.
- Stone remembers.
- Noise has consequences.
- If you can’t fix it, don’t depend on it.
- Nothing is “waste” until every animal, fungus-film, kiln, and cistern has refused it.

So whoever lives there—human or human-like—has become deeply **procedural** without becoming joyless. Their rituals are practical because practicality is sacred. A storm drill is a dance. A floodgate pull is both labor and liturgy. Children play “Spill Court” with pebbles and gutters, learning hydraulics before arithmetic.

They likely divide themselves not by class in the usual sense, but by **relationship to flows**:

- **Condensers** (dawn collectors, wall-wipers, dew measurers)
- **Channel-keepers** (street gradient inspectors, grate clearers, flood marshals)
- **Beast-houses** (herd route planners, pen medics, spoor readers)
- **Stone-listeners** (weep mappers, hum interpreters, crack wardens)
- **Kiln and shell workers** (turning death and discard into structure)

Leadership, if present, would be situational: the person you obey during rain surge might be nobody during dry market days. Prestige accrues to those who are right at the right moment—who read wind shifts, hear hum changes, or spot upstream silt color and call gate changes early.

They are not sentimental about permanence. They know all architecture is temporary agreement with pressure. So they build for failure modes, not ideals. They leave space between districts not from emptiness but from memory: stampede lanes, surge lanes, collapse lanes. Their city is full of absences that are actually infrastructure.

Their religion—whether named or not—would be immanent and material. Not gods above, but obligations among systems. Gliders are protected not because they are holy symbols, but because ecology is a chain of enforceable reverence. To kill one carelessly is both sacrilege and sabotage.

Even their language probably reflects this world: many words for wetness timing, stone textures, animal agitation states, airflow moods. Perhaps no sharp distinction between verb and noun in key concepts—“channel” may also mean “to make way,” “quiet” may mean “to respect distance.”

Emotionally, they may seem restrained to strangers. But this is not coldness; it is acoustic etiquette and risk management. Affection happens close, low-voiced, shoulder to shoulder while wringing dawn cloth into shared jars. Grief is practical: redistribute tools, retie loads, mark the route where someone was taken by surge. Joy is communal competence under pressure: a clean diversion in flood, full cisterns after drought, newborn runners in the pens, children correctly reading a hum in stone.

So: what lives there?

A **hydraulic culture in limestone light**.  
A people who turned unpredictability into calendar.  
Citizens of a city that is half ruin, half instrument—  
and they are the hands that keep it tuned.
