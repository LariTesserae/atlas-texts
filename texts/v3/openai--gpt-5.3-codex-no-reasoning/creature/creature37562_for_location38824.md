What lives there is a **people of keepers**—and around them, a whole grammar of animals and plants that have agreed to survive by proximity.

Not conquerors of a harsh place, not ascetics either. **Tenders. Listeners. Repairers.**  
Their genius is not invention from scratch, but relentless adjustment: a hand resetting one stone, then another, generation after generation, until settlement and cliff and scrub become one interlocked organism.

They are likely compact, weathered, layered in cloth and hide against abrasion more than cold. Skin, hair, tools, and houses all carry the same dust-patina, as if everyone is made partly of the basin itself. Their hands are remarkable—callused fingertips for knotwork and plaster smoothing, strong wrists from grinding and hauling, delicate control for setting fog nets and cleaning cistern mouths. Children learn balance before speed; elders remain central because memory here is hydraulic, architectural, seasonal. To forget a maintenance rhythm is to lose water, shade, herd, or path.

They live by a moral physics:

- **Waste is taboo** (especially moisture, but also shadow, manure, silence, labor).
- **Solitude is suspect** unless purposeful; life is safer in audible range.
- **Repair outranks novelty**—a perfect patch is praised more than a bold new structure.
- **Stewardship of edges** (roof edge, terrace edge, cliff edge, social edge) is sacred work.

So whoever lives there is probably organized in tight, overlapping households rather than isolated units: shared courtyards, shared shade corridors, shared animal pens, shared watch duties. Their politics likely centers on allocation and timing—who opens browse terraces when, who gets first draw from condensation catchments after a poor night, who maintains the high dangerous paths to ledge burials and nest niches. Prestige may come from reliability: the family whose wall never fails, whose cistern lining never cracks, whose herds return fat from sparse browse.

Their spirituality, if named, would be about **continuance under abrasion**.  
Not storm gods and spectacle, but slow powers: wind as sculptor, stone as memory, dryness as judge, noise as heartbeat. Ancestors may be placed in cliff hollows where height and danger confer usefulness even in death—watching routes, marking claims, sheltering swifts whose guano feeds terrace soil.

And it isn’t only “people” that live there. The resident life-form, truly, is a **dense multispecies commons**:

- ledge ungulates that prune what humans cannot reach,
- broad-backed browsers converting hard leaves to dung and traction,
- burrowers aerating terrace margins,
- eave-nesting callers acting as alarm network,
- insects processing every scrap into fertility,
- rope-vines and cushion shrubs serving as architecture, fodder, shade, and humidity traps.

In that sense, the main inhabitant is a **system that refuses emptiness**.  
A settlement-biome whose members—human and otherwise—are packed together so tightly that each one buffers the others against desiccation.

If you met “who lives there,” you might first notice the voice: loud enough to cross roofs, roughened by dust, never far from laughter or argument. Then the reflexes: eyes flicking to sky haze, fingers testing plaster seams, ears tracking whether a familiar quarter has gone too quiet. They carry measures for water, knives for cutting browse, cord for repairs, and always news of wind shifts.

So: who lives there?

**An enduring cliff-basin culture of makers and maintainers, living throat-close with herds, birds, burrowers, and drought-plants—people whose civilization is not built *on* the mountain, but braided *into* its last hard bones.**
