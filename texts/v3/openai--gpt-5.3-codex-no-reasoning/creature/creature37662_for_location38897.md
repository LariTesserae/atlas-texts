What lives there is a **civic ecosystem**—not one species, but a negotiated intelligence spread across plants, animals, machines, and people who’ve stopped pretending those are separate categories.

If you ask for a face, at first you’ll think the residents are the crews in respirators, the rail operators, the guild botanists with clipboards and grafting knives. Then you stay longer and realize they are only one layer of tenancy. The deeper inhabitant is the **grown infrastructure itself**: structural flora trained to bear load, regulate atmosphere, route traffic, and make policy by growth pattern. The city is occupied by citizens with lungs and by citizens with stomata.

The people who belong here are moisture-accountants, canopy engineers, spoor trackers, lichen notaries, pollinator schedulers, salvage millers, predator wardens. They move carefully, as if walking through a factory that is also a nursery and also a parliament chamber. Their culture is patient and procedural: no heroic gestures, only maintenance rhythms. They don’t “build” in the surface-world sense; they **induce forms**, then prune, brace, and bargain with them.

But the most characteristic inhabitants may be the ones that blur utility and life:

- **Load-bearing grove organisms** whose trunks are cored for lifts, ducts, and rail.
- **Light-harvesting membranes** that tilt by timetable and weather the artificial noon.
- **Crosswalk tissues** that open and close with curfew pulses.
- **Voting buildings** interpreted by horticultural guilds as legal persons.
- **Streetlamp blooms** that flower for pollinator traffic as much as illumination.
- **Predators disguised as equipment**, tolerated because they cull grazers that would strip structural bark.
- **Dust-channel burrowers** and catwalk filter-feeders that keep mineral cycles moving.
- **Insect analogues** that farm microbial crusts on heat sinks like tiny industrial agrarians.

Even the “wild” fauna behaves like a workforce in parallel: grazers mineralize bark cycles, gliders move spores between sectors, burrowers aerate dry substrate, scavengers clear fiber offcuts before they desiccate into uselessness. Nothing can afford ornament without function for long—yet function, here, has become its own beauty.

So whoever lives there is this:  
a population that has accepted five laws—moisture, shade, reuse, productivity, and growth-construction unity—and evolved social forms around them. Their temperament is quiet, vigilant, unsentimental, and strangely tender. They are citizens of scarcity who learned abundance in pattern rather than volume.

In short: **a people of gardeners-engineers, and an engineered forest that is also people**.  
Together they are still becoming the thing they live inside.
