What lives there is **a timing-people**.

Not many. Never many. A population sized to maintenance, to vigilance, to memory—more custodians than citizens. If you saw them from a distance you might mistake the city for empty because they move in intervals, like the machinery: absent for hours, suddenly present in purposeful streams, then gone again behind pressure doors, stair shafts, valve houses.

They are built by the place in subtle ways. Compact bodies, heat-tolerant, dark-eyed or light-eyed doesn’t matter so much as the nictitating habits they’ve learned—half-blinks against mineral mist, lashes crusted with salt by day’s end. Skin kept covered not from cold but from condensation and caustic dust: layered wraps, ceramic-thread coats, rubberized aprons when working lines. Their clothing carries the city’s code as much as function: bands denoting which table-depth district they belong to, shoulder tabs indicating vent authority, cuff stitching showing who can override a siphon gate in emergency.

They do not navigate by landmarks first. They navigate by **systems**.  
A newcomer asks “Where am I?”  
A local asks “What cycle is this street in?”

Their language (spoken or signed) is dense with verbs for flow states: rising, backing, flashing, breathing, fouling, settling, sweetening, turning. They have inherited a vocabulary for kinds of wetness most cultures would never separate. Condensate from morning louvers is one word; first flush from metallic rain is another; deep-cistern water drawn after pressure inversion, another still—and each implies what you can drink, what you can wash with, what you must never touch barehanded.

They are religious, but their temples are junctions.

Not metaphorically—literally: little shrines tucked into manifolds, niches beside gauge faces, bell frames wrapped with old tally cords. Their offerings are practical: polished valve keys, wick bundles, descaling acids, ceramic cups of first-clear water. Their saints are remembered not for martyrdom but for calibration: the woman who predicted a seven-district surge from one wrong echo in a tunnel; the child who heard cavitation under a feast-hall and emptied it before collapse; the crew that held a condenser field together through redline heat and kept the table from dropping below famine mark.

Children are raised as listeners. Before they can read inscriptions, they learn to distinguish harmless hiss from seam-failure hiss, ritual bell from threshold bell, ordinary pump knock from the deep thump that means everyone steps away from grated stone. Games teach routing: chalking safe paths through imaginary vent events; counting drip intervals to guess reservoir load; racing to assemble miniature gutter nets before a bucket of water is dropped from above.

Their politics—if you can call it that—follows hydraulic reality. District status rises and falls with reliability of capture and storage. Prestige belongs to those who can keep systems stable across erratic pulses: condenser guilds, cistern archivists, rail schedulers, pressure mathematicians who still work with slate, enamel boards, and inherited analog instruments because those survive steam and mineral drift better than delicate electronics.

Outsiders might call them austere. They do laugh, feast, and love—but around windows of safety. Gatherings are scheduled between expected vent cycles; marriages are witnessed beside public gauges when levels cross auspicious marks; funerary rites include opening a measured trickle into stone channels so the dead “rejoin the circulation.” They do not romanticize permanence. They trust recurrence.

And they are not alone, exactly.

They share the city with the thin, persistent near-life: filament blooms in warm pump shadows, blind pale eels in sump cracks, mirror-shell mites at heat leaks. The people neither sentimentalize nor exterminate these. They classify, monitor, and sometimes cultivate them as indicators. A sudden absence of wall-arthropods near a known warm seam can warn of pressure change before instruments do. A fungal mat changing color might mean condensate chemistry has shifted upstream. In a nearly lifeless place, even tiny lives become sensors, omens, coworkers.

Over generations, the inhabitants have become partly ceremonial, partly infrastructural themselves. They ring bells, turn wheels, chalk marks, recite tables, inspect tide-painted doors, clear salt plates, re-lay porous pavement, and keep breathing lanes clear by social force stronger than law. They are the city’s adaptive tissue: translating mountain exhalation into survivable rhythm.

So what lives there?

- A small human culture of hydro-thermal custodians.  
- A scatter ecology of heat-loving, mineral-feeding small organisms.  
- And, in a real sense, **the city itself**—as a pulse-being of steam, pressure, seepage, and memory—inhabiting its people as much as they inhabit it.

It is a place where “resident” means not ownership of space, but fluency in breath.
