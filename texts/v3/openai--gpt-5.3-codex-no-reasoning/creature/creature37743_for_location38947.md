What lives there is not one species but a **covenant of breath**.

At first you might call them people, and they are—toolmakers, herders, traders, singers, parents, thieves, law-keepers. But in that bowl, “who lives there” widens fast. The residents are also the moth clouds, the lizard sentries, the fungal veils, the dew-nets themselves, the polished walls that drink night air. Life in that caldera is collective engineering: thousands of small bodies, none dominant for long, all co-authoring moisture.

The human inhabitants (if we start there) are likely compact, sure-footed, and extraordinarily precise with their hands. Their wealth is measured in textures: smoothness, seal quality, absorbency, thermal mass, airflow angle. They read stone like weather and weather like scripture. Children probably learn to listen before they learn to count—listen for predator bark patterns, for the pitch shift in vent towers, for the soft patter that means dew has begun to bead on the nets. Their language likely has dozens of verbs for *holding* and *releasing*—moisture, heat, shade, access, obligation.

They are not ascetics despite scarcity. In a place this crowded with life, culture blooms in micro-forms:
- braided cord signatures identifying households,
- lichen-glow motifs marking kin-lines and old treaties,
- drum codes that carry news faster than runners,
- scent-craft (resin and crushed herb blends) used as identity in dim passages,
- market etiquette built around touch, not display, because little shines.

They probably wear layered textiles and membrane-leather wraps tuned to time of day: porous at dawn collection, sealed in dust winds, reflective where sun slats cut across exposed ledges. Jewelry is likely matte—bone, fired clay beads, shell-like chitin, polished seed cases—objects that don’t steal moisture or catch predators’ attention.

Their politics would be hydro-social before anything else. Not kings of land, but stewards of cycles:
- condensate measurers,
- terrace arbiters,
- seed-set wardens,
- airflow mediators,
- predator drivers.
Crime is not sin here first; it is destabilization. Leaving a vessel open at dusk is close to arson. Cutting membrane-plants too early is theft from future mornings.

And alongside them: a dense parliament of animals.

The cliff-runners and gliders are probably semi-domesticated at the edges—never “owned” in the lowland sense, but patterned into daily timing. Barking lizards serve as alarms as much as hunters. Hive insects under eaves are tolerated, coaxed, relocated, harvested, and apologized to. Herd-beasts are bred not for bulk but for balance, lung efficiency, and calm under height stress. Even predators are legible neighbors: feared, tracked, redirected, but granted corridor rights because everyone understands trophic arithmetic.

So who lives there?

A **terraced civilization of moisture accountants** and **vertical ecologists**.  
A people who turned erosion into architecture and scarcity into law.  
A metropolis scaled to an hour’s walk, where every polished surface is a cistern, every shadow a holding zone, every species a worker in the same invisible machine.

If you stayed long enough, the clearest answer might be this:

What lives in that caldera is a way of life that treats water as an event, shade as inheritance, and coexistence as infrastructure.
