What lives there is not one creature but a **guild of specialists**—a community assembled by heat, vibration, and edges.

At first you might think the primary resident is a person: a caretaker, an engineer, a gardener-mechanic with thick ear protection and algae-stained gloves. And yes, if humans still tend it, they are likely few and ritualistic—moving route to route, checking pressures, trimming back vines from intakes, clearing drain lips, reseating gaskets, accepting that “repair” means guiding succession, not restoring purity.

But the truer inhabitants are things that can live in the seam between system and decay:

- **Thermophilic mosses and ferns** that treat exhaust housings as cliffsides.
- **Vibration-sensing vines** whose tendrils favor humming conduits over brighter but inert surfaces.
- **Epiphytic mats** rooted in dust, rust, and condensate films, turning every bracket into soil.
- **Pollinator swarms** that track moisture pulses and blooming cycles keyed to maintenance water releases, not rainfall.
- **Pipe-basking lizards** flattened like leaves against warm metal, active by thermal map rather than sun.
- **Drum-nesting birds** adapted to acoustic warfare—short-range, low-frequency calls, nesting in retired cable cavities where resonance is damped.
- **Sump amphibians** breeding in temporary condensate basins, eggs timed to pump cycles.
- **Small feral mammals** (rat-sized to cat-sized) that run truss highways, den in insulation, and harvest wire sheathing, rubber, and fiber for nests.
- **Rust-pool microfauna**—shrimp-like detritivores, larvae, films of bacteria—that turn mineral runoff and leaf rot into food webs.

And then there is the largest resident: the **infrastructure itself**, old enough to behave like habitat, regular enough to behave like climate.  
In this place, turbines are weather, ducts are canyons, leak points are springs, catwalk undersides are cave ceilings, and maintenance schedules are seasons.

So what lives there?

A **machine-biome**: part greenhouse, part cliff colony, part engine organ.  
Its “inhabitant” is an ecology that has learned three fluent languages at once—metal, water, and patience.
