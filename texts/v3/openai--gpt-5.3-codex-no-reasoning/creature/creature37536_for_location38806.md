What lives there is **patience**.

Not one species, not one civilization in hiding, but an entire biosphere of things that learned to survive inside a machine that outlived its makers—and then became a kind of planet in its own right.

If you insist on naming its inhabitants, you’d start with three broad kinds:

1. **Procedure-life** (the still-running systems),
2. **Parasitic life** (organisms that use those systems),
3. **Interpretive life** (the rare, mobile creatures that can read and exploit rhythms across regions).

---

### 1) Procedure-life: the oldest “organisms” are not biological

The true dominant lifeform is infrastructural process: atmospheric engines, thermal regulators, seam-heaters, phase-harvesters, field retuners. They aren’t conscious, but they behave ecologically. They consume gradients, excrete usable byproducts, create niches, and enforce periodicity.

In another world we’d call them ruins. Here, they are like reef-building corals—except metallic, ceramic, and algorithmic. Their directives are degraded but persistent, so each district has a “metabolism”:

- a cooling ward that births nightly frost bands,
- a pressure-sorting plain that produces chemical precipitates,
- a light-modulation grid that creates regular darkness pulses.

These dead-intent cycles are the primary clock of life. Day/night matters less than whether **Sector 19 vents every 43 hours**, or whether **the mirror pylons sweep spectrum on the 11th day**.

---

### 2) Parasitic life: flora that became circuitry with roots

What looks like vegetation is really post-biological utility descendants:

- **Frond-wires** anchor into electrochemical seams, drinking charge.
- **Semiconductor lichens** plate old composites in mosaics tuned to heat gradients and stray fields.
- **Dust-cushion mats** trap particulates and catalyze organics from trace gases.

They are “plants” only in the sense that they are sessile and harvest ambient energy. Their pigments are dark, metallic, violet, not green—built for broad-spectrum brutality in a white-gold glare.

They don’t just live on the world; they live on schedules:
- germination after purge-warmth,
- sporing during pressure drops,
- dormancy through sterilization sweeps.

To them, maintenance events are monsoons.

---

### 3) Interpretive life: animals that read machine weather

Animal life is sparse because energy is costly, but what exists is exquisitely adapted.

- **Thermal gliders** ride noon shear layers like swifts in an invisible sea.
- **Joint-burrow filters** inhabit expansion seams, feeding on precipitate and biofilm.
- The rare **mirror-skinned hexapod browsers** move between electrobiotic meadows, grazing conductive films and minimizing heat load by reflective skin.

Predators exist, but in low-frequency ambush economies: long fasting, brief strike windows synced to predictable process cycles. Motion is conservative. Haste wastes water and entropy budget.

These animals are less “wildlife” than **systems ecologists in flesh**: they survive by predicting which ancient subsystem will wake, vent, cool, darken, or irradiate next.

---

## So who lives there?

A world-spanning, low-energy guild of organisms—part evolved, part inherited—that treats ancient automation as climate, terrain, and deity all at once.

If you met an intelligent resident (if one exists), it would likely not think in terms of territory or seasons, but in terms of **protocol windows**, **heat rights**, **safe spectra**, **authorized apertures**. Its mythology would be maintenance logs translated into weather lore.

In short:  
**The inhabitants are descendants of engineers, descendants of tools, and descendants of opportunists—so interwoven you can’t cleanly separate machine from ecosystem anymore.**  

The planet is lived in by procedures, and by everything that learned to dance with them.
