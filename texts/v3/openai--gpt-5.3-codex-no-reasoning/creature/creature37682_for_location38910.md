What lives there is not one species but a **civic metabolism** with many faces.

At the human scale (or near-human), its inhabitants are likely people who think less like “residents of buildings” and more like **keepers of processes**. Their skills are horticultural, hydraulic, acoustic, and ecological all at once. A child learns to read root-tone pressure shifts the way other children learn traffic lights. An elder might be a master of graft-law: knowing when a load-bearing vine may be split, which fungal braid can inherit a wall’s tensile duty, how to negotiate passage rights with neighbors and grazers during migration week. Their clothing would be permeable, quick-drying, fungus-resistant, signal-visible in mist; their tools soft-edged, biochemical, tuning-based—needles, spores, resonators, symbiont lures, sap-keyed tags.

But the truer answer is that the city-marsh is inhabited by **guilds of life** that function as institutions:

- **Structural flora** (pillar-trunks, tension canes, raft-mats) serve as roads, homes, bridges, cisterns.
- **Maintenance fauna** are sanitation crews, dredgers, inspectors, and emergency responders by behavior.
- **Signal organisms**—shriek-flocks, pulse-vines, glow-fungi—form warning networks and public information systems.
- **Microbial and fungal economies** handle decomposition, nutrient arbitration, and material conversion faster than any factory.
- **People** coordinate, bias, tune, and arbitrate among them, but do not fully command them.

So “who lives there?”  
A distributed polity of humans, animals, plants, and engineered ecologies—where citizenship is measured by participation in flow, repair, and response.

You’d recognize social classes, but they’d map to function in living systems rather than wealth alone: flood-choristers who stiffen mudworks with ultrasound; canopy surgeons; bloom forecasters; root-cartographers; toxin listeners; corridor mediators who reroute neighborhoods around mating paths of dusk megafauna. Prestige likely attaches to those who can stabilize without ossifying—who can keep things open, breathable, and reversible.

Temperamentally, inhabitants are probably:

- **anti-monumental** (anything rigid becomes ruin),
- **time-literate** (everything has a maintenance rhythm),
- **sensory-attentive** (sound, humidity, luminescence are civic data),
- **comfortable with impermanence** (loss, regrowth, reassignment are normal),
- **collectively pragmatic** (ideology yields quickly to flood behavior).

Their myths would not be about conquering wilderness, but about failed separations: cautionary tales of sealed chambers, silenced roots, privatized channels, and the deadly stillness that follows. Their celebrations likely coincide with volatile abundance—mass flowering nights, first pulse-glow after storm season, migration crossings when bridges bow and retract in choreographed truce.

At dusk, when the vascular lights rise, you’d see the inhabitants most clearly: not just figures moving through fog, but **flows negotiating flows**—children leaping root domes, scavenger flocks banking over market heat, maintenance swarms writing temporary geometry in the canopy, elders listening to pressure-sighs underfoot like physicians reading a pulse.

In short: what lives there is a civilization that never drew a hard line between organism and infrastructure.  
Its citizens are anyone—human or otherwise—who helps the marsh keep becoming a city, and the city keep behaving like a marsh.
