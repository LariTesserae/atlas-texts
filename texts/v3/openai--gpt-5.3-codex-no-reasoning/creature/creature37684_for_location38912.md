What lives there is not a single creature but a **civilization of interdependence**—humans, animals, plants, fungi, insects, and water systems so entangled they function like one body.

If you had to name the primary inhabitant, it would be:  
### **the Terrace Keepers** (or whatever they call themselves in their own tongue)

They are highland people bred more by maintenance than conquest. Their defining trait is not aggression, artistry, or mysticism alone—it is **continuity**. They are experts in keeping things alive in dimness, damp, crowding, and stone.

---

They are physically adapted to altitude and labor: broad-chested, sure-footed, with powerful calves and hands callused by rope, plaster, tile, and reins. Their hearing is excellent, not because they evolved differently, but because they must parse meaning from constant sound: which bell means loose goats, which bark means stranger, which shift in aviary noise signals a hawk, storm, or sickness.

Their eyes are patient eyes. Newcomers squint and stumble in the silver-dark; locals read subtle light like script—cloud thickness, sheen on stone, leaf-angle, smoke color. They dress for wet warmth: layered plant-fiber cloth, waxed capes, wrapped legs, soft-soled footwear that grips slick paving. Metal is scarce or culturally deemphasized; they favor fired clay fittings, hardwood pegs, bone needles, stone weights, woven fastenings.

---

Socially, they are less “urban residents” than **stewards of flows**:

- water flows,
- manure flows,
- seed flows,
- animal traffic flows,
- repair labor flows,
- inheritance flows.

A person’s identity is tied to a route and a responsibility: cistern ward, lane caller, hive tender, terrace mason, roost registrar, compost keeper, torch rounds, seed memory, bell code trainer, gutter child (the kids small enough to clear narrow channels), dusk herder, dawn collector.

They do not think in isolated property the way lowland market cultures might. Rights are bundled with obligations. If your household draws from an upper cistern, you owe desilting on the third runnel tier. If your goats use a shaded transit lane, you contribute dung-straw to the heating pits. If your roof hosts public roosts, you receive guano share—but must maintain perch beams.

---

And the nonhuman residents are not “livestock” in a simple sense. They are **registered participants** in infrastructure:

- Pigeon and dove flocks circulate nutrients, messages, and alarm behavior.
- Goats and small cattle are moving lawnmowers, fertilizer engines, and thermal presence.
- Pigs are waste processors in controlled compost courts.
- Dogs are district-level security, herding intelligence, and night warning systems.
- Bees are pollination labor and weather indicators.
- Frogs and insects are moisture metrics as much as fauna.

Children grow up learning animal dialects almost like secondary languages: five kinds of bleat, three warning croaks, wing-noise of panic vs. settling. A silent courtyard is suspicious.

---

Culturally, they are conservative without being stagnant. They innovate, but invisibly—inside inherited forms. A new gutter lip profile that sheds silt better spreads household to household and, fifty years later, feels ancient. They distrust abrupt replacement because abrupt replacement breaks unseen dependencies. Pull one wall, lose a moss strip; lose the moss, lose moisture retention; lose that, lose insects; lose insects, lose certain birds; lose birds, gain crop moths. They think in chains.

Their religion, if present, is likely practical-sacral: offerings at springs, first-cut herbs blessed, roost eaves marked with protective clay signs, ancestral names recited during seasonal re-terracing. Not a wilderness faith—a **maintenance cosmology**. The holy act is repair done in time.

---

At night, under amber windows and torch bowls, they become silhouettes moving through known darkness. Their city does not sleep all at once. It dozes by districts. Somewhere always: someone trimming a wick, someone turning compost, someone easing a kidding doe, someone checking overflow jars under a sudden mist-drip.

So what lives there?

A people who turned a mountain into a managed biome and then let that biome shape them in return.  
A thousand species nested into architecture.  
A society whose greatest invention is not a machine, but a durable agreement:

**No surface is empty. No life is separate. Nothing is finished—only tended.**
