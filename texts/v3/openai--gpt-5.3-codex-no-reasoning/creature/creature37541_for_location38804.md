What lives there is not one creature but a **civic body**—a wet, metallic commons of small lives that have learned to read schedule, voltage, and pressure as if they were tide tables and seasons.

At the center of it, if there is a center, is a species (or guild) you might call the **Pulsekeepers**.

They are not large—palm-length at most—and at first glance they seem almost forgettable: low, six-limbed, plated in matte mineral-gray that matches oxidized alloy. But they are the ones everything else bends around. Their bodies are split-function: front limbs are delicate, brush-fine manipulators for scraping corrosion lace and fungal film; rear limbs are heavy magnetic hooks for locking against vibration when turbine harmonics rise. Along their flanks sit beadlike pressure pits that can feel the breathing of vents seconds before a human sensor would register change. Their eyes are poor in the mammal sense, but their skull-caps carry mirrored domes that read thermal shimmer, electrical leakage, and polarized reflections in condensation. They “see” failure before failure becomes visible.

They do not build nests. They maintain **routes**.

Their territory is drawn not by walls but by recurring events: drip-lines, heat pulses, diagnostic flashes, sterilization arcs. A route is a timed circuit through these phenomena—cross this grille after the seven-second exhale, pause under the blue strip until the second valve-clack, descend when the sub-bass rises, clear before the ion burst. Young Pulsekeepers learn route-memory the way migratory birds learn stars. Elders carry route-corrections in scar tissue and antenna nicks, passed through touch and click-patterns in crowded pipe shade.

They are descended from designed maintenance stock, almost certainly. You can see the intention still in them: anti-corrosion mouthparts, swappable claw keratin, distributed cognition that allows temporary swarm-minds around complex faults. But design has frayed into ecology. They groom one another not for hygiene alone but to exchange conductive dust colonies that improve static sensing. They harvest mineral fuzz not just as food adjunct but as insulation putty, packing it into hairline cracks where warm mist would otherwise strip a surface too clean for biofilms to regrow. They are janitors and gardeners and, unintentionally, hydraulic engineers.

Around them lives everyone else:

- **Filter-kites** stitching the upper drafts, membrane wings tuning themselves to pressure gradients like living weather vanes.
- **Drain eels** translucent as tubing, body fluids buffered against coolant chemistry, slipping through capillary grooves no vertebrate should survive.
- **Coin grazers** clustered in rasping circles at each algae-glow patch, stripping and reseeding films in cycles that prevent any one species from dominating a panel.
- **Ribbon hunters** quick and almost two-dimensional, resting flattened in cable braids until valve-noise masks a strike.
- **Mite-clouds** so tiny they read as moving dust, feeding on condensate-borne organics and becoming, in turn, moving nutrient for everything else.

But if you ask what *lives there* in the singular, it is this:

A distributed, many-bodied intelligence of habit.

Not intelligence in the grand declarative sense—no monuments, no speech you’d recognize, no plan to leave the hollow. Rather, an accumulated fluency in machine-weather so complete that the chamber’s continued function now partly depends on it. Grates clog less where Pulsekeepers patrol. Corrosion blooms stall where grazers rotate. Overflow events soften where tube-eels keep microchannels open. Even predator pressure prevents any one maintenance lineage from exploding and stripping surfaces bare.

They are not passengers in the machinery anymore. They are a **maintenance layer the original designers did not program**.

And the place has shaped their temperament. They are abrupt but not frantic; noise-hardened, rhythm-bound, suspicious of novelty except when novelty leaks warmth or food. Their conflicts are local and procedural—who owns a drip lip during peak condensate, who yields a shelter seam before sterilization sweep. Courtship is timing display: two individuals matching step to vent pulses over dozens of cycles without desynchronizing. Death is usually mechanical—mistimed crossing, pressure-flip, flash sterilization—but never wasted; carcasses are disassembled by dawn-shift scavengers, minerals returned to route-networks.

If a human sat very still in that chapel-sized hollow, long enough to stop hearing “noise” and begin hearing intervals, they might notice the Pulsekeepers pause at certain old panels whose indicators died centuries ago. They still touch those dead lights in passing, antenna to glass, as if checking a shrine or taking attendance. Perhaps the panel once emitted a cue their ancestors depended on. Perhaps the behavior remains because reliability, once found, is sacred.

So: what lives there?

A small, hard, rain-slick people of the infrastructure—engineered, feral, cooperative, predatory, domestic in their weirdness—who have turned a forgotten maintenance cavity into a functioning estuary of metal and mist. Not masters of the machine, not separate from it, but an emergent organ of its old age.
