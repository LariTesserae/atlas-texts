What lives there is a people of **keepers**.

Not conquerors of the mountain, not romantics hiding in it—keepers in the literal sense: those who keep pressure balanced, joints sealed, channels clear, roots fed, loads shared, and memory continuous. Their culture is less about expansion than about **maintenance as citizenship**.

They are built like the place has built them: layered, practical, and quietly exacting. You’d notice strong calves and careful footing first—everyone learns stairs before they learn speed. Hands tend to be scarred at the knuckles, stained with mineral salts, oil, moss tannin, ceramic dust. Clothing favors waxed wool, rubberized hems, many pockets, and tools that fold flat: crack gauges, valve keys, listening pins, patch clamps. Even formal wear probably includes a weather cape.

They speak softly not from timidity but from acoustics; loudness is wasteful in fog and stone. Their language likely has unusual precision for moisture states, slope stability, and flow timing—different verbs for dripping, weeping, sheeting, beading; different kinds of “open” depending on whether a gate, a valve, or a district is meant. Time is told by pressure windows as much as by clocks. “After crest” might mean more than “at six.”

Their social classes, if present, are probably not richest-to-poorest so much as **upstream-to-downstream**, **load-bearing-to-load-dependent**, **access-to-storage**. Prestige accumulates around reliability: the person who can hear a bad resonance in a buried line, the crew chief whose retaining wall has held twenty winters, the archivist who knows which old quarry seam can be reopened without waking a slip plane.

Children grow up feral only in very specific ways: not wild in forests, but wild in infrastructure. They can trace service routes through six levels of terraces, identify safe moss from slick moss, and read hairline fractures in plaster with eerie confidence. Games involve spillways, echo chambers, and timing races to the funicular cycles. Coming-of-age might include a first night inspection during rain, when every system speaks at once.

Spiritually—whether religious or not—they probably revere **continuity**. Ritual and engineering blur: listening stations are both diagnostics and shrines to attention. Communal moments happen at first crest of cisterns, at reopening of pressure-gated doors, at the annual tightening before freeze season. Their dead may be remembered by what still holds because of them: a stair pinned, a channel corrected, a slope taught to stay.

They are not alone, either. Co-inhabitants include the vent-nesting swifts, pipe-route night mammals, reservoir amphibians, and the city’s botanical skin. People here would treat these less as scenery and more as indicators—if swifts shift nesting height, if amphibians vanish from a basin, if a certain moss browns, something in the system has changed. Ecology is an instrument panel.

And then there is the largest resident: the city itself as a quasi-organism. It breathes, sweats, stiffens, heals, reroutes. In many places humans build a city; here, humans and stone and water are in a long mutual apprenticeship. So what lives there is not just a population but a **maintenance civilization**—patient, technically intimate, weather-literate, emotionally restrained but deeply collective.

If you stayed long enough, you’d realize their core belief might be this:

**Anything that can still carry, hold, wick, root, or warn deserves to remain in the system.**  
Including people.
