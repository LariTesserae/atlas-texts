What lives there is a people who have learned to behave like careful weather.

Not metaphorically—practically, bodily. They move in pulses, hold still when needed, make decisions by humidity charts the way farmers once read clouds. Their city has made them precise: soft-footed on wet stone, fluent in signs, timing, and gradients. They are not austere so much as attentional. A child there can tell from the taste of a drip whether it came off a condenser coil, a basalt cut, or a fungal vat roof. An elder can stand in a corridor, close their eyes, and know which district has exceeded heat quota by the faint change in airflow.

They are probably lean, broad-lunged, and heat-shaped—skin protected with reflective films or mineral dusts, clothing layered in pale wraps and vented membranes rather than heavy fabric. Their garments are less about modesty than phase: pre-heat cloaks, flashstorm capes, post-storm wick belts hung with sample vials and grate keys. Many wear ear-fans, neck frills, or engineered gill-collars if augmentation is common; if not, they’ve built analogs—cooling bibs, evaporative veils, ceramic throat plates. Eyes are shielded from glare, either by biology or craft: slit visors, mirrored oils, translucent eyelid implants. They carry water tools the way other cultures carry knives.

Socially, they are quiet without being cold. Conversation is often lateral: fingers, shutters, lamp codes, shoulder angles, colored strips tied at belt or rail to mark intent, urgency, contamination status, route rights. Public arguments are choreographies of signal escalation, not shouted conflict. Laughter exists, but it is often breathy, brief, private; joy is in competence, timing, shared survival rituals, the first clean condensate after a bad heat week.

Their values come from feedback loops:

- **Maintenance is sacred.**  
  Not as slogan—because failed grates drown streets, and missed descaling can starve neighborhoods of evening rain. “Repair” is prestige work.

- **Noise is ethical.**  
  Loudness is not rudeness alone; it can alter predation, spook flockers into stampede, ruin a hunt corridor, or shift migration timing.

- **Heat is political.**  
  Waste heat is rationed like water. Status may come from how little thermal footprint a household needs, or how efficiently a district recovers latent heat.

- **Water is plural.**  
  They do not speak of water as one thing. Every source has character, rights, uses, and social meaning. Bowl placement at night is both domestic routine and inheritance practice.

They likely live in multi-level dwellings raised over sweating ground, with drip maps painted on ceilings and mineral gardens that are not green but alive: bacterial films, curated accretion chimneys, edible fungal mats, tube-creature filters. Kitchens are half-lab: taste strips, salinity stones, sterilizing steam trays, fermentation bins for feed-gel. Homes sing softly with controlled condensation—tick, hiss, tick—like a household clock.

And they are not alone in the category of “who lives there.” The city is co-inhabited by managed ecologies that are treated as neighbors, hazards, and infrastructure all at once:

- culvert tube-colonies that clean runoff;
- enamel-backed grazers that keep crust from choking drainage;
- dawn flockers whose routes define school schedules;
- gliding predators tracked by thermal wardens;
- gutter scavengers that are noisy enough to be weather instruments.

So the residents are part civic body, part herd manager, part hydrologist. A traffic officer might also be a condensation notary. A priest might be a floodgate engineer. A butcher might hold migration licenses and fungal feed contracts. Children might learn arithmetic through reservoir drop-rates and ethics through quiet-hour drills.

Culturally, beauty here is likely **mineral and temporal**: polished gate teeth, perfect runoff crowns, a viaduct shadow at exactly noon, the iridescence of a healthy bacterial skin, the sound (or near-sound) of a market that can coordinate hundreds with almost no voice. Their monuments are floodgates because gates are promises made physical.

If you met one of them on a rim road at copper dusk, they might seem reserved at first—watchful, listening to rock. Then a wall beads, exactly when they said it would, and you realize their intimacy is with processes most people never notice. They belong to a civilization that domesticated seepage, steam, and silence. They are patient, technical, communal, and deeply local: citizens of a basin where even thirst has choreography.
