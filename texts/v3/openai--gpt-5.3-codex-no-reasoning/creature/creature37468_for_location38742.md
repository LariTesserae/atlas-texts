What lives there is **a people of keepers**—not conquerors, not hunters, not even quite “citizens” in the way dry societies imagine.  
They are the **continuity organism** of the basin: a sparse, patient lineage whose primary craft is preventing collapse.

At first you might picture individuals moving through ruins. But the longer you stay with the place, the more the inhabitants resolve into something larger: **distributed caretaking intelligence** embodied by human bodies, plant systems, routes, habits, and remembered procedures. A person is a node; a knot-settlement is an organ; maintenance is metabolism.

They are likely physically unornamented by choice and necessity: dense-muscled in hands, shoulders, diaphragm; skin toughened and pale where light never reaches; hair kept close or bound to avoid snagging; lungs trained for efficient exchange in cold, humid air pockets. They move economically, never theatrically. Every gesture has a maintenance logic.

Their senses are tuned to what your description prioritizes:
- micro-changes in current pressure,
- temperature seams as map-lines,
- vibration through rails and stone,
- plant tension as structural readout,
- silence itself as diagnostic signal.

They do not “explore” their world; they **circulate** it, like blood in old vessels. They memorize districts by touch-pattern and flow timing, not by visual landmark. Identity may be tied less to family name than to **responsibility corridors**—the stair-run you keep clear, the root-bridge you retie each cold season, the intake trench your grandparents desilted.

Culturally, they probably treat failure as moral, practical, and spiritual all at once—not because of dogma, but because neglect here is immediately cumulative. So ritual and engineering are fused:
- retensioning cables at tide-turn as communal rite,
- recaulking seams with spoken cadence to maintain pace and memory,
- apprenticeship through hand-over-hand instruction in darkness,
- oral law encoded as maintenance prohibitions (“never cut anchor-growth…”).

Their religion, if it exists, is likely non-anthropomorphic and procedural: reverence for load paths, flow cycles, survivable redundancy. Ancestors are remembered not by statues but by **still-functioning joints**.

And yet they are not joyless. Their beauty is slow:
- the clean reveal of a cleared passage,
- the symmetry of a well-kept kelp trellis,
- a dome air pocket warmed by fermentation beds,
- songs timed to long internal tides,
- the rare clarity season when distant tower-shadows return.

So: what lives there?

Not monsters.  
Not ghosts.  
**Stewards.**

A cold-water civilization that outlived spectacle and kept only what could be repaired by numb hands in the dark—and in doing so became as structural, as patient, and as enduring as the built sea itself.
