What lives there is a **people of calibration**.

Not conquerors, not nomads in the usual sense, not even “survivors” exactly. They are more like custodians of an equation that has been balanced so long it became culture, religion, and anatomy all at once.

They are called (in your closest translation) something like **the Still-Marked**, or **Those Who Keep the Angles**.

---

At first glance, they look spare enough to be mistaken for frail: tall, narrow-chested, long-limbed, wrapped in layered mineral-dyed cloth the colors of old stone. But up close they are dense and durable—tendons cabled tight, joints broad and low-friction, skin matte with fine dust-binding oils that keep static from biting too deep. Their hair is usually cropped short or sealed in resin braids threaded with tiny shell disks that click only when touched by hand, never by wind.

Their eyes are their most striking feature: not luminous, not mystical—just extremely steady. They track slowly, precisely, as if every glance is measured against a map only they can see. Children are taught early never to look “at” movement, but “through” it, to catch lagging shadows and doubled edges before stepping.

They speak in quiet pulses.  
Conversation happens with long rests, tiny hand-signs, pebble placements, throat-click numerals. Loud speech is reserved for ritual testing in echo-basins, because in this place a careless shout can come back as tomorrow.

---

### How they live

They do not build permanent towns because permanence belongs to patterns, not structures.  
Instead they maintain a constellation of **brief dwellings**:

- dry-stacked half-domes under overhangs  
- crack-shelters sealed with felted needle-plant fiber  
- condensation basins lined with fired clay and polished bone  
- cache pits marked by cairns keyed to anomaly-lines rather than cardinal directions

A site is inhabited, then intentionally thinned of presence, then revisited on schedules measured in animal generations. Their calendars are not seasonal; they are **positional**—based on when herds cross a specific scar-slope, when membrane roosts rotate to a certain aspect, when a duplicated boulder pair “drifts” into visual alignment from a named ledge.

They keep almost no metal. A needle, a hook, a cutting sliver of worked alloy may pass through ten families in a century. Most tools are stone, ceramic, horn, gut-fiber, lacquered sinew. They are repaired past the point other cultures would call usable. Waste is taboo not morally but mathematically: discarding matter carelessly is treated like introducing error into a proof.

---

### What they do with animals

They are not pastoralists, and not simple hunters.  
They practice **population steering**.

They map colonies and herds on clay boards with pebbles, ash marks, and scratch-lines. These boards resemble star charts because functionally they are: predictive models in a system where the sky is less useful than the ground. A family might spend three days observing one talus slope just to confirm that shell-backed grazers remain within accepted density banding.

They cull quietly and rarely.  
They redistribute eggs.  
They block or reopen burrow galleries with fitted stones.  
They salt certain ledges to draw browsers away from fragile crust fields.  
They leave carcasses in designated “dark seams” where scavenger cascades are expected.

Predation is not feared; imbalance is.

---

### Their understanding of the cracks in reality

To outsiders, anomalies are supernatural events.  
To the Still-Marked, they are **landforms with behavior**.

They classify them with practical names:

- **Returning Paths** (routes that fold distance)  
- **Twin-Stones** (material duplications)  
- **Late Shade** (shadow lag zones)  
- **Ahead Echo** (future-softened acoustic returns)  
- **Open Burrow Windows** (parallax exposures into enclosed spaces)

Children memorize safe approaches the way others learn river crossings. Adolescents apprentice as **Line Readers**, who test anomaly edges with dust drops, weighted cords, and synchronized footfalls. Elders maintain memory-chains: oral records of tiny shifts over centuries. Because although the cracks are stable, “stable” here means slow drift, not still photograph.

Religion, if you call it that, is non-anthropocentric: the world is not broken by the cracks; it is **stitched by them**. Their core taboo is forcing rapid change—large fires, mass stampedes, quarrying at fault intersections—anything that might jerk the stitched fabric.

---

### Social character

They are deeply communal but visually sparse. You may camp near fifty people and see only six at once. Presence is distributed.

Leadership belongs to those best at maintaining invariants, not charisma. A respected elder is one who can say, “This slope held seven bands when my mother’s mother watched it, and it holds seven now,” and prove it with boards, stones, and witness lines.

Their art is subtle and data-rich: incised bone rods encoding migration intervals, woven belts whose knot spacing marks anomaly crossings, low songs with missing beats where space folds. Their humor is dry and often geometric. Their grief is private but not hidden: small rings of sorted pebbles left at sleeping shelves, then slowly dispersed into existing colony patterns so no death introduces abrupt form.

---

So what lives there?

A civilization that learned to become as quiet as its biome:  
**patient, exact, and nearly motionless in aggregate**—yet intensely active at fine scale.

They are human enough to share fire, broth, stories, and wary hospitality.  
But given enough nights among them, you realize they are also something the land has made: a sentient extension of an ancient equilibrium, thinking in ledges and lagging shadows, keeping life dense and time slow in a place where forever is not metaphor but management.
