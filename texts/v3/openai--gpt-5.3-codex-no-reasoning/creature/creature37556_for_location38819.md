What lives there is a **community in first draft**.

Not a single creature, not a hidden beast, but a kind of soft-bodied collective organism made of damp, heat, timing, and opportunists. If that basin has a resident, it is **Succession itself**—young ecology wearing many small faces.

It lives as:

- snail calligraphy laid down nightly and erased by noon,
- ants behaving like circulating blood between green “organs,”
- frog notes like a heartbeat you only catch if you wait,
- dragonflies stitching patrol routes over that tannin-dark eye of water,
- roots quietly levering apart the city’s old grammar.

It is shy by scale. It doesn’t announce itself with noise or flowers everywhere. It grows in **patches, pauses, retries**. One clump thrives while two feet away the ground is bare and thinking about it. It favors edges: under curled crust, along rebar, in moss shade, at puddle margins where bloom and decay trade places.

If you insisted on imagining it as one being, it would be something like:

- a low, warm-breathing animal that exhales mist at dusk,
- skin that cracks, seals, darkens, and peels each day,
- nerves made of ant paths, memory made of spores and seeds,
- eyes borrowed from lizards and sparrows,
- a voice so quiet it speaks through moisture shifts and leaf angles.

It is **urban-born but not urban-made** anymore. The town built the bowl; this resident is what answered the invitation. A patient colonizer. A rehearsal for future ground. A child landscape teaching itself how to become older.

So who lives there?

A thousand minor citizens, yes—but more truly, a single emergent tenant:  
**the beginning of a place, alive and continuously rewriting its own instructions.**
