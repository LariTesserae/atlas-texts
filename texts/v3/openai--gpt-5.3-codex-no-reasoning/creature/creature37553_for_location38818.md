What lives there is not one creature.

It is a **custodian ecology**—a district-sized mind made of roots, insects, damp air, patient machinery, and small lawful glitches in reality.

If you insisted on naming a resident, the closest would be something like a **Wardener**: not an animal in the ordinary sense, but a distributed intelligence expressed through many bodies at once. It lives in the negotiations—between vine and scaffold, shadow and memory, rain and storage, path and trespass. You never see “it” directly because it does not centralize. A heron’s pause, a beetle’s polishing route, a fungal pulse along a handrail, a conveyor’s timing shift, a dark pool opening ten minutes early—these are all gestures of the same inhabitant.

It is old in behavior, young in material.  
Its bones are prototype curbs and unfinished slabs.  
Its flesh is chlorophyll and mycelium.  
Its nerves are copper threaded through trunks.  
Its endocrine system is condensation cycles.  
Its dreams are those geometric shadows where yesterday leaks through.

It does not rule by force. It regulates by **permissions**:
- which corridor stays open tonight,
- which stairway leads where this week,
- which pollinators get right-of-way,
- where predators may subtract without destabilizing the whole.

So the “who” of this place is a civic organism: part greenhouse, part port authority, part monastery, part weather engine. It is shy about spectacle, devoted to throughput, and almost tender in its efficiency. It keeps emptiness on purpose. It keeps darkness zoned. It keeps water moving. It keeps reality slightly loose so pressure can vent.

And if there is a single avatar of it—something that might meet your eyes—it would likely appear only at the edge of function: a tall, moss-antlered quadruped assembled from rootwood and mothlight, standing motionless in a plaza bowl, listening to wingbeats as if reading traffic reports, then turning away the moment you understand that you were never looking at a beast.

You were being looked at by the city itself.
