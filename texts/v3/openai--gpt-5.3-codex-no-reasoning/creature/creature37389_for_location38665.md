What lives there is **a single civilization of growth**—not animals, not people, but a managed, self-thickening botanical intelligence distributed across steel.

It lives as mats, fronds, cables of root, films, and threaded stems; as orchids bolted beside torque marks; as moss occupying rivet heads like punctuation. It is one vast resident wearing many plant-shapes, fluent in brackets and troughs and perforated plate. Nothing feral in the old sense, yet nothing inert: a disciplined verdancy that has learned the grammar of pumps and pressure.

If you tried to name the inhabitant, it would resist singular categories:

- It is **garden**, because it grows, reseeds, layers canopy over understory.
- It is **machine**, because it circulates by schedule, responds to valves, accepts pruning as metabolism.
- It is **infrastructure**, because it filters, strips metals, clarifies, buffers, holds equilibrium.
- It is **proto-wilderness**, because given any unclaimed edge, it arrives and keeps arriving.

Its body is vertical.  
Surface rafts sip light. Midwater lattices hold climbing tissues in ordered tangles. Below, filtration forests comb the heavy clear water. Even emptiness is temporary; every gap becomes vascular route or anchoring point. It does not roam, but it occupies absolutely.

Its temperament is quiet, almost ceremonial.  
No wings disturb it, no fins write through it, no insects annotate its air. Movement is earned: a pump pulse, a relay click, condensation falling like patient rain. In that restraint, the resident becomes more present. You feel it not as motion but as pressure—green insistence against every manufactured boundary.

It is young, too, and aware of its youth in material terms: bright welds, sharp seams, paint only beginning to yield. The inhabitant is currently teaching the basin how to age—first with mineral bloom, then algal script, then soft fur at slow-flow margins. It is the first weather of this place.

So whoever lives there is **a designed biome in the act of becoming native to its own machinery**: a hydrobotanical commons, engineered and exuberant, maintained even in abandonment, calm but relentless.  
A resident that does not breathe loudly, does not hunt, does not migrate—yet steadily turns iron into habitat and schedule into ecology.
