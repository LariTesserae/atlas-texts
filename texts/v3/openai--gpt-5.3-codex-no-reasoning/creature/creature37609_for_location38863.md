What lives there is a civilization before architecture, and an ecosystem before greenery.

Not one creature, but a **pressure-born society of bodies**—most of them low to the ground, wet-backed, armored against abrasion rather than claws. Life in that basin has agreed on a shared shape: broad, stable, close to the earth, built to endure saturation, cold, and constant contact with mineral grit. Even the fast ones run low. Even the hunters don’t leap—they **push**, herd, redirect, and let mud do the killing.

If I had to name the central inhabitant, I’d call it the **Shingleback Grazer**: six-legged, barrel-chested, tile-skinned, with a scraping mouth like a stone adze. It feeds by planing microbial films and mineral foams off seep lines, turning geology into muscle. It doesn’t decorate the land with nests or dens so much as with **feeding signatures**—long pale arcs carved into dark rock, temporary maps of where nutrients bloomed overnight. It is less “beast in landscape” than “moving weather event”: where the herds pass, scavengers gather, predators align, alarms rattle, humans follow.

Around it, everything else occupies the wake:

- **Carrion-runners** with narrow skulls and quick feet, living off what the big bodies shed, drop, or crush.
- **Blind seep-filter colonies** in warm water, pumping silt through comb-limbs and building brittle lime spires—reef-makers of mudland, loud when they fail.
- **Mist-pack predators** that hunt with acoustics and terrain, using concussive barks and bad footing rather than stealth.
- **Pale, heavy scavenger gliders** that circle thermals and descend like falling plaster.

And then humans—present, but never dominant.  
The people here live as **listeners more than builders**. They don’t impose a city; they place temporary edges against movement: ring walls, hide breaks, drying racks, clappers, bells. Their greatest technology is route-memory encoded in sound and substrate: where the shell ridges sit, how fog bends noise, what kind of shine means death. They survive by reading pulses—migration, runoff, seep warmth, night swarm emergence—timing themselves to the basin’s breathing.

So what lives there?

A **post-forest biosphere**: animal-rich, plant-poor, mineral-fed.  
A **culture of low profiles**: in beasts, in tools, in human structures.  
A world where life is not rooted but **circulatory**—scraping, filtering, scavenging, booming, clicking, flowing.

Its true resident is motion itself, made flesh over and over:
tile hide, shell clatter, fog calls, mud sighs, and feet.
