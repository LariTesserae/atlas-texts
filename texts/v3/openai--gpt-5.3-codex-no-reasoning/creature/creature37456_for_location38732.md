Who lives there is not one “species” so much as a treaty.

At first you might think the inhabitants are the people—humid-climate engineers, market runners, flood schedulers, viaduct fishers, maintenance guilds who can hear a failing bearing through three decks of traffic. They are real, and they are native in the deepest sense: not born to the place perhaps, but fluent in it. Their bodies are tuned by habit—wide-soled boots that wick pressure-water, skin sheens that resist fungal-analogue blooms, implants that translate structural vibration into usable warning, lungs trained for heavy air. They do not “endure” the city; they read it. A child there learns condensation drift before alphabet, learns which siren intervals mean move up two levels, which mean shelter in place, which mean the drains are about to reverse.

But then the longer you look, the less human-centered it becomes.

The true resident is a mesh of lives:  
- **people**, yes,  
- **synurban animals** that evolved to treat conduits as rivers and power gradients as migration stars,  
- **maintenance swarms** that are half-tool, half-colony behavior,  
- **metabolic materials** that heal, seal, drink heat, and exhale structure,  
- and the **city’s own predictive systems**, whose opening and closing of routes acts like respiration.

Together they form something like a distributed organism with no single skull.

Its temperament is alert, improvisational, and unsentimental. It rewards responsiveness and punishes assumptions. Creatures that thrive here are those that can do two things at once: feed and filter, shelter and signal, move and monitor. Even the people adopt this rule. A vendor is also a spotter for surge-color in runoff. A transit attendant is also a wildlife negotiator when herder packs clog an access throat. A child’s game might double as acoustic mapping of a neighborhood’s blind spots.

If you wanted to picture “who lives there” as a single being, imagine this:

A broad-backed, warm-blooded leviathan sprawled across a drowned plain, skin plated in stone and dark ceramic, sweating constantly, threaded with capillaries of freight, data, silt, and rumor. Its breath is steam and turbine wash. Its nerves are sirens, light-sign, pipe clicks, market calls, and subsonic load pulses. Its immune system is drones, scavengers, patch-films, and technicians on magnet boots. Its dreams are forecasts. Its memories are layers: tool mark, mineral crust, repair seam, repeat.

And inside that being live millions of smaller intelligences—human and otherwise—none fully in control, all participating.

So: whoever lives there?

**Adapters. Interpreters. Symbionts.**

Not settlers standing apart from environment, but citizens of a wet mechanical ecology where “nature” and “infrastructure” have mated so long they can no longer be cleanly separated.
