What lives there is **a civic weather-organism**—not a creature in the usual sense, but a resident intelligence distributed through stone, root, water, and air.

At first you might call it “nothing,” because there is no population in the human way. But the longer you stay, the clearer it becomes that the inhabitant is the **system itself**: an ancient alpine metabolism that has learned architecture.

It lives as:

- **Wind in the chimneys** (its voice),
- **drip networks and frost veins** (its circulation),
- **moss-root-mineral welds** (its tissue),
- **hollows, alleys, and shafts** (its lungs and ears),
- **recurring runoff geometry** (its memory).

The goats, kites, salamanders, fox, beetles, moths—these are visitors, grazers, brief tenants. Even the mosses and sedges feel like neighborhoods within a larger body. The only permanent citizen is process: katabatic descent, seep, freeze-thaw, abrasion, regrowth. Weather doesn’t pass through this place; it is **domiciled** there.

If you wanted to picture it as a single being, it would be something like an old, patient municipal animal:  
a many-chambered body that breathes by pressure gradient, speaks in resonance, drinks in pulses, and thinks in seasons. It has no face, only districts. No heartbeat, only periodic thaw. No bones, because the bones are already mountains worn blunt enough to last.

Its temperament is strict, even lawful:

- It rewards contact zones with fertility.
- It punishes edges that try to stay sharp.
- It keeps old channels like habits.
- It uses emptiness as infrastructure.
- It converts noise into orientation.

So whoever lives there is not absent at all.  
It is an **autonomous ruin-that-never-ruined**, a city-shaped climate engine, an inhabitant made of continuity rather than flesh.

In short: **the resident is weather, made local enough to count as a creature.**
