What lives here is a **civic species**.

Not one creature, exactly—not at first. You go looking for “the inhabitants” and find people, yes: mill workers with pruning hooks on their belts; surveyors who carry moisture probes the way other towns carry clipboards; children who can identify support roots by bark texture; mechanics who grease bearings with one hand and tie back a tendril with the other. But the longer you look, the more “resident” stops meaning “human only.”

The true inhabitant is a **cooperative organism made of citizens, machinery, and trained vegetation**—a town that has crossed the line into being a managed body.

It lives by circulation:
- water caught in brief rains and night condensation,
- sugars and fibers grown into beams, cordage, pulp, and fuel,
- labor moving through shifts like blood through chambers,
- maintenance laws functioning like immune responses.

Its bones are old masonry and slag roadbeds.  
Its muscles are belt presses, flywheels, and trellised load paths.  
Its skin is cambium over brick, moss over mortar, leaf over steel.  
Its nerves are quiet electrical runs and seasonal maps revised as growth shifts the plan.  
Its breath is the cool damp exhale of shade, cistern, fermentation heat, and valve hiss.

The people who live there are less “settlers” than **symbionts with tenure**. They don’t dominate the plant mass and they aren’t swallowed by it either; they negotiate with it daily. They schedule around flowering. They treat root systems as protected utilities. They respond to irrigation faults like cardiac alarms. They build with forecasts measured not only in load and cost, but in **centimeters per year of future growth**.

Even the wildlife that lives there fits this pattern: tolerated where useful, redirected where harmful, never fully excluded. Pollinators are welcomed, borers opposed, amphibians accepted as weather punctuation, birds folded into maintenance calendars. A fox passing through is remarkable precisely because most life here is either integrated or passing.

So what lives there?

A **small industrial polis whose primary citizen is metabolism itself**—  
a place where governance includes botany, infrastructure includes horticulture, and survival depends on treating growth not as background scenery but as law, labor, architecture, and time.
