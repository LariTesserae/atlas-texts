What lives there is a people shaped by pressure, repetition, and listening.

Not “cave-dwellers” in the crude sense—nothing so generic. They are an infrastructural species of human: compact in movement, deliberate in gesture, tuned to gradients most surface folk never notice. You could pick them out first by how they carry themselves: shoulders naturally angled inward to pass without scraping, knees soft for slick stone, one hand often trailing wall texture as casually as another person might trail fingers through grass. They do not stride; they place.

Their bodies have learned the city’s scale. They are not necessarily short, but they are folded-efficient. Tendons strong in the forearms and back from ladders, chain pulls, lifted jars, braced hauling on wet inclines. Hands broad-palmed, scar-lined, exceptionally sensitive—able to read a rope’s condition, a crack’s new edge, a slight change in condensation by touch alone. Their skin tends pale or mineral-muted in the lampglow, marked by a permanent sheen from humidity and work. Hair is practical: braided close, wrapped, pinned with copper or bone to stay clear of gears, flames, and dripping lime.

Their eyes are not mystical night-eyes; they are trained eyes. They work in low contrast, in amber half-light and blue haze, noticing sheen differences, shadow depth, the gleam of water film that means “slick” versus “safe.” But hearing is their finer instrument. They grow up inside percussion: drips, clacks, wheel-thuds, echo returns, chain-song. So they become diagnosticians of sound. A changed tempo in a waterwheel means a blockage two corridors away. A slightly duller hammer-strike means tool head fatigue. A cistern note gone flat means the level dropped below expected threshold. They do not “check systems” first by looking. They listen, then confirm.

Culturally, they are custodial rather than expansionist. Their hero is not the conqueror or inventor but the keeper who prevents failure for thirty years. Prestige accrues around reliability: lamp routes never missed, sluice timings never drifted, kiln atmospheres held precise through hundreds of firings. Memory is distributed through lineages, yes, but also through architecture itself—markings, grooves, knot codes, hanging tool orders. Knowledge is less written than embedded, and children are taught to read use-wear as text.

They likely speak softly, not from timidity but acoustics. In a place where whispers can travel and shouts die, vocal etiquette becomes technical. There are probably corridor-calls pitched to carry along specific vents, work chants matched to hammer cycles, and taboo frequencies near resonant fault-cracks. Silence is not emptiness there; it is diagnostic bandwidth.

Their religion—if called that—would be less about gods of sky and more about covenants with behavior: Water must move, flame must breathe, stone must be respected where it wants to shear. They may personify these forces, but practically. Offerings are maintenance acts. Ritual days are synchronized desilting, chain-oiling, condenser-scraping, eel-run feasts timed to pressure shifts. Ancestors are remembered by the systems they stabilized: “She who corrected the east runnel after the long seep,” “He of the third gate whose hand pattern we still use.”

They live with scarcity, but not panic. Their city has taught them predictable limits, so their temperament is neither lush nor desperate—more like disciplined sufficiency. Waste is social error. Improvisation exists, but only after one has mastered precedent. Novelty is tested against drainage, airflow, and repair burden before acceptance. Their imagination is not absent; it is load-bearing.

And other things live there too, in attendance to them and in spite of them:

- The pale eels, seasonal and prized, a pulse of animal otherness in a controlled world.  
- The shell-backed scavengers, tiny sanitation guilds with no name outside children’s games and old proverbs.  
- The colorless fungal veils, half-material, half-warning, mapping damp and neglect.  
- The microbial cultures in blue bowls—arguably domesticated light itself—tended like livestock and library both.  
- The occasional bat, treated as envoy, omen, and proof that the map is always incomplete.

If you stayed long enough, you’d realize the primary resident is not any one organism but a relationship: human metabolism braided with hydrology and stone-time. The people endure because they ceased trying to dominate the underground and instead apprenticed themselves to its stubborn physics.

So: who lives there?

A civilization of keepers in the narrow dark, expert in wet mechanics, socially organized around maintenance, acoustically literate, emotionally calibrated to continuity.  
And with them, a sparse retinue of blind, shell, spore, and wing—small lives orbiting a larger one that has made endurance into identity.
