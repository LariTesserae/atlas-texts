What lives there is not one creature but a **guild**—a society without language, made of species and waterworks together.

If I had to name its “resident,” I’d call it **the Keepers of the Drip**: animals that have, generation by generation, become the maintenance staff of a forgotten hydraulic temple.

At first you notice individuals: the blind thread-eels tasting stone seams, the drain-toads booming like struck jars, the ledge-fowl prying shells loose, the stitched-wing gliders stitching dusk with quick arcs. But after a while they stop feeling separate. They behave like parts of one breathing organism spread across channels, hollows, grates, and lampsmoke.

Their true adaptation isn’t claw or beak—it’s **timing**.  
They live by surge-cycles.  
Before each hydraulic pulse, they shift levels with eerie confidence: amphibians climb half a step, eels retreat to side rills, swallows go quiet in their flask nests, gliders cluster high where warm air holds. Afterward, they fan back out and resume the endless work: hunting, nesting, scraping, fouling, clearing, feeding the films that feed the grazers that polish the lips that keep water moving.

So what “lives there,” most fundamentally, is:

- **A feral watershed intelligence** made of small bodies and old stone logic.  
- **An inherited choreography** keyed to drip-rate, echo, and rise-line.  
- **A domestication turned inside out**—not humans tending animals, but animals tending human design until design and habitat are inseparable.

If a single emblematic inhabitant exists, it might be the **brow-backed drain warden** (one of those thick amphibians): scarred, loud, immovably territorial at a gate-throat where flow can choke. It eats what clogs, drives off what burrows wrong, breeds in pulse pools, and calls before surges as if announcing shift change. Ugly, ancient, indispensable.

But even that creature is only a node in the larger resident:

**the basin itself, alive as process.**  
Not pristine wilderness. Not dead infrastructure.  
A wet, echoing commons where architecture became ecology, and ecology became memory.
