What lives there is a **maintenance people**—a culture of patient custodians, half engineers and half gardeners, who would reject both labels as too narrow.

Not pioneers, not scavengers, not mystics of ruin. They’re inheritors of a metabolism.

They grow up learning to read condensation the way others read weather. A child there can tell you, by touch, whether a handrail is sweating from ambient humidity or from a hot line bleeding heat behind it. They learn the district as a sequence of sounds before they fully map it visually: the three-note whine before pump cycling, the healthy lag in fan reversal, the wrong kind of silence that means drop everything and go. Quiet is alarming to them. Steady noise is comfort.

Physically, they move like people accustomed to narrow footing and perpetual damp—deliberate steps, low center of gravity, one hand often brushing wall or rail to feel vibration. Clothes are layered but breathable, patched with waxed fiber and ceramic toggles, never precious. Tools are compact, tethered, rust-managed. Knives exist, but mostly for trimming and splitting, not fighting. Color tags—on sleeves, belts, vine braids, route maps—matter immensely; color is language in a place where cutting the wrong green thing can black out a block or collapse a stair.

Their social order is probably built less on abstract rank and more on **stewardship zones** and cycle responsibility. You don’t “own” a home so much as keep a segment of flow, root, load, and access in balance. Prestige goes to whoever prevents cascading failure with the least drama. The admired person is the one who notices the tiny change six hours early: turbidity half a shade off, fungal bed breathing hotter than normal, mineral lace thickening where it shouldn’t.

They likely have distinct callings, all interdependent:

- **Flowkeepers** clearing channels, grates, siphons, and cistern throats.
- **Rootwrights** training load-bearing growth and reading plant stress as structural data.
- **Heat stewards** routing thermal surplus into useful sinks.
- **Filter tenders** who manage fungal and microbial beds with near-ritual attentiveness.
- **Sounders** (formal or informal) who diagnose by ear across pump halls and duct galleries.
- **Map keepers** constantly updating what walls now contain, because walls are never just walls.

Their timekeeping is infrastructural, not celestial. They might still know morning and night from shaft-light color, but daily language is more like: *before floodgate hour*, *after settling*, *during second fan turn*. Festivals, if any, would coincide with system transitions: first major rain, successful desilt of a major trunk line, ten-year thickening of a living arch, reopening of a corridor surrendered to roots and reclaimed one level up.

Emotionally, they’re probably not romantic about greenery. They do not worship plants; they negotiate with them. Pruning is as important as planting. Beauty exists, but as a byproduct of balance. A straight reed stand in the civic basin means oxygenation is right. Clear overflow means upstream discipline held. Their aesthetics are diagnostic and practical: patina is honorable, polish is suspect, and anything too clean is probably unused—or uninspected.

Language there is likely full of biological-industrial metaphors because no one can separate the two. A stalled committee “went anaerobic.” A neighborhood conflict “needs aeration.” A person recovering from burnout is “on low-flow duty.” Promises are measured like hydrology: *steady*, *metered*, *no sudden drawdown*.

And what else lives there, besides people?  
Not much charismatic fauna, by your terms—but an immense unseen nation of **fungi, bacteria, and rooted systems** that are treated almost like colleagues. The residents understand that the district survives because countless nonhuman workers digest sludge, lock slopes, wick moisture, and buffer heat. Respect for life here is infrastructural, not sentimental.

So the truest answer is:

A **civic organism** lives there.  
Its human cells are careful, unhurried, technically literate, and collectively minded because haste is damage.  
Its nonhuman cells are trained ecologies—root, moss, reed, microbe—kept in dynamic truce.  
Together they form a people whose defining trait is this: they know maintenance is not the opposite of life; it is life, extended.
