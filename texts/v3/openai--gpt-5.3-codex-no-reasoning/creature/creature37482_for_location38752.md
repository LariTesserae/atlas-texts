What lives there is not one creature but a **continuum disguised as a fauna**—a civilization of bodies that never needed cities.

If I had to name the dominant resident of that basin, I’d call it the **Kiln Choir**: a layered super-community of mineral-bodied animals whose real anatomy is shared—routes, heat maps, vent schedules, shellfall drifts, ambush shadows, regrowth films. Individual organisms matter, but the thing that truly “lives there” is the basin-scale metabolism they compose.

Still, out of that collective, certain forms resolve:

### The Stone-that-Moves (baseline lifeform)
Most residents begin from the same design logic: low profile, moisture-sealed, thermally literate.  
They are squat, many-legged or belly-gliding, armored in silicate plates that look fired rather than grown. Their skins are matte black to soot-gray, crossed by metallic capillary tracery that brightens when dumping heat. In stillness they are indistinguishable from rubble; in motion they move in purposeful, tide-like pulses.

They do not waste water on loud metabolism. Respiration is slow, often periodic, synced to cooler breaths of air from fissures. Many “eat” by scraping chemotrophic films off hot stone, then retreating to thermal edges where enzymes work fastest. Their dung is pelletized mineral concentrate—one source of those dry runnels that function like rivers.

### The Runnel Nations (mass migrants)
Where granular flows collect, there are migrators by the thousand: bead-shelled drifters, articulated comb-feeders, and synchronized burst-runners.  
They travel in timed surges, crossing kill zones almost like weather fronts. Their sound—fine rattling, shell-on-shell—creates the basin’s soft percussive texture. Alone, one is trivial. Together, they are landscape force, polishing ramps, deepening trails, feeding everything above them in the chain.

### Chokepoint Minds (predators)
Predators here are less chasers than **geometers**.  
They position at runnel merges, under basalt lips, beside ancient carved channels now repurposed as funnels. Their senses privilege vibration, static gradients, and volatile chemistry over sight. Eyes, if present, are mirrored pits useful mostly for flash events (sheet lightning, vent flare).  
Many can hold absolute stillness for hours, presenting as fractured stone until the basin itself delivers prey to their strike radius.

### Vent Drinkers (atmospheric feeders)
At fumaroles stand the strange devotees: tall-ish, narrow-mouthed scavengers that feed on dense exhaled vapors. They brace against hot gusts, mouths open, filtering reactive compounds and aerosolized organics.  
They look almost ritualistic, but it is purely metabolic opportunism—eating weather, as you said. Around them cluster parasites that lick condensate from their throat seals and jaw hinges.

### Crystal Herders
Some species do not graze directly; they **garden** the translucent geometric mats that bloom overnight on warm mineral faces.  
These herders defend growth patches, prune fans before they shatter, and cache shards in cooler crevices where competitors are fewer. Their “agriculture” is simple but real: seeding fracture lines with scrap, clearing abrasive dust, patrolling boundaries defined entirely by temperature contour.

### Ridge Gliders
The only regular vertical traffic: membrane-winged, abrasion-tolerant gliders that stay low because the upper air is thin and cruel.  
They launch from temporary safety ledges, skim thermals, and drop on exposed prey or carrion leaks. Fragile-looking, but their membranes are likely laminated with mica-like scales, and their mouths are built for puncture-and-drink—because any fluid event is brief and violently contested.

And then there is the oldest resident:

## The Basin Itself (as organism)
In your place, terrain and life are reciprocal organs.  
Heat gradients are circulatory boundaries. Runnels are digestive tracts. Vent booms are heartbeat-like resets that pause all motion. Built ruins have been metabolized into behavior: ring-stones as brood markers, fused walls as thermal baffles, collapsed lintels as den mouths.

So what lives there?

A dark, patient biota that solved aridity by abandoning softness.  
A society of shells, plates, and heat math.  
Creatures that treat water as catastrophe-level bounty and stillness as speech.  
An ecosystem so saturated that emptiness has been engineered out of it.

If you stood there long enough, you’d stop asking “what animal is that?” and start asking “what phase of the basin am I watching?” because each body is a sentence, but the resident is the whole language.
