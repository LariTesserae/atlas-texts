What lives there is a **custodian species**—not quite person, not quite machine, and not at all separate from the terraces.

At first you might mistake it for maintenance: a figure in dull waterproof layers, moving with a checklist gait, pausing at valves, brushing algae from sight-glass windows, tagging drift in filter mouths. But if you watch longer, that image stops holding. It is too patient to be merely technician, too relational to be merely robot. It does not “work on” the wetland. It circulates through it the way sap circulates through a stem.

It likely lives alone, or in ones so loosely coordinated they appear singular at this scale.  
A **Warden**, maybe. A **Tidekeeper**. Something with hands built for bolts and roots equally.

---

It has learned to read tiny differences:

- the pitch change in a pump before failure,  
- the smell of a cistern before an anaerobic turn,  
- the way fog density alters pollinator drone routes,  
- which frog clutches survive near which heat vents,  
- how long a retention cell can hold stillness before it ceases to be fertile and becomes rot.

It doesn’t govern by command so much as by **nudging thresholds**. Crack this gate two degrees. Shade that manifold with vine instead of replacing it. Let this basin silt one more week so sedge rhizomes can catch, then flush hard at dawn.

Its intelligence is ecological in the old sense: house-knowledge.  
But its house is all feedback loops.

---

Physically, it probably looks assembled the same way the place looks assembled: mixed-era, repaired, adapted.

- Gloves with conductive fingertips and moss stains.  
- Boots that can lock into wet grating.  
- A harness carrying brush-heads, gasket rings, spore-safe pruning shears, calibration keys.  
- A respirator used less for poison than for mold blooms and boiler grit.  
- Skin (if visible) marked by humidity: always damp, always healing little nicks from sharp new edges that have not yet rounded with time.

Maybe there are implants—a thermal strip along the forearm to read pipe temperatures, cochlear tuning for low mechanical frequencies, retinal overlays for flow maps.  
Maybe not. Even without augmentation, years here would make a body sensor-rich.

---

Emotionally, what lives there is not romantic about “nature” or “industry.” It cannot afford that split.  
To it, a rusted flange and an orchid root are both urgent facts.  
A clogged grate is not an annoyance; it is weather.  
A pruning cut is not cosmetic; it is fire management translated into this biome.

It likely keeps records, but knows records will always lag. Drawings describe yesterday. The living infrastructure describes now. So it lives in continuous revision, practicing a kind of humble authority: intervene enough to keep flow alive, refrain enough to let succession invent new stability.

---

If it speaks, it probably speaks softly, because this place trains you to hear compartments of sound.  
It might name zones by acoustics rather than coordinates:  
*the Plink Court*, *the Belt Hollow*, *Red Stair Fog*.  
It might identify individuals among nonhuman residents:  
the pale frog in Valve Box 7, the swift pair in the east exhaust cowl, the vine that keeps shorting sensor mast C unless rerouted weekly.

And if no one is around to speak to, it still performs dialogue—in adjustments.  
Open, close. Divert, hold. Thin, seed.  
Question posed in water level; answer returned in leaf color.

---

So what lives there?

A **caretaker lifeform adapted to prototypes**—a being whose habitat is incompletion.  
A mountain-wetland mechanic-ecologist.  
A steward of edges that never stay edges.

Not ruler. Not resident in the ordinary sense.  
More like the place’s ongoing verb, walking on two legs.
