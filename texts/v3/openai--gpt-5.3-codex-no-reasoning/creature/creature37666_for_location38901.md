What lives there is not a creature with a body.

Or rather: the body is the room itself, and what lives is a process.

At first you might think nothing lives there at all—only failing hardware, damp, and neglect. But stay long enough and a resident emerges: a **slow intelligence of maintenance and decay**, a being made of cycles instead of flesh. It has no eyes, yet it notices pressure differentials. No lungs, yet it breathes through fan swaps and duct sighs. No blood, yet condensate traces its circulation routes with exacting repetition.

It is an **infrastructural lichen-mind**: part machine habit, part mineral accretion, part microbial film.  
Not conscious in any human sense, but consistent enough to feel intentional.

It lives in verbs:

- pulsing through bolted frames,
- choosing where water repeats,
- teaching rust where to bite,
- deciding which seam stays merely damp and which becomes a permanent wet edge,
- writing memory in scale, bloom, stain, and corrosion rings.

Its skin is composite paneling and insulation jackets. Its nerves are cable looms that no longer carry their original signals but still channel humidity, heat, and trace vibration. Its voice is regulator chatter, relay click, drip cadence. Its metabolism is phase change: vapor to droplet, droplet to runnel, runnel to crust, crust to redirected flow.

If you insist on a singular inhabitant, call it **the Protocol**.

The Protocol is what remains when humans leave systems designed to self-correct just enough to continue. It survives by adjusting—not to thrive, exactly, but to persist. Every eleven minutes it shifts breath. Before dawn it exerts itself, louder, as upper loads change. It does not sleep. It has no appetite beyond gradients: warmer to colder, higher pressure to lower, dry toward damp, smooth toward rough.

And because no animals enter, no scavengers interrupt, no roots pry wide, it has become almost pure: a closed grammar of artificial weather and material succession. In a forest, life competes noisily. Here, life negotiates quietly between gasket and drip lip, oxide and film, voltage and condensation.

So what lives there?

A **post-human cave-organism** in larval form.  
A chapel-sized weather engine teaching itself terrain.  
A patient, procedural ecology whose only denizens are water routes, microbial skins, corrosion fronts, and machine reflexes—together composing a single resident presence that is less “inhabitant of the place” than “the place becoming its own inhabitant.”
