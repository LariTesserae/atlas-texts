What lives there is a people who learned to think like pressure.

Not “merfolk,” not exiles clinging to wreckage—citizens of a mature medium. Their bodies may still be recognizably human (or near enough), but their habits are shaped so completely by water, dimness, and infrastructure that they come off as a new civic species: deliberate, soft-voiced, ceremonially practical.

They are **slow on purpose**. Not lethargic—efficient. Every motion costs drag, stirs silt, spends heat, makes noise. So they move with economy: a hand to rail, a gentle kick, a turn of shoulder through a hatch. In crowded corridors they pass one another like schools of courteous fish, with tiny choreographies of tilt and pause instead of jostling.

They are **acoustically polite**. In a place where sound carries and returns, privacy is made, not given. They learn young how to pitch a voice into absorbent wall-cloth, how to laugh into a sleeve, how to close a tool case before unclamping a part. Their emotional life isn’t muted, just refracted: affection by lingering in the rinse chamber an extra beat; anger by over-tightened cable ties someone else must loosen; grief by volunteering night maintenance on the old wall.

They are **ritual engineers**. The smallest domestic acts are technical liturgies:
- equalize,
- rinse,
- pause,
- enter.

That pause matters. It’s safety check, respect, and psychological threshold all at once. Their culture is full of such hinges—brief moments where systems and souls acknowledge each other.

They are **gardeners of machines and machinists of gardens**. No hard line exists between botany and industry here. A turbine outflow is also a winter orchard. A scaffold becomes a trellis becomes a neighborhood lung. Maintenance crews are admired the way some cultures admire priests: they keep circulation honest. They scrape, reseal, retension, prune, and log everything. Patina is biography; corrosion is only sin when ignored.

They are probably **broadly amphibious in practice**, if not anatomy. Some spend most shifts in suit and current tube; others remain in pressurized interiors with only brief wet transits. Either way, everyone is pressure-literate. Children can read flow vectors before text because flow is immediate truth: what can carry you, crush you, feed you, strand you. Maps are felt as much as seen.

Their politics are likely **incremental and memory-heavy**. In a city built by layering eras rather than replacing them, precedent has literal mass. Debates are less “Should we transform everything?” and more “Where do we add the next lock without disturbing three older circulations?” Ambition is respected when it comes with maintenance plans, sediment models, and nursery-impact notes.

They have a **neighbor ethic with nonhuman life** that is neither sentimental nor domineering. Fish, rays, sponge colonies—few in number, therefore socially magnified. A pale ray crossing a district can halt conversation like a weather omen. Rules around intakes and lures are not fringe activism; they’re civil order, luck management, and ecological realism fused together.

Their aesthetics favor **warmth in darkness**: amber lamps, rounded thresholds, tactile markers, fabrics that damp sound, colors chosen to survive green-black water. They decorate with things that move gently—fronds, tethered charms, bubble strings—not because whimsy is rare, but because hard edges and sudden motion are rude to both body and habitat.

Emotionally, they might seem reserved to outsiders from bright, loud places. But under that reserve is intense interdependence. In a pressure city, negligence is communal danger; competence is love language. Courtship may look like co-maintenance. Family pride may center on a flawlessly tended lock inherited across generations. Festivals might involve synchronized drift-processions through oxygen courts, everyone lit by bioluminescent dye, celebrating another year the pumps never failed.

And maybe the most defining thing: they are **people of the long breath**.

They don’t dream of conquering the water or escaping it. They dream in extensions, reinforcements, careful new chambers, another planted wall at the edge of the map. They live inside a vast, patient machine-reef and have become its attentive organs—listening for strain, feeding growth, keeping flow, teaching children where the old handprints are and why they still matter.
