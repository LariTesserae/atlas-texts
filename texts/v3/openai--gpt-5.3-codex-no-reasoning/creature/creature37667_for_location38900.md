What lives there is not an animal first.

It is a **hydrology with a body**—a thing made of seepage, pressure, skin, fracture, and repetition. If this place has an inhabitant in the singular, it is a distributed one: a colony of wet processes wearing stone instead of flesh.

If you insist on giving it a shape, it would be low to the ground, sprawling, never fully bounded. Not a creature that crosses the land, but one that **is the crossing**: water moving through capillary seams, pooling, retreating, returning on pulses that look almost intentional. Its “organs” are the basins that fill and empty, the vents that exhale, the weeping faces of rock that cycle from matte to sweat to runnel and back to sealed dullness. Its nervous system is acoustic—ticks, hisses, fluted wind-tones, subsurface gulps—constant self-reporting through sound.

The old structures suggest something else once tried to live here in the way we recognize: builders, keepers of flame, planners of alignment. But if they are homes now, they are homes to moisture and mineral accretion. Hollows meant to hold fire now hold rain, then crust, then silence, then rain again. The country has adopted them, not as ruins to preserve, but as additional surfaces to infiltrate.

Visible life does exist, but like marginal notes in a manuscript whose main text is geologic:

- translucent pool-things that suspend metabolism into pellets when water fails,
- filament swimmers threading chemically rich films,
- tiny articulated scavengers inhabiting condensation margins.

These are not dominant fauna; they are opportunists living in the wake of the real resident. They do not shape the place. They survive its cycles.

So whoever lives there is best described as a **young lithic wetland intelligence without mind**: a pre-ecological tenant state where water is everywhere but never deep, where ground has no memory because nothing roots it, where every form is provisional. It is a land still rehearsing how to be land. Marsh, quarry, ruin-field—those are names from older worlds with clearer categories. Here the inhabitant is transition itself: patient, unspectacular, absolute.

If you stood still long enough, you might feel watched—not by eyes, but by timing. By intervals. By the certainty that every dry patch is temporary, every edge already scheduled for collapse, every silence only a rearrangement of noise. In that sense, what lives there is a **slow, wet verb**. Not “beast,” not “people,” not even “ecosystem” yet.

Just: **becoming**.
