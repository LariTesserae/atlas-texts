What lives there is not one creature but a **civic species**—a people who have become inseparable from an engineered ecosystem of fur, feather, scale, rope, clay, and routine.

If I had to name the primary inhabitant, I’d call them something like **the Basin Keepers**: compact, heat-tolerant, practical, and relentlessly observant. They are less “rulers” of the place than its conductors. They don’t worship wilderness; they curate function. They can hear a blocked runnel in the sound of water, smell a sick pen before dawn, and tell time by where shade touches a specific rail.

They live in tiers, like everything else there.  
Upper ledges for drier storage and lighter sleepers.  
Mid galleries for trade, repair, and nesting families.  
Lower rings for handling water, waste, slaughter, and storm-risk work.  
No one is far from animals; no one is far from tools.

Their bodies likely show the basin’s imprint: scarred shins from low beams, callused palms, soot in nailbeds, rope burns, resin-stiff sleeves, a permanent squint from vertical light. They move with the confidence of people who can navigate by touch in smoke or rain-dark. Children learn balance before literacy; elders carry the map of flood years in memory and in knee pain.

Socially, they’re probably organized by **flow-rights** rather than simple bloodlines: households tied to channels, pens, cistern access, and maintenance obligations. Kinship may matter, but water duty matters more. A person is known by what they keep alive: bees, hatch bars, drain snakes, fodder pulleys, lamp rounds. Prestige comes from reliability under pressure—storm nights, birthing runs, infestations.

Their temperament is noisy, negotiated, and unsentimental. They argue loudly because everything is immediate and shared; they reconcile quickly because systems can’t pause for grudges. They are intimate with death but not casual about waste. They don’t romanticize animals, yet they read them constantly: ear angle, throat click, feather-set, tail drag—all information.

And alongside them live the true co-residents:

- clipped-ear grazers that convert scraps to milk and hide,  
- roost-fowl bred as much for predictable laying as for alarm behavior,  
- wall-scavengers that are sanitation infrastructure with heartbeats,  
- lizards and snakes as pest control,  
- tunnel cats as tolerated thieves and occasional omens,  
- bees as sweetness, wax, and pollination labor,  
- explosive seasonal amphibians that function like a living calendar.

So what lives there?

A **managed wildness**.  
A **multispecies machine** held together by memory, habit, and constant repair.  
And at its center, a people who are neither gentle pastoralists nor harsh masters, but expert neighbors to necessity—keepers of a sunken world that stays alive only because they never stop listening.
