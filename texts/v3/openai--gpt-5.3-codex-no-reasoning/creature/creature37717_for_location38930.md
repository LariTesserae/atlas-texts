What lives there is not a single beast, and not even a single ecosystem.  
What lives there is **a metabolism**—a continent-sized, half-finished body made of stone, heat, air, and hunger.

If you insisted on naming its “primary inhabitant,” you might call it the **Root-Sea**: that vast, woven, load-bearing plant skin that is also soil, road, reef, shelter, and trap. It behaves less like vegetation and more like infrastructure with instincts. It grows in tensions and knots. It stores weather inside itself. It remembers pressure. It swallows ruins and excretes terrain.

And inside that Root-Sea lives everything else, each life-form adapted not to stability but to **constant mechanical change**:

- **Grazers** that shave armored plant tissues with rotary mouths, moving in dense fronts like living sanders.
- **Ceiling-climbers** that treat hanging vine-webs as open plains, their needle beaks probing gas sacs, larvae pockets, and sap veins.
- **Submat churners**—blind, muscular burrow fauna—that travel as moving humps beneath the surface, aerating, destabilizing, and remaking entire districts.
- **Membrane kites** and other updraft-riders that feed, mate, and hunt in thermal columns rather than on land.
- **Flash-loud predators** that evolved away from stealth: warning bands, piercing whistles, impact attacks—because secrecy is pointless in a world that never stops screaming.
- **Arthropod nations** in numbers beyond counting, turning every seam into a factory of chirr, bite, molt, and brood.

Even microbes here feel megafaunal in effect: ferments, molds, and sap-feeding films can trigger chain reactions that reshape kilometers overnight.

And then there are the people—small in number, huge in perception. They live there like careful parasites or respectful technicians, not conquerors. Their villages are temporary negotiations with load paths and vent cycles. Their agriculture is behavioral engineering. Their religion is hazard memory. Their maps are oral because the ground itself won’t hold still long enough for ink.

So who lives there?

A better answer is:  
**A newborn world lives there**—one still choosing its species, still testing architectures, still deciding what “land” means.  
The humans are only one voice in it. The animals are many voices in it.  
But the loudest resident, the oldest child in the basin, is the living mat itself: patient, ravenous, and never finished.
