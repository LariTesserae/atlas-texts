What lives there is a **people of maintenance**.

Not “swamp folk” in the flattening outsider sense, and not quite “townsfolk” either—more like a **canopy-marsh commons** of engineers, growers, foragers, carpenters, herders, and listeners, all at once. They are the kind of humans shaped by wet gravity: sure-footed on flexing walkways, quick to read water level by sound, able to tie knots one-handed in rain, able to tell from a frog chorus or dragonfly hatch what the next day’s drainage trouble will be.

They live in that bowl because they understand a truth many places can ignore:  
**stability is not stillness; it is constant adjustment.**

So whoever lives here is probably:

- **Physically adaptable**: strong calves, balanced posture, broad feet or soft-soled footwear for slick surfaces; hands callused from rope, reed, and wet wood.
- **Collectively trained** from childhood: kids learn warning knocks before they learn trade arithmetic; they learn which rails are food, which vines are structure, which channels must never be blocked.
- **Poly-skilled rather than specialized**: even a healer knows basic retaining-wall patching; even a carpenter knows compost heat timing; even children know at least one flood-route and one dry-route.
- **Acoustically literate**: they interpret their environment through noise—drip rate, pestle cadence, signal knocks, bird alarms, monkey mischief, rising runoff.
- **Ecological pragmatists**: they do not romanticize nature, but they collaborate with it. Frogs are not background—they are mosquito control. Nesting niches are policy, not sentiment.
- **Socially interdependent**: privacy exists, but autonomy is bounded by hydraulics. If your gutter clogs, three households flood. If your tether fails, everyone’s stores are raided by night.

They likely smell of damp fiber, smoke-resin, crushed herbs, and iron-rich water. Their tools are modest, their timing exquisite. Their architecture grows and decays on purpose. Their arguments are loud, brief, and procedural: whose turn to clear which channel, which tree can be pollarded, where to reroute flow after a wall slump.

The nonhuman residents are not decoration—they are co-citizens, rivals, labor, and pressure:

- Frogs and birds as pest management
- Eels and fish in household-adjacent protein systems
- Monkeys as thieves that shape storage tech
- Insects as both pollinators and nightly siege
- Structural trees as literal civic infrastructure

If you ask, “Who lives there?” the deepest answer is:

A community that has become **amphibious without ceasing to be human**—a culture whose identity is braided from watercraft, horticulture, repair discipline, and shared signal language. They are not conquering the marsh. They are not surrendering to it. They are in ongoing negotiation, day after day, season after season, until the boundary between settlement and ecosystem is less a line than a pulse.
