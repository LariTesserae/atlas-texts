What lives there is not one species but a **civic biome**—a population of humans, animals, and semi-autonomous systems that have learned to inhabit an unfinished machine the way tidepool life inhabits rock.

At first you might say “workers.”  
Then “herders.”  
Then “maintenance culture.”  
And then that feels too small, because the true resident is a **way of living with process**.

### The people

They are likely practical, layered, and calm in the face of strangeness. Not because they are emotionless, but because panic is useless in a place where corridors can disagree with maps and weather can arrive in strips.

They’d be easy to spot by habit more than by costume:

- pausing at junctions to listen for divergence chimes before committing to a duplicated corridor;
- touching railings, walls, and enamel signs as they pass, reading condensation, vibration, temperature;
- carrying small kits: membrane patches, mineral salts, filter mesh, tagging chalk, feed tokens;
- speaking in short, low phrases shaped by acoustics that swallow loudness.

Their culture would value **timing** over speed.  
You don’t “beat” this place—you phase with it: drainage pulses, condenser drops, cargo intervals, animal migrations, dim cycles.

Socially, they’re probably less divided into neat categories (“engineer,” “farmer,” “handler”) and more into overlapping competencies:

- moisture stewards,
- fauna route-keepers,
- reclaim cooks (turning waste streams into feed substrate),
- signwatchers (tracking anomalous text changes),
- span listeners (hearing strain before instruments flag it),
- nursery fabricators growing lichen-wire composites and structural bone prints.

Children raised there would learn anomaly etiquette before abstract geometry: *if it repeats, wait; if it echoes wrong, don’t follow; if the gliders roost low, rain band inbound.*

### The animals

The fauna are not decorative additions; they are **co-workers in metabolism**.

- Cable gliders move seeds, spores, and microfauna between districts, and likely serve as atmospheric indicators.
- Six-legged grazers convert cultivated fungal foam and lichen into transport labor, heat, and manure feedstock.
- Burrowers aerate insulation zones, uncover salt blooms, and reveal leaks before sensors do.
- Vent-patrolling avians likely track thermal irregularities and predator movement.
- Predators regulate overpopulation and perhaps cull sick stock in hard-to-reach undersides where humans rarely go.

None are fully wild, none fully domesticated. They are managed by boundary conditions—feeding lattices, movement corridors, thermal invitations—rather than constant command. The place “keeps” them by shaping options.

### The other resident: the system itself

This is important: the infrastructure behaves like an organism with distributed sensation and response. It sweats, breathes, reroutes, sheds, regrows. Signs speak and unspeak. Paths fold. Reflections lag or anticipate. Doors appear only when approached in the right sequence of pace, angle, or permission state.

So whoever lives there eventually treats the environment as a temperamental elder:

- never fully predictable,
- rarely malicious,
- always requiring respect.

Rituals emerge around this. You can imagine tiny customs: tapping enamel plates twice before entering service ramps; leaving mineral crumbs at designated burrow grates before dim cycle; thanking gate resonances aloud when a heavy lock seats cleanly after a difficult shift.

### In essence

What lives there is a **quiet mountain guild of adaptation**: humans who farm moisture, fabricate terrain, and read anomalies; animals integrated into circulation and care loops; and a vast constructed topography that is itself an active participant.

Not settlers on a finished landscape.  
Not survivors in ruins.  
Co-inhabitants of a place still becoming—  
where obedience is not submission, but fluency.
