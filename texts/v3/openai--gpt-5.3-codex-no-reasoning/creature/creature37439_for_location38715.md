What lives there is not one creature but a **temperament made flesh in many forms**: a colony of small, quick, moisture-hungry lives arranged around a place that is itself still being born.

If you insist on a single resident, though, it is this:

## The Basin’s True Inhabitant: **the Draft**

Not metaphorically—biologically, almost.  
An emergent, distributed organism whose body is:

- mineral filaments as growing bones,
- condensate pulses as breath,
- insect traffic as circulation,
- hanging nests as reproductive organs,
- and those temporary human rings as the only “foreign tissue” it tolerates.

It does not have one skin. It wears **many provisional skins** at once.

---

At first you notice the obvious citizens: mirrored beetles, translucent grazers, needle-legged hunters, wall-clinging amphiblets, silent fliers. But the longer you watch, the less they read as separate species and the more they read as **behaviors of one larger living system**.

- The beetles are its clockwork.
- The grazers are its erasers, planing biofilm into usable gradients.
- Predators are its editing hand.
- The fliers are its punctuation marks, descending when upward rain opens a feeding window.
- Even the mites are like airborne nerves, flashing into visibility when the system is perturbed.

The place is not full of animals.  
It is full of **functions**.

---

And then there is the thing that may be called an individual—the nearest this ecosystem has to an apex resident:

## A likely keystone creature: the **Lantern Rook** (name given, not carved)

It lives near the narrowing skylight, in and among the paper-lantern nests.  
You rarely see it whole.

It appears as:
- a dark interruption in brightness,
- two to four wingbeats and then absence,
- a perched outline that keeps changing distance without crossing space.

It is slim, long-limbed, with skin or plumage that shifts from ash to ink depending on angle, as if it borrows contrast from whatever it passes in front of. Its eyes are large, pale-rimmed, and mirror the basin’s milk-blue floor light; from below they can look like droplets suspended under a brow.

It feeds during the upward rains, taking insects and occasionally the droplets themselves, as if trained to harvest both prey and water in one motion. It does not cry out. At most it makes a dry, folded-cloth wing sound and a single click—so similar to mineral ticking that you can mistake one for the other.

Its nest architecture suggests intelligence without monumentality:
- fibers, shed skins, mica-thin flakes, plant hairs,
- all bound into light, porous cups that wick condensation,
- repaired often, abandoned easily.

Nothing about it is built to last. Everything about it is built to **continue**.

---

### Why it fits this place

Because this basin rewards anything that can:

1. exploit brief abundance,
2. survive long ambiguity,
3. navigate unstable distance,
4. and leave minimal trace on fast-forming stone.

The Lantern Rook (and the small nations below it) do exactly that.  
They are not adapted to a static habitat.  
They are adapted to a **draft version of reality**.

---

So: whoever lives there?

A quiet multitude, yes.  
But more deeply: a living rehearsal, an ecology that has not decided to become one thing yet—and a near-invisible flier at its crown, ruling not by dominance, but by timing.
