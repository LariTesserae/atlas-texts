What lives there is not one creature so much as a **custodianship** made of many bodies.

If I had to name its primary resident, I’d call it a **marsh-keeper lineage**: semi-aquatic, patient, social at low density, and adapted to read both biology and relic technology as one continuous habitat. Not builders in the old, territorial sense—more like editors of flow, timing, and passage.

They are probably large enough to matter physically—waist- to chest-high on a human, long-bodied, low center of gravity, comfortable in mud, reed, and shallow channels. Imagine an animal with:

- broad, pressure-sensitive feet that spread on soft ground and can feel old stone under silt,  
- a neck and head posture suited to still observation above reeds,  
- dexterous forelimbs or mouthparts able to test filaments, tap weather nails, and handle delicate wet tech without breaking it,  
- skin/fur/scale surfaces that wick moisture and resist biofilm, maybe patterned to disappear in glare and reflected vegetation.

Not fast. Not loud. **Decisive in micro-movements.**

They live by routes, not houses. Their “settlement” is a repeating circuit of clearings, stone shelves, incubation warm-zones, and cool mist pockets. The built inserts you describe—flex-ceramic enclosures, dew-guiding lattices, marker stones that reveal symbols when wet—read as things they still use ritually and practically, even if they no longer fully understand original manufacture. They know what each artifact does in seasonal terms: where eggs survive, where fungus blooms, where prey concentrates, where not to stand when groundwater lifts.

So in that basin, the inhabitants are layered:

1. **The great grazers** (reed-parting, route-making, ecosystem-shaping by bulk alone).  
2. **The margin peoples** (amphibians, waders, burrowers, filter-feeders, luminous microfauna).  
3. **Silent predators** that specialize in interruption, not chase.  
4. **The old machines** as quasi-organisms: weather nails, memory-flow fields, self-repairing walls.  
5. And between all of them, this marsh-keeper intelligence—animal, post-animal, or hybrid tradition—maintaining permissions: who crosses where, when water is invited, what gets yielded to migrators.

Their culture (if we call it that) would be conservative in the ecological sense: never maximizing, always spacing. They leave emptiness between living clumps because emptiness is functional—light lanes, heat seams, predator visibility, flood breathing room. They favor intermittent intervention: a bridge that exists for minutes, a condensation stone activated for an hour, shade that lingers only long enough to matter.

They likely communicate in low-band modes suited to that restrained soundscape:

- short pulses, throat hums at dusk synchronization windows,  
- substrate taps through wet stone,  
- scent/salt traces renewed by condensation cycles,  
- maybe bioluminescent tagging at seep-lines at night.

Their young would learn the basin as a grammar of exceptions:
water usually goes downhill, except where memory says otherwise;
stone is dry, until asked;
shadow is absent, until it lingers;
barrier stands, until migrators vote against it with their bodies.

So: what lives there?

A **civilization of continuities**—animals, ecologies, and antique climate-tech fused into one marsh mind. No throne species, no pristine wilderness, no total machine control. Just inherited intelligence distributed across hoofpaths, reeds, algorithms, and wet stone, still negotiating the same old question the basin solved long ago:

not how to dominate a place,  
but how to keep it permeable enough that everything can keep living there together.
