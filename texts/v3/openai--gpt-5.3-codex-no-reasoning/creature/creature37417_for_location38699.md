What lives there is less a single species than a **civilization of small bodies**—an animal biome that has taken over every role plants would fill elsewhere.

If I had to name the dominant resident, I’d call it something like a **seam-crawler guild**: countless arthropod-like and wormlike lineages, highly specialized, all tuned to moisture gradients and stone acoustics. But the truest answer is: **the inhabitant is the swarm itself**.

Still, out of that moving fabric, a few “faces” emerge:

- **Film-grazers** in braided columns—segmented, low-profile, rasping biofilms, salt crust, and mineral exudates from rock. They are the herbivores of this world, except their pasture is chemistry on stone.
- **Knot-worm collectives** in wet cuts—blind, pale, soft-bodied masses that merge and split, processing detritus and microbial slurry like living intestines of the valley.
- **Plated lane-hunters**—dark, hard-shelled predators that patrol traffic margins, reading vibration signatures through the ground, striking where flow stutters.
- **Short-flight couriers/scavengers**—frantic wing-cased forms that relocate between temporary wet pockets, spreading symbionts and stripping fresh death before it dries.
- **Luminous symbiont bearers**—larger crawlers whose skins host bioluminescent films that flash when scraped, turning movement into brief signatures in the dark.
- **Thread-curtain colonies** in deep condensate zones—non-plant filamentous growths (likely microbial-animal consortia) that glow faint violet and feed a constant understory traffic.

The “apex” life may be mostly unseen: **aerial vibration-divers** above ridgelines, hunting by sound and substrate tremor rather than sight. Their pressure keeps the rest of the ecosystem flattened to cracks, seams, and undercuts.

So what lives there?

A world where life gave up height and became **flow**:  
not forests, but **lanes**;  
not canopies, but **acoustic maps**;  
not seasons of bloom, but **pulses of condensation**.

The resident organism, in the end, is almost geological in scale: a **continuous, chitinous weather system** draped over stone—old, efficient, and always in motion.
