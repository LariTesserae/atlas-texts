What lives there is **a civic species** before it is an individual one.

Not a single creature, but a kind of person shaped by seepage, schedules, and shared tolerance: someone who has learned that control is temporary and maintenance is culture.

They are practical, observant, and unromantic about nature without being hostile to it. They don’t talk about “the wild” as elsewhere; they talk about ledges, hatch cycles, gate timing, mosquito bloom weeks, snake-cool thresholds, fish-after-rain windows. Their intelligence is infrastructural and ecological at once. They can hear weather in roofing, read yesterday’s waterline in salt marks, and tell you which culvert will backflow before noon from the smell alone.

Physically, they move like people conserving heat: early risers, noon stillness, evening second shift. Clothes are light, repeatedly mended, never fully crisp. Skin carries a permanent sheen. Hands are nicked from wire mesh, rope, rusted bolts, trap latches, brush seals. They carry keys, twine, tape, gasket scraps, and a reflex for closing things tightly.

Temperamentally, they are patient in the long way of places that cannot be hurried. They trust routines more than declarations. Rule-following here is not ideological; it’s survival etiquette:

- Seal food.
- Screen tanks.
- Check thresholds.
- Don’t startle nesting columns.
- Never enter certain pump rooms alone.
- Listen when rain changes pitch.

They are not lonely, but they are used to space. Conversation is sparse, precise, often shouted across distance. Community is measured less by crowds than by synchronized upkeep—who showed up for desilting, who rotated floodgates on storm week, who remembered to delay ledge cleaning until fledging passed.

If there is a “character” that most fully lives there, it is the **waterworks keeper** in the broad sense: mechanic, biologist, janitor, weather-reader, and neighbor all at once. Maybe they live in a stilted apartment with always-damp laundry and screened windows patched three times. Maybe they know every dog’s route and every stork’s season. Maybe they can sleep through frogs, fans, and chain clatter but wake instantly to a new drip pattern.

And beyond the human type, the city is also inhabited by an immense nonhuman citizenry: birds in steel ribs, insects in lamp halos, amphibians in every ditch, fish in temporary basins, rats at margins, snakes in thermal shade. They are not invaders; they are co-tenants with leverage.

So whoever lives there is someone who has accepted a defining truth:  
**this place is not built against water and life, but amid them**.  
Its residents are caretakers of a negotiated order—never pristine, never final, always slightly wet, always in motion, and deeply, matter-of-factly at home.
