What lives there is not a single creature so much as a **way of being shaped by impact**.

If I had to name the primary inhabitant, I’d start with this:  
a people of rafters, line-keepers, spring-watchers—small in number, tendon-lean, rain-scoured, and patient in the dangerous way of those who cannot afford surprise twice.

They are never fully dry. Their skins are glossed with mineral water; their hair (or fur, if you imagine them less human) is kept cropped or tightly bound so rain can’t blind them. Their hands are mapped with old cord-burn scars, hook punctures, and pale seam-lines from stone flakes. They move low and braced, knees always slightly bent, as if the ground itself were a deck—and here, it often is. Even on still ledges they stand like people expecting the next lurch.

Their hearing is different from lowland hearing. In the constant roar, ears become almost secondary. They read vibration through soles, fingertips, jawbone against wood. Children are taught storms as rhythm before language: surge pulse, slack pulse, pull pulse. A novice asks, “What did you hear?” An elder corrects them: “What did the planks tell you?”

---

They build little and rebuild constantly.

Homes are not houses in the valley sense. They are **anchored shelters** tucked into lee-cracks above flood marks, made of layered hide, reed mat, and flexible frame—structures meant to deform and survive rather than resist and shatter. Every sleeping place is tied in at three points. Every tool has a leash. Every wall includes drains. They trust knots more than doors.

Their settlements are scattered around the rim in half-hidden pockets, each no more than a few platforms, drying lines, and storage niches sealed with stone plugs. Nothing ornamental that cannot double as warning, weight, or measure. Beauty exists, but practical beauty: a flawlessly spliced cord, a trap that rides a surge without flipping, a platform that sings the right pitch underfoot when pressure rises below.

---

Diet and labor revolve around timing rather than ownership.

They harvest needlefish in short windows when currents braid predictably. They lower baskets into warm upwellings for plankton-thick feed runs. They collect moss where spray slackens, scrape mineral crusts for trade, and gather eggs from cave-swallow ledges during rare calm spells. The great blind-backed things in the water are mostly left alone—not from reverence exactly, but from treaty-by-experience. Anything that old in a young basin is considered part of the basin’s thinking.

No one says “I caught this” without also naming conditions:  
“Caught on second hiss, west cross-current, after the cloud dropped.”  
Food is credited to weather as much as skill.

---

Their culture is built on impermanence with precision.

They keep records, but not on paper. They cut marks into sheltered stone: surge heights, collapse years, spring-temperature shifts, deaths, rare calms, unusual surface geometries. A lineage may be remembered as a sequence of storms survived rather than ancestors begotten.

Names are fluid. A child receives a use-name first (“Third-Line,” “Reed-Hand,” “Overhang-Born”). Later they may earn a storm-name from an event witnessed or endured (“Hex-Rain,” “No-Wake,” “Fifth-Stain”). To “name tomorrow’s shape” is taboo because prediction can become hubris, and hubris here is fatal.

Their spirituality—if you call it that—is hydrologic animism:  
the mountain is not a god but a **process with attention**.  
Rain patterns are read as drafts.  
Upwellings are breaths.  
Rockfalls are edits.

Some believe the basin is still deciding what kind of place it will be, and everyone living there is, in effect, temporary annotation.

---

And yes, there may be one more inhabitant.

Not monster, not deity—something between ecology and myth: an intelligence distributed through pressure, flow, and resonance. People glimpse it when rain impacts lock into impossible hexagonal lattices that hold for heartbeats too long, or when multiple spring-eyes pulse in sequence like thought. It has no face, but it has behavior. It “prefers” certain channels. It “tests” weak shelves. It “answers” careless anchoring with sudden down-pulls.

Whether that mind is real or projected scarcely matters; acting as if it is has kept generations alive.

---

So: who lives there?

- Rain that behaves like architecture.  
- Water that acts muscular, not passive.  
- Stone still in adolescence.  
- Sparse, stubborn plants rehearsing colonization.  
- Flashing fish, cliff swallows, and deep blind feeders.  
- And a people who have made a civilization of contingency—  
  not conquerors of the basin, but literate in its violence.

In a place like that, survival itself becomes a kind of native species.
