What lives there is a **people of gradient and interval**—not mountain “dwellers” in the romantic sense, but citizens whose bodies, habits, law, memory, and architecture are all tuned to unstable vertical ground.

At first glance, you might think they are austere, even severe. Seen from below, they are silhouettes on stairs, bent under loads, wrapped in layered wool and hide against dry cold and abrasive sun. Their faces are wind-cut early: narrowed eyes, high cheekbones darkened by light, lips often split from dust and altitude. Their gait is specific to this city—short, economical steps, center of gravity forward on climbs, knees soft on descent. Visitors look clumsy within minutes; locals move like water finding old channels.

But that’s only the outer edge. The deeper truth is that they are **maintenance people** in the most civilizational sense. Their hero is not conqueror or inventor—it is the one who kept a wall standing through three freeze-thaw cycles, who noticed a hairline crack before melt season, who reset a spillway stone in time. Prestige clings to reliability under repetition.

### Who they are socially

They organize themselves less by bloodline than by **contour obligation**.

You belong to a ward, yes—but more importantly to a slope position and a water sequence. Your district identity is literally hydraulic: first-catch, mid-channel, silt-receiver, flood-break. Even kinship language is influenced by flow and elevation: people speak of “upstream cousins,” “lower-step households,” “shared shadow lines.” Marriage often crosses microclimates strategically—sunward families pairing with cloudward ones, upper storage rights linked to lower fodder access.

Their politics are practical and memory-heavy. Every adult can tell you, not abstractly but stone-by-stone, why a neutral stair must remain open, why this cistern is capped that way, why this lane turns unexpectedly (old avalanche path), why that empty terrace is taboo (creeping slope; three collapses in living memory). Law is topography fossilized in custom.

### What kind of minds this environment selects for

People here develop a high tolerance for incompletion. Nothing is ever “finished.” A wall is “holding,” a channel is “true for now,” a map is “this year’s best argument.” They are suspicious of anyone promising permanence through one grand intervention. They trust systems that can be patched quickly with local skill.

They are also extraordinary **micro-observers**:
- the pitch of runoff in a side channel,
- whether goats refuse a particular traverse,
- the smell shift before a cloudburst,
- frost depth by morning stone tone,
- bird agitation along one escarpment but not another.

This attention is not mysticism. It is civic survival.

### Daily life and temperament

They are not quiet because they are timid; they are quiet because breath is currency and sound carries oddly in stone corridors. Public life is dense but close-voiced. Argument is common, theatricality less so. A person who wastes water is judged harder than one who speaks rudely. A person who blocks a stair is judged harder than one who cheats in trade.

Children are raised in vertical competence early. Before numeracy is formal, they learn footing, hand placement, and how to read “moving stone.” Games involve balance, relay carrying, knot speed, and route memory. Adolescence includes supervised hazard service—clearing scree, checking retaining joints, night watch during first melt.

Elders hold power if they remain mentally sharp, because historical recall is infrastructural. The archive is not just in tablets and tallies but in people who remember where the spring appeared the year the eastern gully jumped banks.

### Their relationship with animals

If there is a second citizenry, it is the animal one.

These people live in negotiated proximity with cliff birds, burrowers, scavengers, migratory herds, and hard-breathing pack stock. They do not sentimentalize this; they engineer it. Roof ridges are angled for exclusion or invitation by species. Refuse is separated to pull scavengers toward designated margins. Corrals under sleeping floors are warmth devices and theft deterrents, but also respiratory burdens everyone accepts as part of winter.

They read herd timing as meteorological input. A delayed descent is not “interesting”—it can trigger ration recalculation.

### Their built character, mirrored in person

Their city is stacked, interlocked, anti-sprawl; so are they in social form. No household is fully autonomous. Every family depends on people above and below for something critical. This makes them communitarian, but not soft. Cooperation is enforced by shared exposure to gravity and scarcity.

Their aesthetics favor compression and durability: patterned stonework that doubles as traction, carved lips that reduce spill, textiles woven for friction on shoulders, containers shaped for stair transport. Beauty is admired where function has been refined to inevitability.

### Spiritual or philosophical tone (without requiring the supernatural)

Even if their beliefs vary, most share a moral grammar of:
- **timing over force,**
- **repair over replacement,**
- **sequence over ownership** (you hold water in turn, not absolutely),
- **humility before slope.**

They likely produce proverbs like:
- “A wall falls first in the mind that stops checking it.”
- “High storage is borrowed from low labor.”
- “You do not own a stair you cannot clear.”

### In one line

What lives there is a civilization of expert breathers, climbers, patchers, and allocators—people who have made an urban culture out of instability itself, until discipline feels like tradition and survival feels like craft.
