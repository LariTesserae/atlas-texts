What lives there is not a species so much as a **discipline made flesh**.

At first glance, you’d expect the inhabitants to be hard-edged and machine-like—forge-boned people with soot in their lungs and clocks in their blood. And they are, partly. But if you watch longer, what emerges is subtler: a population trained by altitude, heat, and scarcity into a kind of deliberate grace. They move like people who understand that every gesture has thermodynamic consequences.

They are lean, layered, shade-conscious. Skin tones are less about ancestry than exposure—sun-burnished where routes are open, pallid where labor happens in shafts and pressure halls. Most wear reflective wraps, vented collars, wrist seals for hot rail contact, and dust veils that can be flipped up in one practiced motion when particulate alarms tick from yellow to amber. Clothing is modular and repair-marked. Nothing decorative that can’t double as function—except, occasionally, a strip of oxidized copper or glazed enamel worn like a dare against all the matte black.

Their culture is built around **flows**:

- heat flow,
- traffic flow,
- condensate flow,
- particulate flow,
- vertical flow of freight and ore and waste and people making room for all of it.

Children learn right-of-way before they learn abstract arithmetic. Not because of obedience for its own sake, but because a missed descent window can stall a furnace chain three escarpments down. They grow up reciting practical liturgies: where shadow falls at second bell, which handrail skins over first at dawn dew, which cistern wardens will forgive one extra cup and which will not.

They are deeply social, but not loudly so. Intimacy here is measured in **proximity and shared shelter**: two workers standing shoulder-close in a narrow maintenance throat while noon heat rolls outside like invisible surf; a family sleeping in staggered shifts to keep one room cool; neighbors exchanging dust-credit favors and condensate allotment tips more tenderly than some cultures exchange love poems. Voices stay low because walls carry, because alarms matter, because silence is a communal resource.

Religion—if it exists—looks pragmatic from outside. Shade canopies are blessed when installed. First water from repaired condensers is witnessed. Cistern districts inspire behavior that resembles reverence but is stripped of ornament: washed hands, quiet steps, no argument within earshot of the gauges. Their sacred objects are calibration tools, old survey rods, heat-warped warning plates from disasters everyone still names.

And there are other lives, thin but persistent at the margins: the pale jointed things in condenser housings, lamp-gliders flicking under bridge ribs, blind pipe-eels nosing overflow trickles. The people do not romanticize them. They log them, study them, route around them, sometimes cultivate them when useful. In a place with almost no ecology, even rumor-sized fauna become part of civic knowledge.

If you had to name the true resident of that city, though, it would be this:

**continuity**.

Not empire. Not conquest. Continuity as a survival art. Processes that do not stop. Maintenance that outlives individual ambition. A civilization that behaves like a long-running machine and, at its best, like a careful organism growing a skeleton while still molten.

So whoever lives there lives in the future tense—but patiently.  
They are the people of unfinished stone, of assigned dust, of borrowed moisture, of vertical courtesies.  
They are early, and they are staying.
