What lives there is not a creature in the ordinary sense first—it’s a **discipline**.

Then, underneath that, a people.

And under them, something thin and iridescent that may be closer to weather than biology.

---

The high country is inhabited by **keepers of behavior**: humans who survive by tracking not just *where* water is, but *how* it is choosing to act that season, that week, that hour. They are less like mountaineers and more like liturgists of seepage and echo. Their settlements aren’t villages in the social sense so much as instruments tuned to specific quirks in stone—one pocket for reliable condensation, another for a spring that occasionally inverts, another for safe passage across a fold zone when cloud flattens false depth.

They probably look worn into their environment the way channels are worn into basalt: spare, layered clothing lacquered against wet and mineral salts; wrapped hands; soft-metal tools suspended to avoid static snaps; rope harnesses polished by generations of repair. Their faces and speech would carry altitude—dry lips, careful breath, sentences timed between wind notes. They walk as if every horizon is a suggestion, not a fact.

They are not many. They do not spread. They **maintain**.

Each family or cluster likely inherits a narrow hydrological territory and a memory archive: which seep turned bitter after lightning three summers ago, which travertine pillar can still bear ladder weight, which echo in which gorge means “distance is folded, wait for cloud.” Children learn counting by drips, not beads. They learn direction by reverberation before they trust sight. They are taught that geometry can lie at noon, that sound has seniority over vision, that an “honest spring” is morally distinct from a capricious one.

Their culture is probably practical to the point of sacredness. Rules are short, transmissible, and tested against death. But over centuries those rules become metaphysics without changing wording:  
- “Mark water twice” is engineering, yes, but also ontology: quantity and character are different realities.  
- “Build nothing square in a fold zone” is structural caution and a cosmology of humility before slipping space.  
- “Sleep above the third mineral line” is sanitation, safety, and a boundary with whatever fog becomes when it lingers.

So who lives there?  
**Hydrologer-clans, echo-navigators, mineral masons, weather listeners.**

---

And then there is the other life—the films in shaded cracks.

They are harvested, but not farmed in any simple way. They seem to be **pattern organisms**, responsive to humidity pulses, light violence, dissolved metals, maybe even electrostatic charge. They don’t read as flora; they read as a slow, distributed skin the mountain grows where wetness persists. Their violet-copper iridescence suggests chemical complexity beyond chlorophyll economies; they may store charge, catalyze mineral deposition, or mediate micro-condensation. The locals treat them with restraint, scraping the renewable surface and preserving the anchor layer—an ethics born from necessity and close observation.

These films may be the closest thing the basin has to a primary biosystem: tiny, old, metabolically alien, making possible sealants, moisture indicators, and perhaps subtle atmospheric effects no one fully explains. If so, then the people do not merely live *on* the mountain. They live in partnership with a non-animal, non-plant intelligence of pattern and persistence.

---

So the answer is:

- **Sparse human communities** specialized in reading unstable space and anomalous water.  
- **A cryptic chemofilm lifeform** that creeps, dries, reanimates, and underwrites material culture.  
- And, in a deeper sense, **acoustic weather and restless geometry** as co-inhabitants—active forces the people treat as neighbors with temperaments, not background conditions.

In that high country, to “live there” means to become a reliable part of a negotiated system: stone, seep, signal, echo, and caution held together generation by generation.
