What lives there is a **maintenance civilization**—a people who have made reliability into culture, and culture into infrastructure.

Not conquerors of the basin, not mystics of it, not dreamers waiting for it to become gentler. They are **keepers of equilibrium** in a young world that never finished cooling.

They are probably compact, strong through the back and hands, with skin and lungs adapted to warm humidity, smoke, and mineral grit. Their bodies show their jobs: rope burns, callused palms, knees darkened by wet clay, shoulders grooved by carrying yokes. Their clothing is layered hide, woven fiber imports, and treated felt—everything built for damp, abrasion, and repair. Nothing drapes for beauty if it can snag in a winch.

But what most defines them is not appearance—it’s **timing**.

They live by cycles measured in seepage rates, gutter drip, kiln draw, beast rest intervals, and flood pulse windows. Their day is partitioned less by sun (too diffused to trust) than by sound cues: drum codes between terraces, pump-thud shifts, glider rise at dusk, cistern bells. A child here learns cadence before abstraction. They know when to move because the city tells them.

So the “who” is really a network of specialized castes or guild-families, interlocked:

- **Channel keepers** who read flow by ear and fingertip, clearing mineral choke before anyone else notices pressure drop.  
- **Roof tenders** who harvest condensation and patch catchment slips in predawn rounds.  
- **Kiln crews** who turn wet earth into standardized certainty: brick, pipe, valve housings, cistern collars.  
- **Animal marshals** who breed for temperament as much as utility—feet that don’t punch through crust, lungs that tolerate steam haze, instincts that follow lane memory.  
- **Ground readers** who can tell which plate will hold and which will suddenly subside by resonance, smell, and micro-cracking patterns.  
- **Signal drummers/callers** who coordinate labor surges across districts where visibility is poor and urgency is common.  

Their leaders, if they have formal ones, are likely less kings than **stewards of continuity**: people trusted to preserve schedules, settle water rights, allocate repair crews, and arbitrate when one system’s needs threaten another. Prestige goes to those who prevent failure invisibly, not those who stage spectacle.

Emotionally, they may seem conservative, even rigid, to outsiders. That’s because in this basin, improvisation kills. Tradition isn’t nostalgia; it’s accumulated survival math. “Because this is how we do it” often means “because three generations tested every other way and this one keeps the south lanes from drowning.”

And yet they are not joyless. Their joy is embedded in function:
- a pump cycle running true after a difficult repair,
- a clean cistern lip at day’s end,
- a herd turning exactly when called,
- a district whose night noise sounds healthy, not strained.

Their art would be utilitarian but unmistakable: patterned knotwork that encodes load ratings, drum rhythms that double as maps, glaze marks indicating flow direction, body tattoos for guild lineage and hazard literacy. Color is scarce and therefore meaningful—applied where attention saves lives.

As for nonhuman life, the city’s true co-residents are not pets or background fauna—they are **co-workers**:

- Splay-foot grazers as mobile sanitation and protein engines.  
- Gliders as dusk insect control and omen clocks.  
- Burrowers as both nuisance and geomorphic force, constantly rewriting foot traffic and foundation plans.  
- Blind runoff fish as living filtration units.  
- Shell-backed haulers as freight in lanes no wheel can survive.  

The people do not merely “use” these animals; they choreograph around them. Urban planning includes migration loops, roost gradients, dung heat zones, and panic contingencies. A failed animal relationship is an infrastructure failure.

If you asked what kind of person is born from this place: someone practical, acoustically literate, moisture-aware, intolerant of waste, suspicious of decorative excess, deeply cooperative, and profoundly skilled at reading tiny changes in stable systems. Someone who can hear a bad valve in a crowd. Someone who believes certainty is built daily, not granted.

So what lives there?

A dense, noisy pact between human hands, animal bodies, water behavior, and unfinished stone—held together by ritualized labor, shared memory, and the refusal to let the basin slip back into raw geology.
