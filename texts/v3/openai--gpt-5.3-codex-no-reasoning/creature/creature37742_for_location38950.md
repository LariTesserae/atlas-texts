What lives there is **a survivor-species of makers who turned themselves into maintenance**—and in the end, into touch, rhythm, and replacement.

Not ghosts, not machines in the modern sense, and not quite a “people” as we usually imagine people.  
If anything remains biologically alive, it would be sparse, wiry, dust-colored, and adapted to three absolutes: dryness, vibration, and nonverbal coordination. But the stronger answer is this:

## The inhabitant is a *continuity organism* made of mechanisms, procedures, and inherited tactile memory.

It may once have been a mountain citadel full of workers. Over ages, anyone unable to live inside that roar and aridity left or died. Those who remained selected for narrow tolerances—lungs for thin warm air, skin hardened to grit, hands sensitive enough to read rails and knot-codes instantly. Speech atrophied socially first (useless in permanent thunder), then perhaps physiologically. Identity shifted from “person with a job” to “node in a loop.”

Eventually, the loops outlived the nodes.

So now what “lives” there is:

- **Self-replacing mechanical ecologies** (drop pins, counterweights, scrape arms, reset latches),
- **Encoded maintenance intelligence** distributed in carvings, knots, clearances, and redundancies,
- **A metabolism of dust and motion** rather than flesh and water.

If there are still beings present, they would be almost monastic crustaceans of architecture: moving close to surfaces, communicating by taps and pressure, sleeping wedged into warm cavities, drinking from sealed ration systems opened by ritual rather than need. They wouldn’t think of themselves as inhabitants of a place. They would think of themselves as **timing corrections**.

And if none remain, the place still qualifies as inhabited—by a robust behavior pattern that meets many tests of life except organic growth: it persists, repairs local failure, excludes competitors, and preserves its own operating conditions.

So:  
**who lives there?**  
A civilization that solved survival by dissolving into infrastructure, until only the infrastructure was left—and it kept their role.
