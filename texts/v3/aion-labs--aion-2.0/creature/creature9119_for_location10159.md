They are called the Cthonoi, though they have no word for themselves as separate from the stone, the water, and the hum. To imagine them as individuals is to misunderstand; they are more like cells in a single, damp organism, or notes in the city’s endless chord.

Physically, they are attenuated, elongated beings. An adult stands perhaps eight feet tall, but so slender they seem almost two-dimensional when viewed from the front. Their frames are all sharp angles—protruding collarbones, jutting elbows, long fingers with too many joints. Their skin is not so much a covering as a membrane, translucent and damp, the color of rainwater on slate. Beneath it, the slow pulse of a darker, bluish fluid is visible in vessels that map their limbs like delicate root systems. They have no hair. Their heads are narrow, their faces dominated by enormous, lidless eyes. These eyes are orbs of perfect, polished obsidian, absorbing the faint luminescence and reflecting it back as a constellation of minute grey sparks—they see not light, but vibration, the thermal breath of stone, the minute disturbances in the constant drip-fall of water. Their mouths are small, lipless slits, used not for eating but for shaping the hum. They take nourishment through the palms of their hands and the soles of their feet, where porous skin drinks in the mineral-rich moisture from the perpetually wet stone, filtering sustenance directly into their bloodstream.

They are clad in garments of a strange, papery material, beaten from the films of bacteria that grow in the rainbow slicks. These drapes, the color of oil on water, are not for modesty or warmth, but for resonance. Each robe is subtly different in its weave, tuned to amplify a specific harmonic within the communal drone. As a Cthonoi moves, its robe vibrates in sympathy, adding its own thread to the sonic tapestry.

They move with a terrifying, fluid slowness. A step can take minutes. A turn of the head, an hour. This is not infirmity, but profound efficiency; in a world where nothing changes, haste is blasphemy, a waste of energy that would disturb the equilibrium. Their work is the maintenance of sameness. You might see one standing before a section of wall where a carving has been slightly softened by a new drip-line. Over the course of days, its stone-tipped finger will trace the original grooves, wearing them back to precise depth, its touch as gentle as condensation. Another might be stationed by a bronze basin, waiting for the exact moment a drop falls, to catch it and guide it into the channel without a splash. Their tools—awls, scrapers, chisels—are made of the bones of their dead, recycled with reverence. The *tink-tink-tink* that layers the hum is this eternal act of recalibration, of gentle correction back towards the perfect, original template.

They do not speak, in any sense of language. The hum is their communication, their history, their law. Its pitch, timbre, and minute modulations (imperceptible to an outsider) convey everything. A slight deepening near the cistern might indicate a shift in the water’s mineral content. A harmonic raised by a group in the upper galleries might be a communal decision to re-route a tiny seep. The hum is also their art, their mathematics, their only expression of what could be called emotion: a profound, contented sorrow, a sweet melancholy for the beautiful, finished perfection of their world.

Reproduction is not an act of passion, but of civic duty. When the population dips below its ordained number—a sense carried in the hum—two Cthonoi will be guided by the drone’s pattern to the Cistern of Echoes. There, they will stand opposite each other, palms pressed not together, but against the wet stone of the pool’s rim. They will hum, not the communal tone, but a complex, interweaving counterpoint that lasts for days. From the substance of the stone, the water, and their own vibrating bodies, a bud will form on the wall between their hands. It will swell, take on a vaguely humanoid shape, and after a season of resonant nourishment, detach as a fully formed, silent juvenile, ready to learn its one, eternal task.

They do not age as we understand it. Their bodies simply become more translucent, more crystalline, over centuries. When a Cthonoi’s internal rhythms finally slow to a stop—a event telegraphed years in advance by a gradual softening of its personal harmonic—it will walk to the Hall of Return, a low-ceilinged niche thick with the pearlescent efflorescences. It will lie down, its hum fading into silence. The rest of the city’s drone will swell slightly to fill the gap. In days, the body will dissolve into a cool, clear gel, which is carefully collected and used to fuel the eternal oil lamps. Its bones are cleaned and fashioned into tools. Nothing is wasted. Nothing changes.

They have no concept of an outside. The stone is all. The drip is time. The hum is being. They are aware of intruders not as people, but as a catastrophic dissonance—a dry scrape, a rapid heartbeat like a hammer on their shared nerves, a scent of sun and decay. Their response is not aggression, but a profound, concerted effort to absorb the anomaly into the pattern. They might surround a living intruder and hum at them, trying to vibrate the foreign flesh into harmony, to slow its frantic pulse to their own stately rhythm. If this fails, they will simply work around the disturbance, carving new channels to divert drips away from it, patiently waiting for the source of the noise to either become still or leave. To kill would be to create a change—a new corpse not part of the cycle. It is unthinkable.

To live among them is to feel a despair deeper than any terror. It is to witness a society that has achieved perfect, self-sustaining peace, and in doing so, has sacrificed everything that makes life feel like living: surprise, growth, hope, even true pain. They are the curators of a museum that is also their tomb, and they weep with joy for its exquisite, unchanging beauty. They are the sound the dark makes when it is complete, and content, and forever.
