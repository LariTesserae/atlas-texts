The inhabitants are not separate from the bowl; they are its expressions, its moods made flesh and stone and quiet purpose.

First, the moss-folk. They are not tall, and their skin carries the grey-green pallor of deep shade and mist-filtered light. Their hair, where it is not shorn practical against the damp, is the black of wet slate, often braided with strands of luminous fungal mycelium that glow softly with the same blue-green as the path-lights. They move with a fluid, considered grace, their footfalls silent on the smoothed stone. Their eyes are large, dark, and perpetually reflective, adapted to the gloaming. They speak, but rarely, and their language is a series of hums, clicks, and exhalations—more akin to the sounds of the place itself than to human speech. Their hands are their primary tools: strong, clever, with fingers that can feel the minute textures of moss and the finest seam in rock. They build, but they do not quarry. They guide the slow stone-borer creatures—massive, blind grubs with grinding mouthparts—to where new stone is willing to be shaped, then work the exposed surfaces with tools of hardened fungal chitin and water-smoothed pebbles. Their homes are not possessions; they are careful insertions into the existing order, and a dome whose moss roof thins is a subject of quiet communal concern, its restoration a ritual of patting and seeding and whispered encouragement to the spores.

Then, the grazers. The six-legged slate-beetles are the landscape’s gardeners. Their carapaces are not uniform; each is a unique mosaic of charcoal grey, deep blue, and iron-red, polished to a dull sheen by constant passage through moss and mist. They move in a slow, deliberate procession, their multiple legs articulating with a fluid, mechanical precision that makes no sound. Their heads are low, their mandibles constantly, gently cropping the moss, not destroying it but pruning it, stimulating denser growth. A path they take becomes, within days, a velvet-green trail. The moss-folk observe their routes and plan their own pathways accordingly, never obstructing. There is a mutualism here: the beetles keep the moss healthy; the moss, in turn, exudes a mineral-rich moisture the beetles sip from their own limbs.

In the fern-trees dwell the air-jellies. Colonies of translucent, pulsing sacs, they are the bowl’s lungs. They filter particulates and spores from the air, their interiors swirling with faint, captured light. They are passive, but vital. To harvest one is unthinkable to the moss-folk; they will sometimes cradle a fallen, dying jelly and place it gently at the base of a fern, where it dissolves into a nourishing gel for the roots.

And in the deepest crevices, where the mist pools into cool, silent milk, are the watchers. These are the lithic beings—the young mountain’s half-formed thoughts. They have no fixed shape. One might be a congregation of pebbles holding a configuration that suggests a multilayered eye. Another might be a smear of unusual lichen on the wall that feels, unmistakably, like attention. They are not creatures of biology, but of geology and perception. They observe the bowl’s formation. They watch the moss-folk not with curiosity, but with a kind of slow, tectonic recognition. To stand before one is to feel the immense, patient pressure of a mountain thinking about itself. The moss-folk acknowledge them with a deep bow of the head, a gesture of respect to the consciousness of the place that allows them to dwell within its cupped hands.

Finally, there are the unseen symbionts. The insects that cultivate the fungal lights, tiny things with iridescent wings that flicker only in total darkness. The microbes in the water that make it bead upward on certain mosses. The entire system is an inhabitant—a single, slow-breathing entity. The moss-folk are but one conscious note in its quiet, endless hymn. They live not upon the land, but within its breath. Their entire existence is a response to the drip, the creak, the soft roll of mist—a life of exquisite, silent attendance.
