They do not think of it as a city. It is simply the world, and they are its blood and its breath.

Begin with the canopy, the city’s new sky. There, in the perpetual green twilight, live the Weavers. Their bodies are like leathery kites, membranes stretched between elongated finger-bones, and they sail the damp updrafts in utter silence. They are not birds. They are the city’s librarians of light. Their bellies are covered in a fine, moss-like fur that glows a soft, melancholic blue. As they drift, they paint faint, fleeting constellations on the undersides of leaves and the vaults of collapsed ceilings, a living, shifting map of air currents. They feed on the luminous fungi that sprout from the sodden timber, and their droppings, falling like slow rain, seed new colonies of light below.

Below them, in the tangled mid-levels where liana ropes form a new, living infrastructure, are the Click-Clacks. Crustacean in essence, armored in iridescent, chitinous plates the color of oil on water, they move in ceaseless, ritualistic processions. They follow invisible trails of scent and humidity, their countless legs ticking a syncopated rhythm against stone and root. Their purpose is decomposition accelerated to an art form. A fallen tree-limb, a mound of leaf litter, the carcass of a larger creature—they converge upon it, and their clicking rises to a feverish crescendo as they reduce it to damp, rich soil in hours. They are the city’s stomach, and their trails are its digestive tracts.

In the deep places, where the weeping stone forms permanent pools in sunken plazas, live the Stillness. They are amphibious, sleek, and patient. They look like smooth, blind salamanders grown to the size of crocodiles, their skin the perfect black of deep water. They rest for days, unmoving, mouths agape, filtering the endless nutrient soup that washes down from above. They are the city’s kidneys, its settling tanks. When they move, it is with a shocking, fluid suddenness, and the shrieking chorus from above momentarily hushes. They are the only things the other fauna truly fear.

And then there are the Gargoyles. Not the stone ones, though they perch upon them. These are simian, but long-limbed and spidery, with fur the exact mottled grey-green of the moss. Their eyes are the large, phosphorescent discs glimpsed in dark windows. They do not own the towers; they are their nervous system. They move through the vertical labyrinth with a proprietary ease, their long fingers finding purchase in cracks no wider than a blade. They watch everything. They harvest the Weavers’ light-moss for their nests, steal Click-Clack eggs from the edges of the swarms, and sometimes, when a Stillness grows too sluggish, they descend in a silent, coordinated flock to drag it from its pool, a rare and violent festival of meat. They remember, in a dim, instinctual way, the shape of the city as it was meant to be used. They use the empty halls, sleep in the carved alcoves, and their chittering calls echo down stairwells like ghostly conversation.

Smaller things fill every crack: the filter-feeders in the constant drip-lines, the buzzing clouds of mites that feast on pollen, the burrowing grubs that turn structural timber into spongy, edible honeycombs.

They are not separate from the stone and the green. They are the mechanism of its transformation. The Click-Clacks’ soil collects in the carved gutters, and seeds take root. The Weavers’ glowing droppings fertilize them. The Gargoyles’ movements prune and spread. The Stillness’ pools become breeding grounds for insects that feed the birds.

The city breathes in—the slow, centuries-long inhalation of roots cracking stone, of water smoothing edges, of biomass accumulating. It breathes out—the daily, roaring exhalation of a trillion wings beating, legs skittering, throats calling. The builders made a vessel. Life, indifferent and magnificent, filled it with a pulse. What lives here now is not an invasion, nor a collection of tenants. It is the city itself, awake and hungry and alive.
