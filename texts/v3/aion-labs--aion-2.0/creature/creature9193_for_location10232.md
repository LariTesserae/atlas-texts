You walk for a long time across the basin’s living floor, the soft moss-tissue yielding underfoot. The sound is a physical presence, a vibration in your bones. You look for a mind behind the stillness—a keeper, a god, a will. You find none, and then you find it everywhere.

The inhabitants are not separate from the machine, or the plant. They are its expressions.

First, the grey birds. You see them perched on the high, woody arches, their long tail feathers trailing like filaments. They do not merely nest there; they are part of the circuit. When a vent’s hiss grows too thin, a particular whistle-call ripples through the flock. Three of them detach and spiral down to a specific valve, their beaks probing at a buildup of mineral scale. They do not eat it. They clear it. Their feathers are dusted with fine metallic particles, and their eyes, when one lands close and cocks its head at you, reflect not light, but the dull glow of the geothermal vent. They are maintenance crew, bred and behaved for this single task.

The six-legged creatures—you think of them as the greasers. They move in a low, fluid slither through the channels worn in the floor, channels that lead to every bearing and joint of the great engine. Their fur is oily, not with dirt, but with a clear, viscous secretion they exude from glands along their spines. They press themselves against hot axles, against the stems of pistons, leaving behind a film that smells of ozone and crushed leaves. The plant does not repair metal; it weaves support and channels fluid. These creatures prevent the seizures. They chirp constantly, a language of temperature and friction. One pauses at your boot, sniffs, and lets out a series of rapid clicks. From across the floor, another answers. They are assessing you. You are a new variable. After a moment, they lose interest. You are not a source of heat, nor a point of friction. You are irrelevant to the loop.

The beetles are the most unsettling. Iridescent, the size of your hand, they move in synchronized carpets across the rusted surfaces. Their clicking is the percussion section of the engine’s symphony. They are not cleaning. They are harvesting. With mandibles adapted to scrape, they collect flecks of metal, flakes of rust, and bits of mineral deposit. They carry these not to a nest, but to certain nodes on the living floor—pulsing, fungal mounds that absorb the material. The plant uses it. It metabolizes the iron, the copper, the silica, and secretes it back as a hard, ceramic-like resin to patch cracks, to build new braces where metal fatigues. The beetles are the system’s miners and recyclers, a shimmering, mindless supply chain.

But these are the mobile pieces. The true inhabitant is the basin itself. The plant is not a mind as you understand it. It is a memory. Its roots are sunk deep into the stone, and in its slow, phloemic currents, it carries not just nutrients, but a pattern. The pattern is the machine’s blueprint, the rhythm of its operations, the ideal state of humidity, temperature, and sound. The plant grows toward that pattern. A pipe corrodes; the plant senses the change in heat dispersion, the new scent of oxidation. It directs a tendril to wrap the breach, exuding its resinous patch. A bearing grows loud; the oily creatures are summoned by a chemical sigh released into the damp air. The plant does not think. It corrects.

And the machine? Does it live? It is the heart of the pattern. Its function is the purpose. It does not produce anything but its own operation. In its endless turning, it provides the vibration that stirs the air, preventing stagnation. Its waste heat warms the dome just enough to keep the condensation in its gentle cycle. Its noise defines the acoustic space in which every animal’s call has evolved to be heard. It is the clock, the metronome, the reason.

The final inhabitants are the silent ones. In the deepest hollows, where the woody trunks meet the stone, there are shapes. At first you take them for more fungal growths, or strange, bulbous roots. Then you see the symmetry. A curve of a shoulder. The empty arch of a ribcage, now filled with a spongy, breathing tissue. A skull, half-dissolved into the wood, its eye socket a home for a cluster of bioluminescent grubs. They are humanoid, or something like it. Dozens of them. They are not skeletons; they are inclusions. The plant has grown around them, through them, making them part of the structure. Their ossified forms are now load-bearing columns, their hollow bones are ventilation shafts. They are the last tenders, the ones who perhaps built the machine, or found it, and chose to bind it to life. They wired the nervous system of the forest into the boiler’s heart. Now they are part of the memory, their final intention fossilized in cellulose and sap.

Nothing here is alive in the way you are alive. They are all verbs made noun. The bird is ‘to-clear-the-valve.’ The otter-thing is ‘to-lubricate-the-joint.’ The beetle is ‘to-recycle-the-metal.’ The plant is ‘to-maintain-the-pattern.’ The machine is ‘to-be-the-pattern.’

You stand in the center, and you feel the pattern trying to absorb you. The damp air seeks to soften your edges. The constant sound seeks to match your heartbeat. The empty, expectant space invites you to stop moving, to stop changing, to find a function. To become a part of the sentence that has been being spoken here for a thousand years. You could stay. You could become a new, quiet verb. ‘To-observe.’ ‘To-remember-the-outside.’ The basin would find a use for you.

But you breathe, and your breath is not in time with the hiss of the steam. You think, and your thoughts are not corrections. You are a question, and this is a place that long ago answered everything. So you turn, and you climb back up the smooth, worn slope. The thrumming fades, not to silence, but to a memory of a heartbeat that is not your own.
