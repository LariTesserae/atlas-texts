They are called, in the guttural hum-and-click language that must be shaped against the constant din, the People of the Damp Throat. Their bodies are the first testament to the basin’s rule. Short, yes, and broad, built for stability on slick hummocks and in narrow lanes. Their skin is not merely the color of clay; it *is* clay—a porous, fine-grained hide that secretes a thin, oily film to repel the constant moisture, giving them a permanent, subdued sheen. It toughens with age into a leathern hide, crosshatched with fine cracks that never become wounds. Their hands are wide, fingers thick and strong, with broad nails like chisels evolved for digging, for molding, for holding onto things in the wet. Their eyes are their most striking feature: large, lidless, and dark as the deep water, protected by a transparent, nictitating membrane that sweeps across horizontally every few seconds, wiping away the condensation. They see not in color, but in gradients of heat and movement; the grey world is alive to them in the shimmer of a swimmer’s wake, the faint thermal glow of a slime-mat.

They build not for beauty, but for echo and resonance. Their rounded huts are not just dwellings; they are instruments. The specific thickness of a clay-daubed wall, the angle of a weeping-stone support, the careful drilling of a finger-width hole in a ceiling—all are tuned to filter the endless roar. Inside, the deafening tapestry of the basin resolves. The high whine of the gnats is muted; the grating of the beetles becomes a soft, rhythmic scratch; the barks of the swimmers sound distant and formal. In this curated sonic landscape, they speak. Their language is one of pressure and release, of clicks formed at the roof of the mouth, of hums resonated in their chests. It is a language that cannot be shouted; it is felt as much as heard, a private code woven into the public noise. They speak of practical things: the yield of a mat-harvest, the mending of a net, the slow softening of a foundational stone. They do not have a word for ‘quiet.’ The closest concept is *‘still-sound’*—the moment when all noises find their accepted place, and the mind ceases to fight them.

Their work is the work of careful taking. At dawn—a time marked not by light but by a subtle, collective shift in the chorus of the throat-humming amphibians—the harvesters move out onto the wider water channels on rafts of lashed, petrified stems. They use nets woven from the sinews of the furred swimmers to skim the bioluminescent mats, gathering the cold, glowing jelly into clay pots. Others, the clay-speakers, work the hummocks, feeling for the right consistency of mud, mixing it with a paste of crushed beetle-shell to make a mortar that sets harder than stone. Children, their skin still slick and new, are set to the endless task of clearing the water channels of encroaching slime, not to keep them clear, but to guide the growth into more harvestable patches. They are a people of profound patience. A project—the extending of a lane, the raising of a new hut—takes years. Time is not a resource to be spent, but a medium in which they float, like the still water.

They believe they are the clay’s dream. Their myth, passed on through the tactile memory of shaping wet earth, says that the basin was once silent and formless. The great rock-weeps grew lonely in the dark and began to hum. That hum stirred the clay, and from its dreaming warmth, the first people oozed forth, their bodies taking shape around the rhythm. Their purpose is to listen, and in listening, to shape. To build the city is to give the basin’s song a structure to bounce off of, to complete a circuit. Death is a return to the mud, a relinquishing of form back to the dreaming mass. A body is laid not in a grave, but in a special, slow-moving channel where the chemical reactions are strongest. Within days, it is gone, consumed by the slime-mats and the blind life, its essence cycling back into the community through the harvested paste. There is no grief, only a quiet acknowledgment of a loan being repaid.

They know no other life. The perpetual twilight, the warm wet, the roar—these are not conditions to be endured. They are the foundations of reality. A visitor from a world of sun and silence would seem to them a terrifying phantom, a being of brittle dryness and deafening quiet, utterly alien and bereft of the sustaining noise. To them, the sound is not an assault. It is the breath of the world. They hear within it the patterns of their lives, the rhythm of their work, the hum of their own blood—which, if you were to listen very closely, matches perfectly the low, foundational pitch of the basin itself.
