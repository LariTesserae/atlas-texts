They are the Stillborn. That is the closest translation, though they have no word for themselves. They are the reason the Mossward is not a ruin.

They are few. Perhaps no more than thirty individuals occupy the compressed stone puzzle of the settlement. They are slight of build, their frames narrow and their limbs long, a shape evolved for slipping through the tight, mossy corridors and for conserving heat. Their skin is the color of water-polished bone, or of the pale, cork-like wood of the cliff-dwelling trees—a muted, greyish white, almost translucent in the sharp, cold sunlight. It is cool to the touch, and dry, with a fine, granular texture not unlike sandstone. They are hairless, save for fine, silver filaments that grow in delicate, lichen-like patterns at their temples, the nape of the neck, and along the spine. These filaments are not hair; they are sensory organs, trembling perpetually to the micro-currents of the damp, still air, reading the humidity, the faintest breath of movement.

Their faces are long, their features fine and seemingly worn smooth by time, like the stones of their dwellings. The eyes are large, liquid, and utterly black, absorbing the dim light and the occasional foxfire glow with equal efficiency. They have no discernible irises or pupils, only depth. They blink slowly, membranes sliding sideways with a soft, papery sound. Their mouths are small, lips thin and pale. They smile rarely, and when they do, it is a slow, gradual softening of the entire face, not a baring of teeth. They have teeth, but they are small, flat pebbles of enamel, suited for grinding the tough, fibrous stalks of the ferns and the slow, pulpy flesh of the furred lizards.

They wear garments of layered moss-felt and a supple, papery bark beaten soft. The colors are the colors of the Mossward: iron-green, liver-brown, granite-grey. They do not adorn themselves with jewelry, but with living grafts. It is common to see a hand-sized patch of the faintly glowing moss cultivated on a shoulder or chest, a soft, grey beacon in the dim hours. Others have encouraged the hexagonal-patterned vines to take root in the porous stone of a pendant or a belt, the tiny leaves a permanent, slow-growing embroidery.

Their movement is the movement of the Mossward itself: deliberate, economical, silent. They glide rather than step, their bare feet—broad, with long, prehensile toes—reading the glossy cobbles and the spongy moss with intimate knowledge. They do not run. There is no need. Their pace is a slow, steady procession, a physical manifestation of patience. To watch one traverse the maze of paths is to see the irrational geometry made sensible; they turn where there seems no turn, pass through gaps that appear solid with vine, and arrive without hurry, without wasted motion.

They communicate, but not as we understand it. They have a spoken language, but it is used sparingly, for names, for warnings, for rare moments of ceremony. The words are soft, breathy exhalations, clicks formed at the back of the throat, sighs that ride the chilled air. Most of their exchange is tactile and visual. A slow placing of a hand on another’s forearm, the pressure and duration conveying a question, an affirmation, a shared observation. A tilt of the head, a specific pattern of blinking. They can stand in shared silence for hours, their silver filaments trembling in unison, and emerge from it having reached a complex consensus.

Their society is one of seamless maintenance. There are no leaders, only deep, ingrained patterns of responsibility passed down through generations that are not counted in years, but in the growth-cycles of the great cliff-vines. Their work is the work of gentle curation: with bone tools, they carefully scrape excess moss from the ancient doorways, not to remove it, but to shape it. They guide the rivulets that seep from the rock with subtle channels of polished stone, directing moisture to a new cluster of ferns. They harvest the fat lizards and the flightless birds with a tender, respectful efficiency, taking only the oldest, and using every part. They gather the eggs of the pale crickets not to eat, but to redistribute, ensuring the silent population thrives in every corner.

They sleep in the round, stone cells, curled on beds of dried moss, their glowing grafts providing a faint, dreamlike light. They dream, it is believed, not of past or future, but of the Mossward’ perpetual present—dreams of stone-cooling, of moss-creep, of the slow, silent journey of a single water droplet down a mile of granite.

They are not immortal, but they are interminable. They age with a slowness that is geological. A crease at the corner of an eye might deepen over a century. Their movements grow even more deliberate, more distilled, until one day, an individual will simply stop. They will settle onto a favorite sun-warmed stone, or in the doorway of their cell, and their large, black eyes will slowly film over with a translucent grey membrane. Their breathing will cease. And then, with a care that is both ritual and practicality, the others will gently carry the body to the deepest, greenest cleft in the amphitheater’s wall. They will place it in a seated position, pack the mouth and eyes with glowing moss spores, and weave the hexagonal vines through the still fingers and around the limbs. Within a decade, the body will be indistinguishable from the stone, a new nucleus for the relentless, patient, loving growth. They call this “returning to the pattern.”

They are the mind of the Mossward. The consciousness of the cold, bright stillness. They are its memory, its caretakers, and its ultimate expression. To meet their large, absorbing eyes is to understand that you are not looking at a person in a place, but at the place itself, looking back.
