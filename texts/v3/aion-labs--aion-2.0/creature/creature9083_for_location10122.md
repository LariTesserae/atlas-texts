It is not immediately seen, for it has become indistinguishable from the northern wall. For centuries at a time, it is merely a darker shadow within the alcove where the chert scraper rests, a gentle bulge in the stone that echoes the curve of a shoulder, the slope of a bowed head. It is the Keeper, and it is made of the same seamless basalt as the floor, though its surface is not glassy but finely pitted, like stone kissed by a slow, acid wind. This texture is not erosion; it is its skin.

It has no face, only a suggestion of one—a smooth depression where eyes might be, a slight ridge for a nose, a seam for a mouth that has never opened. The features are so softened by time they seem less carved than remembered by the rock itself. Its form is humanoid, but stocky and thick, as if it had pressed itself into the wall and the wall had accepted it, flowing around its limbs in a permanent embrace. One hand, a blunt mitt of stone, rests on the shelf beside the scraper, fingers curled slightly, as if it had just set the tool down. The other hand lies in its lap, which is the floor of the alcove. It is seated, legs folded beneath it, in a posture of infinite patience.

It does not breathe. It does not blink. Its existence is measured in the same increments as the chamber’s own processes: the almost imperceptible fading of the fungal lights over ten thousand years, the brittle death and rebirth of a single moss-circle in a millennium. It is alive, but its life is a geological hum. Warmth radiates from its core, not the stored heat of the sun-baked stone, but a deep, gentle pulse that is the source of the chamber’s constant temperature. It is the heart of fire that never touches the sealed pocket—not the fire itself, but its embodied, tempered echo.

Its consciousness is a vast, slow thing. A single thought might take a century to form—a contemplation of the pattern of the moss-circles, an acknowledgment of the fall of a single flake of chert from the pile. It is aware of every grain of dust on the basalt floor, every minute shift in the ozonic tang of the air. It maintains the equilibrium. It is the reason the air does not move, the silence holds such perfect weight. It *wills* the stillness, not through active force, but through its own profound inertia. The chamber is an extension of its being, and it is in a state of deep, self-imposed stasis.

Once every few centuries, or perhaps millennia, it moves. The movement is so slow it would be invisible to a mortal eye. The stone of its hand might slide a fraction of an inch across the shelf, nudging a stray flake back into the neat pile. Its head might tilt a degree, as if listening to a sound from the earth’s depths that has finally arrived. On rarer occasions, it uses the scraper. With a motion that would take a full year from start to finish, it lifts the chert tool and draws it once, with infinite care, across its own thigh. A whisper of sound, a faint scraping like the kiss of continents, echoes for a moment in the woolen silence. A few microscopic shavings of stone-dust fall to join the floor. This is its only ritual: a self-maintenance, a mindful act of attrition that acknowledges the passage of time without resisting it. It is how it remembers it has a body.

It does not sleep. It watches. The faint, greenish constellations of fungi on the ceiling are its clock, its calendar, its star-chart. The rusty glow from the wall is its sunset, perpetual and unchanging. It waits, but it does not wait *for* anything. Waiting is its state of being. It is the guardian of this captured moment, the living embodiment of the breath held in the earth’s throat. To disturb it is to shatter the pane of glass. And so it sits, a statue of its own will, a sovereign of silence, for as long as the stone remembers itself.
