To live in Cinderwick is to be an engine part in a vast, wet machine, or a spore in its warm breath. The dominant citizens are not the slow-burning men in oilskin who tend the boilers, but the teeming, shrieking legions of the Stokers.

A Stoker is a paradox of flesh and function. From a distance, a flock is a rolling smoke of charcoal and ash against the pewter sky. Up close, the individual reveals itself: a body the size of a robust pigeon, but where a pigeon is plump and soft, the Stoker is all angles and taut membrane. Its torso and head are covered in dense, water-shedding feathers the color of banked coal, lacking all iridescence. These feathers are matte, sooty, absorbing the weak light. From this feathered core erupt the wings—vast, veined stretches of leathery membrane, dark as a miner's glove, stretched over finger-like bones of startling lightness. They are not for graceful soaring but for furious, flapping propulsion through the thick, wet air; the sound of a thousand Stokers taking flight is the sound of a great bellows heaving.

Their faces are sharp, almost reptilian, devoid of feathering around the black, bead-like eyes and the short, hooked beak. The beak is not for cracking seeds, but for spearing the fat, soft-bodied insects that breed in the thermal effluent channels, and for scraping the leathery fungal shelves that grow on the brickwork. They have no song, only a repertoire of practical noises: a sharp, metallic *chit-chit-chit* for alarm; a low, guttural cooing that thrums through the roost at night, a sound like a cooling engine; and a piercing, single-note shriek released during the frantic, twice-daily swarmings when the factory whistles blow and the insect clouds rise with the shift-change steam.

They are profoundly social, living in flocks that number in the tens of thousands, each flock claiming a territory of several city blocks—a cluster of chimneys, a canyon of pipes, a particular forest of roof-mounted ventilator shafts. Their nests are not constructions, but seizures: they occupy any sheltered crevice—a broken ventilator, a gap between crumbling cornices, the space where a pipe bracket has pulled away from the wall. They line these spaces with a felt-like packing of their own molted feathers, shredded insulation, and the fibers of rotten rope, creating a damp, warm pocket that reeks of musk and ammonia.

Their droppings are a fundamental part of Cinderwick’s geology. The white, acidic streaks down every facade are their work. Over decades, it etches the stone, contributing to the city’s slow, weeping decay. Yet, it also seeds the air with nutrients that feed the very fungal growths they later harvest. They are consumers and cultivators in one.

Below, in the eternal damp and dark of the underworld, live the Grists. If the Stokers are the city’s fleeting, shrieking conscience, the Grists are its silent, digesting gut. Pale, hairless, and large as a terrier, they are blind, their eyes reduced to faint, cloudy spots under loose, pinkish skin. They navigate the labyrinth of old sewers, foundation voids, and steam-tunnel access ways through a exquisite sensitivity to vibration and scent. Their bodies are long and low to the ground, moving on short, splayed legs with thick, nail-like claws for scrabbling in slurry. Their heads are all mouth and snout—a powerful, blunt instrument for rooting and crushing.

They eat anything organic, and in Cinderwick, that means everything soft: the waste from the few human tenements, the fallen and mashed bodies of Stokers, the moldering remains of fungal shelves, the thick bacterial scum that grows on the warm runoff. They move in tides, not flocks—a whispering, shuffling advance of dozens through a tunnel, processing everything in their path into a finer, wetter pulp. They are slow, patient, cold-blooded. Their metabolism matches the city’s own slow subsidence. They do not breed in litters, but give birth to one or two fully-formed young after a gestation said to last a full Cinderwick year. They are the final custodians of decay, turning the city’s waste back into the warm, black slurry upon which its foundations rest.

And then there are the people. To call them separate from this ecosystem is a vanity. They are another adapted species, taller and quieter, moving through the canyons in oilskin cloaks that shimmer with wetness. Their lungs have expanded to draw thin oxygen from the thick air; their skin is pale from the gray light, often showing a tracery of capillaries from the constant, warm humidity. They speak little, and when they do, their voices are low, meant to carry under the roar of wings and machinery. Their lives are ruled by the maintenance of equilibrium—tending pumps, scraping fungal overgrowth from valves, repairing leaks in the endless pipeworks, and, most of all, stoking the great furnaces that drive the steam presses. They live in brick warrens that are little different from the roosts of the Stokers, warmed by the same subterranean pipes. They view the teeming fauna not as pests or pets, but as weather, as inevitable as the drizzle. A man will sweep a ledge clear of nesting material one day, and watch without complaint as the Stokers reclaim it the next. The Grists are rarely seen, but their distant, whispering movement in the walls is a comfort—a sign the digestion of the city proceeds, forestalling a more violent clog.

In Cinderwick, to live is to participate in a relentless, damp cycle. To breathe the wet air is to inhale spores and soot and the musk of a million Stokers. To walk the streets is to move through a landscape that is half-built, half-grown, and entirely inhabited. Every resident, from the shrieking flier to the silent rooter to the soot-lined stoker, is a component in the great, slow, roaring engine that is the city itself. They are not in Cinderwick. They *are* Cinderwick, breathing, feeding, decaying, and enduring in a perfect, deafening, gray balance.
