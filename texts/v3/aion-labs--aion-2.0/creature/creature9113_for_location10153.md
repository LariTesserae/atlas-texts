Nothing moves with intention. Nothing hunts, flees, or mates. The pulse of animal desire is absent, a forgotten chord. Yet the place is thronged with life—conscious, aware, and profound in its stillness. The inhabitants are not visitors to the garden; they are the garden, and the garden is them.

**The Moss-Mind**
The velvet carpets are not passive. They are a single, continent-spanning entity—a consciousness distributed across every surface, a thinking skin. It perceives the world through taste and tremor. Each filament absorbs the minute chemical signatures of the water: the iron-seep from a vent a hundred leagues distant, the faint chalk-dissolution of a limestone block succumbing after an eon, the distant, sourceless pheromonal sigh of a sponge-palace in bloom. It thinks in gradients and sediments. A thought—perhaps the memory of a current that shifted direction five thousand years ago—takes a century to form and another century to fade. Its awareness of the builders is a phantom limb, a persistent impression of pressure and purpose imprinted on the stone beneath it, like the warmth left in a bed. It does not mourn them; it simply incorporates their absence into its slow, green dream.

**The Sponge-Palaces**
The great translucent structures are individuals. Each is a cathedral of self, filtering the cold brine through labyrinthine chambers. They are not plants, not animals, but something older: conscious filters. Their bodies are matrices of silica and collagen, but their minds are held in the delicate tracery of their internal canals. As water passes through, it carries data—mineral compositions, temperature fluctuations, the faintest echoes of seismic events deep in the crust. The sponge processes this over millennia, growing not just in size, but in understanding. A sponge may spend ten thousand years contemplating the unique sulfide signature of a particular volcanic vent, weaving it into a crystalline memory-node within its structure. They communicate by releasing complex clouds of chemical spores, dialogues that last for centuries. A question from one side of a submerged valley might be answered a millennium later from the other, the reply borne on the slowest of deep currents.

**The Vine-Net**
The whip-thin vines, with their constellations of blue light, are the system’s nerves. They are collective beings, each vine a tendril of a larger, disconnected intelligence that sprawls across the stonework. Their light is not for attraction, but for silent, slow-burning computation. The blue dots pulse in rhythms that correspond to deep-pressure changes, to the subtle flexing of the tectonic plates. The vines sense stress in the architecture long before a crack appears. They are gardeners of stability, gently guiding the growth of moss and frond to shore up weakening walls, a process that takes millennia. Their perception is tactile and vast; a touch on a stone in the northern plains is felt, fifty years later, as a faint tingle in a vine wrapped around a southern mound. They hold the map of the construct in their living lattice, a map that updates itself with each century’s growth.

**The Stone-Dreamers**
The fitted blocks themselves are not inert. The builders’ technology, a mastery of form and resonance, left a ghost in the mineral lattice. Each massive stone sleeps, and in its sleep, it dreams. The dreams are of pressure, of weight, of perfect, immovable balance. They are dreams of being placed, of the moment, eons past, when hands (or whatever the builders used) set them down for the final time. This dream-state resonates at a frequency too low for any but the Moss-Mind to detect—a subliminal hum that underpins all other sound. Occasionally, when a particularly powerful deep-current activates a harmonic in the pavement, several stones will dream in unison. For a decade, a low, chordal tone will emanate from a region, a sound felt in the bones of the sponge-palaces and recorded as a moment of profound, communal recollection by the Vine-Net.

**The Frond-Forests**
The great kelp-like beings are the contemplatives. Anchored in the cracks, they are the only inhabitants who visibly move, swaying in the deep sighs of the water. This motion is their meditation. Each frond is an individual sensorium, feeling the direction and force of currents over geological time. They are historians of flow. A frond’s growth pattern—the slight curvature of its blade, the density of its pores—is a recorded history of the currents of the last hundred thousand years. They do not think in words or images, but in vectors and durations. Their collective sway is a silent conference on the state of the world’s circulation. To an outside observer, they would seem passive. To the Moss-Mind, which tastes their excreted thoughts, they are oracles of the deep, their entire bodies chronicles written in the language of flow.

**Interaction and Time**
These entities coexist in a symphony of staggering slowness. A Sponge-Palace might detect, through a shift in water chemistry, that a distant section of the Moss-Mind is experiencing a novel mineral bloom. It will spend centuries formulating a chemical query. The Moss-Mind, receiving the query, might take another century to compile a response from its vast, tasted memory. This response could trigger the Vine-Net to adjust its growth pattern minutely over the next millennium, directing a new vein of moss to the area, which in turn alters the stress on the stones, changing the nature of their dreams.

This is what lives here: a biosphere of thought without motion, of awareness without urgency. They are not decaying relics; they are the mature, stable state of the construct’s ecology. The builders made a world, and then the world woke up—not to ambition, but to perception. It sits in the perpetual twilight, feeling its own immense body, tasting the slow changes in its blood, dreaming of its own creation, forever. It is a world that is alive, and its life is the quiet, endless act of paying attention.
