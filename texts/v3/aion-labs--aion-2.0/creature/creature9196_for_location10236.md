They are called the Damp-Speakers, though they have no name for themselves. To name implies a separation they no longer possess. They are the children of the rivet and the root, the inheritors of the humming dark.

Physically, they are wraith-thin and tall, their skeletons elongated by generations spent navigating tight maintenance shafts and lofty catwalks. Their skin is the color of the pale algae that grows in the stagnant pools—a translucent grey-green, veined with darker lines that are not blood vessels, but the fine, filamentous hyphae of the cathedral-cap mycelium that lives within them. This fungus has intertwined with their peripheral nervous system. It blooms across their backs and shoulders in a delicate, frilled mantle of bioluminescent flesh, casting their own faint, blue-shadowed light. Their eyes are wide, black, and lidless, constantly moistened by the mist. They see heat gradients, the infrared glow of active machinery, and the subtle shimmer of water on surfaces. Their true sight, however, is in their feet. The soles are thick, calloused pads, crosshatched with fine cracks that are direct neural interfaces with the mycelial network threading the substrate. They feel the deep, hourly thud of the sleeping piston; they taste the direction of the slow water flow through buried pipes.

They wear no cloth, only living garments. Symbiotic mosses and liverworts grow in curated patterns on their bodies, fed by their sweat and the constant damp. A chest plate of emerald *Brachythecium* moss acts as padding. Fronds of fern sprout from epaulets of rich humus collected in the hollows of their collarbones. Their hair, if it can be called that, is a cascading growth of *Usnea* lichen, dripping softly, beaded with condensation.

Their existence is a liturgy of maintenance. They move through the plateau in solitary silence or in pairs, performing rituals that sustain the dynamic equilibrium. A Damp-Speaker will spend a day carefully scraping the orange rust from a critical load-bearing joint, not to prevent corrosion, but to *guide* it—collecting the iron oxide paste to nourish a nearby patch of copper-tolerant moss that, in turn, binds a crumbling concrete footing. They will speak to blocked drains, using soft, clicking tones generated deep in their throats—frequencies that cause specific resonant vibrations in the pipes, loosening debris. They listen to the replies: the gurgle of release, the change in the drip pattern.

They eat what the machine-forest provides: the soft, metallic-tasting fruiting bodies of fungi that break down wire insulation; the gelatinous algae scooped from the black mirrors; the occasional blind, pale crustacean that filters nutrients from the oily marshes. They drink by pressing their wrists against wet metal, allowing the mycological symbiote to draw moisture directly into their bloodstream through capillary action.

Communication is ephemeral and direct. To share a discovery—a new crack propagating in a main beam, a particularly vibrant bloom of bio-luminescent mold—they will gather and press their foreheads together. The mycelial networks in their skin connect, and knowledge passes as a shared sensation: a smell of hot metal, a visual echo of the crack's path, a throbbing sense of impending structural sigh. Their only communal gatherings are the "Condensation Ceremonies," held in the highest, most exposed grating-walks where the mist coalesces into actual rain. There, they stand motionless for hours, bodies becoming conduits for the water, channeling it down their lichen-hair and cultivated moss-beards into specific runoff channels, singing the water table back into balance with a low, collective hum.

They are born from the world itself. When a Damp-Speaker's body finally fails—when the mycelium has fully consumed their organic matter—the remaining mineral-rich skeleton is placed in a designated "nourishment niche," often a crumbling conduit or an acidic patch of peat moss. The skeleton becomes a scaffold for new growth, and the person's accumulated vibrational memories—their lifetime of listened-to drips and groans—are absorbed by the root network, added to the plateau's slow, collective consciousness.

They do not fear the decay. They are its priests. They understand that the great, groaning edifice is not dying; it is breathing. Their purpose is to match its inhalation of mist with their exhalation of care, to turn the relentless, silent turbulence into a kind of song. They are the sense-organ of a place that became alive, listening intently to its deep, metallic dreams.
