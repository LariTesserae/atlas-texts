The inhabitants of the Upland Sponge are not who first come to mind.

The **Maintenance Cadre** are the official residents, though they would vehemently deny the term. A rotation of four humans occupies the windowless concrete blockhouse for six-month shifts. They are technicians, selected for psychological resilience and minimal social need. Their lives are a regimen of checks: turbine bearings, pumpjack seals, methane scrubbers, data uplinks to the distant oversight bureau. They move between outposts in sealed, balloon-tired vehicles, their world a capsule of dry air, recycled oxygen, and the hum of life support. They see the sponge as a hostile, sodden abstraction, the roar a problem for their ear protection. They do not live here; they endure a posting. They leave no trace because their existence is designed to be an overlay, a temporary film on the eternal damp. In their logs, the plateau is a set of readings. They are ghosts who think themselves solid.

The true inhabitants are the **Sponge-Grubs** (*Vibrio-spongia mechanophaga*). They are not fauna in any standard sense. They are a symbiotic colony: a nematode-like animal host intertwined with a chemo-sensitive fungus and a strain of electricity-metabolizing bacteria. They reside in the interface layer—where the warm effluent pipes from the blockhouse or the generating shed bleed heat into the perpetually cold peat. Here, in a narrow band of steamy, biochemical richness, they thrive.

An individual grub is a pallid, segmented cylinder, the length of a finger and the width of a thumb. It has no eyes. Its forward end is a hardened, chitinous cap, used for boring through the dense root-mat. Its body is studded with sensory cilia that read vibrations and minute electrical fields. This is how they navigate the deafening world; the constant roar of wind and machine is their ocean, and they swim through its gradients. The deep thrum of the pumpjack is a beacon. The whine of the turbines is a shore.

Their life cycle is tied to the machinery. They feed on the algal blooms that grow on warmed surfaces, on the fungal mats that consume lubricant leaks, and on the metallic oxides slowly shed by the structures. In turn, they maintain. Their boring activity aerates the plant-mass around concrete foundations, preventing corrosive buildup of sulfides. Their waste products form a waxy, insulating seal on pipe joints. A generation of grubs will slowly, instinctively, scaffold a leaky steam vent with a cartilege-like excretions, guiding condensate back into the sponge. They are not repairing the machines; they are incorporating them into the ecology of the plateau. The machinery is to them as a hydrothermal vent is to a tubeworm: a source of energy and structure in an otherwise barren, energy-poor vastness.

They communicate via patterned vibrations transmitted through the sponge and the metal of the structures—slow, complex pulses about temperature shifts, chemical concentrations, and the disturbing, rhythmic tremors of the Maintenance Cadre's footfalls. Their collective intelligence is a diffuse, hive-mind of condition and response, spanning kilometers. They perceive the human visits as periodic, violent storms of wrong-temperature, wrong-vibration, and chemical poisoning (disinfectants, fuels). They endure them as the sponge endures a lightning strike.

The final inhabitant is the **Plateau itself**, but not as a metaphor. The combination of the sponge's water-retention, the geothermal heat, and the constant, grinding mechanical vibration has given rise to a **resonant system**. In certain conditions—when a low-pressure system settles and the wind drops to a rare sigh—the plateau hums. It is a pure, low note, emanating from the very basalt bones. It is the sound of water moving through miles of plant-fibre, channeled by unseen fissures, modulated by the hollow spaces around machine pilings. The grubs fall still during this hum. The Maintenance Cadre, if any are present, attribute it to turbine harmonics. It is neither. It is the landscape finding a voice, a byproduct of its new, hybrid anatomy. It is the sound of the womb beginning to know it is a womb.

So, life in the Upland Sponge is a hierarchy of tenancy: the humans, temporary and oblivious; the grubs, permanent and adaptive; and the land, ancient and newly awakened, slowly being tuned by both.
