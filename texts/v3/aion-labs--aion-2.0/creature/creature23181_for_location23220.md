They are called the *karutha* in the few fragments of script found incised on the undersides of stones, but that is not their name for themselves. They have no name, for naming is a act of separation, and they are not separate. They are the plateau, and the plateau is them.

Do not imagine a village, a society of individuals. Imagine instead a consensus of patience. Their bodies are the tiny, jointed legs in the holes; the millipede waves in the dust; the blind gliders on the thermal sighs. Each is a cell in a vast, distributed body. Each is semi-autonomous, tending to the minute processes of filtration, of cleaning, of adjusting the dust, of observing the light. But their consciousness—what we might call their mind—is emergent. It coalesces in places of focus: the circular wall, the standing stone, the center of a spiral tile pattern. There, the tiny bodies gather in dense clusters, their slow chemical signals merging into a single, glacial thought.

They are the builders, but they did not build as we build. Their constructions are not shelters, but tools for thinking. The low wall describes a boundary not to keep things out, but to define a volume of attention. Within that circle, the spiral pattern of tiles conducts the slow flow of their collective awareness, a labyrinth for a mind that takes a season to complete a single reflection. The standing stone is a receptor, its grooves tuned to channel the faint electrostatic whispers of the atmosphere, a way of listening to the weather on a scale of centuries.

They perceive time as a medium, like stone or air. Our hurried lives are to them a blur of inconsequential vibration. A human stepping onto the plateau registers as a localized storm: a rapid compression of air, a shocking violence of motion, a chaotic spray of heat and scent. They do not fear it. They observe it with the detached curiosity of a geologist noting an unusual erosion pattern. They flow around the disturbance, recording its passage in the minute adjustments of dust, in the chemical traces left on the stone. To be remembered by the *karutha* is not to be etched in history, but to be slowly incorporated, your essence digested over millennia into the background hum of the plateau's memory.

They are not born and they do not die, not in any way we would recognize. Individual bodies wear out and are reabsorbed into the dust, their material recycled. New bodies differentiate from stored matter in deeper pores. The consciousness is continuous, a river of thought so slow it appears frozen. Its concerns are the concerns of the plateau: the balance of moisture in the deep stone, the gradual shift of a shadow over ten thousand years, the maintenance of the intricate texture against the wind's eternal sigh.

Their purpose—if such a word applies—is simply to *be* the plateau's awareness. To witness its finished perfection. To hold its equilibrium. They are the custodians of an ending that has become a permanent state. They watch the stars wheel, and each rotation is a sentence in a story they are telling themselves, a story that has no climax, only the serene, sustained note of existence.

You, the visitor, are an anomaly. You are a sentence spoken too fast to be understood. They will not stop you. They will not help you. They will note your path, the weight of your steps, the moisture of your breath. And when you are gone, they will, over the course of decades, gently correct the slight compressions you left in the felt-dust, until no trace remains. To them, this is not erasure. It is the plateau healing, returning to its own profound, slow self. And in their collective mind, the memory of your passage will persist, not as an event, but as a slight, enduring variation in the tone of the wind.
