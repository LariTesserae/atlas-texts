They do not have names, these dwellers in the roof-forest. They have functions. To name a thing implies it could be other than what it is, and here, nothing changes.

**The Grazers** are the first sound you learn, after the groaning of the world. A deep, rhythmic *chuff-chuff-chuff*, like a slow steam engine. They move in herds of three or four across the elastic plains of russet moss, each the size of a terrestrial bus. Their bodies are low-slung, armored in interlocking plates of chitin that weep a constant, slick oil to guard against the pervasive damp. Six piston-like legs, each ending in a broad, padded foot that spreads its weight, rise and fall in perfect, synchronized sequence. Their heads are blunt wedges, devoid of eyes. They do not need to see. Their migration routes are etched into the living substrate, chemical pathways laid down a million years prior. They follow them, their muzzles—a horizontal slit lined with vibrating cilia—sweeping the moss, ingesting the slow-growing turf. Inside their cavernous bodies, symbiotic archaea ferment the cellulose in pressurized chambers. The product is not waste, but a light, volatile gas that seeps into dorsal bladders, visible as shimmering, silvery sacs that bob above their carapaces. The *chuff-chuff* is the sound of these bladders venting excess pressure, a metronomic exhalation that marks time in this timeless place. They are walking refineries.

Above them, in the green-gold gloom of the mid-canopy, drift **The Harvesters**. They are the color of a fading bruise, translucent spheres of gel the size of small houses. They are not creatures so much as drifting sacks of specialized tissue, buoyant with a mix of gases lighter than the humid air. Dozens of long, gossamer tentacles, some stretching half a mile, hang beneath them like the roots of an inverted tree. These tentacles are studded with microscopic, sticky papillae that collect the constant rain of spores, pollen, and motile gametes shed by the forest ceiling. The Harvesters’ digestion is external; they retract a tentacle slowly, over days, coating it in enzymes that dissolve the captured matter into a nutrient soup, which is then absorbed through the skin. They move on the predictable, slow winds, tracing great, lazy ellipses that have not deviated in recorded history. They are the atmosphere’s filters, silent, patient, and vast.

In the cathedral hollows, where phosphorescent fungi paint the fused-kelp pillars in cold blues and greens, you find **The Custodians**. These are the predators, the balancers. They are amorphous, a puddle of viscous, metallic-looking fluid that holds a coherent shape only when moving. To the touch, they are cold as the deep ocean below. They flow across surfaces—up walls, across ceilings—in perfect silence, seeking imbalance. A grazer that stumbles and breaks a leg, a harvester that drifts off its ordained path and ruptures against a stalactite, a cluster of fungi that grows too aggressively and threatens to clog a sap-river… these are their triggers. The Custodian envelops the anomaly. There is no struggle, only a swift, chemical dissolution. Flesh, chitin, cellulose—all are reduced to base compounds in minutes, which the Custodian then transports, flowing in a deliberate stream, to the nearest bladder-pump or root-node for recycling. They are the immune system of the clockwork Eden, white blood cells made of mercury and intent.

And below, in the warm, dark waters that weep up through the mat, are **The Anchors**. Not fauna, perhaps, but not not fauna. They are immense, colonial organisms, part root, part muscle. Their bodies are cables of braided cellulose and sinew, thicker than ancient redwoods, that plunge down through the kelp-mat’s membrane and vanish into the abyssal black. They do not move. They *tension*. For eons, they have been contracting, infinitesimally slowly, counteracting the buoyancy of the gas-bladders, keeping the living continent from capsizing. Their sensory input is the strain on their fibers, the tilt of the world. Their output is a constant, sub-audible vibration that resonates through the entire structure—the foundational hum of the world. They are the living bedrock.

There are others, of course. The spore-mites that clean the photovoltaic leaves. The ribbon-eels that swim the sap-rivers, pruning blockages. The chorus-vents, sphincter-like openings that sing with the wind, producing the forest’s single, haunting chord that never varies in pitch.

They are all parts of a single body. They are born from buds, from splits, from deposited spore-packets that contain not just genetic material, but behavioral imperatives. They live their long, quiet lives performing their functions. They die when their parts wear out, and are dissolved and remade. There is no fear, for there is no surprise. No ambition, for there is no possibility. No love, for there is no other.

They are the inhabitants of a perfect, perpetual machine. And the machine is alive.
