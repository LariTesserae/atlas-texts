They are not tenants. They are secretions. The chamber’s thick, chemical air condenses into carapace and claw; its vibrations coalesce into hunger and motion. What lives here is a catalog of pressures endured, a bestiary of the indispensable.

The true rulers are the films. Before the scuttling, before the grazing, there is the slow, wet slide of the *iridescent cling*. It coats every surface that is not actively burning—a living skin of chemosynthetic bacteria in a hundred desperate partnerships. Some are sulfur-eaters, painting the rock near vents in lurid streaks of mustard and jaundiced yellow. Others are iron-oxidizers, leaving rust-red smears like dried blood. They thrive in the superheated steam, metabolizing poison into a thin, slimy biomass. This is the only photosynthesis here, and it has nothing to do with light. It is a silent, constant conversion of stone’s breath into edible film. The chamber’s primary producer is a creeping, luminous mold.

Upon this foundation, the clickers rise. The armored beetles—*Ignis-scuta* in a forgotten taxonomy—are the chamber’s mobile mechanics. Their bodies are interlocking plates of chitin, forged in hues of dull bronze and burnt umber, perfect camouflage against the basalt. They are not blind, but their eyes are simple lenses that detect only the gradations of the ambient magma-glow. They navigate by vibration and heat. Their world is a map of thermal gradients: the bliss of a freshly cracked warm flow, the danger-signal of a cooling slab that might soon fracture. They scuttle in ceaseless, deliberate patrols, their six hooked legs ticking against the stone like tools. They harvest the bacterial films with rasping mandibles, but their greater purpose seems to be circulation. They trample the films, spreading spores. They clear tiny vents of mineral debris with their hardened heads. Their constant, metallic clicking is the chamber’s steady pulse, a counter-rhythm to the deeper rumbles.

In the steam-plumes, the *vapor-ghosts* dance. These are the wingless insects, pallid and elongated, with antennae twice the length of their bodies. They look like discarded puppet strings given a twitchy life. They swarm the hissing vents, not to feed on the bacteria directly, but to drink the concentrated organic compounds volatilized in the steam—a fine, toxic mist of soup. Their existence is ephemeral and frantic. They are born, live, and dissolve in the billowing clouds, their bodies so delicate that a fall onto the hot rock desiccates them to a papery husk in seconds. They are the chamber’s fleeting thoughts, born of vapor, returning to vapor.

And in the deeper shadows, where the amber light bleeds into profound darkness, the absorbers lie. These are the reptiles—*Calor-saurus*, if one dared to name them. They are heavy, low-slung, their skin a pebbled, ash-gray hide that resembles cooled pumice. They have no eyes; their faces are smooth slopes, save for the line of their jaw, which is pockmarked with deep, heat-sensitive pits. They see the world as a topography of blazes. A cluster of beetles is a warm, buzzing knot. A major fissure is a sun. They move with a tectonic slowness, their bellies often flush to the warm stone. Their metabolism is a direct loan from the mountain’s heart; they spend hours motionless, basking in geothermal radiation, digesting the handful of beetles or a mass of vapor-ghosts caught in a sticky tongue-lash. They are the chamber’s stored energy, its slow-burning capacitors. Their occasional, low hoot—a sound felt in the sternum more than heard—is the place sighing in its sleep.

There are no predators in the sunlit sense. There is only transformation. A beetle becomes absorber waste, which fuels a different bacterial strain. A vapor-ghost’s husk crumbles into mineral dust. Even the absorbers, when they finally die, do not rot. The heat bakes them into a stiff, leathery monument that slowly sinks into a sinter deposit, becoming another layer in the floor.

They live by the chamber’s single, brutal rule: you must be of the heat. To cool is to die. To be cut off from the chemical steam is to starve. Their lives are perfectly adapted loops within the greater, geological loop of the mountain’s forging. They are not individuals; they are processes wearing flesh. The click of the beetle is the sound of a piston. The swarm of the vapor-ghost is the visible bleed of a pressure valve. The silent bask of the absorber is a thermal regulator. Together, in their loud, hot, crowded world, they are the living symptoms of the mountain’s heartbeat. They are the life that the engine incubates, not for purpose, but simply because it can.
