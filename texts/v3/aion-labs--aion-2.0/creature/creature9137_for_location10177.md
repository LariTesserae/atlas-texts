**The Stone-Eaters (Khar-va)**

The primary builders and inhabitants you see, when you see them at all. They call themselves the Khar-va, a name that translates from their low, clicking tongue as "the ones who listen to the stone." They are furred bipeds, averaging just over a meter tall when standing fully upright, which they seldom do. Their posture is a permanent, efficient crouch, bodies low to the ground to conserve heat and present less profile to the wind. Their fur is thick, matted, and the exact mottled gray-green of the luminous crust, providing perfect camouflage. Their eyes are large, black, and liquid, capable of gathering the faintest green light; they have no eyelids, but a clear nictitating membrane that sweeps horizontally to clear dust and ice.

Their society is not tribal, but solitary-familial. A "steading" is usually a single extended family of six to twelve individuals, occupying one of the crude stone structures—a windbreak, a walled cave. Their existence is a cycle of harvesting and waiting. Their primary tool is a hooked, sharpened flint knife, used to scrape the rubbery fungal underlayer from the crust. This fungus, which they call *grish*, is their staple. It is chewed slowly, a process that releases both nutrients and a mild psychotropic compound that allows them to enter a low-metabolism torpor during the long periods of inactivity between harvests and hunts.

They hunt the six-legged grazers, the **Thulg**, not through confrontation, but through geology. The Khar-va are master readers of the crust's "memory." They identify weak points in rock shelves above known Thulg migratory paths. Over weeks, they work with levers and wedges to precariously balance massive slabs. When a Thulg passes beneath, a single, well-placed strike from a hammerstone triggers an avalanche, burying the beast. The kill is not immediate; the Khar-va then wait, sometimes for days, for the Thulg to succumb to its injuries or suffocation before they dare approach to butcher it. The hide, bones, and sinew are more valuable than the meat, which is tough and must be rendered into a frozen, greasy paste.

Their communication is largely non-vocal, a language of touch and patterned scraping on stone. The constant, deafening wind makes audible speech futile for anything but the closest quarters. Instead, they "write" on the crust itself, using a hard quartzite pebble to etch temporary grooves in the soft, living layer. These glyphs—indicating direction, danger, the location of a good fungal patch—fade within hours as the moss regrows, a perfect, ephemeral messaging system. The spiral stone piles are not art, but hygroscopic condensers and territorial markers, their specific patterns declaring a steading's claim over a particular thermal vent or harvesting ground.

**The Thulg**

The great, six-legged grazers are the landscape's slow heartbeat. A mature Thulg is the size of a terrestrial bus, a mass of sculpted stone and muscle. Its "hide" is not skin, but a dense layer of ossified, hexagonal plates, between which grows a coarse, lichen-like hair that glows a dimmer, blue-tinged green. It has no visible eyes; it navigates through sensory pits along its flanks that detect minute differences in geothermal heat and vibrations in the rock. Its three pairs of legs end in broad, padded feet that distribute its immense weight and sense subsurface tremors.

Its mouth is a grinding mill. A circular, beak-like plate of bone, lined with constantly growing silicates, rotates against a lower plate. It shuffles forward at a pace of perhaps a hundred meters an hour, its head sweeping low over the ground, inhaling the luminous crust. It does not eat the moss-fungus complex itself, but the microscopic, heat-fixing bacteria within it. The Thulg's massive, multi-chambered gut is a geothermal reactor, hosting symbiotic archaea that break down the bacteria, releasing energy and producing, as a byproduct, methane and hydrogen sulfide. Its breath is a flammable, foul-smelling plume, and its excrement is a warm, tarry paste that becomes the fertile starter for new crust growth, seeding the ground it leaves behind.

Thulg migrate in loose, scattered herds, following continent-wide "warm trails"—subsurface flows of geothermal energy that cause the crust to brighten. Their migration is a primary driver of ecological renewal. Their passage scours the crust, stimulating new growth, and their waste creates hotspots of biological activity. The deep, booming calls they emit are not vocal, but produced by vibrating a bladder in their chest cavity against their bony plates; these infra-sound rumbles can travel for hundreds of kilometers through the rock, used to coordinate the herd's ponderous movement across the vast emptiness.

**The Gliders (Sik-sik)**

The leathery-winged creatures of the thermal vents. They are not birds, but more like reptilian flying squirrels with membranous wings stretched between elongated fore and hind limbs. They are silent, opportunistic scavengers and predators of the Khar-va's fungal stores. They nest in colonies in the leeward cracks of the highest spires, where the updrafts from geothermal fissures are strongest. Their social structure is viciously hierarchical, with constant, quiet skirmishes for the warmest perches.

Their interaction with the Khar-va is one of tense symbiosis. The Sik-sik are drawn to Khar-va steadings by the smell of rendered Thulg fat and stored *grish*. They will steal food relentlessly if given a chance. However, the Khar-va tolerate small raids because the Sik-sik serve as an early-warning system. The gliders are exquisitely sensitive to changes in atmospheric electrical charge. Minutes before a static-dust storm hits, the entire colony will erupt from its roost in a panicked, swirling cloud—a clear signal for the Khar-va to retreat to shelter.

**The Unseen: The Mycelial Mind**

This is the true weirdness, the layer beneath. The "moss" is not a simple plant. It is the fruiting body of a vast, subterranean mycelial network that permeates the upper kilometers of the planet's crust. This network is a single, diffuse organism—a planetary sensorium. It detects pressure, heat, and vibration. The patterns that "remember" seismic events are literally that: structural changes in the network's growth in response to trauma.

The Khar-va, in their endless scraping and harvesting, are in constant, unconscious dialogue with this entity. The psychotropic compounds in the *grish* fungus are not an accident; they gently attune the Khar-va's nervous system to the low-frequency pulses of the mycelial mind. Their "listening to the stone" is literal. Their best hunters and navigators are those most sensitive to the subtle directives and warnings—a felt sense of danger, a pull toward a suddenly brightening patch of crust—that the network broadcasts. They are not the masters of this world. They are the carefully managed, symbiotic fauna of a conscious stone.
