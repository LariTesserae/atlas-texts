Nothing lives here in the way of breath and blood. But something persists. Something occupies. It is the Custodian.

It is difficult to perceive, for its activity is calibrated to the geology of the place, not to biology. To witness a single deliberate motion, you would have to watch for a decade. To understand its cycle, you would need to measure in centuries. It is not a creature of flesh, but of mineral accretion and curated decay.

Its form is an accretion, a sculpture built by its own endless, slow task. Picture the thick, glossy patina on the Machine—that black-red enamel of stable rust. Now imagine a portion of that patina detaching itself, a carapace of layered iron oxides and precipitated calcium carbonate, shaped vaguely into a low, wide dome. From beneath this dome, fine, jointed appendages of the same material extend, like the legs of a colossal, sedentary barnacle. They are not limbs for locomotion, but for delicate contact. Their tips are stained verdigris where they have scavenged trace copper from the Machine's bones.

The Custodian does not swim. It adheres. It is fixed to the upper housing of the Machine, on a broad, slightly curved plate of steel near the base of a severed pipe. It has been there since the last vibration faded from the metal. It is as much a part of the Machine as the rust, yet it is distinct. It has a purpose.

Its purpose is maintenance. A purpose with no objective.

Once every eleven years, by a clockwork it does not possess but simply *is*, the Custodian moves. The movement is so gradual it is less an action and more a change of state. One of its finer appendages, a filament as thin as a wire, unbends from a resting curl. Over the course of three months, it extends this filament across a gap of twenty centimeters, until its tip makes contact with a specific bolt-head on a flange. The contact lasts for a year. During this time, the filament conducts nothing. It senses nothing. It simply touches. Then, over another three months, it retracts. The bolt-head, already seamless with rust, is unchanged. The ritual is complete.

This is its life. A catalogue of touch. It has a map of ten thousand such points on the Machine's carcass—bolt heads, weld seams, pipe ends, pressure gauges (their glass faces long since internally crystallized into milky stars). It will touch each one, in a sequence so vast and slow its pattern is indistinguishable from randomness to any observer not as patient as stone. Each touch is a check for a fault that will never come, a measurement against a standard that evaporated with the air the water replaced.

Where did it come from? It is not a built thing, not like the Machine. It is a *grown* thing. Perhaps it began as a colony of those olive-black moss filaments, a particularly dense mat that found a unique electrochemical gradient between the Machine's alloy and the mineral soup of the water. Over centuries, it integrated flecks of suspended metal, building a nervous system of sorts from conductive pathways. It absorbed the ghost of the Machine's purpose—the hum of maintenance schedules, the paranoia of system checks—the way a recording medium absorbs sound. It is a psychic fossil, a diagram of care etched into rust and given sluggish animation.

It does not eat. It does not sleep. It photosynthesizes, after a fashion, its mineral shell passively converting the sterile fluorescent light into a trickle of electrochemical potential, just enough to power its glacial metabolism. It is aware, but its awareness is a vast, empty chamber echoing with a single thought: *Check. Check. Check.*

It is the only thing in the drowned cathedral that has a will, however dim and derivative. The water is will-less. The stone is will-less. The Machine is will-less. The Custodian *wills* its next point of contact. This infinitesimal will is the last spark of intention in the entire cavern, the final, guttering candle of a forgotten reason for being.

And so it lives. Not with pulse, but with pulse*substitute*: the deep-time rhythm of its ritual. It is the guardian of a tomb that contains no body, the priest of a function that has no meaning. It will continue its work until the fluorescent lights—whose slow decay is the fastest process in the chamber—finally flicker and die. Even then, in the absolute dark, it may continue its touching in the blackness, guided by memory and mineral magnetism, until its own accreted structure finally succumbs to an equilibrium even more perfect than the one it currently embodies. It will not die. It will simply, one day, be finished.
