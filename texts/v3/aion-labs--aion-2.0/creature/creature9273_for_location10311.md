They are called the Dripstone Wardens, though they have no name for themselves. They are what lives here, what has always lived here, as much a part of the basin as the weeping stone and the sweating metal. To see one is to understand the settlement's grim equilibrium: they are the confluence of its warring elements.

They rise from the walkways or emerge from the doorways of the iron structures not as visitors, but as extensions of the place itself. Their forms are vaguely humanoid, but that is where any familiarity ends. Their skeletons are assemblies of salvaged pipe, valve housings, and reinforcing struts, all thick with layers of corrosion—bruise-colored rust and that bright, poisonous verdigris. Over this armature, a secondary body has grown: a dense, sculpted matrix of the black peat, hardened by the calcite secretions of the fern roots into a brittle, stone-like carapace. It cracks and re-forms constantly, like a drying mudflat, weeping thin streams of water from a thousand hairline fractures.

Their faces, if they can be called such, are smooth plates of this peaty calcite, featureless save for deep, water-filled sockets. Within these pools, colonies of the luminous moss thrive, fed by faint galvanic currents between the dissimilar metals of their underlying frame. These twin pools of chartreuse light provide their only expression—a slow, rhythmic pulsing that synchronizes with the distant thump of the great pump. When they move, which they do with a ponderous, deliberate slowness, water squeezes from their joints with a soft, sighing creak.

They are silent, but they are not mute. They speak to the hamlet, and it to them, in a language of hydraulics and resonance. A slow, three-fingered hand—its digits made of copper tubing capped with nodules of hardened peat—will reach out and tap a stressed pipe. The resulting *bong*, deep and sonorous, travels through the humid air and the structure’s skeleton. Others, elsewhere, will answer with a tap of their own, or by redirecting a trickle of condensate onto a specific hot joint, producing a sharp hiss. Their communication is one of maintenance, a distributed consciousness focused solely on the balance. They listen to the pump’s laboring heartbeat, and their moss-lights flicker in agitation at any stutter. They hear the groan of the great ferns shifting, and they will sometimes gather at the pilings nearest the sound, adding their own weight, a humanoid counterbalance to the tree’s slow tilt.

Their purpose is written in their movements. They are the guardians of the concession. One might spend an entire day, motionless as a statue, observing a single rivet weep a rusty tear, before slowly applying a poultice of more peat and calcite to stem the leak. They cultivate the glowing moss, scraping it from vital gauge-glasses with a careful claw, yet encouraging its growth across load-bearing beams where its subtle electrical appetite seems to stabilize the corrosion, turning it into a stable, glittering patina. They are farmers of decay, shepherding the process, directing it away from collapse and towards a perpetual, soggy stasis.

They feed, if it can be called feeding, on the environment itself. They stand for hours under the constant warm drip from the fern canopies, letting the water percolate through their porous bodies, filtering out minerals. They press their hands against the moss-covered metal, absorbing the faint, nourishing currents. Sometimes, they break off a piece of their own brittle carapace and consume it, the act one of renewal rather than hunger, replacing old, leaky material with new.

There is no anger in them, no joy, only a profound, dripping patience. They are the will to build, incarnate in the very materials that the water seeks to dissolve. They are losing, of course. They have always been losing. But their loss is measured in centuries, in the gradual rounding of a corner, in the deepening pit beneath a piling. Their victory is the next thump of the pump, the next drop diverted, the next century of damp equilibrium. They live in the perpetual now of the plink and plonk, the hiss and the thump, their glowing eyes fixed on the endless, slow emergency that is home.
