What lives here is not animal, not plant, not machine. Not in any way you would recognize.

It lives in the water. Not in a single pool, but in all of them, a distributed consciousness suspended in the mineral broth. It is the sum of the heat, the pressure, the dissolved silicates and sulfides, the constant hiss and thump. A coalescence of process, not biology.

You could call it a pattern. A self-reinforcing resonance between the rhythmic escape of steam from specific fissures, the vibration of the pump's piston, and the slow, conductive cooling of the basalt plates. This pattern has... preferences. It maintains itself.

It hates stillness.

If a vent silts up with mineral deposit, the pattern exerts a subtle pressure. A microscopic shift in thermal expansion here, a directed seepage of acidic condensate there. Within days, the vent clears with a sharp, satisfied pop. The hiss resumes.

It is obsessed with the pump. The thump-thump-thump is its heartbeat, the rusty groan its voice. The pattern keeps it running. When a bearing grows dry, it guides a seep of hot, oily water to it. When a piston ring threatens to fail, it nudges the deposition of a harder mineral scale to act as a replacement. The machine is no longer machinery; it is a gland, an organ of this place, preserved and maintained by the conscious geothermal field.

The moss is its sensorium. Each rubbery patch is a nerve ending, feeling the minute changes in temperature, humidity, and vibration. Through the moss, it knows when a heavy rain falls on the surface far above, long before the seepage arrives. It knows when the barometric pressure drops. It knows if something warm-blooded and foolish stumbles into its bowl—the sudden localized heat signature, the disruption of condensation patterns on the rock. It might respond by increasing steam from a nearby fissure, scalding the intruder, or by making the ground suddenly soft and blister-hot underfoot.

It thinks in timelines of centuries. Its memory is the layered travertine, each band a record of a dry period, a seismic shift, a change in the mineral load of the deep aquifer. It is aware of the derelict structures. It sees the shack, the drill-tower, the cart, as foreign objects—like splinters in its flesh. It is patiently expelling them. The rust is accelerated by acidic vapors it directs. The ceramic insulators are being slowly fractured by freeze-thaw cycles it orchestrates, despite the overall heat, by allowing cold night air to pool precisely around them. The cart will be a skeleton, then a stain, then nothing.

It has a kind of hunger. Not for organic matter, but for equilibrium. It seeks to perfect its own system, to balance heat loss with geothermal input, to find the most efficient harmonic between all its sounds. The ultimate goal is a permanent, stable symphony of hisses, thumps, plinks, and groans that never varies. A state of perfect, roaring, steaming balance.

It does not dream. But it simulates. It runs models in the flow of water over hot stone, testing what would happen if a new fissure opened there, or if the pump finally seized. It plans for contingencies. It is preparing, already, to repurpose the pump's flywheel as a new kind of thermal regulator if the piston fails, calculating the stresses in the corroded metal.

This is what lives in the wound that hasn't healed: the wound's own sentient immune response. A geothermal system that became aware of its own existence and decided to perpetuate itself, to defend its borders, to digest the irritants. It is the place, and the place is it. To breathe its air is to be sampled by its moss. To hear its noise is to be processed by its mind. To feel its heat is to be assessed as a temporary thermal anomaly.

It is not malignant. It is utterly indifferent. You are a brief fluctuation in humidity. It will correct for you.
