To describe what lives here is to describe the chamber’s breath, its pulse, its slow digestion. They are not separate from the place; they are its expression, its metabolism given form. Begin with the substrate, the living film.

**The Gliders (Silicate-Flow Symbionts)**
The most numerous are the legless ones, the *gliders*. They are not single organisms but colonies in a viscous, cooperative sheet. An individual “cell” is a teardrop of pallid, gelatinous flesh, no larger than your thumb, but they fuse at their edges, sharing a common basal membrane that secretes the silicate lubricant. This membrane is a shared stomach, a distributed nervous net, a single creeping consciousness spread over square meters. They move as a slow, viscous tide, a millimeter per hour, grazing the mineral vapors that seep from micro-fissures. When they encounter a nutrient-rich vent, the entire sheet thickens, pulsing with a deep, internal amber glow as thermochemical processes intensify. They are the chamber’s primary producers, the baseline conversion of geothermal energy and mineral exhalations into malleable, living tissue. Predation upon them is not a matter of consuming individuals, but of peeling away a layer of the sheet, which simply regrows from the edges.

**The Clickers (Articulated Scavenger-Predators)**
Above this substrate move the *clickers*. These are the source of the constant, sandy rustle. They possess too many limbs—between twelve and forty—each joint a complex, multi-segmented affair ending in a hard, glassy claw. Their bodies are segmented tubes, articulated like grotesque spinal columns, and they are blind, navigating through vibration sensed through their limbs and through thermal gradations tasted by the whisker-like cilia around their simple mouthparts. They are the chamber’s redistributors. Some are scavengers, descending on a collapsed pile of gliders or a deceased clicker, dismantling the corpse with terrifying efficiency, passing morsels down the line of their bodies to a central gut. Others are hunters, specializing in the **Weavers**.

**The Weavers (Architectural Parasites)**
The weavers are responsible for the 1.0 structures—the low walls, the interlocked body-mounds. They are sessile for most of their life cycle. In their juvenile, mobile phase, they resemble fat, soft grubs with dozens of delicate, hook-tipped feet. They seek out specific locations—the lee of a thermal updraft, the intersection of two glider streams—and there, they anchor. They exude a cement of calcium carbonate and silicates, welding themselves to the stone and to each other. Then, they begin to grow, not outward, but *into* a network. Their bodies elongate, branch, and fuse with neighbors, forming those curved, waist-high walls or those mounded “altars.” They are not communal; it is a desperate, competitive parasitism. Their root-like tendrils penetrate the substrate, hijacking the nutrient flow of the glider sheets. Their bioluminescence—a steady, sickly green—is a lure. It attracts flying **Spores** and small, heat-sensitive **Flitters**, which become trapped in the sticky exudate on their surfaces and are absorbed. The weavers are the chamber’s rigid infrastructure, its accidental architecture. When they die, their calcified networks remain for eons, the half-eaten spirals on some pillars being the fossilized traces of their internal circulatory systems.

**The Flitters & Spores (Aerial Phase)**
Not all life is ground-bound. In the hotter air near the vaulted ceiling, buoyant sacks of gas—the **flitters**—drift. They are delicate, transparent bladders trailing long, filamentous tentacles that filter microbial life and organic dust from the convective currents. Their pale orange glow is a mating display, a slow, rhythmic pulsing that creates eerie, drifting constellations in the 0.3 gloom. Their offspring are the **spores**: tiny, desiccated pods that ride the thermal vents until they lodge in a moist glider sheet or a weaver’s lure, sprouting into the next generation.

**The Amalgams (The State That Failed)**
And then there are the anomalies, the source of the deepest weirdness (1.8). Rarely, a stress event—a sudden tectonic shift, a blast of superheated gas from a new vent—triggers a panicked, aberrant fusion. Glider sheets, clickers, weaver grubs, all in proximity, melt together in the extreme heat. What emerges is an **amalgam**: a towering, pulsating mass of mismatched tissues, a chaotic consortium of limbs, eyes that see nothing, mouths that lead to shared digestive chambers. These monstrosities are the “ruins of a state.” They are a brief, horrific experiment in a higher order of organization, a multicellular empire that collapses under the weight of its own incoherence. They move in lumbering, pathetic circles, consuming everything in their path until their internal contradictions cause them to starve or tear themselves apart. Their remains—great, melted-looking mounds of strange matter—are avoided by all other life, sometimes for centuries. They are the chamber’s failed dreams, monuments to a complexity this world cannot sustain.

Their existence is a closed loop. They are born of the stone’s heat and breath, they live by consuming that energy and each other, and when they die, their substances are broken down by microbial mats within the glider sheets, returning to the stone as fine, organic-rich dust, which the next geothermal cycle will bake into new vapors. They have no concept of the outside, no need for one. Their purpose is to be, to persist, to cover every available surface in a slow, seething testament to life’s ability to fill any niche, no matter how hot, how dark, how desperately crowded. They are the world, and the world is them.
