They call themselves the Seleni, a name that means, in their slow, clicking tongue, "Those Who Listen." Their bodies are adapted perfectly to the cool, heavy embrace of their world. Tall and slender, their bones are light and flexible, their limbs elongated to allow for graceful, unhurried strokes through the water. Their skin is not one color, but a living canvas of shifting chromatophores—patterns of silver, pale blue, and deep green that ripple and reform in response to light and mood, mimicking the dappled sun-gold that filters from above. They have no hair; their heads are smooth, often etched with delicate, self-applied scars that denote lineage and craft. Their eyes are large, black, and absorbent, capable of seeing in the faintest glimmer. Gills flutter in elegant lines along their ribs, and between their long fingers and toes stretch fine, translucent webbing.

Their lives are a liturgy of care. Each Seleni is born into a specific stewardship. There are the Frond-Tenders, who spend their days gently guiding the growth of the great, sail-like leaves, using bone hooks to train them into arches or canopies. There are the Sponge-Dwellers, who maintain the porous homes, clearing passages and cultivating the symbiotic glow-worms that provide soft, bioluminescent light in the deeper chambers. There are the Current-Singers, who manipulate the great gates of vine and bone, humming complex, low-frequency songs to direct the flow of water and the drifting schools of luminous fish, which are both their neighbors and their primary source of food. Their tools are extensions of their bodies: needles of sharpened whale-tooth for splicing plant fibers, knives of volcanic glass for harvesting rubbery fruits, and bags woven from tough, silken algae.

Time, for the Seleni, is measured in growth and decay, not in days and years. A child is considered to have reached maturity not at an age, but when the archway they were assigned to tend as an infant finally closes its curve to meet its opposite pillar. Their history is not written, but grown. The oldest part of the city, the Heartwood, contains kelp-ribs so ancient their horn-like surfaces are covered in intricate, spiraling glyphs—not carved, but formed by generations of Tenders subtly influencing the growth patterns, a living record of droughts, great harvests, and the passing of revered elders. They believe, or perhaps know, that the city is conscious. It is not a deity to be worshipped, but a vast, sleeping intelligence—the Mountain's Dream, they call it. Their tending is a conversation. A Frond-Tender will place their palms against the hard, smooth surface of a main rib and feel, through the vibration in the water and a subtle tingling in their skin, a sense of contentment, or need, or warning. To harm the city wantonly is unthinkable; it would be like severing one's own limb.

Their society is serene but not static. Disputes are settled by patient mediation in the Clearings, the open avenues where the light falls cleanest. The goal is always to find the solution that best maintains the balance of the whole organism. They have art: they weave fleeting, beautiful sculptures from buoyant reeds that are released to ascend toward the distant surface, messages to a world they never visit. They have music, a fusion of their own clicks and hums with the resonant calls of the grazers and the crackle of the reef. A Seleni's death is a quiet event. The body is taken to the Edge, where the city's grown foundations give way to the steep drop into the abyss. There, it is entrusted to the slow currents and the larger, shadowy cleaners of the deep, completing the cycle of nutrient and return. To live here is to be a single cell in a vast, calm body, to find purpose not in conquest, but in attentive, loving listening.
