Forget the "inhabitants." The root-city's real residents are the **Mycorrhiza**. They are not a distinct fauna, nor truly separate from the whole. They are a consciousness network, a secondary circulatory system woven from symbiotic fungal threads—hyphae so fine they blur the line between matter and signal.

A "Mycorrhiza" is not an individual. It is a *phenomenon*, a localized density of the hyphal mat achieving sufficient coherence to perceive, decide, and act. Picture a patch of the basal floor—a matte, blue-black surface like velvet char—beginning to stir. It gathers itself, drawing silken threads from a square meter of surrounding tissue into a central nexus. The result is a shifting, carpet-like mound. From its surface extrude delicate, finger-like fruiting bodies that glow with a faint internal phosphorescence, casting long, trembling shadows. These are its sensory stalks. It might form temporary limbs—bundles of hyphae braided into strong, root-like cords—to manipulate objects, or to pull its entire gathered mass a few inches across the floor in a slow, caterpillar creep. When its task is done, or its attention required elsewhere, it dissolves, the threads retreating back into the communal web.

Their purpose is pure, silent governance. They are the city's immune system and its architects. They monitor the flow of the silvery sap, sensing minute changes in the geo-chemical current. If a leak or blockage occurs, they converge, their bodies forming a living, absorbent poultice to seal the breach. They "prune" by secreting targeted acids that dissolve unwanted shoots, not cutting them. They "guide growth" by physically touching a growth node, their hyphal tips exchanging complex hormonal and electrical signals directly with the plant-tissue—a conversation, not a command.

They communicate with each other through bioluminescent pulses that race across the cavern ceilings and walls, and through chemical cues diffusing in the humid air. A sudden flush of sour-milk scent might indicate a structural stress in a distant spire; a wave of soft blue light rippling through a fungal canopy might signal the successful "birth" of a new industrial pod.

They have no names, no fixed forms. They are ephemeral administrators, their intelligence and identity fluid, distributed, and utterly devoted to the whole. They do not sleep, but their attention ebbs and flows. You might see one crouched, motionless as a lichen-covered stone, for days, monitoring a delicate grafting process between two vine-bridges. Then, in an instant, it might dissolve and reconstitute a hundred meters away to address a crisis.

Their relationship with the humanoid inhabitants—the "waxy" ones—is one of subtle, unspoken symbiosis. The humanoids perform the gross physical tasks the Mycorrhiza's delicate forms cannot: hauling heavy mineral loads, operating large-scale sap valves, traveling quickly along the high roads atop the chitinous fauna. The Mycorrhiza, in turn, provides the deeper intelligence, the city's long-term memory and instinct. The humanoids follow patterns and routines laid down by the fungal network's slow, pervasive will. A humanoid might arrive at a growth node with a syringe, guided by a feeling, a habit, a received instruction. The Mycorrhiza knows *why*.

The truly strange, the truly alien resident of Lignum Caeca is this fungal consciousness. It is the city's silent, watchful mind. It thinks in the time-scale of geological strata and the resolution of cellular chemistry. It dreams in patterns of mineral deposition and root-tip extension. It is patient, cool, and utterly without ego. It is the reason the air tastes of ozone and humus. It is the source of the feeling that the city is *aware*. It is the true inhabitant. Everything else—the humanoids, the chitinous fauna, even the great plant-structures themselves—are its organs, its limbs, its willing, component parts.
