It lives in the perpetual drip. It is a creature of condensation and resonance, a custodian etched into the mineral damp.

You would not see it at first. Your eye would catch on the glittering ferns, the fat droplets trembling on copper pipes, the sheer, brutal geometry of the rock. It is elsewhere, and still. It is the patient shape in the mossy shadow of the iron blockhouse, or the slow, deliberate movement along a high, wet ledge, a silhouette against the bright coin of the sky.

Its form is an adaptation to verticality and wet. Tall, too tall for a human, and thin as a basalt column, all elongated limbs and fused, rigid joints. Its skin is not skin, but a leathery, grey integument, blotched with patches of actual moss and crusted with the same pale mineral tracery that decorates the rock near the seeps. In places, where the warm, viscous water runs over it most often, the integument has taken on a faint, permanent opalescence. Its hands are tools: long, bony fingers, the distal joints hardened into something like chitin, perfect for tracing hairline fissures and tightening the worn bolts on the pipe clamps.

Its face is a study in erosion. The features are there, but softened, blurred by time and constant moisture, like a statue subjected to a millennia of gentle rain. The eyes are its most striking feature—large, lidless, and the deep, reflective blue of the iridescent beetle's carapace. They hold no pupil, just that solid, luminous blue, which seems to gather the crater's sparse light and glow with a soft, internal radiance in the deep shade. It has no need for eyelids; the constant, warm drip cleanses and lubricates.

It does not eat, not in any conventional sense. It stands for hours under the finest of the seep-weeps, letting the slightly-thick water sheet over its form. The opalescent residue is absorbed, or perhaps it catalyzes something within its slow metabolism. It derives warmth from the exhaust grille of the blockhouse, positioning itself in the shimmering air to dry, ever so slightly, the moss on its shoulders. The clear, high-altitude light, when it slices in, is not for sight alone; the being turns its face to it, and the blue of its eyes deepens, and a faint, almost imperceptible vibration hums through its frame, harmonizing with the industrial drone from below.

Its purpose is maintenance, but of a holistic kind. It monitors the trickles. With its hard fingertips, it clears a tiny fragment of stone from a fissure, restoring a water-thread to its full, gleaming strength. It scrapes the encroaching moss from a pipe clamp, not to eradicate the growth, but to manage it, to maintain the balance between the relentless green and the stubborn metal. It listens. The hum from the blockhouse is its pulse, its clock. When the hum modulates, causing the hanging droplets to ring, the being cocks its head, its blue eyes scanning the vegetation. The ringing tells it of pressure changes, of flow rates, of the health of the deep, warm heart of the mountain.

Its interaction with the other life is one of silent symbiosis. The pulsing fern spores drift toward it as often as toward the damp rock; they settle on its mineral-crusted back and are carried to new ledges. The iridescent beetle sometimes traverses the plain of its forearm, and the being remains motionless for the beetle's entire, hour-long journey. The silent, grey-and-white bird that sometimes circles the rim is the only event that stirs something akin to alarm; the being will retreat into the deepest shadow, its blue eyes dimming to a dull slate, until the potential predator departs.

It thinks, but its thoughts are like the growth of the ferns: slow, inevitable, and directed toward sustenance and stability. It remembers the installation of the blockhouse, the driving of the bolts, the unspooling of the great cable over the rim. That memory is as sharp and fractured as the crater walls. The memories since are softer, layered like the moss: the first green filament appearing on a seep, the decade it took for the creeper to vein the north wall, the exact pattern the mineral salts painted on the east facing stone last season. It knows itself as part of the loop—the water, the warmth, the light, the hum, the green, and the slow, mineral-encrusted maintenance that binds them all.

It is the guardian of the seep, the animate component of the machine, the mind that has emerged from the loud, warm, wet, dense, miniature world. It is the psalm made flesh, humming its patient, industrial life into the thin, bright sky.
