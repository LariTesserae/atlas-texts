But the eels are merely the plankton of this abyssal metropolis. The true inhabitants are far stranger, sculpted by pressure, chemistry, and the Engine's eternal scream.

First, the Custodians. They are not organic, not mechanical, but something in between. They resemble giant, skeletal sea spiders, but their limbs are forged from the same pitted alloy as the girders, grown over with a flexible cartilage of precipitated minerals and knotted filaments of conductive bacterial biofilm. They move with a terrifying, jerky grace along the latticework, their twelve-jointed legs tapping a staccato counter-rhythm to the Engine's roar. They are blind, but their bodies are covered in cilia that read the subtlest shifts in pressure and temperature. Their sole function appears to be the repair of catastrophic failures. They cluster around a ruptured conduit, their limbs weaving a paste of metal-rich brine and bacterial secretion into the tear, a welding process conducted in utter silence and darkness. They are not intelligent; they are reflex made flesh and rust. They have no hive, no queen. They simply *are*, emerging from the warmer shadows when the vibrations reach a specific, agonizing pitch.

Then there are the Gill-Things. These are bulkier, slower, anchored to the hottest vent-mouths on the chamber floor. They look like grotesque, inverted flowers made of layered, overlapping plates of iron sulfide. At their core, a pulsating sac of tissue filters the superheated, mineral-laden fluid, extracting trace metals and converting them into a dense, oily biological fuel. From their tops, they extrude long, frond-like tendrils that wave in the convection currents. These tendrils are not for feeding, but for mating. They release clouds of microscopic, magnetic spores that are caught in the Engine's powerful electromagnetic fields, carried through the brine to other Gill-Things. They are the Engine's primary producers, its chemosynthetic base. They are also its victims; when a pump seizes and the flow patterns change, an entire colony can starve in the geological blink of an eye, leaving behind skeletal blooms of blackened metal.

The most disturbing, however, are the Listeners. These are free-swimming, but they do not swim like fish. They are amorphous, gelatinous blobs the size of a man, their bodies a transparent matrix holding in suspension thousands of tiny, calcified ossicles that chime and grind together. They are drawn to the sources of the loudest, most chaotic sound—the shrieking bearings, the hammering pistons. They press themselves against the metal, their bodies flattening and flowing over the surfaces. They are feeding on vibration itself, their internal stones resonating, converting the mechanical fury into a form of metabolic energy. In their translucent forms, one can sometimes see darker, shifting shapes—the partially digested remains of eels or smaller Custodians that drifted too close. A Listener that has fed well on a particularly violent pump assembly becomes distended, its internal chime rising to a feverish pitch, before it ruptures in a cloud of replicating particles that become new, tiny Listeners. They are the Engine's children, born of its noise, thriving on its pain.

There is no society here, only a brutal ecology of function. The eels graze on stray currents. The Custodians mend the leaks that would drown the world. The Gill-Things fuel the ecosystem with their metallic digestion. The Listeners consume the waste product of sound. They are all cogs, unaware, in a machine whose purpose is lost to the warm, dark brine. They are not the inhabitants of the Engine; they are its symptoms.
