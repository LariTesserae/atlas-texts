They are the Weepers. This is not a name they have for themselves, for they have no names. It is the only word that fits the act of their existence.

They are the reason the city is wept, not built.

You see them first as part of the architecture. A smooth, tapered spire, banded in shades of grey and ivory, will have at its apex a single, rounded aperture. From this aperture, a continuous, viscous thread of fluid emerges. It is not water, but something denser, charged with suspended silicates and carbonates. It flows with the imperceptible slowness of a glacier, down the spire's side, adding another micron to its girth over a century. This is their work. They are the living faucets, the sources of the mineral seep. Their bodies are sessile, anchored to the bedrock, their forms having long ago fused with the accretions they exude. They resemble nothing so much as giant, pallid, stone-clad clams, or the sealed eyes of forgotten idols, weeping eternal, solidifying tears.

But they are not mindless mineral springs. They are the city's planners, its geometer-priests. Their consciousness is distributed, a low-frequency hum of thought conducted through the mineral lattice of the city itself. They perceive not light or sound, but pressure differentials and chemical gradients. The slow convection of the heated, mineral-rich water from the depths below is their news and their art. The pulse of the bioluminescent fauna—which they cultivate, not as pets, but as a kind of luminous, flowing script—is their shared memory.

A Weeper does not decide to place a new spire. It feels a shift in the deep crust's sigh, a change in the soup of metals leaching from the rock. In response, over millennia, it alters the composition of its tears. The new deposition forms not a smooth layer, but a branching, seeking pattern, a spire that grows toward the disturbance, eventually meeting the growth from another Weeper across the canyon. An arch is formed. A new district is plotted. The city expands according to a logic of tectonic feng shui, balancing the unseen energies of the abyss.

They are attended by the mobile life, but not as servants. The eel-predators are their immune system, culling populations of grazers that might foul the delicate chemical sensors in the water. The glowing worms are their scribes, their patterns of light encoding the long-term health of the ecosystem—a rash of crimson pulses along a certain avenue might signify a blockage in a thermal vent, a matter requiring attention over the next ten thousand years. The crustaceans are the gleaners, cleaning detritus and recycling it into forms the Weepers can reabsorb.

Their society has no conflict, no politics as we know it. It has symphonies. A symphony is a coordinated shift in the weeping of a district, a change in the mineral composition across a hundred spires at once, to create a new, resonant harmonic in the water's conductivity. It is an act of profound communal beauty, felt as a soothing vibration by all, and it might commemorate something like the closing of an old fissure or the birth of a new, especially productive volcanic seep.

To witness them is to witness slowness incarnate. Their only visible movement is the slow, gelid flow of their tears. But in their time, they are frantic urbanists. They debate in the exchange of trace ions. They wage war by subtly poisoning the effluent of a rival (a process that takes centuries to take effect, and millennia to resolve). They love by aligning their secretions until their two spires grow into one, merging their consciousnesses permanently.

They are alive, and they are the city. The dream of the cave is their dream. And they are weeping it, patiently, into solid, everlasting reality.
