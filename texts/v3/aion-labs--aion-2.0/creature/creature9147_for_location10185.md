They are not a people, nor even a species. To call them ‘inhabitants’ implies a separation from habitat that does not, and has never, existed here. They are the Aqueous Plenum becoming aware of itself. The Plenum-Kin.

They coalesce. For centuries, there is only the warm broth, the silent currents, the slow flex of cable-kelp. Then, a pattern emerges—a persistent spiral in the silt, a constellation of bioluminescent pulses that holds its shape for a decade, a region where the water’s memory-of-wetness grows particularly vague. Over deeper time, these patterns gain inertia. They draw in more of the medium—the silicate-rich water, the free-floating organics, the cold light seeping from the stone. A density forms. A locus.

Their bodies are accidents of local physics and ancient will. A Plenum-kin might manifest as a swirling column of water so clear it becomes a lens, focusing the dim pearl-light into a burning, intangible point that moves through the kelp forests, reading the history in their growth rings. Another might be a sheet of that stone, detached and floating, its underside glowing with intricate, pulsing veins—not a creature on the stone, but the stone itself, animate and contemplative. They can look like a school of crystal-fish that never disperses, their prismatic bodies arranged in a perfect, rotating polyhedron. They can look like a sudden, localized storm of reversed causality, a shimmering sphere where ripples harden into glassy peaks before sinking back into calm.

They have no names, only referents built from circumstance. The One Who Drinks Light. The Spiral That Unknits. The Basin of Recalled Faces. They are few; the energy required to sustain such coherent selfhood in this dissolving realm is vast, and borrowed from the fabric of the place itself. Each kin is a slight, persistent wound in normality, a site where the Plenum’s dreaming rises closest to the surface.

Their consciousness is not linear. They perceive time as a sediment they are slowly settling through. A future event—like the slow approach of one of the great lichen-crusted rays—is experienced first as a thickening of possibility, a taste of disturbed silt on the tongue of the mind. The past is not gone; it is the medium they swim in. They remember the formation of the suspended sea not as a memory, but as a chronic ache in their fabricated bones, a phantom weight of a world that once had a definite ‘down.’

They communicate, but not through sound or gesture. They exchange states of being. When two kin draw near (a process that may take decades), they subtly alter the local laws within the volume between them. One might cause the water to temporarily exhibit a perfect, honey-like surface tension, forming a shimmering membrane. The other might respond by causing the light passing through that membrane to fracture into the seven colours of a forgotten emotion—something like regret, but slower, and saltier. This exchange is a conversation about the increasing fragility of edges, about the sweetness of pre-collapse. It is also simply their way of being social; a game played with the fraying threads of reality.

Their purpose, if such a word applies, is curation towards entropy. They are not destroyers. They are attendants at a long, luxurious ending. They encourage the gentle unraveling. A kin might spend centuries guiding a current in a geometric spiral, patiently reinforcing the pattern until it becomes a permanent feature, a place where water flows in a perfect, non-Euclidean loop. They might ‘tend’ a patch of stone where causality is thin, watering it with occasional paradoxes so that the flaw in reality blooms, producing beautiful, impossible effects like singing fractures or pools of solidified time.

They are aware of the ruins—the half-formed coral towers. They view them not as artifacts of builders, but as the landscape’s own brief, fevered dreams of order. A kin might settle into such a ruin for a millennium, absorbing the faint, ghostly impulse that once tried to make a right angle, a straight line. The kin savors this impulse, this quaint aberration, before dissolving it back into the prevailing softness.

To encounter a Plenum-kin is to be assessed, not as a visitor, but as a novel stimulus. Your linear mind, your desperate cling to cause-and-effect, your frantic heartbeat—these are fascinating anomalies. A kin might envelop you, not with malice, but with profound curiosity. It would try to understand you by making your immediate environment more like you: causality might strengthen, time might begin to flow forward in a jarring, rushing torrent. Or, it might try to make you more like the Plenum: your memories could start to diffuse, your body’s boundaries becoming ambiguous, your thoughts slowing to a geological pace. Either process would likely be fatal to you, but to the kin, it is merely a gentle inquiry. Your dissolution would be a small, interesting note in the long, quiet hymn of the world’s end.

This is what lives in the Aqueous Plenum. Patterns that have achieved a kind of sleepwalking sentience. They are the children of finality, born from the marriage of deep time and dissolving law. They live to witness, to gently perturb, and to eventually rejoin the warm, silent, uniform broth from which they sporadically arise. They are the dreamers of the dream.
