They are the Clay-Born, though they do not call themselves that. To each other, they are simply the People, and this is the World.

Physically, they are lean and compact, built for the low ceilings and narrow passages. Their skin, washed perpetually in the damp, glows with a peculiar luminosity—not like the moss, but a soft, pearlescent sheen, as if their very pores have learned to reflect the scant light. It is pale, but not sickly; it is the color of wet clay, of riverstone, of the shelf-fungi's underside. Their eyes are large, dark, and liquid, capable of widening to drink in every photon from the moss-lights. They blink slowly, accustomed to the constant, gentle precipitation of mist. Their hair, if grown long, is kept tightly braided or coiled to avoid the dripping cups; most wear it cropped short, a dark, damp cap.

They dress in layers of felted fungi-fiber and cured whisper-rat pelts, soft and absorbent. The clothing is loose, allowing the constant warmth to circulate, but cinched at wrists and ankles to keep out the ever-present, curious beetles. Their feet are broad, splayed, and tough as cured leather from a lifetime on the smooth, wet brick.

Their society is a silent democracy of constant, mutual adjustment. There is no single chief, but there are deep experts: the Cup-Tender, who knows the song of each drip-cup and the subtle shifts in the aquifer's seepage; the Moss-Wife, who tends the luminous patches and harvests their light without killing the glow; the Rat-Whisperer, who reads the skittering chorus in the ceiling and negotiates the balance between pest and partner. Authority is fluid, ceded to the one whose senses are most attuned to a particular need.

They speak, but sparingly. In the loud percussion of the drips, the voice is a fragile instrument. Their language is tactile, built on touch-signs pressed onto a forearm, and a spoken component of soft consonants and hummed vowels that ride under the *plink-plonk*. They have no word for "sun," but a dozen for different qualities of flow in the central channel. Their names are often onomatopoeic: a man who moves with a particular, soft step is called *Plonk*; a woman with a talent for hearing the faintest scratch of a larvae in the brick is *Skrit*.

Their days are not divided by light and dark, but by the Great Emptying and the Quiet Murmur. The Great Emptying is the communal labor of emptying the hundreds of drip-cups into the channel system, a rhythmic, sloshing ceremony that changes the chamber's acoustics for a blessed, quieter hour. The Quiet Murmur is the time after, when the drips are few and far between, and the soft conversations by the channel rise to fill the space.

They are a people of profound intimacy and profound solitude. Privacy is a physical impossibility in the dense, occupied niches, yet a deep interiority is cultivated. To sit, hands busy mending a net, while listening to the unique pattern of drips from your own section of ceiling, is considered a form of meditation. Their greatest art is pottery—the shaping of new drip-cups on a slow-turning wheel. Each cup is a biography: the maker's thumb-print, the curve to catch a specific seep, the slight imperfection that gives its *plink* a distinctive pitch. A person is often buried with their favorite cup, placed over their heart, to keep a personal rhythm in the silence that follows.

They fear not darkness, but silence. A stopped drip is a minor crisis. A sudden cessation of the whisper-rats' rustling sends a chill deeper than any cold. Their world is a negotiated truce with entropy, and every day is spent gently nudging the balance back toward the beautiful, noisy, damp equilibrium they call "the Hold."

They are not prisoners of the deep. They know of the Up, the world of blistering light and desiccating wind, of frantic, unpatterned noise. They send scouts there, sometimes, to trade felt and dried crayfish for rare salts or metals. These scouts return haggard, ears bleeding from the cacophony, skin cracked. Their tales of the vast, hungry emptiness above are told in hushed tones, a reinforcement of the chosen truth: the Underwell is not a refuge. It is a masterpiece. They are its careful, loving keepers, the human component of a living, breathing, dripping engine of survival. They have not adapted to their world. They have *become* it, cell by cell, drip by drip, until the line between keeper and kept is as smooth and worn as the bricks beneath their feet.
