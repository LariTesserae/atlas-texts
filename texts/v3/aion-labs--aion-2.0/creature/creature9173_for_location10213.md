They call themselves the Tenders of the Veil, though the term is more a concept hummed against the skin than a spoken word. To an outsider, they might first appear as another growth of the deep moss, another shadow in the perpetual twilight. Their skin is the color of the mesa’s stone where it is damp—a pale, greyish ochre, dappled with darker patterns that shift subtly with health and emotion, like the slow chromatophores of a deep-forest frog. These are not tattoos, but a living response to the filtered light and the chemistry of the air. They are hairless save for fine, downy filaments on their limbs and scalp, which trap the mist and channel its beads to the skin.

Their eyes are large, black, and liquid, absorbing every photon in the gloom. They have no whites; the entire orb is a dark, reflective sphere, giving them a perpetually serene and depthless gaze. Their frames are slight, bones light as bird bones, muscles long and sinuous for climbing and balancing in the aerial lattice of their home. Their hands and feet are broad, with long, flexible digits and opposable toes. The pads of their fingers and toes are slightly textured, like the skin of a tree frog, granting them preternatural grip on wet bark and slick vine.

They wear no clothing as such. Their bodies are draped and netted in living filaments—a cultivated, symbiotic moss that grows in specific patterns across their shoulders, backs, and loins. This moss-web drinks the constant mist directly, cooling them and providing a constant, minor nutrient exchange. For ceremony or travel across the more exposed rim, they might drape themselves in cloaks of woven fern-down or beetle-wing shawls that shimmer with fugitive rainbows.

Their society is a silent symphony. Speech, in the clamorous green din, is often inefficient. They communicate through a complex language of touch—pressure of fingertips on forearm, the brush of a foot against another’s calf as they pass on a narrow branch—and of controlled breath. The exhalation pattern that calls a child is different from the one that signals a predator’s approach (though true predators are few, mostly large arboreal snakes and a silent, owl-like bird). Song is reserved for the cycles of the mist: low, harmonic drones that hum through the chest and are felt as much as heard, aligning their communal rhythm with the mesa’s breath.

They are not farmers, but weavers and guides. They do not plant seeds in the soil, for the soil is sacred decay, the mesa’s memory. Instead, they cultivate the air. They train vines along specific pathways to strengthen bridges. They gently harvest the spore-dust from certain fungi to encourage growth on new platforms. They collect the sweet, dripping nectar from orchid blossoms not with cups, but by letting it pool in the large, waxy leaves of specific plants they have positioned for the purpose. Their most important tool is a long, hollow thorn, used to siphon water from the droplets that gather in the great bearded curtains of moss—a direct, careful transfer from the air to a gourd or to the mouth.

Time is their medium, not their taskmaster. A project is the lifetime training of a bridge-vine. A decision is made over days of silent consensus, felt in the changing quality of the communal breath during the evening mist-rise. They have no chiefs, only Listeners—those whose dappled skin-patterns are most sensitive to the health of the forest, who can place an ear against a tree trunk and hear the faint, thirsting crackle of a root deep in the stone, or the happy flow of water within.

They fear not death, but stillness. To become a body that does not decompose, that does not feed the moss and the fungi, is the only failure. Their dead are laid in the hollows of the great buttressed trees, covered in a paste of glowing mushroom spores, and consumed back into the green, damp dream within a turning of the moon. Their children are born in the dense, warm dark, and their first sensation is the taste of the mist on their parents' moss-clad skin.

They know of the world below only as a myth of thirst and sharpness—a warning whispered on the wind that moans at the rim. The cracked plains are a ghost story. Their entire universe is the soft, crowded, noisy, eternally damp womb of the canopy, a world sustained not by river or rain, but by the faithful daily exhalation of the sleeping earth far beneath them. They are its caretakers and its flesh, its voice and its silence, living in an endless, gentle now that began when the first mist condensed on the first leaf, and will end only when the last leaf finally drinks its last drop.
