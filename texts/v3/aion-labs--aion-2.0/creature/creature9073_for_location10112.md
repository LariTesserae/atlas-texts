It is called the Keeper of Stillness, though it has no name for itself. It lives here, as much a feature of the chamber as the basalt cube or the lily-column. Its form is an accretion, a coalescence of the chamber's own materials and processes. Over centuries, the fine pale silt, the filaments of emerald algae, and the minerals leached from the limestone have gathered and been bound by a gel-like mucus secreted by the glasswort. This composite has shaped itself around a humanoid armature of crystalline calcium carbonate, formed droplet by droplet from the water itself. The result is a figure that seems carved from sea-foam and old stone, sitting cross-legged upon the central cube, directly in the path of the descending spear of light.

It is neither truly alive nor dead, but in a state of perpetual, low-energy being. Its skin is a rough, porous crust, verdigris-green where the algae thrives, pale ivory where the stone shows through. Its face is a smooth oval, featureless save for two deep recesses where eyes might be, pools of shadow that hold a faint, phosphorescent gleam, like sunlight filtered through a hundred feet of water. Its long, slender fingers are permanently interlaced in its lap, webbed with translucent membranes that ripple in the gentle current. From its back and the crown of its head, fronds of the coarse brown seaweed grow, not as parasites but as integrated extensions, swaying like hair in the slow hydraulic sigh.

Its existence is defined by reception and release. All day, it sits motionless in the solar column, its porous body drinking the light. The energy sustains the symbiotic algae within its crust, which in turn exudes the nutrients that maintain the brittle mineral structure. It does not eat, does not sleep. Its only movement is a rhythmic, almost imperceptible pulsing of its torso, synchronous with the inflow and outflow of water through the vents—the chamber breathing, and it breathing with it. Once per day, as the golden disc of light completes its crawl across the floor and begins to fade, it performs its sole conscious act: it slowly, stiffly, raises its right hand and extends a single calcified finger. From the fingertip, a single, perfect droplet of denser, mineral-rich water forms. It hangs, gathering the last of the light like a prism, before detaching and falling to the cube's surface with a soft *plink* unheard in the watery medium. The droplet rolls into a nearly invisible channel carved into the cube's top, a channel that leads to the crack where the glasswort forest grows. This is its tending. This is its purpose.

It has no thoughts, only a vast, placid awareness—of the warmth, the pressure, the sound of the flow, the dance of the silt. It is the sensory organ of the room itself. It remembers nothing, for nothing changes here to remember. It anticipates nothing, for the cycle is eternal. It simply *is*, a node in the circuit of water and light, a silent witness to the slow, patient work of the sea as it begins, over millennia, to claim this ordered space. It will sit here until the mortar finally fails and the walls slump, until the light-shaft is choked with sediment, and even then, it may simply become a lump of benign stone, covered in algae, its final offering droplet still poised, forever, at its fingertip.
