In the drowned cathedral, the true inhabitants are not merely the things that swim, but the things that *remember*. They are the Choir.

A Choir-member is not a single organism. It is a colony, a slow thought made flesh and glass. Its base form is a bulb of living silica, grown from the same secretions that build the towers, anchored at a major nexus of the chitin-bridges. From this translucent, fist-sized node extends a web of fine, glowing filaments—hyphae of nerve and nutrient—that branch through the surrounding architecture, threading into the pores of the towers, fusing with the sponge-matrix. These filaments are its senses and its synapses. They taste the minute chemical shifts in the water: the panic-pheromones of a hunted school, the slow, mineral-rich exhalations from the geothermal vents, the reproductive signals of the pulsing jellyfish-lanterns. They feel the vibrations of every passing creature, every groan of shifting bone-stone. This data is not stored in a brain, but in a cascading chemical code within the colony's shared fluid, a living library of current-events that stretches back decades.

The most striking feature is the Choir-member's “voice.” From the central bulb dangle a dozen or more delicate, ribbon-like appendages, each several meters long. They are not arms, but living instruments. Composed of a complex gelatinous matrix, they are lined with thousands of cilia that can vibrate at precise frequencies. They do not move to grasp, but to *sing*. Their song is a low, subsonic thrumming, a patterned series of pressures felt more than heard. This song is a constant broadcast, a real-time interpretation of the data its web has gathered. A coordinated hunt by the eels becomes a staccato rhythm. A tower’s structural stress becomes a deep, worrying drone. The approach of a large filter-feeder is a rising, inquisitive melody.

The Choir does not think as we understand it. It does not have desires, fears, or a sense of self. It is a process. It receives the city’s state and translates it into harmonic vibration. But here lies the deeper weirdness: the city *listens*. The other inhabitants are attuned to these songs. The drifting cephalopods adjust their routes in response to a certain harmonic cluster, avoiding areas broadcasting “predation active.” The schools of finger-eels are subtly herded by the patterns, their collective motion guided into the most productive filter-feeding currents. The predatory eels themselves seem to use specific chords as signals, timing their ambushes to a shift in the ambient drone. The Choir does not command; it conducts. It makes the hidden patterns of the ecosystem audible, and the ecosystem dances to its own revealed music.

A Choir-member’s lifecycle is measured in centuries. It grows as the city grows, extending its filaments along new construction. It might slowly migrate, a bulb detaching and re-anchoring elsewhere, leaving behind a dormant network that slowly decays. When it dies, its silica bulb, now inert, is gradually overgrown and incorporated into the tower’s structure, a fossilized library. New colonies bud from existing nodes, sharing fragments of the chemical memory, so the city’s history—its long, slow narrative of shifting currents and population blooms—is passed down, not through genes, but through ingrained chemical habit.

To witness a Choir-member is to see the city’s nervous system laid bare. In the total dark, its filaments glow with a soft, internal blue, mapping the lattice-work in a faint nebula of light. Its singing ribbons move with a hypnotic, slow-motion grace, each independent yet part of a harmonious whole. You would feel its song as a complex pressure on your skin and in your lungs, a physical sensation of information. It is the closest thing this world has to a priest in its cathedral: not praying to a higher power, but incessantly, beautifully, articulating the liturgy of the deep. It is the city speaking to itself, in a language of vibration, ensuring the eternal, warm equilibrium of the dark.
