Here, the residents are not occupants. They are the architecture, the geology in motion. They are the geophages.

To see one at rest is to mistake it for a weathered outcrop. Its body is a massive, low-slung hull of interlocking plates—a carapace of chitin infused with silicates, stained and crusted with the same ochre, grey, and black lichens that paint the stones. An adult stretches twenty meters from blunt, rounded head to the gradual slope of its rear. It stands less than three meters high, a living mesa. Its underside is hidden, a secret territory of thick, pillar-like legs—dozens of them, each ending in a broad, padded foot that spreads its immense weight without a sound.

They have no eyes, not as we know them. Their heads are blunt wedges, dominated by a broad, ventral mouth. The mouth is not an orifice but a living mill: a series of overlapping, ridged plates of bio-mineralized enamel, harder than granite. When feeding, these plates vibrate infinitesimally, a rasp that reduces rock to a fine, moist dust. They ingest this dust, along with the lichen and the thin, rubbery mosses. Their digestive tract is a slow, alkaline sea, where symbiotic microbes over centuries leach rare nutrients from the inorganic paste. What emerges behind them is not waste, but a fertile, damp silt—the only true soil on the plateau, deposited in long, gentle berms along their highways.

They sense the world through pads on their feet that read vibrations in the stone, and through chemoreceptors in fleshy pits behind their jaw plates. They taste the air for the scent of water, for the pheromone trails of the herd, for the distant, faint tang of a decaying elder. Their perception is a map of gradients: of moisture, of mineral content, of the geothermal warmth that seeps up through fissures. They navigate by the planet's own magnetic whisper, and by memory written in stone.

They move in herds of tens of thousands, yet they are not social in any frantic way. Their coordination is a slow consensus, a shift in pressure and scent that ripples through the collective over days. A decision to alter course by a degree is a week-long debate conducted in silence. Their migration routes are their culture, their history etched into the continent. From above, these routes are not random. Over centuries, they trace vast, faint spirals, concentric rings, and branching patterns that resemble fungal mycelium or circulatory systems. These are the glyphs—unintentional, perhaps, but purposeful in their geological impact.

They are born from leathery eggs buried deep in the warm, moist silt berms of the herd’s path. A hatchling is the size of a large dog, soft-shelled, and it spends its first decades scuttling beneath the bellies of its elders, feeding on the rich, pre-processed silt. It takes a century for its plates to harden and acquire their lichen gardens. Their lives are long, five, six hundred years. Death comes not from predation—there are no large predators here—but from the eventual failure of some vast, internal process. A geophage simply stops. It settles onto its belly, its leg-pistons locking. Within days, its internal heat fades. Then the decomposition begins, which is itself a form of industry.

The geophage's corpse becomes a sudden, temporary ecosystem. First come the ghoul-mites: minute, six-legged carrion-eaters that boil up from the silt. They are pale, blind, and number in the millions. They strip the softer tissues in a frenzied, silent wave. Then, as the carcass cools, the mineral-feeders arrive: slow, slug-like organisms that dissolve the chitin-silicate plates, returning them to the earth. The process can take decades. The final skeleton—a great, arched cage of mineralized ribs—may stand for centuries more, a landmark for future herds.

Other life exists in their shadow. On their backs, in the gardens of lichen, tiny, flightless “lichen-birds” hop and peck. These are not true birds, but warm-blooded, feathered reptiles the size of a thumb, their metabolism sped up to a dizzying blur compared to their hosts. They keep the lichen growth in check and eat parasitic spores. In the damp, deep cracks of the plateau, unrelated to the herds, blind, white worms as thick as a man’s arm filter the meltwater for microorganisms. They are the only other large fauna, and they never see the sky.

The weirdness—the 1.1—is woven into their existence. The shadows that pool too deeply seem to gather around resting herds, as if the light is being siphoned to fuel some deep, internal process. The perfectly spherical boulders found occasionally are, in fact, not boulders. They are geophage eggs from a bygone era, fossilized and polished by eons of wind, their internal life long extinguished. And sometimes, on utterly still nights, a herd will stop, every individual motionless. Then, from the stone beneath them, comes a faint, harmonic hum—a resonance as the plateau itself seems to vibrate in sympathy with their collective, silent presence. It is a sound felt in the teeth and the bones, a low note from a world thinking with a mind made of stone and slow, cold blood.
