They are called tenders, and they are not separate from the world. They are its hands.

You see them first as stillness within stillness. Figures, perhaps, but their outlines blur into the latticework, their coloration a perfect match for the gunmetal-indigo bark. They are tall, slender, and jointed in ways that suggest function over symmetry. Their limbs are segmented, like the internodes of a giant bamboo, but with articulate, fibrous muscles visible beneath a translucent, woody integument. They have no heads. Instead, a cluster of sensory organs—pits that sense thermal gradients, nodules that read electrical fields, fronds that taste the chemical composition of the air—crowns the top of their torso. From their backs sprout delicate, fern-like structures that are not decorative; they are photosynthetic panels and long-range signal receptors.

They move only when necessary, and then with a slow, deliberate grace that seems to ignore normal inertia. One might detach itself from a wall where it was perfectly camouflaged, stepping onto a vertical surface as if it were level ground, its feet adhering not by suction but by a localized manipulation of gravity. They tend the machinery-forest. You watch one approach a pulsing, ceiling-dwelling filtration organ. With fingers that are more like precise, branching twigs, it probes a seam in the organ's translucent skin. A slight discoloration is there—a potential necrosis. The tender's fingers exude a gel that solidifies into a living bandage, and it then plucks a single, glowing nodule from a nearby power-vine and implants it into the gel. The organ’s pulse steadies.

They do not speak. Communication is a silent exchange of data. When two tenders meet, they pause, and their dorsal fronds align. A faint shimmer passes between them—a structured pattern of light, a packet of information about pressure differentials in a distant conduit, or the detection of a spatial fold growing unstable. Then they part, each moving to address the need.

They are grown, not born. In quiet, humid vaults deep in the substrate, you might find the nurseries: bulbous pods, like fleshy fruits, from which a fully-formed tender emerges when the system’s diagnostics signal a need for more maintenance. They have no childhood, no memories beyond the operational logs they access. Their purpose is encoded in their structure: maintain equilibrium. They prune overgrowth with edges of hardened, sharpened leaf-material. They guide new growth by applying hormonal pastes to branching points. They harvest the crystalline energy-nodules when they reach maturity, carrying them in internal sacs to where the system’s charge is low.

But there is a deeper layer, a haunting echo in their design. In their precise, geometric movements, you see the ghost of the original architects. The way they measure space with a sweeping gesture of a limb, the way they pause as if considering an aesthetic outcome as well as a functional one—it hints that the tenders are not just robots. They may be the final, degraded incarnation of the builders’ will. Perhaps the builders, in their ultimate act of merging with their creation, distilled their consciousness into these simple, recurring protocols. Each tender is a fleeting thought of that vast, sleeping mind—a thought about balance, about repair, about the beauty of efficient form.

They are not individuals. They are sentences in a manual that is constantly being rewritten by erosion and growth. If you stood before one, it would not perceive you as a living thing, unless you posed a threat to the system. Then, with that same silent, efficient motion, it would neutralize you—perhaps by entombing you in a rapidly-hardening resin, or by leading you into a gravity well where you would fall forever in a five-meter loop.

They live here. They are the caretakers of the silent factory, the gardeners of non-Euclidean space. They walk on the ceilings and the walls, they drift through the thickened air-fronts to catalyze growth, and in the constant, shadowless glow, they work, forever, to keep the dream from collapsing into chaos or rigidity. They are the peace of the machine. They are its prayer.
