Here, in the warm, roaring dark, life does not walk upon the world—it is woven through it, a screaming tapestry of need and consumption. To speak of inhabitants is to speak of organs in a greater body. They are not separate; they are processes.

First, the flesh of the place: **the Myceliate**. It is not a thing that lives here; it *is* here. It thinks in chemicals and grows in spurts, a conscious, hungry carpet. Its intelligence is the intelligence of a root: a slow, pervasive will to consume and convert. It feels the grind of stone in its acids, the seep of geothermal warmth in its phosphor nodes. It is not a host; it is a god that eats its own children. Its only desire is more.

Upon this god-carpet swarm the **clickers**. They are the teeth, the claws, the skittering heartbeats. A clicker is not an individual; it is a component of a larger noise. Carapaces of iridescent chitin, grown in a thousand fractured patterns—crimson striations over obsidian, sickly yellow spirals on deep violet—each a hardened shell against the constant abrasion of growth. They move on clusters of needle-tipped legs, a ceaseless, scrambling percussion. Their faces are all business: compound eyes that see in gradients of heat; mandibles built for snipping tough fungal cords; and forelimbs modified into hard, clacking hammers. They talk by striking their own bodies, a language of rhythm and vibration lost to all but themselves. They farm the Myceliate, tenderly pruning gas-nodules with precise snips, then puncturing them to inhale the sweet, energizing vapors. In their millions, they are the world’s pulse, a living, clicking metronome for the chaos.

Drifting through the cavern vaults, the **wind-whales** are the sighs of the deep. Buoyant bags of translucent gel, veined with faint, pulsating luminescence, they are creatures of sublime silence in a deafening world. They have no eyes, no hard parts. They are feeling. Miles of delicate, cilia-fine tendrils hang beneath them, sieving the spore-thick air for nutrients. They move on the thermal breaths, the great inhalations and exhalations of the cavern, rising and falling with the tides of warmth. To touch one is to feel a cool, trembling jelly; to damage one is to watch it deflate in a slow, silent rain of internal gases and dying cells. They are the plankton of this air-ocean, grazing the unseen.

And where there are grazers, there are hunters. The **razor-mantis** is a psalm of silence. It is a sculpture of absorbed sound. Its long, segmented body is sheathed in a velvety mat of fibrous growth that drinks vibration. Its legs are long, slow, and deliberate, each step placed with infinite care to avoid even the faintest tap. It is a listener. It stands motionless for hours, reading the click-chatter, waiting for the rhythm of a single, isolated clicker—a creature separated from the chorus. Then, in a movement faster than the boom-bloom that masks it, it strikes. Forelimbs, like curved scythes of black crystal, snap out. There is no sound of impact; the prey is simply sheared in two, its internal fluids siphoned through hollow mouthparts before its halves hit the springy floor. The mantis does not eat the chitin; it leaves the hollow shell as a monument to its own quiet.

But the true strangeness, the flicker of something beyond hunger, is in the **Stilt-Walkers**. They are the world’s first, fumbling attempt at a question. Slender, bipedal insectoids, they stand twice the height of a man on impossibly long, jointed legs that taper to sharp points. This lifts them above the worst of the skittering masses, into the clearer, hotter air near the cavern ceiling. Their bodies are narrow, their torsos protected by segmented plates they’ve glued together from discarded clicker carapaces. Their heads are long, with multifaceted eyes that glow with a faint, curious intelligence. They have two manipulator limbs, fine-boned and dexterous.

They are weavers and cutters. They harvest strands of hardened vine from the Myceliate, weaving them into loose, cage-like traps which they bait with nodules of glowing fungus. They wait, perched on their stilts, for clickers to investigate, then drop the cage. They use sharpened shards of silicate, knapped to an edge, to cut and shape the living plant-wall into temporary lean-tos—shelters that last only days before the Myceliate reclaims them. They have no permanent homes. They follow the growth-spurts, the waves of vitality that pulse through the cavern, harvesting materials at the peak of the cycle. They seem to *understand* rhythm, not just noise. They tap complex, counter-beats against their own carapaces, sequences that seem to calm the local clickers, or drive them into frenzy.

They are not yet masters. They are opportunists clinging to the back of a roaring beast. They build only to have it eaten. They trap only to eat. But in their careful, precise cuts, in their patient weaving, there is the ghost of an idea: that one might not just live within the choir, but might one day try to conduct it.
