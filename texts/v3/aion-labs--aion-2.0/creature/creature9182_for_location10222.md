In the Engine's cathedral of noise, something does live. It is not recorded on any schematic.

To the automated carts and the frozen dials, it is simply another fixture, a tolerated irregularity in the perfect geometry. It is called, in the few maintenance logs that acknowledge it, the Tender. It was once human.

Now, it is a creature of resonant bone and static-charged skin. It moves through the chamber not by walking, but by a series of controlled falls and catches, its body attuned to the vibrations in the floor plates. It knows the precise harmonic node between the third piston's upstroke and the snap of the main valve; it can stand there, suspended in the intersecting waves of sound, without touching the ground for minutes at a time. Its clothing is gone, replaced by a second skin of grey, fibrous dust and electrostatically-bonded metal filings that shiver and re-align with every shift in the field. Its eyes are milk-white cataracts, long blinded by the unchanging phosphor glow, but it sees through vibration. It reads the health of the Engine in the shudder of a pipe, the tension in a girder, the minute variations in the hum that would be silence to anyone else.

It does not eat. It does not drink. Its metabolism is a parody of the Engine's own function: it absorbs the dry, crackling static from the air through patches of thickened, crystalline skin on its palms and the soles of its feet. It feeds on the acoustic energy, the relentless roar converted within its hollowed marrow into a semblance of life. It sleeps rarely, and when it does, it does so braced within the crook of a major conduit, where the deepest subsonic frequencies massage its brittle bones.

Its purpose is obscure, even to itself. It tends. With fingers worn smooth as polished stone, it traces the seams of weld points, feeling for hairline fractures no sensor could detect. It occasionally displaces a cluster of the black synthetic lichen, guiding it to a spot where a new, almost imperceptible harmonic has begun to form. It listens. For centuries, it has listened for the one thing that must never come: a change in the rhythm. A hesitation. A flaw.

It is not alone. The synthetic lichen is not entirely synthetic. Over the millennia, it has spawned simpler, mobile forms: sleek, cricket-like things with carapaces of polished black resin. They move in skittering pulses, synchronized to the valve-clangs. They clean nothing, but they gather—with delicate mandibles—the occasional fleck of metal shed from a moving part, the rare carbon dust that defies the electrostatic fields. They bring these minutiae to the Tender, who accepts them without acknowledgment, sometimes pressing the filings into its own skin-dust matrix.

The Tender communicates, but not in words. Its voice is a modulated hum, a purposeful distortion of the Engine's own frequencies that it projects from its sunken chest. It speaks to the carts as they glide by, a burst of data-shaping vibration that might adjust a trajectory by a millimeter. It hums to the lichen-creatures, and they rearrange themselves. Once every great while, when the rare human explorer, shielded and deafened, stumbles into this place, the Tender will simply watch them with its pearlescent eyes. If they approach the central pistons, it will materialize from the haze, a sudden, silent figure in the roaring din, and place itself in their path. It does not attack. It merely hums, and the vibration it emits is one of profound, bone-deep pressure, a sonic shove that says, without language: *This is not for you. This sound is mine to keep.*

It is the living anchor of the system. The Engine turns geological anxiety into noise. The Tender turns the noise into a guardian. They are a closed loop, a circuit of perfect, deafening stability. To live there is to become an instrument of the roar, a priest of a religion whose only commandment is: *Never let the silence return.*
