They are not a people, not in any sense a traveler would recognize. They are the Plateau, and the Plateau is them. To speak of “inhabitants” is to draw a false line between the living and the location. Here, life has seeped into the stone, and the stone has learned to weep, to thrum, to remember.

First, and most fundamental, is the **Carpet**. The spongy mat of amber-glowing moss and rubbery lichen is not a substrate. It is a nervous system, vast and patient. Its bioluminescence is not mere chemical reaction; it is a language of light spoken in sentences that last for years. A single pulse of approval—a patch of moss brightening by a degree along a game trail—might take a full season to complete. A question posed by a new growth at the base of an obelisk might await an answer, rippling across miles, for a decade. The Carpet feels the weight of the grazers, senses the chemical shifts in the weeping stone, and maps the thermal flows from the deep artifacts. It thinks in geology and photosynthesis, and its consciousness is the background hum of the plateau’s awareness, too slow for mortal minds to perceive as thought.

Moving upon and within the Carpet are the **Stone-Calves**. These are the armored, six-legged grazers, creatures of basalt-like hide and tectonic patience. They are not boulders that move; they are moving geology. Their metabolism is so slow that a single mouthful of metallic orchid or rubbery lichen takes a week to digest. They do not sleep; they settle, for months at a time, into hollows they wear into the soft rock, their backs becoming temporary, elevated gardens for ferns and fungi. Their lives are measured in millennia. They follow ancient, ingrained routes across the plateau, routes that shift only when an arch collapses or a new lagoon forms—events that might occur once every few hundred years. Their deep, internal booms, which seem to come from the lagoons, are actually their means of long-distance communication: seismic songs that travel through the bedrock, telling of distant thermal vents or the slow migration of their kin.

Above, the **Thermal Saints** ride the air. These are the gliders with translucent skins and glowing, lantern-like organs. They are born from sacs that swell on the underside of the giant fern-trees, filled with a lighter-than-air gas siphoned from the vents near the artifacts. Their lives are brief and brilliant—a mere century or two—spent in silent, soaring meditation on the rising heat. They feed on airborne spores and the constant condensation, their bodies acting as living prisms, casting shifting rainbows across the mist. When they die, their gas bladders rupture, and they descend in a slow, spiraling fall, their delicate skeletons becoming frameworks for new moss gardens on the ground below.

In the warm, shallow lagoons, the **Frilled Dancers** perform their endless, silent liturgy. These eel-like beings, with manes of photosynthesizing fronds that ripple like slow fire, are attuned directly to the plateau’s foundational thrum. Their coiling movements are not random; they are a precise, collective manipulation of the quantum-stable thermal fields that seep from the underwater artifacts. They are not tending the machines; they are dancing with them, their movements helping to stabilize and diffuse the energy, preventing hot spots from forming and boiling the lagoons dry. To watch them is to see physics made fluid, a ritual older than recorded history.

And then there are the **Remnants**. The “someone” who once left marks of adaptation. They are not gone. They have simply… integrated. You might mistake one for a peculiarly shaped moss-covered boulder at the foot of an obelisk, until you notice, over the course of an hour, that it has breathed—a slight contraction, a soft exhalation of steam. They are humanoid in only the vaguest sense, their forms blurred by centuries of symbiotic growth. Stone-like skin, veined with iridescent blue, matches the basalt. Moss grows in patterned patches across their bodies, and their eyes, when they open them (a process that can take days), are deep pools of still water reflecting the glowing vines. They move perhaps once a year, shifting position to place a hand on a humming lattice, or to gently redirect a root-bridge over a widening crack. Their thoughts are as slow as the erosion. They are the plateau’s memory, the will that once guided the root-bridges and hollowed the fungus shelters, now surrendered to the ecosystem’s own vast, patient intelligence. They remember the purpose of the artifacts not as knowledge, but as instinct—a deep, bodily need to be near them, to touch them, to ensure the thrum continues.

Lastly, there are the **Ephemerals**. These are the fast things, the shockingly brief sparks of life that seem like hallucinations to the plateau’s normal rhythm. Jewel-bright insects that live and die in a single day, their wings clicking in frantic counterpoint to the deep booms. Schools of translucent fish that flash through a lagoon and vanish into a sinkhole, their entire species-cycle playing out in a month. They are the plateau’s fleeting dreams, its momentary diversions. The Stone-Calves do not notice them. The Carpet absorbs their brief chemical signatures without comment. But the Thermal Saints sometimes catch them on the wind, and for a moment, their glowing organs pulse just a little faster.
