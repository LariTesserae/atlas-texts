They are the children of the plateau. Their life is the rhythm of the rock, the pulse of the light, the slow grinding of the world-engine.

**The Grazers** are the first you learn to see. They are not herds, but continents. Each is an architecture of interlocking silicate plates, a mobile mountain range. Their limbs are not legs but great, telescoping pistons of basalt and fibrous cartilage, moving with the imperceptible certainty of tectonic adjustment. They do not walk; they *rearrange*. Over a decade, one might migrate a kilometer, its path a smoothed, polished trough in the crystal forest floor, a highway for smaller things. Their mouths are not openings but entire landscapes: broad, sloping fields of porous stone that weep digestive acids. They lower these plains—over months—onto a thicket of reactor-crystals. The ensuing chemical dissolution, the drawing up of nutrient-rich fluids, is a season’s work. You could watch for a lifetime and see only the faintest sagging of the great head, the slow seep of blue luminescence being extinguished under its mass. Their thoughts, if such a word applies, are vast and patient. A single decision—to alter course, to begin digestion—might take years to formulate, a consensus reached across a neural network of quartz and conducting gel. They perceive in millennia. Your entire civilization’s history would be a fleeting, bright spark in the corner of one obsidian eye.

**The Epiphytes** are not true fauna, but the fauna of the fauna. They live on the Grazers, in the sheltered valleys between plates, in the warm vents of their geothermal exhausts. They are faster. Some are bulbous sacs of translucent skin, filled with swirling, opalescent fluids that filter metabolic byproducts from their host’s atmosphere. They pulse gently, on a weekly cycle. Others are like metallic lichens, spreading intricate copper-colored filigree across the stone flesh, forming symbiotic nerve-extensions, sharpening the host’s perception of seismic vibrations. They are the plateau’s immune system and nervous system, living on its mobile islands.

**The Foragers** are the plateau’s only swiftness. They are not small—many are the size of boulders—but they move in bursts that a human eye could follow, if barely. They are all angles and piercing tools. Their bodies are assemblies of hard, sharp parts: diamond-tipped drill limbs, cutting saws of oscillating crystal, manipulator filaments fine as glass hair. They swarm—over centuries—in the wake of a Grazer, scavenging the slag, pruning the reactor-forest, repairing minor fractures in the geothermal pistons. A single task—repairing a one-meter fissure in a rock-piston’s biofilm—might occupy a clan of Foragers for a generation. They work with a silent, relentless purpose, a telepathy of chemical trails and resonant vibration. They are the plateau’s hands, its delicate tools.

**The Watchers** are the most alien. They are not many. They stand at the high edges of mesas, or atop the tallest crystal spires. They are slender towers of layered, reflective stone, like impossibly thin stalagmites crowned with a single, complex geode. Inside that geode, fluids and gases shift, refracting the eternal light. They are not eyes, but *computations*. They track the sun’s subtle arc across centuries, the slow dance of the Grazers, the health of the reactor-fields. They measure. They calibrate. Their output is not speech, but adjustment: a subtle change in the pheromone gradient that guides the Foragers, a resonant frequency emitted into the bedrock that might, over a decade, stimulate a dormant geothermal vent. They are the plateau’s consciousness, its guiding intellect, cold and luminous and patient beyond mortal comprehension.

There are also the **Unseen**: the things in the deep fluid channels, serpentine lengths of molten glass that nurse on the core’s heat; the spore-clouds that bloom from the reactor-crystals every few centuries, glittering hazes that rewrite the genetic code of the forest; the ghostly, mycelial networks in the rock itself, the true connective tissue of the whole system, conducting information and energy at the speed of geological stress.

Reproduction is an event of cataclysmic slowness. A Grazer, after feeding for eight thousand years, will enter its generative phase. Its core temperatures rise. Over a century, it splits along a dozen fault lines, not into two, but into twenty or thirty smaller, proto-Grazers, each a rolling mound of hot, pliable stone that will spend its first millennium hardening and defining its form. The parent mountain becomes a nutrient-rich plain, its body the nursery for its young, who slowly consume it as they grow.

They do not have wars. They have adjustments. If a reactor-forest over-extends, draining geothermal resources, the Watchers will guide Grazers to prune it back over centuries. If a geothermal vent fails, Foragers will spend millennia engineering a replacement, diverting fluid channels with exquisite precision. It is a system of perfect, ruthless, slow efficiency. There is no waste. There is no decay. There is only transformation, reconfiguration, and the endless, grinding, luminous production of… something. Perhaps the plateau itself is the product. Perhaps it is building something vaster, something that will only be complete when the sun above finally swells and dies.

They are alive. They are the land. And you, with your flickering heart and your frantic mind, are less than a mote of dust in their breathing. You are a transient chemical imbalance on a single crystal facet, to be corrected and absorbed in the next slow cycle of the rain.
