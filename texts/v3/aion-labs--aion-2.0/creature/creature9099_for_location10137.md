The denizens of the Mycelial Plateau are not visitors to its realm; they are its organs, its expressions, its slow thoughts given form. They live within the green breath, and their lives are measured not in seasons, but in the gradual unfurling of a fern or the century-long growth of a root-mass.

First, there are the **Moss-kin**. They are not a species, but a phase. They begin as spore clusters, settling in the moist hollows of the great bromeliads or upon the spongy bark of the fused-root trees. For decades they are mere films of emerald and umber, photosynthetic skins drinking the perpetual drip. But when conditions align—a particular constellation of humidity, the faint chemical sigh of a dying giant—the film thickens, differentiates. Soft, finger-like projections extend, weaving into a mat that gains mobility. A Moss-kin is a rug of living vegetation, a square meter of velvety moss and tiny, glowing fungal knots, that detaches from its birthplace and begins a slow migration across the plateau floor. It moves by infinitesimal capillary action, absorbing water from beneath and exuding it ahead, sliding on a self-created film of moisture at a pace of a few meters a year. Its purpose is redistribution: it carries within its body a specific mix of symbiotic bacteria and fungal threads, and it seeks out areas of imbalance—a patch of ground where decay has stalled, a stone too bare. There, it settles, merges, and seeds a new equilibrium. To witness a Moss-kin’s journey is to watch geology in motion; they are the plateau’s immune response, its gentle healers.

Then there are the **Sapience-vines**. These are the closest the plateau comes to having a nervous system. They are not individual plants, but a continent-spanning network of woody lianas, all genetically identical, all interconnected through root grafts and airborne pheromone signals. Each vine is as thick as a man’s thigh, its bark a mosaic of grays and greens like lichen-covered stone. They do not climb randomly; they grow in deliberate, geometric patterns—spirals up root-columns, perfect hexagons across wetland pools, long, straight lines along vanished paths. They are photophobic, avoiding direct bioluminescence, and seem to seek out the ancient builder-structures, twining through archways and over platforms with a curious, almost reverent precision. Their intelligence is phytological: they sense pressure, light gradients, chemical imbalances, and water flows across thousands of square miles. In response, they adjust their growth. A Sapience-vine might constrict slightly around a root-column that is weakening, providing support. It might exude a sweet, narcotic resin near a colony of browsing fauna, calming them, guiding their movement. They have no consciousness as we know it, but a vast, vegetative awareness. The low-frequency hum that vibrates through the ground is believed to be the sound of their slow internal flows—a hymn of hydraulic pressure and sugar transport.

The large, shaggy browsers are the **Hummungh**. A single Hummungh is a small ecosystem. Its six legs end in broad, pad-like feet that distribute its immense weight over the yielding ground, leaving temporary, water-filled footprints that become instant micro-ponds. Its hide is not fur, but a dense forest of moisture-loving epiphytes—ferns, mosses, even tiny orchids—that root directly in its thick, porous skin. The Hummungh feeds by lowering its wide, rubbery mouth to the algal mats and inhaling, filtering moisture and microorganisms through bristle-like teeth. It moves in herds of three or four, following the faint trails of the Moss-kin, their passage helping to churn and aerate the substrate. They communicate through deep, subsonic rumbles conducted through the ground, and their life span is measured in centuries. When a Hummungh dies, it does not fall. It simply stops, its legs locking into place. Within a year, it is a mound of vibrant growth, a new hillock for the Moss-kin to colonize, its skeleton a framework for future vines.

In the water-filled bromeliads and quiet pools live the **Glass-wraiths**. These are delicate, gelatinous beings, translucent as clean water. They have no fixed shape, flowing like amorphous blobs, their only solid structures being a network of fine, silvery nerve-threads that glow with a soft blue light. They navigate by tasting the electrical fields of their surroundings, and they feed on decaying matter and stray spores, cleaning the water. But their true function is artistic, or perhaps mnemonic. When a Glass-wraith encounters a significant event—the fall of a giant leaf, the passing of a Hummungh herd, the strange, carved pattern on a sunken builder-stone—it incorporates minute particles from the event into its body. Over time, these particles align along its nerve-threads, creating intricate, three-dimensional patterns within its form. Older Glass-wraiths are walking tapestries of historical data, their insides a swirling galaxy of captured pollen, mineral dust, and bioluminescent bacteria. They are living archives, and they are drawn to the Sapience-vines, often resting against them, as if uploading their stored memories into the vegetative network.

Lastly, there are the **Tenders**. These are the mystery, the possible heirs to the builders. They are tall, slender bipeds, but to call them humanoid would be a cruel simplification. Their skin is the color and texture of weathered birch bark, patterned with lines that mimic fungal gills. Their heads are hairless, elongated, with large, dark eyes that lack pupils but are veined with bioluminescent capillaries, allowing them to see in the full spectrum of the plateau’s faint light. They have no visible mouths; they absorb nutrients and moisture directly through their palms and the soles of their feet when they stand still. They are few, and they are silent. They move with a fluid, unhurried grace through the gloom, never seeming to disturb a drop of water. Their purpose is curation. A Tender might spend a week gently pruning a Sapience-vine to encourage a specific growth pattern. They might carefully transplant a Moss-kin to a new location, their long fingers weaving it into the substrate. They collect the amber-like resins, not for themselves, but to place them in specific niches where the nutrients will be most needed. They occasionally gather before the ancient archways or moss-covered platforms, standing in silent communion, their bodies emitting a soft, pulsing light in complex sequences. They do not build; they guide. They are the plateau’s conscious gardeners, the stewards of the equilibrium, their minds likely intertwined with the Sapience-vine network. They are the reason the builder-ruins feel “apologetic”; the Tenders are not their descendants, but their successors, a life form that learned the ultimate lesson: to build is to impose, but to tend is to belong. They are the final, perfect expression of the Mycelial Plateau’s ethos—to be so integrated that you become indistinguishable from the world’s slow, green breath.
