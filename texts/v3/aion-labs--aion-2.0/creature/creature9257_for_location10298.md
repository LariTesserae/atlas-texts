They call themselves the Sodden, or sometimes the Gilded, though the latter is more a term of poetic endearment than a daily identifier. To an outsider, they might seem a single, extended organism, moving with the same slow, deliberate rhythm as the stone-lungs on the walls.

Physically, they are slight, narrow-shouldered, built for compression. Their average height is low, a concession to the low doorways and cramped vertical spaces. Their skin is pale, not from lack of sun, but from a lifetime beneath a bruise-colored sky and the perpetual film of moisture. It has a peculiar, bluish translucency over the knuckles and temples, like the membrane of a deep-water creature. Their hair is fine, straight, and often the color of wet straw or the grey of weepstone, worn cropped short or tightly braided to avoid snagging on the guide-string canopies. Eyes are wide, the better to gather the soft, diffuse light, and are most commonly the green of the moss or the grey of the mist.

They dress in layers: a base of felted golden moss, carded and beaten until it is soft as lamb’s wool, worn next to the skin for its warmth even when damp. Over this, tunics and leggings of leather, tanned with the tannins of bog myrtle into a supple, near-black material that sheds the constant drizzle. Footwear is the most intricate craft: boots of layered leather and moss-felt, soled with slabs of a rubbery fungus that grows in the deepest cisterns, providing a grip on the slick moss-pack that is silent and sure.

Their society is not hierarchical in a vertical sense, but concentric. At the physical and social heart is the central cistern, the carved basalt heart. Around it live the Water-Speakers, the oldest families, whose role is to listen. They interpret the drip-patterns—the *plink*, *tisk*, *pat*—for shifts in humidity, for the health of the stone-lungs, for the subtle messages of the coming condensation. Their decisions dictate the daily adjustments to the guide-string arrays, the opening and closing of sluice gates. Their authority is soft, a whisper that carries.

Radiating out from them are the guilds, not by trade but by element. The Moss-keepers trim, harvest, and cultivate. They know the golden moss’s moods, where it will fruit, where it grows thin and needs a slurry of weepstone dust to thrive. The Stone-singers quarry and shape the weepstone. Their work is a quiet song of flint and bone, tapping, grinding, keying stone to stone so that the weeping surfaces are always oriented to feed the moss, never to pool and stagnate. The String-weavers maintain the vast, whispering canopy overhead, replacing reeds, tensioning lines, ensuring the fallwater finds its way home.

Children are born into the damp, and their first breath is the iron-scented air. Their first sound is the drip-clock. They are carried in moss-padded slings until they can walk, then set to simple tasks: gathering the glistening slugs from the pathways, polishing the cistern’s lip with smooth stones, learning the names of the different drips. Education is osmotic, absorbed through the skin and the ears.

Their weirdness is not overt. It is in their adaptations. Many have a symbiotic lichen growing in fine, lace-like patterns on their backs and shoulders, beneath their clothes—a personal, mobile version of the stone-lung, helping to regulate their own humidity. Their digestion is tuned to the moss-fruit and the rare, pale vole; they cannot tolerate dry grains or sun-ripened fruits. Their sleep cycles are tied to the mist-exhalations of dawn and dusk. They dream in shades of green and the sound of water finding a path.

They are a people of profound intimacy and profound silence. Conversation is sparse, functional, carried out in low murmurs that blend with the other background sounds. Touch is constant—a hand on a shoulder to guide in a tight passage, the press of bodies sharing warmth in a workshop. Privacy is a luxury of momentary solitude, found in the mist at the rim of the bowl, looking out at the unseen, drier world they call the "Great Thirst."

They are not sad, nor joyful in a boisterous way. Their prevailing emotion is a deep, somatic contentment, the satisfaction of a cog perfectly meshed in a wheel. They are the Gilded Sodden. They are the mind of the moss, the hands of the drip, the careful erosion that sustains. To live here is not to inhabit a place. It is to be a function of it.
