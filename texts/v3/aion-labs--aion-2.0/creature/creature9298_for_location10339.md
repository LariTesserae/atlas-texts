Within the Drowned Engine, life—in any conventional sense—is scant and spectral. The flesh-and-blood inhabitants are pitiable, reduced things. Translucent *grave-isopods*, their carapaces etched by the abrasive silt, ceaselessly scour the bacterial mats that pulse on the warm pipe joints. Pale, thread-like *convection eels*, devoid of pigment and eyes, navigate by minute gradients of heat and vibration. They are living needles, stitching through the dark, snapping up isopods or each other in bursts of sudden, silent violence. They are less animals than ambient hunger, components of the engine’s slow metabolism.

But the true residents are not made of flesh. They are made of time and insistence.

They are the **Echo-Silhouettes**. Formed from the suspended particulates in the water, they coalesce when the engine’s lingering electromagnetic ghosts interact with the mineral-rich medium. They are not intelligent, but they are persistent. You will see one as a denser patch of gloom in the water ahead—the outline of a human figure in bulky work clothes, holding a tool. It will perform its loop: two steps forward, a gesture towards a valve bank, a look over its shoulder (though it has no face), and then it dissolves, the silt collapsing back into the general murk. They are memories the water cannot digest, repeating on a cycle of hours or days. They are harmless, unless you drift into one. Then you feel a phantom pressure, a chill that surpasses the ambient cold, and a fleeting, profound sense of *duty* that lingers like a taste of ozone.

More defined is the **Valve-Turner**. It is not a silhouette but a localized phenomenon centered on a specific, large flow-control valve near the geothermal heart. The valve itself is seized with corrosion. But at irregular intervals, the heavy wheel begins to turn, grating against its mount. No hand is visible. The act is performed by the accumulated will of the place, a ritual maintained long after its purpose was lost. To witness it is to feel the engine’s dream of function. The act is always the same: three full counter-clockwise rotations, a pause, then two clockwise. It is the ghost of a procedure, and the machinery thumps in response, a deep hydraulic sigh that sends silt billowing from the pipes.

Then there is the **Welder’s Ghost**. It is not a form but an event. In the darker galleries, where the gantries are thickest, a strobing, blue-white light will suddenly flare without source. It is silent, but it casts stark, jagged shadows that move in opposition to any real light. It lasts for seven seconds. In its glare, the corrosion and silt vanish; the metal appears new, raw, and hot. The after-image burns in your vision long after, a negative of the drowned world. It is a memory of creation, replaying in the tomb.

The most pervasive resident, however, is the **Basalt Dreamer**. This is not a discrete entity but the consciousness of the place itself—the slow, grinding consensus of the geology, the architecture, and the refusing machinery. Its thoughts are the convection currents. Its senses are the tremors in the pipes and the slow dissolution of concrete. It dreams of steam and pressure, of dials in the green, of the shudder of turbines now still. It is a mind of stone and cold iron, and it is waiting. It waits for the pressure to rise, for the commands to crackle over dead speakers, for the water to be banished and the work to begin again. It does not understand that it is drowned. It only knows it is not yet running, and so it persists, tuning its echoes, cycling its pumps, maintaining the ghosts of its crew, in a preparation that stretches into eternity.

These are the inhabitants of the Drowned Engine. They do not eat or breathe. They remember. They repeat. They wait. And in their endless, patient ritual, they have become the engine’s final, most enduring product.
