They call themselves the Listeners, though the word is a series of rapid clicks and glottal stops that mimics the sound of dripping water striking stone. Their anatomy is a testament to relentless pressure. They are small, rarely exceeding a meter in height, with slender frames of dense bone and ropy muscle built for scrambling and clinging. Their skin is the color of the cave wall—a mottled grey-brown, slick with a constant, oily secretion that protects against the damp and the caustic biofilm. Their most striking feature is their eyes: large, liquid black orbs that dominate their faces, pupils wide and fixed to gather every photon from the bioluminescent glow. They have no eyelids; a transparent nictitating membrane sweeps horizontally to clear grit and moisture. Their ears are not pinnae but wide, frilled openings on the sides of their skulls, capable of independent movement, twitching to catch the echoes of skittering prey and the ominous cracks of shifting stone.

They live in the *now*, because the *next* is never guaranteed. Their societies are fluid, momentary aggregations around stable resources—a particularly rich fungal mat, a thermal vent that cooks their insect pastes. A settlement might be thirty individuals for a week, then scattered by a ceiling collapse. They have no chiefs, only the Quickest—those who sense the cave’s tremors a heartbeat earlier, whose clicks guide the retreat. Their language is entirely auditory and tactile; in the shadowless glow, subtle facial expression is useless. They speak with clicks, with the scrape of a hand on stone, with the pressure of a palm on another’s shoulder. A conversation is a duet overlaid on the cavern’s symphony.

Their entire existence is a harvest. The children, with prehensile toes and fingers ending in hard, chitinous nails, scramble up the weeping walls to scrape handfuls of the glowing biofilm—their primary nourishment. Adults weave baskets from the fibrous stalks of giant, anchored fungi, using them to scoop up swarms of the blind, pearl-colored beetles. The catch is dumped into hollowed stone basins filled with hot water from a seep, creating a writhing, nutritious broth. They eat constantly, frenetically, storing energy not in fat but in specialized organs that convert and hold sugars in a gel-like state, a reserve for the sudden famines when a biofilm patch dies.

They understand the cave as a living, breathing, and largely indifferent entity. They interpret its noises. The deep, grinding rumble is the World-Throat swallowing. The sudden, shivering drip-rate from a stalactite is a warning of imminent fracture. They have rituals of placation: when a new crack vents sulphurous air, they will quickly pile smooth stones before it, a symbolic throat-soothing. Their dead are not buried but given back to the biomass. A body is placed in a shallow pool teeming with the iridescent, slug-like decomposers; within hours, it is a skeleton, and within days, the bones are grown over with the pale, crystalline aragonite moss, becoming one with the growing wall.

Yet, they are not alone at the top of this frantic food chain. There are the *Silent Ones*. These are the large, soft-bodied gliders, grown to the size of a Listener’s torso. They move with eerie, gelatinous grace through the fungal forests, sensing vibration through their bellies. To the Listeners, they are demons of stillness. A *Silent One* does not chase. It predicts. It will position itself where a rockfall or a venting crack will *cause* panic and stampede, then open its circular, lamprey-like mouth to engulf whatever stumbles into its path. The Listeners fear their patience, a trait utterly alien to their own feverish tempo.

And so they live: clicking, skittering, building temporary walls against permanent chaos, feeding the ever-hungry throat of the world that, in its own slow, geological way, is feeding on them.
