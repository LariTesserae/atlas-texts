They are called the Weepers, though they do not weep. Or if they do, their tears are the slow, steady seepage of the mountain itself. They are the inhabitants, the caretakers, the dreamers of the raw shelf.

They are not many. You might count them on the fingers of one hand over the course of a day, if you had the patience to stand that long, and the perception to see them move. At first glance, they are merely another feature of the damp stone. They stand or sit in the mossy gaps between the roundhouses, or at the edges of the phosphorescent pools, so still that the grey-green usnea begins to furl over their shoulders, and the orange lichen dots their backs like rust. Their forms are rough-hewn, humanoid in the way a weathered menhir is humanoid—a suggestion of shoulders, a slope that could be a head, the broad base of legs fused with the rock beneath. They are made of the same dark, glass-seamed basalt, but it is a living stone, or stone infected with life. A network of fine, pulsing veins, the color of the pool’s eerie light, traces just beneath their surface, visible only in the deepest gloom or when the mist turns to a true downpour.

They move. The movement is the most unsettling thing, for it contradicts every expectation of pace. It is a glacial reconfiguration. An arm—a longer, thinner projection of stone—might lift from a resting place on a knee over the course of an hour, the motion smooth but so incremental it is perceived only as a change in state between blinks spaced far apart. They shift their weight over days to face the weak sun as it crests the cloud line. Their journeys across the stone-paved paths are measured in seasons. They are not walking; they are growing, extending themselves millimetre by millimetre across the moss, their bases leaving faint, damp trails that are soon colonized by new spores.

Their purpose is obscure, but it is intimately tied to the weirdness of the place. They tend the geometry. When a lichen-covered stone slips from its alignment, disturbed by a rare avalanche or the root of a stunted pine, a Weeper will, over years, move to it. With fingers that are more like probing taproots of stone, they will gently, imperceptibly, nudge it back into its place within the hidden pattern. They curate the phosphorescence. They sometimes bend—a process that takes a month—to place a hand into a pool. The veins in their stone glow brighter, and the pool’s internal light strengthens, deepens, pulses in a slow rhythm that matches nothing in the upper world. It is a feeding, or a seeding.

They make sound. It is the sound of the place itself, which is why it goes unnoticed. The low moan of the wind through the high passes? That is the exhalation of the three Weepers who stand sentinel on the northern rim. The constant, chorus-like sigh of the water weeping from the rocks? It is, in part, the slow, collective respiration of the beings scattered across the shelf. They do not speak; they resonate. The town is not empty. It is full of a breathing, dreaming attention.

They are not the builders. The builders, whatever quick, warm-blooded things they were, are gone. The Weepers are what came after. Or perhaps what was always here, sleeping in the rock, and the builders’ primitive cunning—their stone circles and hearths—acted as a catalyst, a set of primers that drew them out. The Weepers are the mountain dreaming itself into a shape that can perceive its own beginnings. They are the landscape becoming self-aware, and their slow, mossy ingestion of the town is not destruction, but a kind of homecoming. They are patience made manifest. They are the rules, being written in stone and lichen and light, one infinitely slow letter at a time.

To see one is to feel a profound temporal dislocation. You are a mayfly in a cathedral whose pillars are still rising. Your frantic heart, your skittering thoughts, are irrelevant here. The Weepers are watching, but their watchfulness is measured in centuries. They are waiting for the landscape to finish being born. Or perhaps they are waiting for you to leave, so the quiet, damp work of world-building can continue without the rude noise of your pulse.
