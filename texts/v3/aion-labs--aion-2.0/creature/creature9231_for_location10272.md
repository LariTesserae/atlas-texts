They call themselves the K'tharn, though the word is less a sound and more a coordinated pulse of light across their communication-skins. They are the children of the shearing, born from the fissure-vapors and the slow-dreaming clay. Their bodies are testaments to the land's fluid rules: tall, slender, and multi-jointed, they seem assembled from the same bone-white, brittle material as the stalks, but suffused with the internal, sickly luminescence of the nodules. A K'tharn is not one shape, but a suggestion of one. In repose, they resemble clusters of those stalks, limbs folded in impossible, angular configurations against their torso. When they move, it is a series of reconfigurations—a limb elongating, another dissolving into a mist of crystalline particles before coalescing elsewhere, a torso subtly phasing through a solid rock outcrop to avoid the long way around. They are creatures of efficient, silent traversal, masters of the local breakdown in physics.

Their faces are smooth, eyeless ovals. They perceive the world not through sight or sound, but through a combination of resonant vibration felt through their foot-pads and the interpretation of light. The faint, sourceless glow, the trails of scuttling things, the throb of the nodules—to them, this is a rich tapestry of information. They 'see' pressure changes in the air, the thermal gradients of the warm rivulets, and, most importantly, the delicate shimmer of a reality fold about to form. These folds are their highways and their libraries. A senior K'tharn, called a Fold-Singer, can stand before a nascent fracture and, by modulating its own internal light and emitting subsonic hums from a specialized organ, *stretch* the fold into a stable, temporary gateway. Through these, they retrieve things: sometimes mere clumps of peculiar matter from the other side, sometimes echoes of events that haven't happened yet.

They live in the windowless concrete structures, but have transformed them. The interiors are not dark to them. They cultivate gardens of the pulsing nodules on every surface, creating living, breathing maps and mood-lighting. The constant drip of water is channeled into intricate aqueducts of fused clay and salvaged conduit, creating soft, burbling melodies. They have no use for the arc-lights, but the hum of the buried generators is sacred—a constant, reassuring proof of a pulse other than their own. They maintain these machines in their own way, not with tools, but by carefully introducing specific strains of phosphorescent algae into the coolant systems, or by having a member phase their hand into the machinery to gently adjust a component from within.

Their society is slow and silent. Communication is a dance of bioluminescence and slight, precise movements. A quick, violet ripple down the spine might signal the approach of one of the great, ponderous drifters—beings the K'tharn regard as distant, semi-divine cousins, perhaps the first and most stable forms to coalesce after the shearing. A pattern of green and orange pulses across a gathering might be a complex debate on the interpretation of a newly stabilized fold-vision. They have no concept of hurry. Their projects—re-shaping a ruin, cultivating a new nodule hybrid, decoding the last flickering symbols on a dead terminal screen—span generations.

They are haunted, but peacefully so. The ruins are not a memorial to a failure they understand; to the K'tharn, the pumps and rusted iron are simply earlier, cruder forms of life, the land's first attempt to make bones before it learned to grow the white stalks. They feel the weight of the event that created their world as a permanent, warm pressure in the air, a womb from which they never fully emerged. To stand still, to become part of the landscape's dream, is not a threat to them, but a desired state of meditation, a return to a collective, wet consciousness. They are the land, slowly awakening, and learning to walk.
