To live in the Wet Old Plain is to be a creature of the seep, the hummock, and the endless drone. The inhabitants are specialists in saturation, their forms and functions honed by the constant presence of water just a sigh away from the surface.

In the tea-colored pools, life is a slow ballet of shadows. The broad-backed fish are not swift river darters, but ponderous, dark-scaled things—ancient lineages of carp and bowfin that patrol the murk. They move with a weighty grace, their fins stirring up clouds of peat-stained silt. They are bottom-feeders and ambush predators, their mouths capable of a sudden, vacuum-like expansion to suck in unsuspecting tadpoles or water beetles. With them, in the cooler depths near the weeping rocks, dwell colonies of freshwater mussels, their shells the color of old bone, filtering the stagnant water with a patience that makes the cycads seem hasty. Their lives are measured in decades, each year adding a thin, mineral ring to their armor.

Upon the trembling peat and in the saturated black soil beneath, the engineers toil. Earthworms here are not the pink, slender things of gardens, but large, lustrous, and blue-black, thriving in the oxygen-poor muck. Their constant, blind tunneling is one of the plain’s subtle engines of aeration and mixing. Alongside them, in labyrinthine burrows that always flood, live the squat, glossy water beetles and the larval forms of a hundred flying things—pale, squirming architects of future clouds of sound. And everywhere, in films and threads and gelatinous colonies, are the silent multitudes: bacteria and fungi that perform the final, essential alchemy, reducing the fallen sedge and the dead fish into the black, fragrant peat that is the plain’s very flesh.

The hummocks of sedge grass are not merely plants; they are citadels. Their tough, fibrous roots bind the peat into stable, raised islands in the soft world. In their dense thatch live the noisemakers. The frogs are small, with skins patterned in perfect camouflage of mottled brown and green. They do not sing in melodious choruses, but in a relentless, metallic stridulation, a vibration that is felt as much as heard, emanating from their throats and the very ground. Their cousins, the crickets, add a higher, sharper layer to the din, scraping legs against wings in a rhythm that syncs with the humidity. From within these hummocks, too, peer the bright, black eyes of the wetland mice—small, dense-furred creatures with water-resistant coats. They build intricate nests of shredded sedge above the waterline, their quick, silent runs along hidden pathways the fastest motion on the plain.

The air above is a highway and a killing field. The dancing midges, those tiny, stinging motes, rise in Biblical clouds from every damp spot, their collective hum the baseline of the plain’s soundtrack. They are pursued by the dragonflies—javelins of iridescent blue and green. These are not the dainty damselflies of streams, but robust, territorial hunters, their four wings rattling like parchment as they hang motionless before exploding forward to snatch a midge from the swarm with an audible click of bristled legs. Higher still, on the rare thermals that rise from the sun-warmed pools, drift the broad-winged hawks. They are mud-colored, their eyes sharp for the gleam of a mouse’s flank or the slow ripple of a fish near the surface. Their dive is a sudden, soundless punctuation in the constant noise.

And then there are the sentinels. The herons and egrets, those statues of concentrated hunger. They stand for hours in the shallows, their stillness so profound they seem part of the landscape, until the lightning-strike of their necks uncoils and a beak descends to impale a fish or frog. They are the plain’s quiet aristocrats, aloof from the frantic industry below. In the lower branches of the gnarled water oaks, sometimes a great horned owl rests by day, a tufted, silent lump waiting for the shift to dusk when the mouse trails become busy. Even the trees themselves are hosts: the beards of moss on the weeping rocks are miniature forests for mites and springtails; the broad, leathery leaves of the cycads cradle temporary pools where mosquito larvae writhe.

Nothing here is rushed, but nothing is idle. Every creature is a cog in the slow, wet machine of nutrient and energy, tuned to the rhythm of seep and evaporation. They are born from the water, feed on what the water nurtures, and return to the water in death. Their lives are cycles within the greater cycles of peat formation and hummock growth. They do not conquer the Wet Old Plain; they are its expression—its wet, slow, loud dream made flesh, carapace, and feather.
