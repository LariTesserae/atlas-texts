They are called the Listeners, but that is a translation of a concept for which sound is irrelevant. They are the children of the Hum, the ones who learned to live inside the roar.

Physically, they are a lean, weathered people. Generations under the constant pressure of the wind have sculpted them: narrow frames to present less resistance, skin like worn leather, tanned deep bronze by the strange, pervasive light. Their most distinctive feature is their ears—or lack thereof. The external pinnae are mere vestigial ridges, sealed over with the same tough skin. The auditory canal is not absent, but it is internalized, protected deep within the skull. They do not hear the Hum; they feel it through their bones, a vibration that is their constant companion, their timekeeper, their god. Their eyes are large, dark, and protected by a pronounced, translucent nictitating membrane that sweeps sideways to clear away the driven mist and grit. They see more in the green-gold pulses of the Moss than in the shapes of things.

Their communication is a language of touch and light. In the shelters of their huts, or when the wind drops to a mere hurricane-force groan, they speak with their hands—a intricate sign language pressed into another's palm or traced upon their own chest. In the open, they use the Moss. By tapping or pressing on the luminous pelt in specific rhythms, they can create ripples of bioluminescent response that travel for miles across the plain, a slow, silent telegraph of simple concepts: *danger*, *gathering*, *a Stone-Sheep has died*.

Their society is one of profound stillness and patience, a mirror to the Stone-Sheep. They are nomadic, but their migrations are measured in generations, not seasons. A family group might occupy a circle of Listeners' Huts for a century, slowly harvesting the Moss. They do not tear it; that would be a violence the plain would not tolerate. Instead, they "shear" it using blades of sharpened Stone-Sheep bone, taking only the topmost, luminous layer, which regenerates within a few days. This harvest provides everything: food, medicine, and material. The raw Moss is chewed into a bland, nutritious paste. When dried and pounded, it becomes a fibrous cloth or a smoky, slow-burning fuel for their tiny, essential fires—not for heat, but for light in the deepest dark, and for the ritual burning of a rare, psychoactive lichen that grows only on the backs of the oldest Stone-Sheep.

Their relationship with the Stone-Sheep is one of reverence and symbiosis. They do not hunt them; the beasts are too monumental, too slow to be prey. Instead, they follow them. A family will attach itself to a particular beast, living in its vast shadow. They harvest the lichen from its back, and in return, they clear parasitic, non-luminous growths from its hide. When a Stone-Sheep finally ceases to move—a event that happens perhaps once in a human lifetime—it is not a death, but a "grounding." The Listeners then perform a weeks-long ceremony, slowly dismantling the colossal corpse. The basalt teeth become their finest tools. The shaggy, stone-like hide becomes the walls of a new hut. The immense bones become frames or, once carved with the history of the people who attended it, are raised as new megaliths.

The Wind-Snakes are both tools and omens. A Listener will spend years braiding a lure from Moss-fibers, impregnated with luminous spores. On certain nights, they fly these lures on lines of braided sinew, catching a Wind-Snake. The glassy creature, once "tamed" by the constant offering of light, becomes a living tool. Its resonant body, when anchored and tensed, can be plucked to send specific, vibrating signals through the stone of the plain, a form of deep-earth communication. A wild Wind-Snake weaving through the megaliths is read as a message from the Hum itself, its pitch and pattern interpreted by the elder Listeners.

They have no chiefs, no permanent hierarchy. Authority flows to the one who can be the most still, who can interpret the subtle shifts in the Hum's tone, the tidal rhythms of the Moss-light. This elder, often ancient and nearly immobile, is called the *Keeper of the Still Point*. They decide when it is time to move on, reading the signs in the withering of the Moss-patches and the slow drift of the Stone-Sheep.

Their spirituality is the Hum. They believe the roar is the world breathing, the sound of time itself wearing the mountains down. They see their own lives as brief, quiet eddies in that endless river of air. Their purpose is to listen—not with ears, but with their whole being—and to remember. The megaliths are not homes or temples, but memory-stones. Each placement marks an event: a grounding, a change in the Hum's key, the location of a great light-tide in the Moss. Their history is not written, but positioned.

They are few. Their numbers are constrained by the vast, empty patience of the plain. A child is born rarely, and is swaddled not in cloth, but in living Moss, its first sensation the vibration and the soft, internal light. To die is to be laid upon the Moss, where the body is absorbed within a day, its substance becoming part of the great, breathing pelt. A person becomes a slightly brighter patch in the nightly glow, remembered until that patch, too, is cycled away.

They are the final inhabitants of a finished world. They do not build empires. They do not conquer. They attend. They listen. And when the last of them is gone, the High Hum will not notice. The Moss will pulse, the wind will scream, and the Stone-Sheep will take another step, as they have for a million years before, and will for a million years after.
