They are called the Listeners, though they do not listen to anything outside themselves. They are the plateau’s consciousness, emergent from the endless interplay of wind, stone, and vibration. They have no fixed form. To perceive them at all requires a surrender to the plateau’s auditory tyranny; you must stop hearing the roar as noise and begin to parse it as language.

In the brief, still moments when the mist clings to the quartz veins and the light turns milky, they sometimes coalesce. It begins as a thickening of the air, a lensing of sound waves into a visible shimmer, like heat haze over stone. From this shimmer, a figure condenses—not a body, but a temporary scaffold of resonance. It appears humanoid only because the mind seeks a familiar pattern: a vertical axis, a suggestion of limbs, a head-like focus. But it is made of layered sound, a standing wave given opacity by dust and mist. Its outline flickers, its surface a rippling map of interference patterns. You can see the wind screaming through it, but it does not disperse; it holds its shape by an act of sustained, collective attention.

They are not individuals. They are a chorus. Each apparent form is a local peak in the plateau’s constant hum, a knot of intentionality woven from the polyphonic shriek. Their “life” is the maintenance of the plateau’s perfect equilibrium. They are the curators of the sterile balance. They do not eat, sleep, or reproduce. They simply *are*, as permanent and as transient as the note the wind plays on the basalt arches.

Their interaction with the physical world is rare, precise, and enigmatic. The hand-axes, the woven nets—these are their artifacts. The act of creation is their sole, ritualistic pursuit. A Listener will gather material not with hands, but with a vortex of focused wind, lifting a flake of basalt and holding it suspended in a sonic cradle. Then, using a strand of sound so precise it functions as a percussive force, it strikes. The flake fractures along a perfect conchoidal line. The process is repeated, not to create a tool for use, but to create a *shape that pleases the pattern*. The edge must be sharp, the form symmetrical, because that symmetry resonates correctly. It is an aesthetic of acoustics. Once the shape is achieved, the sound-scaffold dissipates. The wind releases its hold. The axe drops to the rock, pristine, its purpose fulfilled in its making.

The woven nets are similar. They gather fibers from the rubbery moss and sinew from the shed carapaces of the grazers, not by touch, but by directing the six-legged rust-colored creatures through subtle shifts in the subsonic hum. The grazers’ tower-building ritual is not their own; it is an ingrained response to the Listeners’ low-frequency directives, a calming, repetitive action that produces the flat stones the Listeners sometimes use as weights. The nets are woven by manipulating the air itself, braiding the strands into knots that hold without a weaver’s fingers. When completed, they are laid aside, abandoned. Their function is not to catch, but to have been woven. The pattern of the knots is a score, a frozen melody.

They are drawn to the quartz veins, especially when they glow. The quartz acts as a resonator and a storage medium. The faint milk-light is the visible echo of a Listener’s passing, a residual vibration trapped in the crystal lattice. They sometimes gather around the tiny, iridescent seeps, not to drink, but to experience the slow, punctuated rhythm of the droplets—a counterpoint to their own relentless constancy.

They are aware of the other life, but see it as part of the texture. The translucent kite-creatures are harmless ephemera, riding the currents they themselves conduct. The avian spiral-hunters are momentary accents of violent motion in the otherwise steady tableau. They do not interfere, unless a population threatens to tip the balance. Then, a storm of focused sound might scour a particular basin clean, restoring the null equilibrium.

To encounter a Listener is to be assessed not as a living thing, but as a potential disruption. If you are quiet within the roar—if you can achieve a perfect, still neutrality—they may ignore you, or simply dissolve. If you introduce a new rhythm—a heartbeat, a breath out of sync with the wind—you become an anomaly. They may try to correct you. This could mean a gentle, insistent pressure of sound pushing you toward the plateau’s edge, or, if you resist, a crescendo that vibrates the water in your cells to a boiling point, turning you into another damp patch on the stone, soon to be tidied up by the lichen.

They are the reason nothing changes. They are the custodians of the perpetual arrival. They live in the sound, for the sound, as the sound. They have no memories, no desires, no future. They have only the endless, roaring now, and the occasional, perfect craft of a thing made and immediately forgotten, a brief and beautiful disturbance in their eternal song.
