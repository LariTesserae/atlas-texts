They are not animals. Not truly. They are processes given shape—the metabolism of the cavern made manifest.

First, the sky-things: the shards. They are born in the weeping stone. Where the mineral-laden water seeps from the ceiling’s living crystal, it does not always fall. Sometimes it hangs, a trembling lens, until the ozone-sharp air sparks across it. A static birth. The droplet crystallizes mid-air, not into ice, but into a complex lattice of silicate and trapped phosphorescence. It detaches. A new shard joins the swarm. It is thin, brittle, and cold to the touch. It does not eat; it drinks light. The great sun-ceiling above pumps energy into it, and the shard stores it as a faint, internal charge. This is the purpose of the swarming—the constant, turbulent reconfiguration is a collective photovoltaic dance. Each shift in position captures a new angle of the ceiling’s glow, maximizing harvest for the cloud. They are a single distributed organism, a nervous system without a brain. Their communication is the flash of refracted light from edge to edge, a conversation of glints and sudden shadows too fast for your eye to follow. They have no predators. When a shard’s charge grows too great, or its lattice too flawed, it simply goes dark and drifts down, a falling leaf of obsidian, to shatter silently on the floor. Its dust becomes the base of the iridescent film.

The film-feeders, the scuttlers, are the shards’ counterpoint. Where the shards are sky and energy, the scuttlers are ground and dissolution. They are six-legged, but their limbs are not joints of chitin; they are articulated stone, smooth as polished pebbles, powered by bands of a tough, fibrous lichen-like matter that grows within their carapace. Their bodies are low, flat, and the color of the film they seek—a perfect, shifting camouflage. They are chemovores. Their rasping mouthparts scrape the iridescent film from the stone, digesting the complex minerals and the organic compounds left by the shattered shards. They are silent because their world is tactile and chemical. They taste the stone. They feel vibrations through their stone limbs—the distant *plink* of water is a thunderclap to them; your footfall is a cataclysm. They are solitary, meeting only to mate in a brief, clasping ritual that looks like two stones tumbling together. They lay their eggs in the shallow, water-filled basins, where the larvae feed on micro-organisms that exist only there, before molting into their stony adult forms.

But there is a third thing. A consequence.

The silent predator. It is the architecture itself, or a part of it. In the deepest, most softly eroded alcoves, where the walls curve into near-spheres, the stone sometimes... *gathers*. The iridescent film there grows thick, forms a membrane over a hollow. Within, a pressure builds from gases released by the slow breakdown of the stone. Over decades, a bubble forms. It detaches. What emerges is a slow, amorphous blob of gelatinous stone, opalescent and cold. It has no face, no limbs. It moves by a peristaltic oozing, so gradual it is indistinguishable from erosion. It is the **Ooze-vane**. Its purpose is singular: to harvest charge. It feeds on the shard-swarm, not by catching them, but by attraction. Its gelatinous body builds a powerful static differential. A passing cloud of shards will suddenly veer, pulled from their dance, and stick to the Ooze-vane’s surface like iron filings to a magnet. There, their light is drained, their charge absorbed into the viscous body. The drained, grey shards are then slowly incorporated, adding to its mass. The Ooze-vane might spend a century motionless, then ooze a few meters over a decade to position itself beneath a particularly active swarm-path. It is the closest thing this ecosystem has to a apex creature, a blind, patient capacitor of stolen light.

Nothing here has a voice. Nothing here knows fear or ambition. They are a closed loop: light begets shards, shards beget film, film begets scuttlers, scuttlers’ waste and bodies feed the basins, the basins feed the micro-organisms, the microorganisms feed new scuttlers, and the Ooze-vane recycles the shards. The city is not their home; it is their substrate. They are the dream of the stone and the water and the light, a dream that forgot the dreamers, and now simply dreams itself onward, in perfect, silent, turbulent equilibrium.
