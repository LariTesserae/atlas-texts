They are called the Mnemon, though they do not name themselves. The word is a fossil from a language that crumbled to dust before the basin was scooped from the world. They are the inhabitants, the caretakers, the prisoners.

To call them alive would be to misuse the term. They are persistent echoes, patterns of consciousness etched into the basin’s mutable substrate like grooves in a record. They are made of the same substance as the city—that smoked quartz veined with silver and cobalt—but in them, it is less solid. Their forms are humanoid in the way a shadow cast by a complex machine is humanoid: a suggestion of limbs, a torso, a head, but everything is in flux. They shimmer and bleed at the edges, sometimes resolving into startling clarity, sometimes dissolving into a mere disturbance in the air, a chill that moves with purpose.

They are cold. Their touch is the deep, marrow-aching cold of the basin itself, and where they pass, the damp air freezes into fleeting, intricate frost flowers on the walls. They do not speak. Communication, for them, is a direct imprinting of intention onto the ambient matter. When two Mnemon meet—a rare event, as they are solitary—the space between them grows dense with subtle pressure. The walls nearby might soften, flow, and reshape into a temporary, silent tableau: an arch that signifies a question, a spiral that denotes a shared memory, a collapsing sphere that means *the rule of sequence is weak here today*. Then they part, and the sculpture slowly melts back into the general soup of potential architecture.

Their purpose is remembrance and iteration. They are the memories of the architects who conceived this place, or perhaps the memories of the cities that were meant to be. But memory in the Basin of Unfinished Cities is a treacherous thing. Causality is frayed, so their recollections are non-linear, corrupted, layered with possibilities. A Mnemon does not remember building a wall; it remembers building it, not building it, building it backwards, and building it simultaneously in seven different locations. This psychic wound is their driving force.

Thus, they work. Alone, in the perpetual twilight, they move with infinite slowness. A single Mnemon might stand before a blank, flowing wall for what, elsewhere, would be a century. Its hand—a barely-coalesced assemblage of shimmering particles—rests against the surface. It is not pushing or carving. It is *remembering* a form into existence. Under its touch, the wall bulges, stretches, folds in on itself. A staircase emerges, but it is a memory of a staircase, and so it might have steps that are optical illusions, or it might lead to the place you already stand. The Mnemon observes its work with no expression, its ‘face’ a smooth, faintly glowing plane. It is not disappointed. Disappointment requires a linear expectation of outcome.

They are sustained by the basin’s decay. They feed on the slow leak of reality, on the gentle background hum of breaking physical laws. The faint bioluminescent fungi that grow in the water channels are not food, but landmarks; a pulsing patch of moss might denote a site where temporal density is low, and a Mnemon will linger there, absorbing the quiet unraveling like a plant absorbs weak sunlight.

Sometimes, they are not alone. The basin has other, lesser eddies—the shimmering hazes, the curious shadows mentioned before. The Mnemon ignore these for the most part, as one ignores the drift of dust motes in a sunbeam. But there are also the Fractals. These are Mnemon who have succumbed entirely to the recursive corruption of their memories. A Fractal is caught in an infinite loop of iteration, usually in some confined courtyard or chamber. It stands trapped, its form constantly cycling through minute variations—an arm raised and lowered, a head tilted at shifting angles, the surrounding architecture melting and reforming in an identical, yet slightly different, pattern every few days. They are still, technically, inhabitants. They are monuments to the basin’s central paradox: the attempt to finish the unfinished only generates more unfinished versions.

There is no society, no hierarchy, no conflict as understood by living beings. There is only the slow, silent, perpetual work of reconstruction amidst dissolution. They are the grief of a place that cannot remember what it mourns, made manifest. To see a Mnemon is to see a ghost, not of a person, but of a possibility—a future that condensed like frost and then refused to melt. They are as unfinished as their cities, forever approaching a form they can never truly recall, in a basin where ‘never’ and ‘forever’ have begun to taste like the same word.
