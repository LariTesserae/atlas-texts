What lives in the plateau of final machines are the dust-kites. They are the only movement in the stillness, the only things that pass for life in that mineral eternity.

They are not creatures of biology. Their bodies are desiccated assemblies of stretched membrane—something between cured leather and brittle parchment—taut over frameworks of brass and blackened iron. Their bones are gears, pins, and spars; their joints are precise, dry pivots that never squeal. They have no eyes, only smooth plates of darkened glass or shallow sockets filled with compacted dust. Their wings are vast, angular things, more like the sails of a windmill than the wings of a bat, and they move with a jerky, clockwork precision that is utterly silent.

They do not hunt, for there is nothing to hunt. They do not eat, for there is nothing to consume. Their sustenance is subtler. They glide on the warm thermals rising from the geothermal vents, tracing slow, patient arcs over the city and the dust-flats. They are drawn to specific phenomena: the faint magnetic ghosts lingering in the cores of the great dormant engines; the static charge that builds on the edges of the ceramic mesas; the last, fading thermal signatures deep within the brick fortresses. They are scavengers of energy, not matter. They harvest the plateau’s final whispers of activity, the last tremors of a world that has shut down.

Their purpose is maintenance of the equilibrium. They are the shepherds of stasis. When a pool of the mercury-like fluid evaporates into grey dust, a dust-kite will often be seen circling low over the spot, its wingtips stirring the particles. When a single, inexplicable *clang* echoes from a factory hall, a kite will sometimes alter its course, drifting toward the source as if to absorb the vibration. They are attracted to the rare orange lamps, not for the light, but for the slow chemical decay that sustains it—they seem to siphon the entropy from the reaction, prolonging its centuries-long burn.

They do not communicate. They have no society, no hierarchy. Yet there is a pattern to their flight, a vast, slow choreography that spans the entire plateau. It is a pattern of balance. If too many gather in one sector, the ambient temperature there drops by a fraction of a degree; they disperse, and the warmth restores. They are part of the plateau’s circulatory system, a circulatory system for a body in perfect, endless sleep.

They are not born. They simply are. Occasionally, one will cease its gliding and settle on a high ledge or a rusted conveyor. It will fold its wings and become utterly still. Over millennia, the gentle, abrasive wind will wear at its frame, smoothing the brass, thinning the membrane, until it is indistinguishable from the dust it rests upon. And elsewhere, perhaps near a vent where the heat is strongest, the dust and metallic filings in the air will, through some unknowable confluence of static and residual intention, coalesce into a new, jerking form that will unfold crude wings and join the silent ballet. The number remains constant. They are a closed loop.

They are the final thought of the city. They are the memory of purpose, distilled into a silent, endless patrol. They live in the plateau of final machines because they *are* the plateau of final machines—its will made manifest, not to act, but to preserve the profound, finished silence. To watch over the long, slow wearing-down of all things, and to ensure that not even that erosion disturbs the perfect, warm, and starless peace.
