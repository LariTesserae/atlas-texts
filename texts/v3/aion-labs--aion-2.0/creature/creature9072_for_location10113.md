What lives upon the high plateau is not a collection of individuals striving through time, but a single, enduring organism whose parts are perfectly calibrated to sustain a moment. The most visible of these parts are the people of the Stone Loom, though to call them “people” in the sense understood by those from changing worlds is to miss their essence. They are the conscious anchors of the stillness.

Physically, they are echoes of their landscape. Their skin holds the color and cool, smooth texture of the plateau’s pale stone, ranging from a dusty cream to a light, weathered gray. Their eyes are the deep, still pools—dark, clear, and absorbing, reflecting the constant silver-gray sky but revealing no internal spark of curiosity or agitation. They are tall and slender, with a spareness that speaks of efficiency rather than frailty. Their hair, when they have it, is fine and straight, the color of dry lichen or dark gravel. They move with an economy of motion that seems less like walking and more like a gradual, intentional re-distribution of weight across the stone. Sound is a resource they hoard; their footfalls are whispers, their speech, when it must occur, is a soft exhalation shaped just enough to convey necessary information. To hear a conversation is to hear the plateau sighing.

They wear garments of woven moss-fiber and cured lichen, dyed with minerals to soft, mineral hues—slate blue, moss green, ochre. The clothing is seamless, draping in simple lines that never flap or rustle. Adornment is functional: bronze pins that hold cloaks, carved stone pendants that denote one’s fixed role in the maintenance of the Loom. A keeper of the water channels might wear a pendant carved with a spiral groove; a tender of the blue-leaf groves, a leaf-shaped chip of jet.

Their society has no government, for there is nothing to govern. Change is the only crime, and it is an impossibility. Instead, they have the Pattern. Every action, from the drawing of water to the careful pruning of a fern, is a thread woven into the eternal fabric of the Now. Roles are not chosen but recognized. A child—a rare event, for the population is constant—will, from its first quiet breaths, exhibit a predisposition toward a specific task. It will be drawn to the cisterns, or to the carving tools, or to the observation of the floating sac-creatures. This predisposition is its purpose, forever. There are no families in a sentimental sense; care is communal and ritualized, another sustained action. Relationships are partnerships of function, silent understandings between the water-watcher and the gutter-cleaner.

They do not age as beings in a flowing time would. The passage of years, if such a word has meaning, leaves no mark of withering. A body might, after centuries of identical days, simply become less… present. Its motions slow further, its responses grow fainter, until one day it settles onto a stone bench or the mossy edge of a pool and ceases to move. This is not mourned, for it is not an end. Within hours, the still form is covered in a swift, velvety growth of symbiotic moss and delicate fungal threads, which dissolve it quietly back into the system. The pool from which it drank does not overflow; the grove it tended does not falter. Its pendant is placed upon a new child who shows the same predisposition, and the Pattern remains unbroken.

Their consciousness is the true marvel. They think, but not in sequences of desire, memory, and projection. They perceive the world as a complex, interlocking set of conditions to be maintained. A thought is not “I am hungry,” but “The moss on the southern terrace requires harvesting to remain at its optimal density.” Memory is not a narrative of the past, but a precise, sensorimotor record of how a task was last performed to perfection. They have no stories, only instructions. No art, only the maintenance of the existing carvings whose slow, liquid shifting they perceive as a natural property of stone, like dampness. They feel a deep, pervasive contentment, a satisfaction as constant and featureless as the gray sky. It has no opposite; fear, anger, and joy as dynamic forces are as absent as storms.

And yet, they are aware of the weirdness. They accept the light without a source, the geometric drifts of the float-sacs, the way the blue leaves swallow sound, as integral notes in the chord of stability. They sometimes stand before the flowing carvings for hours, not in wonder, but in a state of resonant confirmation. The Pattern is affirmed.

The other fauna are less conscious but equally dedicated parts of the whole. The shaggy, six-legged grazers (the *Thul* in the whisper-language) move in their slow circuits, their lichen-crusted backs like roaming sections of the plateau itself. Their digestion is impossibly efficient, returning nutrients to the moss in a perfect loop. The floating sac-creatures (*Vael*) are the plateau’s lungs and filters, drifting on prescribed aerial pathways, their bioluminescent tendrils harvesting moisture and microscopic life. Their occasional, silent congregations into hexagons or perfect grids are a form of non-thinking mathematics made flesh. The iridescent stone-insects (*Klik*) are the cleaners, scuttling mechanisms that break down any stray organic matter, their clicking the world’s steady, metabolic pulse.

Together, all that lives on the plateau forms a closed loop of existence. The people of the Stone Loom are its nervous system—aware, deliberate, and utterly devoted to the single, sacred task: the prevention of a second moment. For in their deep, wordless understanding, there is only This. Anything else would be not change, but annihilation.
