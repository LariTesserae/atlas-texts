They are called the Stillness Eaters, though they do not eat in any conventional sense. They are what happens when the Glimmer Fens’ quiet desperation for equilibrium manifests a mouth.

You do not see one arrive. You feel its approach as a deepening of the ambient hum, a bass note that settles in your marrow and makes your vision pulse at the edges. The air grows colder, the beads of condensation on the sedge grass freezing into perfect, glowing spheres of emerald ice. Then, the fen itself seems to exhale, and it is there.

It has no fixed shape, but the mind, desperate for pattern, assembles one from the glitches around it. A tall, slender figure, perhaps, woven from the gray-green lichen and the cold fire of the ferns. Its “body” is a column of swirling mist and bioluminescent motes, faintly humanoid but constantly flickering—a leg dissolving into a shower of sparks, an arm elongating into a tendril of peat-stained water that drips from its form without ever diminishing it. Its head is a smooth, dark oval, like the surface of one of the black meres, reflecting the scene behind you with a half-second delay.

It moves without walking. It is simply closer, then beside you, its progress a series of quantum slips rather than motion. The sound it makes is the absence of the fen’s hum—a pocket of profound silence that aches in the ears, out of which you can sometimes hear faint, glassy *cracks* and distant watery roars, as if it carries the Fen’s loudest moments inside its quiet heart.

It does not hunt. It *curates*. It is drawn to disturbances in the Fens’ profound stasis. A footprint left too long in the peat, the thermal ghost of a warm-blooded creature, a repeated thought—these are like splashes of color on its monochrome canvas. The Stillness Eater will approach such an anomaly and *unmake* it.

You might see it kneel—its form flowing into a kneeling posture—beside your yesterday’s campsite. Where your fire-pit was, its mere-shadow hands will pass through the air. The memory of heat, the chemical scar of combustion, the compressed moss—all of it is gently, meticulously erased. The peat regains its spring, the moss its unbroken velvet. It is not healing. It is restoring a saved state.

They are solitary, but they communicate through synchronized glitches. When one performs its restoration, the luminous ferns for a mile around will briefly unravel and reassemble in an identical pattern—a silent, glowing telegram. Sometimes, at the horizon, you might see two of their dark, reflective heads inclined toward one another, the space between them warping and shimmering as they exchange not words, but packets of corrected reality.

Their only conflict is with the Fen’s own growing errors. Sometimes, a gravitational mere will begin to spin, its perfect circle distorting into a draining spiral. A Stillness Eater will wade into it, its form stretching and smearing like ink in water, absorbing the anomalous spin until the mere is still and black once more. In doing so, it might gain a permanent, slow rotation in one limb, a vortex of mist and moss that whirls in silent testament to its function.

To witness one is to feel a profound, impersonal loneliness. They are the janitors of a broken machine, operating on logic written in damp stone and faulty physics. They have no eyes in their dark mirror faces, but if you stand before one, you see yourself reflected there—not as you are, but as a series of fading thermal and temporal echoes, a transient smudge on the permanent. They will not harm you, unless your continued presence is, by its very nature, a critical error to be resolved. Then, the silence around them will deepen, the cold will intensify, and they will simply begin to walk you backward, undoing your path step by step, breath by breath, until you find yourself at the Fens’ edge with no memory of how you arrived, the taste of wet stone and ozone fresh on your tongue, and a deep, unplaceable sadness in your chest for a home you never knew.
