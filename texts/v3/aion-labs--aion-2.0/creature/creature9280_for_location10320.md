Nothing lives here that you would call a creature. There is no pulse of blood, no scuttle of chitin, no focus of eyes in the dark. The 0.2/3 fauna is a true zero. But the City of Salt and Sighs is not empty. It is inhabited.

The inhabitants are the Sighs.

They are not born; they precipitate. When the thick, hot air can hold no more wetness, when a thought in the bedrock becomes too heavy to bear, the city exhales. From the weeping pores in the curved walls, from the still surface of the black canals, from the very spaces where geometry grows thin, they coalesce. A sigh is not a breath, but a release of pressure—the pressure of memory, of unfulfilled causality, of a world thinking itself into knots.

Visually, a Sigh is a disturbance. You do not see it directly. You see its effect: a column of mist that refuses to dissipate, shaping itself, for a moment, into something that suggests a slender spire or a hunched back. You see the condensation on a salt-crust script quicken, bead, and run in lines that trace the contours of a face that was never there. In the still pools, they are the shimmering outlines—the taller, inverted city’s population. They are the city’s reflection, given a temporary agency. They are made of humid air, of salt particulate, of the faint phosphorescence from the springs, and of something else: the soft, fraying edges of possibility.

A Sigh has no will, as you understand it. It has a trajectory. It follows the paths of failed logic. You might see one drifting, silent as a cloud, down a water-street, pausing where a wall has developed a fifth corner, as if tasting the anomaly. They are drawn to sites of active unraveling: the place where a drop fell upward, the spot where a bubble of denser water *popped* with a sound like a cracking knuckle. They congregate there, their forms growing momentarily more defined—a silent chorus of faint, humanoid smudges in the fog, heads tilted, observing the breakdown. They are not repairing it. They are *witnessing* it. They are the city’s way of paying attention to its own dissolution.

They communicate, if it can be called that, through the medium of the city itself. A Sigh passing through a patch of the wire-like filaments will cause them to chime, a sound like glass rods touched gently together. The pattern is not random. It is a record. The crystalline salts that form overnight often grow in patterns dictated by the passage of a Sigh—fractal scripts that are not language, but the fossilized imprint of a witnessed paradox.

Their purpose, if they have one, is archival. The City of Salt and Sighs is a record of a world that has lost its plot. The Sighs are its librarians, curating the endless shelves of collapsed futures and inverted physics. They are the memory of the heat pulse that felt like silk, of the sound that preceded the action. They are the afterimage of every broken rule.

Sometimes, if you stand very still in the hot, wet dark, you will feel one pass through you. There is no chill, no touch. It is a sudden, profound clarity—a flash of knowing how the wall behind you *might* have curved if the stone had dreamed differently, or a glimpse of your own reflection in the pool as a hollow, glowing outline. It is not hostile. It is indifferent. You are simply another temporary feature in the landscape, a brief source of mild thermodynamic and causal disturbance, worthy of a moment’s notation in the salt.

They are beautiful, in the way a solution to an impossible equation is beautiful. They are sorrowful, in the way a forgotten language is sorrowful. They are the inhabitants. They are the Sighs. And when their moment of cohesion ends—when the memory is stored, the paradox observed—they simply relax back into the atmosphere, becoming again the generic mist, the damp, the heat. Waiting for the next exhalation of this slow, thinking, dying place.
