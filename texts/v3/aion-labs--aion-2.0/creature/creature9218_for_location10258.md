They are not tall. The thin air and the constant, gentle labor of fitting into the stone has compacted them. Their frames are wiry, their shoulders slightly rounded from a lifetime of moving under low lintels and carrying loads along the curving paths. Their skin is the color of weathered wood or pale stone, etched fine around the eyes and mouth not with age, but with the memory of the wind. Their hands are the story of the place: broad palms, thick fingers, nails short and hard as slate. They are tools for gripping, for stacking, for feeling the moisture in the moss. Their eyes are the grey of the sky, or the dark brown of the seep-hollows, and they have a way of looking that is not a glance, but a slow settling, like water finding its level.

They dress in layers of felted wool, from the few tough sheep kept in a lower, slightly richer valley a day's careful descent away, and in cured hides from the scarce, darting creatures of the shelf. The colors are undyed—greys, browns, the dirty white of wool. Their garments are repaired endlessly, a patch over a patch, the stitching a topography of its own. They wear no jewelry, for there is nothing to make it from, but an individual might be known by the particular pattern of darns on their sleeve, or the way their belt is woven from strips of worn leather.

Their work is maintenance. A man might spend a morning, or a week, re-laying a single section of the stone path where a slab has settled. He will not quarry a new one. He will find a stone from a tumble at the shelf's edge, test its fit with his hands, chip at it patiently with a smaller stone, and settle it into the gap. The action is rhythmic, unhurried. A woman might sit in the lee of a wall, carding wool, her movements so slow and efficient they seem like a natural process—the teasing apart of fibers as inevitable as lichen spreading. Children, from the time they can walk, are given small tasks of observation: to watch a cistern and note when the drip-rate slows, to follow a particular patch of moss and report if its green deepens or fades. Play, when it occurs, is mimicry of these tasks: stacking pebbles, pretending to card tufts of dry grass.

They live in family clusters, the buildings of a single rib-path housing grandparents, parents, children. There is no central authority, no chieftain or council. Decisions that affect the whole shelf—when to collectively gather reeds for thatch, whether to adjust the course of a seep-collection channel—are made through a process of slow consensus. A matter is mentioned. It is then not discussed, but carried. For days, weeks, individuals will ponder it while performing their tasks. They will visit one another, not to argue, but to share a bowl of thin stew and sit in silence. Eventually, a direction emerges, as moisture beads on stone. It is felt by all, and acted upon. Disagreement is not conflict; it is a sign the matter needs more carrying. Violence is almost unthinkable, a wasteful expenditure of energy as absurd as trying to hurry the stone's weathering.

Their language is spare. It has many words for subtle variations of damp (*sil, keth, ullen*), for the quality of stone (*tarr* for solid bedrock, *lisk* for a useful flat piece, *vor* for crumbling, unreliable stone), for the stages of moss-growth. It has few words for abstraction, for hypotheticals. Their grammar favors the passive and the present continuous. One does not say, "I will repair the wall." One says, "The wall is being repaired," locating the action in the world, not the self. There is no future tense, only a present that extends. Stories are not narratives of heroes, but meticulous descriptions of past events: the Great Thatch-Renewal of three generations past, the season the seep from the eastern hollow turned sweet. They are told not to entertain, but to remember a state of the world.

They dream, but their dreams are not wild. They dream of walls standing firm, of cisterns full, of the steady *plink, plink, plink* of water. A nightmare is a dream of silence—of the wind stopping, of the drips ceasing. To wake from such a dream is a profound relief; to hear the soft exhale of the air is a blessing reaffirmed.

They do not worship gods. They perceive will, but not in anthropomorphic forms. The mountain is not a deity; it is a condition. The damp is not a spirit; it is a process. Their respect is practical, observant. They know that to take too much moss is to damage the reservoir; to block a seep is to starve a cistern downstream. Their morality is ecological equilibrium extended to human interaction. The greatest wrong is to create imbalance, to introduce a hastening or a hoarding where there should be flow and share.

Time, for them, is not a river, but a sediment. They mark it by the wearing of a threshold stone, by the gradual silvering of a thatch roof, by the generational shift in the pattern of repairs on a cherished pot. A person's life is not a journey toward a goal, but a gradual deepening into their place within the shelf. Elders are valued not for wisdom in the abstract, but for the depth of their memory—they are the living record of the shelf's minor fluctuations. When someone dies, their body is wrapped in their most-patched garment and carried to a designated cleft in the northern rock wall. It is laid there, and stones are carefully stacked to seal the cleft. It is not a tomb, but a return. The minerals will leach slowly back into the shelf. They speak of the dead as "settled."

They have no concept of progress, and thus no concept of stagnation. A visitor from the lower, hastier world might see their life as one of poverty and stifling sameness. They would not understand the visitor's pity. They have everything the shelf provides, which is precisely enough. They feel no lack. Their wealth is the quiet, the surety of the slow processes continuing, the lack of want. Their art is the fit of stone upon stone, the pattern of repairs on wool, the precise note of a correctly struck rock during paving.

To such a visitor, they are courteous but profoundly puzzled. They will offer water, a seat out of the wind, a share of their simple food. They will answer questions with factual, minimal descriptions. They will ask few questions in return, for the visitor's world of rushing and gaining is as alien and slightly frantic to them as a sudden, violent storm. They will watch the visitor's restless eyes, their quick movements, their desire for *meaning* or *story* beyond what *is*. And when the visitor leaves, descending the path to where time flows faster and shadows are sharp, the people of the shelf will feel a collective, unspoken release. The brief, faint vibration of imbalance will fade. The long, quiet breath will resume. They will return to their tasks, to the patient fitting of their lives into the stone, into the damp, into the endless, gentle now.
