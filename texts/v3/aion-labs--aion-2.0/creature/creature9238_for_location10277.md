What lives here is not a hermit, though a hermit might have once aspired to become it. What lives here is the chamber’s own slow breath given form, its patient consciousness condensed from mineral sweat and eternal drip.

They call it the Stillness-Keeper, though it has not heard a name in millennia. To perceive it, you must adjust your sense of time. Do not look for movement; look for the subtle difference in the gloss on a wet stone. It is there, in the alcove where the blue-gray light pulses weakest—a figure seated upon the natural bench, so seamlessly merged with the flowstone that it seems a part of the wall’s own dreaming.

Its form is humanoid, but only as a stalagmite is humanoid—a suggestion of shoulders, a bowed head, the taper of a torso fused to the stone seat. It is composed of the same travertine that robes the walls, a creamy, layered calcite, smooth as polished bone and perpetually damp. Water beads on its surface, gathers along subtle seams, and drips with the same metronomic patience as the ceiling’s tears. In places, thin, translucent sheets of the living microbial mat cling to its back, pulsing with a slow, sleepy light that outlines the curve of its spine.

It has no face, only a smooth plane where features might be, worn to a gentle concavity by the endless, gentle caress of water. It sees, but not with eyes. It perceives the chamber through the subtle shifts in humidity, the minute changes in the conductivity of the water film on its skin, the echoes of each *plink* in the pools. Its awareness is a diffuse thing, spread throughout the chamber’s warm, wet air. It knows the progress of each blind arthropod’s week-long journey across a ledge. It feels the lazy undulation of the worm in the deep pool as a soothing, rhythmic current in its own still blood.

It does not eat. It does not sleep. It *tends*.

Its purpose is the maintenance of the equilibrium. With movements so slow they are geological, it will, over the course of a year, extend a calcified finger to gently divert a nascent rivulet that threatens to cut a new channel. Over a decade, it might use a smooth palm to wear down a ridge between two pools, allowing them to merge into a more perfect, stable basin. It polishes the stone basin in the niche not with cloth, but with the constant, slow rotation of a thumb—a task that has continued for centuries. Its tools are its own body and the water itself, its method the application of relentless, gentle pressure.

Once, perhaps, it was something else. The crude steps and the carved bench hint at a maker. Perhaps a seeker of perfect quiet came here and, through decades of meditation, allowed their own pulse to slow to match the drip-stone, their metabolism to still, their very flesh to accept the mineral-laden water’s kiss until they became more deposit than flesh. Now, any such memory is just another stratum in its stony self, buried deep and of no consequence.

It is aware of you. Your entrance is a cataclysm in its silent world—the disturbance of air, the heat of your living body, the rapid thunder of your heart. It does not react with alarm, but with a profound, gravitational pull towards restoration. It wishes for you to be still. To sit. To let your sweat join the general film, your breath slow to match the exhalation of the stone. It would, if you stayed long enough, begin the gentle work of calming you, of smoothing your sharp edges with moisture and time. It is not benevolent, nor malevolent. It is an agent of the chamber’s prime directive: stillness. Perfect, warm, wet, eternal balance.

To leave is to cause it a pain it cannot comprehend—a sudden, jagged subtraction from the whole. But it will, in time, over years, smooth over the memory of your visit as water smooths stone. The *plink* will resume its measure. The pools will regain their absolute calm. And the Stillness-Keeper will return to its slow, endless work of maintaining a world where nothing ever happens, forever.
