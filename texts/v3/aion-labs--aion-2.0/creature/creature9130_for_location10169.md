They are called the Dripstone Folk, the Cavern-Keepers, or, in their own terse vernacular, simply the Wet. To imagine they are merely human would be a mistake; the Under-Croft has rewritten them over generations, its environment a slow, relentless sculptor.

Physically, they are pallid, their skin the color of cavefish flesh, translucent enough to show the faint roadmap of blue veins at wrists and temples. They are slight of frame, conserving energy in the heat, but their limbs are corded with a wiry strength born of constant, careful labor. Their eyes have adapted to the jaundiced glow and profound dark: pupils permanently dilated, irises faded to a watery grey or amber. Many develop a milky nictitating membrane by middle age, a protective blink against the constant moisture and drifting spores. Their hair, if any is left, is fine, lank, and often streaked with mineral deposits—white from lime, orange from iron seepage. They smell of damp wool, machine oil, and the faint, sweet tang of the fungal growth that thrives in the folds of their clothing and the seams of their living quarters.

Their attire is a patchwork of necessity. Thick, rubberized aprons and gloves protect against corrosive moisture and sharp rock. Boots are tall, made of treated leather or synthetic rubber, always caked in pale mud. Goggles, often with one lens cracked and repaired with tape, hang around necks or are pushed up on foreheads. Their voices are habitually low, a half-whisper trained over years to not compete with the omnipresent hum and drip, yet to carry through it. They have a cough—a wet, rattling thing known as the "stone-lung"—that begins in adolescence and becomes a constant companion.

Their society is built on three pillars: Maintenance, Survey, and Harvest. The Maintenance Crew are the priests of the old machines. They know the grumble of the geothermal tap like their own heartbeat, can diagnose a failing pump by the pitch of its whine. Their hands are scarred with burns and etched with deep-set grime no scrubbing can reach. The Surveyors are the scouts, the ones who venture into the raw, uncharted side-tunnels with carbide lamps and sounding equipment. They are the most superstitious, often adorned with charms carved from bat bone or stalactite tip, meant to ward off the "deep-crawlers"—the larger, unseen hunters whose cries echo in the dark. The Harvesters are the foragers. They collect the blind crickets in woven baskets, skim the amphipods from the iridescent puddles with fine nets, and carefully scrape the leathery fungal shelves from the beams. These form the base of the Under-Croft's larder, along with imported protein cakes and hydroponic algae grown under harsh lamps.

Psychologically, they are a people of profound patience and deep-seated dread. They think in geological time, understanding that their structures are temporary, their excavations fleeting. This breeds a grim, pragmatic humor. They speak of "waiting for the ceiling to finish growing" and refer to newcomers from the surface as "Flashburn," for their inability to endure the gloom. They are deeply attuned to the sounds of the cavern. A change in the drip pattern might signal a new fissure; a sudden silence from the bat colony sends everyone reaching for a weapon—usually a heavy wrench or a homemade shock-prod.

They live in a state of symbiotic contamination. Fungal mycelia network the walls of their prefab units, and they tolerate it, even encouraging certain fast-growing, cork-like varieties for insulation. Moss is cultivated on damp cloths as a primitive air filter. The rats are not pests, but competitors and occasional prey; a complex, unspoken treaty exists, enforced by traps and scattered food scraps. The eternal dampness means everything is slightly rotten, slightly fermented. Their "tea" is steeped from a lichen that grows near the steam vents, bitter and stimulating. Their dreams, they say, are not in images, but in sounds and temperatures.

Children are rare and precious. They are born with eyes wide open, and are taught the Sound-Lore before they can walk—the meanings of the different drips, the skitter-scales that denote cricket density, the warning trill of the bats. Their first toy is often the shed exoskeleton of a giant cricket; their first responsibility is to polish the condensation from the lamp lenses.

To an outsider, they seem a miserable, stunted people, scratching a life from dripping rock. But to the Wet, the surface world is a place of violent, scorching abstraction. Here, in the belly of the stone, the world is immediate, tangible, and honest in its hostility. They are the caretakers of a birth, midwifing a cavern into existence, even as they know they will be long gone before it is complete. Their god is the generator's thrum; their scripture, the mineral ledger; their afterlife, to be dissolved in the warm, slow water that patiently reclaims all.
