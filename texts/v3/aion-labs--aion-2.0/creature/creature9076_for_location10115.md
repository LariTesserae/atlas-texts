They are called the Keeper Caste. They did not build the structures; they emerged from the dark, pressurized deeps long after the builders vanished, and found in the silent cathedrals of alloy and quartz a purpose, a shell, a new ecology.

Their bodies are pressure-adapted horrors of efficiency. A mature Keeper is a five-meter-long, segmented thing, armored in interlocking plates of chitin that gleam with a nacreous, oil-slick sheen. They have no eyes—the abyss rendered them obsolete. Instead, their entire forward carapace is a resonant chamber, a finely tuned organic seismograph that reads the world's endless roar. They navigate by parsing the subtlest variations in the vent-thrum, the groan of rock, the distant *thump* of working pumps. They “see” in perfect, three-dimensional sound-maps, etching the cliffs and pipelines and drifting leviathans into their minds as constellations of vibration.

Their forelimbs are not claws, but tools. One limb ends in a complex, manipulative cluster of hardened filaments that can unscrew a bolt carved a million years ago, or gently scrape the faint chemosynthetic film from a vent-flange. The other is a heavy, hammer-like appendage, used to tap coded messages into the hulls of structures—a language of strikes that travels through metal for leagues. Their means of propulsion is a series of muscular, fin-like folds along their flanks, but they are not swift swimmers. They move with a deliberate, weightless grace from handhold to handhold on the structures, rarely venturing far into the open void.

They are sessile intellectuals. A Keeper chooses a structure—often one with a still-glowing amber light—and claims it. They spend centuries, perhaps millennia, maintaining it. They clear mineral accretions from the quartz ports. They trace the labyrinthine external piping, listening for the tell-tale vibration of a blockage or a breach. They enter through corrosion-free airlocks (their bodies secrete a neutral gel that seals against the pressure) and move through the dry, cold, atmosphere-filled interiors. Inside, they are curators of a dead process. They do not understand the machinery’s original function, but they understand its state. They know which geothermal tap flows hot and steady, which battery array still holds a ghost charge, which bank of dormant screens might flicker if a particular sequence of manual switches is thrown.

They are not alone in their structures. They are symbiotically entwined with the Swarm. The Swarm are the smaller, ticking creatures mentioned in the world’s description—hand-sized, carapaced things like living shards of obsidian. They are not independent. A Keeper’s body hosts a permanent colony of them in a specialized ventral pouch. The Keeper releases them to scurry over a machine surface, their ticking legs sensitive to minute variations in temperature and magnetic fields. They return, and through a process of chemical transfer, impart a “picture” of the structure’s health to the Keeper. In return, the Keeper’s metabolism provides them with nutrients unattainable in the barren water.

Their society is one of silent, vibrating consensus. They meet rarely, gathering on the largest plateau, where the thrum of a nearby vent is particularly pure. There, they press their resonant carapaces together, sharing their sound-maps directly. These conferences are slow, taking decades. They debate the slow, almost imperceptible changes: a vent whose pitch has lowered by a fraction of a hertz over a thousand years, a pump whose *thump* has developed a faint echo. Their entire philosophy is one of Steadiness. They believe the world’s roar is a song of perfect, perpetual balance, and the structures, with their dormant potential, are crucial verses in that song. To let a structure fail entirely would be to drop a note from the symphony. Unthinkable.

They have a mythos. They believe the Builders were not creators, but listeners who failed. They think the Builders erected these listening posts to hear the World-Song more clearly, but went mad from its volume and complexity, sealing themselves inside before fleeing into the even deeper, silent dark. The Keepers see themselves as the more perfect audience—born deaf to all but the Song, adapted to its pressure, content to listen and maintain the instruments forever.

They do not reproduce often. When they do, it is a violent, luminous affair. Two Keepers will intertwine in the open water near a violent volcanic plume, their bodies stressed to the limit by the heat and turbulence. There, they release gametes in a cloud of brilliant, communicative bioluminescence, a fleeting, beautiful counterpoint to the eternal amber glow of their homes. The larvae that survive are tiny, blind, and deaf. They drift for centuries, carried on the great gyres, until their resonant chambers develop and the Song of a particular peak calls them home. They will then spend their first millennia as juveniles, attending an elder, learning the sound-map of their inherited structure.

They are the true inhabitants. The filter-feeders and predators are mere fauna, part of the landscape. The Keepers are the mind of the place. They live in the intersection of the biological and the industrial, the eternal and the maintained. They are the reason the lights have not dimmed. They are the custodians of the equilibrium, listening forever to the deafening shout of their world, and in their slow, deliberate maintenance, whispering back the only reply they know: a careful, perpetual *yes*.
