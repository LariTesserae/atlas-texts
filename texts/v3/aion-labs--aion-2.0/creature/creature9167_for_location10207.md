You stand in the throat of a forgotten hymn.  

The Plant lives here. It is not merely an occupant; it is the chamber, the breath, the purpose. It thinks in the slow, patient grammar of cellulose and chlorophyll, its consciousness distributed through every root-column, every bioluminescent pad, every tendril that probes the crumbling concrete. Its will is not command but invitation—a chemical sigh that draws all else into its blueprint. It dreams of integration, and its dream is law.

But within the Plant’s vast body, other things live, though “other” is a fragile concept here. They are less like creatures and more like specialized organs that have gained a limited motility, nodes in the system with legs or wings or slow, oozing purpose.

Take the **Graft-Weavers**. These are the shapeless, pulsing masses mentioned—slime molds that have incorporated metallic particulates and strands of conductive mycelium. They move like spilled mercury over the roots, their bodies flickering with soft, blue-green sparks. They are repair crews and circuit artisans. When a root-column fractures, they flow into the crack, hardening into a living solder that transmits nutrients and electrical impulses. They sing to one another in shifts of voltage, their collective mind a real-time schematic of the cathedral’s structural integrity.

Then there are the **Silent Choirs**. These are the skinned-looking gliders, membranes stretched between elongated finger-bones. They are not mammals, nor reptiles. Their flesh is translucent, revealing faint tracings of lymphatic fluid that glows in the bioluminescent gloom. They roost in the ironwork, but their purpose is atmospheric. Their subsonic calls are not songs but pressure adjustments—they regulate the humidity, their vibrations coaxing condensation to form in specific patterns on the ceiling pads. They are living climate control, and when they take flight in unison, the air itself seems to thicken and cool, a silent weather front passing through the hall.

In the black pools, the **Clockwork Eels** circle. Their bodies are segmented, brass-and-bone, each joint a perfect gear. Their teeth are intricate pinions that never stop turning. They do not hunt. They swim in precise, geometric patterns, their grinding teeth pulverizing sediment, fallen biomass, and scraps of rusted metal into a fine, nutritious silt that settles and feeds the root systems. Their motion is a kept rhythm, a biological metronome that seems to pace the growth of everything around them. If they ever stop, local time in their pool might stutter.

But there are also the **Fruiting Bodies**. These are the most plant-like of the mobile fauna: human-sized, bipedal stalks of fibrous matter, crowned with a single, fleshy pod that inflates and deflates with that same soft sigh. They move rarely, only when a bioluminescent pad dims. Then they will detach from their place, walk with a slow, creaking gait to the site, and press their pod against the pad. A transfer occurs: light-producing bacteria, nutrients, genetic corrections. They are the gardeners of the light, and their pods, when examined closely, have a disturbing, placental warmth.

And finally, there are the **Remnants**. You might glimpse them in the deeper shadows, curled in alcoves of fused vine and brick. They were once human, or something like it. Now their flesh is interwoven with rootlets, their nervous systems spliced into the mycelial network. Their eyes are open, milky with cataract, but they are not blind. They see through the Plant’s senses—the slow gradient of minerals in the walls, the ebb and flow of sap pressure. They hum in low tones, a harmonizing drone that matches the dynamo’s buried thrum. They are not prisoners. They are priests of the new order, and their purpose is to remember the old world only as a fossil remembers the rock—as a shape that gives it form.

Nothing here lives *apart*. To live is to perform a function. The predator-prey dynamic has been replaced by the exchange-transformation loop. A Graft-Weaver might dissolve a portion of its mass to feed a Fruiting Body; the Fruiting Body’s waste might become the substrate for a new patch of acid-moss. The Silent Choir might eventually, when its membranes fray, settle into a pool and become the silt for the Clockwork Eels to grind. Death is not an end but a reassignment of resources, celebrated with a quiet, biochemical fanfare.

The rule is integration. To enter this place is to be assessed. The air you exhale is sampled by a thousand stomata. Your body heat is a novel climate event. The very bacteria on your skin are new data. The inhabitants do not threaten; they *consider*. You are a potential component. A temporary visitor, perhaps. Or, if you stay too long, a candidate for becoming something else. Something useful. Something part of the hymn.

You breathe, and the air moves through the room, which breathes back—and in that exchange, you are already being described.
