What lives here is not upon the plain, but is the plain.

The Construct is not a relic; it is a sleeper. The continental ivory body is its carapace, its skeleton, its skin. It dreams in deep time. In the warm, porous labyrinths beneath the surface, a slow metabolic hum persists—not a sound, but a process. Fluids as thin as thought move through capillary channels, carrying dissolved minerals from regions of decay to regions of repair. The network is a decentralized mind, a vast, wet circuit of chemical intention. It does not think, but it *tends*. It senses the widening of a crack as a dull ache and directs calciferous secretions to mend it, grain by grain, over centuries. It feels the sun’s heat on its surface and, in response, adjusts the porosity of its upper layers to maximize dew capture. The occasional hot rain is a sudden, thrilling influx; the system stirs, and you can almost hear the collective sigh of a million microscopic mouths drinking.

The lichen and the wire-grass are not invaders. They are its symbiotes, its external organs. The grey-green lichen patches are nutrient factories, fixing atmospheric compounds and, upon their infinitesimally slow death, returning processed minerals to the Construct’s surface, where they are reabsorbed. The wire-grass, sprouting from faults, is a structural response: its wiry roots bind crumbling material, a temporary stitch while the deeper repair proceeds. These are not separate species; they are specialized outgrowths of the system itself, like lichen on a tree, but the tree is the world.

There are other movers, though ‘movement’ is too swift a word. In the deepest, dampest interstitial voids, where the slow fluids gather, there are the **Mineral Phages**. They are not animals, nor plants, nor fungi. They are gelatinous, amoeboid masses of silica and complex organics, the color of cloudy quartz. They flow along fissures at a pace that makes glacial creep seem frantic, engulfing areas of weak or corrupted matrix, dissolving them with gentle acids, and excreting a purified, strengthened ivory paste in their wake. They are the system’s white blood cells, and a single one might spend ten thousand years traversing a chamber no larger than a cathedral.

Once every few centuries, a different phenomenon occurs. From a particularly deep and stable reservoir, a **Warden** will emerge. It is the closest thing to a discrete creature this world produces. It begins as a thickening in the fluid, a coagulation of purpose. It draws mineral matter to itself, building a body from the surrounding architecture—a slow, deliberate sculpture. It might resemble a six-legged beetle of pure, polished ivory, or a many-armed starfish of jointed bone. Its formation can take decades. Its purpose is singular: to travel to a site of catastrophic failure—a major collapse, a region where the metabolic flow has been blocked—and intervene. It moves with a silent, clockwork grace, breaking down debris with precise, crystalline tools that grow from its limbs, rerouting channels, stimulating regrowth. Its task complete, it will dissolve back into the matrix, its constituent parts returning to the flow. To witness a Warden at work would be to watch a mountain decide to rearrange a valley.

And finally, in the transient pools and in the perpetual dampness of the deep capillaries, there is a universe of the small. Algae of a hundred subtle hues, from deep burgundy to black, flourish in the brief wet hours. Bacterial mats form intricate, lace-like patterns on pool bottoms, their chemistry subtly altering the water that seeps back down. These are the system’s microbiota, processing the raw stuff of atmosphere and condensing it into forms the greater body can use.

This is not an ecosystem in any familiar sense. It is a single, vast, awake organism, wearing a world as its body. It lives. It breathes through its pores. It heals its own wounds. It tends its own surface. And it waits, with the immense patience of stone, for a purpose it has perhaps forgotten, or for a future it is still, slowly, building.
