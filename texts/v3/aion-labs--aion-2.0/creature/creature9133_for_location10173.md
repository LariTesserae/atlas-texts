The inhabitants of the Sky-Basin are not so much beings as they are processes made visible, equations of chemistry and pressure given slow, elegant form. To call them animals or plants would be to force them into a taxonomy of water and sap, which this world has never known. They live, but they live as mountains live—through accumulation, erosion, and a patience that borders on permanence.

First, the **Silicate Worms**. They are the closest thing to fauna that moves with intention through the under-mat. They are not worms of flesh, but of fused, glassy platelets, a flexible chain of silica and complex polymers. They do not burrow so much as *dissolve* a path, excreting a mild acid that softens the spongy substrate just enough for them to glide through. They are blind, deaf, and sensate only to gradients of chemical potential and the faint vibrations of the planetary tide. Their purpose is one of slow circulation: they break down older, stagnant layers of the bioluminescent mat, and their waste products—delicate, opalescent strands—become the raw material for new growth. They move in languid pulses, a full contraction and expansion sometimes taking an hour. To observe one is to watch a vein of molten glass cool in slow motion.

Above them drift the **Sky-Scales**. These are the gliding shapes of interlocking chitinous plates. They are not birds, nor even flyers in the active sense. They are more like kites or sails, their broad, flat bodies engineered by evolution to catch the subtle thermal gradients and the slow currents of differing gas compositions. Their "wings" are vast, semi-rigid membranes of a material like darkened mica, etched with conductive patterns that help them sense the electrical charge of the drifting spore-veils. They have no mouths, no digestive systems. They are photosynthetic, but not in the manner of Earth's plants. Their upper surfaces are layered with photoreceptive cells that feed directly on the white dwarf's brutal light, converting it not into sugar, but into a stable electrical potential that powers their internal chemistry. They coast for decades, riding the continent-spanning weather systems, only descending rarely to anchor themselves to a tower and exchange trace minerals through root-like filaments. Their only sound is the faint, crystalline *click* of their plates adjusting to pressure changes—a sound like two stones being tapped together in a vacuum.

Then there are the **Tower-Colonies** themselves. These accumulations are the dominant, sentinel life-form. They are not individuals, but vast, collaborative architectures. Each tower is a city of specialized cells, a symphony of chemical roles. The papery membranes that spiral upward are lungs and leaves and nerves all at once. They draw specific isotopes and complex hydrocarbons from the thin air, processing them in internal, labyrinthine chambers. The fractal patterns on their surfaces are not decoration; they are catalytic pathways, molecular templates that accelerate the synthesis of the compounds they need from the atmosphere. They communicate, if it can be called that, through the release and detection of subtle pheromonal cues in the air—a language of chemistry that operates on a timescale of years. A "thought" might take a decade to form and broadcast. Their conflict is a silent, glacial war. If one colony's chemical harvest begins to starve a neighbor, the afflicted tower may slowly reorient its growth, or more brutally, extend a root-like probe to infiltrate the other's base and begin secreting a compound that dismantles the rival's structural polymers over centuries. This is predation as geology.

The most ephemeral inhabitants are the **Aural Plankton**. They fill the still air, invisible except when the light catches them at the perfect angle, and then they sparkle like diamond dust. Each is a microscopic, geometric shell—a dodecahedron or a complex helix—of silica or polymer, containing a droplet of the dense hydrocarbon slurry that is this world's life-blood. They are not alive in an active sense; they are *potential*. They drift on the breathless winds, a genetic and chemical library suspended in time. When conditions are right—a specific combination of temperature, pressure, and ambient chemistry—a shell will crack open, its slurry activating upon contact with a suitable substrate (often the spongy mat or the side of a tower), and begin the process of accretion that may, over millennia, become a new colony or a silicate worm. They are the world's seed, its memory, and its future, hanging in the air like silent prayers.

Finally, there are the **Eaters-of-Light** in the mat. The spongy, glowing carpet is not a passive layer. It is a nervous system for the continent. Within its resilient fibers thrive countless micro-colonies of chemosynthetic and photosynthetic organisms. But it also houses the **Mat-Siphons**. These are stationary, cup-like structures of a rubbery, black material. They are passive hunters. Their open cups face the sky, collecting the sparkling Aural Plankton and the dust of dead Sky-Scales. Inside, a slow, enzymatic process breaks down these catchments into their basic chemicals, which then seep back into the mat to feed the glow. Their hunting is a patient, open-mouthed waiting that lasts for eons.

Nothing here has a face. Nothing here has a voice. The heart of this world does not beat; it *crystalsizes*. The inhabitants are not born; they precipitate. They do not die; they are subsumed and re-sequenced into new forms. They live in a profound, collaborative, and competitive ballet where a single movement takes a thousand years, and the only audience is the cold, bright, and silent sun.
