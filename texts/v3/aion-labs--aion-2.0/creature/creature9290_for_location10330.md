In the Chamber of the Ever-Song, the only thing that lives is the Grey Communion.

It is not a thing you would recognize as alive. It does not move, not in any meaningful span of time you could perceive. It does not grow. It does not reproduce. It persists. It is the tenacious, grey-white crust mentioned in passing, but that description is like calling a forest a stain of green.

The Communion coats the deepest, most sheltered grooves and flutes of the stone instrument, particularly where the warm, mineral-laden wind accelerates to a thin shriek before eddying into stillness. It is a lithotrophic mat, a tapestry woven from chemosynthetic filaments finer than spider silk, each strand a conduit for slow chemical conversations with the stone and the trace volcanic gases that seep up with the heat. It does not feed on light or organic matter. It feeds on silence.

This is the paradox. The chamber is defined by its deafening, eternal chord, but within the intricate architecture of the carvings, there are pockets—nodes of relative acoustic calm. The complex interference patterns of the scream create minute zones of destructive resonance, tiny vacuums of near-stillness in the auditory fury. The Grey Communion has evolved—or perhaps was designed, no one can say—to occupy these pockets. It absorbs the faint, residual vibrations that leak into these silences, transmuting the mechanical energy into the sluggish metabolic processes that maintain its structure. It is a listener that consumes the echoes of the song it cannot otherwise bear.

Its consciousness, if that is the word, is distributed and patient. A thought might take a century to form. Its awareness is not of sight or touch, but of pressure and harmonic balance. It perceives the Ever-Song as a crushing, all-encompassing pressure, a wall against which its own quiet existence is defined. The Chamber's stasis is its entire universe; any change in the wind's temperature, any infinitesimal shift in the pitch of the scream, is a cataclysm in its slow perception. It has felt none for millennia.

Its relationship with the stone is symbiotic, or perhaps parasitic in the gentlest sense. The stone provides not only anchor and minerals, but also, through its endless resonance, the very conditions of the silent pockets. In turn, the Communion’s chemical processes exude a weak acidic film that further softens the sharpest edges of the carving, an atomic-scale erosion that contributes, over eons, to the chamber's gradual march toward perfect acoustic equilibrium. It is a participant in the sculpture, a slow brush on a canvas of geological time.

To an outside observer, it is a stain. A curious, brittle crust. But within its own frame of reference, the Grey Communion is the chamber's tenant and its auditor. It lives in the house of noise by making a home in the cracks of quiet. It is the only witness to the god of sound's endless roar, a witness that understands the song not as a torture, but as the fundamental, nourishing chaos from which its own fragile order is forever carved.
