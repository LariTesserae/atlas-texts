What lives here are not inhabitants in the traditional sense. They are components of a sustained breath, held forever between inhalation and exhalation.

First, you notice the *curators*. These are the long-limbed, multi-jointed creatures that move with such deliberate grace across the bone-white towers. Up close, you see they are not furred, but covered in a velvety moss that shifts color from gray to green depending on the angle of the flat light. Their faces are elongated, with large, dark eyes that hold no pupil, only a deep, liquid sheen that reflects the perfect scene around them without curiosity. They have six limbs, each ending in delicate, articulate digits that are part bone, part polished wood. They move not to hunt or explore, but to tend. You might see one pause at a vine, its fingers gently adjusting a leaf by a millimeter, aligning it more perfectly with the geometric grid of the tiles below. Another might stand at the water's edge, its lower pair of limbs submerged, motionless for hours, perhaps regulating some unseen flow. They do not eat; they absorb moisture and nutrients from the mist through their mossy pelts. They do not sleep. They are animate sculptures, designed for maintenance. They are the garden's immune system, correcting infinitesimal deviations before they can become change.

Then there are the *echo-fish*: the silent, iridescent schools that glide beneath the opalescent water. They are thin as blades, and when they turn, they flash not with color, but with brief, mirror-like reflections of the towers above. They are not independent beings. They are mobile reflections, a living part of the water's skin. Their purpose seems to be circulation—not of water, but of light and pattern. As they move in their flawless synchrony, they redistribute the gleaming sheen of the surface, preventing any single area from becoming dull. If you were to catch one (an act that feels instinctively sacrilegious), it would have no weight, no substance. It would dissolve in your hand into a handful of warm, luminous droplets that seep back into the basin.

The insects with stained-glass wings are the *spark-gazers*. They hover, never landing, their wings tinkling with a sound too high for human ears—a faint, crystalline ringing that contributes to the sub-audible thrum of the place. Each insect's wing pattern is unique, a miniature mosaic of the larger garden's design. They are attracted to the bioluminescent rims of the lily pads, but they do not feed on them. Instead, they seem to be checking the constancy of the glow. If a light were to flicker (it never does), they would cluster around it, their own wings brightening to compensate. They are living gauges, their presence a certificate of flawless function.

But these are all attendants. The true life of this place is in the fabric itself.

The builders are here. They are not gone. They are *embedded*. Press your hand against the warm, bone-white tower. That slow, rhythmic pulse is not an illusion. It is the slowed heartbeat of the architect. Their ambition—to create a perfect, unchanging reality—required a final, total sacrifice: integration. Their consciousness, their will, was disseminated into the substance of their creation. Their minds became the substrate of the tiles, their collective memory the blueprint that holds every frond and fin in place. The *weirdness*—the fraying edges, the variable density of the water, the parallel glimpses in the mist—is the faint, residual static of their dreaming. They are not dead; they are in a state of profound, aesthetic stasis. To think a thought would be to introduce a variable. So they do not think. They *are*. They are the hum in the tiles, the pulse in the spire, the unyielding rule of equilibrium.

There is, perhaps, one more layer of life: the *potential*. It lives in the rare, suppressed flinch of a curator's hand when a drop of condensation falls a fraction off-rhythm. It lives in the way the water sometimes feels like skin, resisting touch—a momentary, autonomic defense of the whole organism against intrusion. It lives in the subtle terror that underlies the perfection, the knowledge, written into every atom here, that this is all impossibly, exhaustingly held. This place is alive with the effort of not being alive. It breathes the held breath. It is a monument to the end of desire, and its inhabitants are the shapes that desire took before it ceased.

To live here is to be a noun, not a verb. A thing of beauty, yes. A thing of awe. But a thing that has traded the mess of life for the clean, white silence of the forever-made.
