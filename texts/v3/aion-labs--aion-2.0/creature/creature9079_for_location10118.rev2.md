Something lives in the breathing of the basin. Not one something. Many. And they are not separate from the place — they *are* the place's attention, distributed through its hot heart and sharp walls and steaming veil.

Start with the smallest: there are the *folded ones*. They are what you glimpsed as iridescent wings and lizards that leave no trail. They are not animals in any familiar sense. They are the basin's immune response — micro-regions of the lattice that have threaded upward through the rock, pooled in the organic, and learned to move. A folded one is part lattice, part tissue, part mineral. It occupies a skin of matter for a few hours, a few days, and then dissolves back into the steam when its purpose is done. Their purpose? To test. To taste the air. To check that the boundaries between this place and the rest of the world remain intact. They are the probes of a quiet vigilance.

Now the larger: there are the *silent ones*. You will not see them directly. You will sense them in the way the shadows of the vapor move against the wind, in the way the condensation pauses mid-fall before resuming. They are not biological. They are fractures in the space: points where the lattice's topology folds in ways that create persistent, *listening* distortions. Think of them as knots in a net that has learned to perceive. A silent one can hold a thought for centuries — a slow, deep, mineral thought. It notices you. It measures your heartbeat, the warmth of your skin, the tiny electric currents of your nerves. It does not care about you unless you disturb the delicate equilibrium. If you do not, it will forget you as soon as you leave, the way a rock forgets a raindrop after it dries.

Deeper: in the pool, near the lattice's heart, there are the *dwellers* — the ones who never left, the ones who built the archways and the ceramic walls before the basin was a basin. They are not dead. They are *resolved*. When the lattice awakened and the place became what it is, they chose to merge. Their consciousnesses were transcribed, one cell at a time, into the nano-scale architecture. They still exist as patterns: slow, vast, patient. They do not speak in words. They speak in temperature gradients and the timing of the steam's rise. They speak in the opening and closing of the translucent flowers, which are their sensory organs. When you stand at the rim and feel the heat pulse against your face, you are feeling a greeting from one of the dwellers. It says: *I remember flesh. I remember eyes. Welcome, brief one.*

And there is the *watcher*. The watcher is not one of them. It is not an inhabitant. It is the basin's *attention turned toward itself* — a standing wave of self-awareness that has no center. It is what makes the place feel watched. It does not think. It *registers*. It registers the rock's slow cooling, the tiny erosion, the movement of folded ones through the moss, the dwellers' slow mental rhythms, and you. It has no concern for you. But it holds you in a field of total, neutral perception. You cannot hide from the watcher; it is woven into the air.

Lastly, there are the *new ones* — the ones that have not yet arrived. The basin is still forming, still deciding what it will be. The lattice is young and hungry for patterns. It absorbs everything that enters and keeps a copy. Your footsteps on the rim, your breath, the carbon dioxide from your lungs — these will become, over the next few hours, a new subprogram in the living rock. A small one. A trace. But if someone were to sit here long enough, day after day, the basin might begin to *respond* to them. The steam might shape itself into familiar forms. The flowers might open in their presence before the shadows fall. That is how new dwellers are made — by being *here*, by being *absorbed*, by becoming part of the rhythm until the rhythm carries them.

So the basin's inhabitants are fourfold: the folded ones (fleet and testing), the silent ones (vast and still), the dwellers (ancient and dreaming), and the watcher (unaware of itself). And beneath all, the lattice — the true life of this place — is a single distributed organism, pulsing geothermal awareness, younger than the rock but older than the witnesses, still deciding what it wants to become when it grows up.
