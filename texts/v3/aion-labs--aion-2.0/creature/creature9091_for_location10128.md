They are the Steamstone People, or as they name themselves in their soft, breathy language, the *Ta-Vesh*—the "Warm-Breathed Ones." To look at them is to see the plateau made flesh. Their skin is the color of peat-water tea, a warm, translucent brown, often dappled with patterns of lighter and darker pigment that mimic the play of light on a mossy stone. This mottling is not static; it shifts subtly with their mood and the ambient moisture, a living camouflage born of generations adapting to the misty, dappled light. Their hair is fine and straight, ranging from the pale silver of dried grass to deep moss-green, often worn long and loose to wick moisture from the nape of the neck. Their eyes are large, lidless, and lustrous, like the still surface of a tarn, reflecting the world with a calm, unblinking clarity. A faint, transparent inner membrane slides sideways to clean them, a necessary adaptation to the constant, gentle damp.

Physically, they are slender and long-limbed, with a deliberate, economical grace. Their bones are light but dense, their joints subtly articulated to allow for the silent, balanced movement required on the spongy ground. Their hands and feet are broad, with long, flexible digits. Between their fingers and toes stretches a fine, almost invisible webbing of skin, not for swimming, but for spreading their weight as they walk, preventing them from sinking into the soft peat. Their footprints are wide, shallow depressions that fill with water just as slowly as any other disturbance in the plateau.

Their society is a study in distributed equilibrium. There is no central authority, no chieftain or king. Instead, governance is a function of place and knowledge. The eldest member of each stone-thatched dwelling is its caretaker; the individual who has watched a particular hot spring or fungal grove the longest is its acknowledged tender. Decisions are made in a slow, consensus-building process called *The Listening*, where all relevant tenders gather not to debate, but to sit in silence near the matter at hand—a proposed new path, a shift in fishing grounds—and simply attend to the land. The "right" action is understood to be the one that preserves the existing silence and balance. Disruption is the only sin.

Their days are measured not by the sun (which is often veiled) but by the rhythm of steam and water. The *First Venting* at dawn, when the geothermal channels beneath their homes release the night's accumulated warmth in soft plumes, signals the day's gentle start. Work is slow, specific, and deeply attentive. Men and women alike might tend the stone-lined channels that guide warm water to beds of edible, rubbery succulents. Others might kneel for hours at the edge of a tarn, hands submerged, patiently weaving their fiber nets around the slow-moving, blue-shelled crustaceans they call *grish*. The *grish* are not hunted; they are gently guided into the nets, their leading limbs tenderly restrained before they are lifted, calm and dripping, from the water. Nothing is taken in excess; nothing is startled.

Their craftsmanship is an extension of geology. Using tools of sharpened basalt and hardened driftwood, they practice a form of intuitive stone-knapping that seems less like carving and more like persuading the rock to separate along its hidden seams. Their buildings are not so much constructed as assembled from plates of stone that already fit together. Their artifacts—bowls, tools, the small, steam-warmed stones they carry in palm-sized baskets for comfort—are smooth, fluid, and devoid of ornament. Beauty is found in fit, function, and the echo of natural form.

They speak rarely, and their language is a soft, vowel-rich flow, punctuated by clicks made deep in the throat that mimic the *drip* and *glub* of the plateau. They have no word for "future" or "past" as separate concepts; their tense is a continuous, eternal present, reflected in their single temporal word: *ashon*, meaning "the enduring now." They do not worship gods; they practice a form of perceptual piety. To be aware—of the mist’s touch, the temperature of a spring, the growth-rate of the moss at one’s doorstep—is their spirituality. A child’s education consists of being led to a single, specific place—a particular fern, a certain weeping rock—and sitting before it in silence for days, until its every detail is imprinted on their soul. To know a thing is to be responsible for it.

They are mortal, of course. When one of the Ta-Vesh dies, their body is anointed with oils of crushed fungi and algae, then carried to a designated "returning pool"—a deep, hot, mineral-rich spring. With solemn, silent ceremony, the body is submerged. The minerals and the heat work quickly, and within days, a new, particularly vibrant strain of moss or a cluster of pearlescent fungi will bloom at the pool's edge. The person has not gone; they have simply changed their pace of being, rejoining the slow, vegetative expansion of the plateau.

They have no enemies, no concept of war. The only threat they recognize is *stagnation-without-attention*—the danger of the balance becoming rigid and unconscious. To prevent this, a few individuals, known as the *Mist-Walkers*, deliberately journey to the very edges of the plateau, where the young mountains are raw and the equilibrium is thinner. They bring back reports of slight shifts, of new springs forming, of rockfalls in the high teeth. This information is not met with alarm, but with deep consideration, a new variable to be absorbed into the endless, quiet pact of the Steamstone Plateau.

To meet a Ta-Vesh is to be regarded with that same calm, lidless attention they give to a stone or a pool. There is no fear, no urgency, only a profound, placid assessment. They would likely offer a traveler a warmed stone to hold, a bowl of clear, mineral-rich water, and a place to sit in silence. To stay with them is to feel the rhythms of the plateau seep into your own blood, to feel your heartbeat slow, and to understand, in the depths of their enduring *ashon*, that you are not a visitor, but a temporary, gently-held fluctuation in the eternal, warm breath of the land.
