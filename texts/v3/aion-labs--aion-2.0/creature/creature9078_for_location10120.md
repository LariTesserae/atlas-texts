They are the Dusters. Not a name they gave themselves, for they have no language left that shapes sounds in the thin, dry air. It is a name that comes from observation, from the way they move, and from what they are made of.

If you are patient, and if you stand in one of the narrow, winding paths between the frozen monuments long enough, you will see one. At first, it is easy to mistake them for a trick of the soft reality—a flicker at the edge of vision where a spire’s shadow should be but isn’t. Then, a subtle reconfiguration of the gray and umber dust that coats every plate. A gathering of particles, a slow coagulation.

They are small, perhaps the height of a child, but there is nothing childlike about them. Their forms are vague, approximations of bipedal shapes, as if remembered from a dream and sculpted in hurried clay. They have no clear faces, only slight depressions where eyes might be, and a lower slit that could be a mouth. Their surfaces are the exact color and texture of the plain’s sintered crust—dull, dusty, a mixture of lead and dried blood. They are, in essence, walking fragments of the ground itself.

They are not many. In a day of watching—though days do not exist here—you might see two or three. They move with a shocking slowness that makes the frozen geometric fauna seem restless by comparison. A single step can take an hour. The lifting of a crude hand, fashioned from five knobs of aggregated dust, can take half that. Their motion is not smooth, but a series of infinitesimal jerks and settlements, like a glacier calving in slow motion. They leave no footprints; their feet are merely broader, flatter accumulations that merge seamlessly with the plate they stand on when they are still, which is almost always.

Their purpose is obscure. They tend the primitive structures—the windbreaks, the pebble circles, the lean-tos. But ‘tend’ is too strong a word. They approach a circle of white pebbles over the course of a week. They might, with a movement so gradual it is perceived only as a change of state, nudge one pebble half an inch to the left. Then they will be still for another week. It is maintenance at a geological pace, a ritual of preservation so slow it is indistinguishable from decay. The structures are not being built up; they are being subtly rearranged, as if the Dusters are solving a puzzle with no solution, over and over.

They interact with the great frozen creatures only by proximity. A Duster will station itself at the base of a towering black helix or beside a collapsed lattice of bone-white struts, and it will simply stand there. For months, years, centuries. It is unclear if they are worshipping, studying, or keeping some form of vigil. Perhaps they are listening. The silence, after all, is not empty; it is full of the finished work, and maybe to the Dusters, that work has a texture, a frequency.

They do not eat the brass-colored lichen. They do not drink, for there is nothing to drink. Their energy, if they have any, seems drawn from the plain itself—from the subtle wrongness in the geometry, from the pressure of the static sky. They are not born, nor do they die in any observable way. Sometimes, a Duster will simply cease its microscopic motions. Over decades, its form will soften, its edges blur, and it will settle back into the mosaic of plates, indistinguishable from the ground. Elsewhere, perhaps, a new coagulation will form. It is a cycle of persistence, not of life and death.

They are the caretakers of the museum, but they are also its final exhibit. They are what is left of whatever mind first stacked stone against the wind here. That mind has been leached of urgency, of desire, of story. The Dusters are memory without recollection, intention without goal. They live because ‘live’ is the only verb that remains, stretched so thin it has almost no meaning. They are the inhabitants: the slow, dusty echo in the cathedral of frozen forms, the only thing that still, in the most minimal sense, moves.
