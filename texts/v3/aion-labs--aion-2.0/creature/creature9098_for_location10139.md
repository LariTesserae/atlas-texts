They are creatures of resonance and damp, their forms and rhythms dictated by the plateau's foundational hum. To list them as species feels incomplete; they are better understood as components of the landscape's slow industry, each performing a role in the processing of rock, water, and cold air.

The most visible are the **graveldwellers**, the low-slung herds that flow across the spongy ground. They are bulky, barrel-chested beasts, standing no higher than a man's waist, their bodies draped in long, matted hair the color of wet slate and peat. This shaggy coat is not merely insulation; each coarse filament is hygroscopic, actively drawing moisture from the mist and channeling it in minuscule beads down to the skin, where it is absorbed. They drink through their hides. Their heads are low, faces broad and blunt, with wide nostrils that filter the thin air and exhale plumes of vapor that hang about the herd like a localized fog bank. Their most striking feature is their hooves—broad, flat, and semi-porous pads that spread their weight on the unstable substrate. With each step, a soft *plock* sounds as water is squeezed from the clay, but the step itself is a careful, seismic act. They move in response to the plateau's hum, their migration routes following subterranean resonances that signal areas of fresh mineral exudation. They are not grazing on the sparse sedge; they are harvesting the mineral-rich biofilm of algae and bacteria that grows on the weeping rock faces, their rasping tongues leaving clean, damp stone in their wake. Their digestion is a slow, cold ferment, and their dung is a catalyst, seeding the damp gravel with the precursors for new moss.

Above them, the **tinsel-flits** perform their aerial calculus. They are small, no larger than a sparrow, but their bodies are sheathed in iridescent plates that refract the dull light into sudden, sharp flashes of blue-green-copper. Their wings beat not with the soft flutter of feathers, but with a brittle, metallic clatter—the sound of tearing foil. This is no accident. Their flight feathers are modified into overlapping keratinous scales that vibrate in sympathy with the atmospheric hum. They do not simply fly; they are tuned. Their mesmerizing, synchronized flocks are not just for predation or protection; they are a form of atmospheric sensing. The precise interference patterns of their wingbeats, read across the swarm, map minute variations in air pressure, humidity, and—crucially—the sonic gradients of the hum. They are living anemometers and seismographs, their collective movement a real-time diagram of the plateau's energetic output. They feed on the clouds of silica-midges that rise from the warmest of the weeping rocks, catching them with quick, precise snaps of beaks that click like quartz striking quartz.

Below, the **engineers** work. These are the burrowing insects, their segmented bodies the size of a human thumb, armored in burnished brass carapaces that never tarnish in the constant damp. They move in ceaseless, single-file lines, each individual carrying a granule of gravel, a pellet of clay, or a precious fragment of nutrient-rich detritus. Their industry is literal earth-moving. They are not building hills, but rather preventing them. Their networks of shallow tunnels are a drainage and distribution system, subtly guiding the first flows of water, preventing chaotic erosion and encouraging the even, slow saturation that defines the plateau. They are tuned to vibration as well; the rhythm of their march is a perfect counter-rhythm to the foundational hum, a biological damping effect that stabilizes the soil. Where their trails concentrate, the moss grows thickest, fed by the micronutrients they deposit.

And then there are the **stillnesses**—not fauna, but a symbiotic entity. The patches of grey-green moss are not passive. Their root-like rhizoids secrete a mild acid that synergizes with the sonic vibration, accelerating the exudation of water from the rock. In return, the weeping provides a constant, mineral-rich drip. The moss also generates a faint, localized heat, a byproduct of its slow metabolism, creating the "micro-climates" mentioned. In these tiny, warm zones, the first, simple invertebrates thrive, which in turn attract the tinsel-flits and provide food for the engineers.

There are predators, of course. **Shroud-stalkers** are lean, long-legged canids that move with a terrifying silence, their fur absorbing sound and their footfalls, like those of the graveldwellers, spreading wide on the mud. They hunt not by sight in the shadowless world, but by listening to the disruption of the hum. A graveldweller herd masks the vibration; a lone, sick one creates a dissonant echo. The stalker hears this and moves in. Their kill is swift, a clamp of jaws on the windpipe, and they consume their prey where it falls, leaving the bones to be slowly absorbed by the damp clay. Their role is that of a quality-control mechanism in the herd, culling the weak, ensuring the grazers' attunement remains precise.

What lives here, then, are not merely animals occupying a niche. They are active participants in a system of nascent geology. They are tuned instruments in the plateau's orchestra, their lives—their grazing, flying, digging, and dying—all contributing to the profound, industrial work of building a world.
