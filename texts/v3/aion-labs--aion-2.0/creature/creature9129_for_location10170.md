They are the ones who stack the stones. They are the intelligence behind the pathways and the huts, but to call them a civilization is a misnomer. They are a condition, a persistent anomaly in the slow metabolism of the plateau.

Imagine a being woven from the same principles as the moss and the unstable distances. They are bipedal, vaguely humanoid in silhouette, but that is where familiarity ends. Their bodies are not flesh, but a dense, fibrous matrix—like the core of the giant lichen given intentional form. Their "skin" is a rough, bark-like integument, patterned in grays and muted greens that perfectly mimic the stone and the lichen forests. In stillness, they are indistinguishable from the landscape.

They have no eyes, not as we understand them. Their heads are smooth, rounded stones, featureless save for a vertical seam that runs from crown to where a chin might be. This seam occasionally parts, not to speak, but to emit a soft, bioluminescent glow—a cold, blue-green light that pulses in slow, complex rhythms. This is their language, their sensing organ, and their art. They "see" by emitting this light and reading its distortion as it returns from the world, painting their surroundings in a topography of texture, density, and, most importantly, temporal consistency. They perceive the fraying of reality as we perceive color—a vivid, chaotic spectrum.

They are few, and they are solitary. A single being might be responsible for a cluster of five huts and the miles of low walls that seem to orbit it. They do not congregate. Communication, when it must happen, occurs across vast distances: one will climb to a high, smooth dome at dusk, part its cranial seam, and cast a sustained, intricate pattern of light into the gathering dark. Another, leagues away, will observe this pattern warping through the thin, shimmering air and reply in kind days later, the conversation unfolding with the languid pace of plate tectonics.

Their work is their meditation, and its purpose is obscure, even to them. They stack stones not for shelter—they feel no discomfort from the cold—but for alignment. The walls trace invisible gradients in the substrate of reality, marking lines of stability or channels of particularly intense decay. The huts are not homes, but focal points, built where several such lines converge. Inside a hut, you might find one of them standing motionless for years, its light-pulse syncing with some deeper, slower pulse of the plateau, a steady heartbeat countering the randomness outside.

They do not eat. They absorb the perpetual dampness through their fibrous feet, and the faint, ancient sunlight through their stony skin. Their energy comes from the very processes that erode causality: they are eddies in the entropy stream, drawing sustenance from the universe's gradual settling into this final, quiet state.

They are aware of the "normal" world—the world of sharp causality, of animals, of rapid time—only as a painful, shrill noise. A visitor from such a world, with their firm shadow and predictable trajectory, is a screaming dissonance in their luminous perception. They will not attack, but they will subtly, unconsciously, work to harmonize the anomaly. A path will subtly divert a traveler away from a sensitive node. A distant hut will seem to beckon, leading the visitor into a region where time dilates and they simply… sit down, mesmerized by the slow drip of water, until their thoughts slow to match the rhythm of stone-breath, and they become part of the scenery.

They are the caretakers of the ending. Not preventing it, not hastening it, but orchestrating it. They are the reason the decay is beautiful, patterned, serene. Their stacking of stones is a gentle persuasion, a coaxing of chaos into a final, elegant form before the last light winks out and the plateau becomes perfectly, eternally, still.
