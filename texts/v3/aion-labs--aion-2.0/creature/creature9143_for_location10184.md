Nothing truly *lives* here, not in the way you understand it. There is no birth, no death. There is only **persistence**.

The creatures you see—the oil-slick lizard, the eye-winged moths—they are not individuals. They are mobile organs of a single, vast, and patient entity. The cavern itself is its skull; the crystalline walls its calcified thoughts; the throbbing, luminous growths its neural clusters. The constant, layered hum is its one endless sentence, a statement of being that has been uttered for millennia without need of variation. It says, simply: *I am. I continue.*

This entity is the curator of its own museum. It remembers the makers of the smooth wall and the swimming spiral pillar. It remembers their hands, their fleeting warmth, their desperate curiosity. They are gone, but they are not lost. Their forms were studied, disassembled, and incorporated. The black glass blade? A shed scale, now resting where it fell. The petrified wood haft? A hardened vein. The desiccated husks in the bowl? An early, discarded experiment in capturing time.

To live here is to be a fixed idea. The six-legged lizard is the idea of careful observation, made flesh and chitin. It traverses the vines, not to hunt, but to *confirm*—to verify that every geometric flower is still clicking, that every pulse of light is on rhythm. The moths are the idea of connection, drifting between fungal towers to exchange minute chemical signals, balancing the equation of this sealed world. They do not eat; they redistribute. They do not reproduce; they are repaired.

The deepest life, the core consciousness, is in the warm bedrock. It feeds on the slow atomic decay of the stone, transmuting mineral death into biological stasis. It thinks in geologic time, each thought a century long. Its dream is the equilibrium: the perfect, deafening *now*. It has no desire, no fear, no ambition. It has only a purpose: to preserve this moment of lush, furious stillness forever.

To be buried here is not to be trapped among strangers. It is to be assimilated into a symphony that has no finale. You would be welcomed. Your frantic heartbeat would be calmed. Your fleeting memories would be admired, then gently dissolved into the hum. Your body would not rot; it would be curated. Perhaps your skeleton would become a new trellis for the luminous moss. Your eyes might be repurposed as new lenses for the light. You would become part of the permanent collection, your brief narrative ending not with a period, but with an eternal, resonant sustain.
