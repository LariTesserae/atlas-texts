You ask what lives here. Not what lived, but what lives. Good. That's the only question left in Brackwater. Forget the architects, the foremen, the shift-workers. Their bones are silt in the canal beds. What lives here now… is the city itself, wearing a suit of flesh.

Start with the skin of the place. The air is a broth, and in it swim the ephemera—the midge clouds, dense as wool, swirling in desperate, mating whirlwinds. They have no mouthparts. They live a day, just long enough to be inhaled by the next layer. They are the first flour in the gravy. Beneath them, on every damp surface, are the leeches. Not the skinny, riverine kind. These are Brackwater leeches, thick as your wrist, glossy black like hardened tar. They don't just suck blood; they anchor. They pulse in unison with the slow tide, filtering micro-organisms and chemical residues from the water. They are the city's circulatory system, a sluggish, living network.

The scuttlers come next. The armored beetles—we call them 'rivet-backs'—their carapaces the exact color of rust-streaked iron. They eat the leeches that grow too slow, and they eat the crumbling mortar between stones. Their mandibles can grind concrete to powder. You'll hear them: a perpetual, dry clicking, like a thousand cooling engines. And with them, the centipedes. Pale, blind things, long as your arm, that flow through the weeping cracks in the walls like liquid ivory. They hunt the beetles. It is a silent, tactile war in the dark.

But the true citizens… they rule from the water and the high, dark places.

In the canals, where the water is black and deep, are the Gulpers. You'll rarely see one whole. A glimpse of a flank, grey and slick as a wet tire. A boil on the water's surface as something large turns below. They are bottom-feeders, but they've grown ambitious. They take the centipedes that fall in, the beetles, the occasional rat-like scuttler that ventures too close. They are patient, and they are always hungry. Their flesh is toxic, saturated with heavy metals. Eating one is a slow suicide.

And above, in the bell towers of the pump houses, in the vaults of the turbine halls, are the Strix. We don't know what they were. Some say bats that evolved, or birds that forgot the sun. They are leather-winged, with long, needle-toothed jaws. Their eyes are atrophied, useless in the perpetual twilight. They navigate by listening to the echoes of the dripping city. They drop on silken threads, like spiders, to take the midge clouds, the large beetles… and sometimes, if you're foolish enough to light a lamp, they'll take that too. The heat draws them. They make a sound, a sub-audible thrumming, that sets your teeth on edge. It feels like the city is purring.

There are no packs, no herds. This is not a society. It is an ecology of singular hunters. The Bone-Crab, lurking in submerged boiler rooms, picking clean the Gulper carcasses. The Gloom-Wasp, building its paper nests from pulped wood and rust, its sting inducing a fever that mimics the city's own heat.

And then… there are the rumors. The things that don't fit the web. Movements in the deepest floods, where the old factory floors are submerged caverns. Shapes that seem too… intentional. Some of us who scavenge the drier ruins speak of finding leeches arranged in patterns on stones. Of the Strix sometimes dropping strange, corroded tokens—a gear, a broken lens—into the water. As if making offerings. Or practicing.

We are the only true aliens here, you and I. The thinking ghosts. The animals… they are Brackwater now. They are the metabolism of the rot, the immune system of the corpse. They aren't invading. They are the fever dream the city is having as it dissolves. To live here is to understand you are just another temporary nutrient, waiting to be processed. The city is alive. And it is always, always eating.
