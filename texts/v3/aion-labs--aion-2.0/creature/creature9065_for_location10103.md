They are called the **Mycorrh**, though they have no word for themselves as a separate concept. To be is to be of the Hollow, of the Root. They are not its caretakers, nor its masters; they are its nervous system, its mobile appendages, its fever-dream of self-awareness.

Physically, they are slender and tall, adults often reaching two meters, but so attenuated they seem like shadows stretched wet upon the walls. Their skin is the color of waterlogged heartwood — a greyish brown, dappled with patterns of darker pigment that mimic the striations of bark and the dappling of fungal light. It is cool to the touch, and smooth save for fine, downy hair that traps a layer of insulating air and moisture. Their limbs are long, their fingers and toes equally so, ending in broad, flat pads that adhere slightly to slick surfaces, leaving faint, damp prints. They are hairless save for the head, where thick, ropy locks, the texture of wet hemp, fall in matted strands, often threaded with living moss, glowing lichen, or the shed exoskeletons of jewel-insects.

Their faces are where the symbiosis is most evident. They have no visible ears; sound is perceived through vibrations in the jawbone and a series of sensitive pits along the neck. Their eyes are large, black, and liquid, capable of gathering the faintest luminescence. The pupils are vertical slits, like those of a deep-sea fish, and they lack whites entirely — the orb is all dark iris and deeper black pupil, giving them a perpetually startled, absorptive look. They have no noses as such; two vertical slits above the mouth flare slightly with each breath, filtering the spore-thick air through a maze of bony filaments. Their mouths are wide, lips thin and pale. When they speak, their voices are soft, sibilant, a language of clicks, hums, and glottal stops that carries well in the damp air and doesn’t disturb the fragile acoustics of the Hollow.

They are, as the description noted, cold-blooded. Their internal temperature matches their surroundings. This dictates the rhythm of their lives. They are torpid in the deepest cold, clustering in heaps around the clay hearths, their metabolisms slowing to a bare murmur. When they must move, they first bask — not in sunlight, but in the radiant heat of their small, resin-fired pots, or press their backs against the rare, warm spots in the root-wall where some deep chemical process generates a gentle exotherm. Their clothing is functional architecture: layers of felted moss, cured salamander-skin cloaks, and caps of woven fungal leather, all designed to trap what little warmth their bodies or their environment can provide. The ever-falling ghost-leeches are not pests to them, but a minor nuisance, and sometimes a resource; a Mycorrh will calmly pluck a fat, heat-engorged leech from their own arm and pop it into their mouth as a salty, jelly-like snack.

Their society is matrilineal and fungal in its organization. There is no central chief, no council of elders. Instead, knowledge and decision-making propagate through the community like mycelial networks. Key individuals, usually the oldest females who have memorized the growth-cycles of the Root and the migration patterns of the cavern’s larger fauna, act as **Nodes**. They hum the news, the warnings, the decisions. Others listen, repeat, modify. Consensus emerges not from debate, but from a gradual harmonization of the communal hum. It is slow, deliberate, and astonishingly effective for a people whose world changes at the pace of growing roots.

Their relationship with the megaflora — the **Root-Mind** — is not one of worship, but of tense, intimate negotiation. They do not believe the Root is a god; they *know* it is a vast, slow, vegetable consciousness with wants. It wants certain paths cleared of debris. It wants the parasitic fungus on its south wall scraped away. It wants the warm bodies of the Mycorrh to sleep in certain alcoves, their exhaled carbon dioxide and trace body heat stimulating growth in a particular direction. In return, it provides. It grows a platform exactly where a new family needs one. It guides its root-tips to form a natural barrier against a flooding seep. It even, on rare occasions, withholds its toxic pollens or guides a predatory tunnel-eel away from the settlement. The Mycorrh “listen” to the Root by pressing their palms and cheeks against its wet flesh, feeling the minute tremors of sap-flow, the subtle contractions of growing fibers. They “speak” to it by painting specific patterns of fermented mud and crushed luminescent fungus on key junctions, which the Root seems to taste and comprehend through its epidermal cells.

Their technology is an extension of their bodies and their environment. Bone from the giant, blind salamanders is carved into needles, awls, and fish-hooks. Flint and sharp shale are knapped into cutting edges. But their masterwork is in fibers and knots. They weave rope from braided root-hairs and sinew so strong it can hold a grown Mycorrh’s weight. They make nets to catch the swimming larvae in the pools, and baskets to carry gleaned fungi and edible root-tubers. Their most prized possessions are **heat-pots** — small, intricately carved clay vessels with ventilation holes, inside which a slow, smokeless fire of compressed peat and resin burns for days, providing a portable heart of warmth.

Their art is ephemeral and functional. They weave patterns into their moss cloaks that tell family histories. They arrange collections of iridescent insect wings on beds of damp peat, creating shimmering, temporary murals that decay in days. Their music is the music of the Hollow itself: they mimic the drip-rhythms, the sigh of transpiration, the click of insect mandibles, using hollow bones, stretched skins, and their own vocal cords. A Mycorrh “song” is often indistinguishable from the ambient soundscape to an outsider, until one realizes the patterns are too perfect, too synchronized.

They fear the **Root-Burrowers** — pale, worm-like carnivores as thick as a tree trunk that sometimes tunnel into the lower peat, causing whole sections of the settlement to collapse into sucking, icy water. They fear the **Silence**, a rare phenomenon when the drip stops, the insects go still, and the Root-Mind seems to hold its breath — a prelude, they believe, to a major growth-spurt or a catastrophic collapse. And they fear, in a abstract way, the **Above**, the world beyond the bruised violet sky they glimpse through the few, tiny chimneys that vent their hearth-smoke. It is a place of terrible, desiccating wind and a light that burns, a mythic wasteland.

They are a young people in a young world, feeling their way through the dark. They are not noble savages; they are pragmatic, quiet, and deeply strange. Their love is expressed by sharing warmth from one’s own heat-pot. Their grief is shown by allowing the ghost-leeches to cover the body of the deceased, drawing out the last of its heat until it matches the cold of the peat. They are the children of the Root, and their entire civilization is a whispered conversation in a dark, wet, living room.
