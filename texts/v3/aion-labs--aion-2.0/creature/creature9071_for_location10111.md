They are the Wet-Born, the Clay-Walkers, the People of the Slow Green Pulse. They do not name themselves thus; these are descriptions, not titles. To each other, they are simply the We, the Us, the ones who press hands to steaming rock and feel the world’s heartbeat through the stone.

Physically, they are lean and elongated, their frames a economy of tendon and long bone perfected for a world of vertical climbs and chest-deep wading. Their skin is the color of waterlogged clay—a dappled gray-green that darkens to near-black along the spine and limbs. It is not smooth, but finely pebbled, like the hide of a deep-water creature, and it secretes a thin, clear mucilage that beads the constant moisture away and prevents the clinging fungal spores from taking root. Their hands and feet are broad, with long, flexible digits connected by resilient webbing that is nearly transparent. They are as deft at knotting fibrous vines as they are at sifting through silt for blind crustaceans.

Their faces are dominated by large, lidless eyes that drink in the green gloom. The pupils are vertical slits, like those of a cephalopod, able to contract to pinpoints against the rare, diffuse glare of a sun-break. They have no external ears; sound is felt through vibrations in the jawbone and the sensitive skin of their temples. Their mouths are small, lipless slits, useful for the soft suction feeding of fungal sap and pulpy liverwort, and for the quiet, clicking language they speak—a language more of pressure and echo than of clear tonals, perfect for the muffled world.

They wear nothing but harnesses and pouches made of cured amphibian hide and woven fungal fiber, carrying their few tools: flake-knives of volcanic glass, bone hooks on sinew lines, drills made from hardened vine-thorns. Their adornment is living: they embed phosphorescent fungal nodules into small cuts along their arms and shoulders, which heal over, leaving permanent, softly glowing patterns that denote lineage and role. A vine-tender’s glow is a steady, cool blue; a clay-daubers’ a warm, pulsating amber; a deep-diver’s a intermittent, rapid green.

Their society is a mycelial network of obligations and silences. They live in the bulging clay mounds, each home housing an extended kin-cluster. There is no central chief, no council of elders. Instead, there are the Listeners—those whose nodule patterns have faded to a near-invisible white—who spend their days with their foreheads pressed against the great fungal monoliths or submerged in the deepest, pulsing pools. They do not hear words, but they sense the slow needs of the plateau: a vine-bridge growing weak here, a rock spire growing unstable there, a shift in the mineral seep from below. They emerge, dripping, and make a series of precise gestures. The gesture is seen, understood, and acted upon by those whose role it touches. A city repairs itself, not by command, but by silent, consensus osmosis.

Their time is not marked by sun or moon, but by the Great Pulse. Every few hundred of their heartbeats (a span outsiders might call a week), the bioluminescence in the deep pools swells to a bright, sustained emerald wave that washes through the shallows and makes the whole fungal forest glow in sympathy. It is a time of stillness. All work ceases. The Wet-Born stand, or float, or recline on their platforms, their own embedded nodules humming in resonance. This is their sabbath, their collective breath. They believe—they *know*—that this is the dreaming breath of the World-Below, the vast organism of which their plateau is but a single, dreaming lobe. They are its conscious tendrils, its hands. Their city is not built; it is the World-Below’s thought of order, manifested through their labor.

They are not hunters, but tender-gatherers. They farm the giant liverworts, pruning them to encourage thicker growth for bedding. They milk the weeping fungi for their sweet sap, which they ferment in clay pots into a thick, intoxicating slurry. They set bone-hooks in the deeper channels for the great, blind salamanders, and they harvest the pale crustaceans from beneath rocks. They are, above all, weavers. Their greatest art is the management of the living vines. They guide their growth, splice them, train them over chasms to form new bridges, and harvest only the oldest strands for cordage. Their city is a guided, symbiotic growth.

They have no concept of war. Conflict is a waste of energy in a world where energy is slow and precious. Disputes are settled by immersion: the aggrieved parties are tied together and submerged in a designated pool until the muffled world below forces a shared, breathless perspective. They rarely die violently. When one grows too old to climb or listen, they perform the Last Walk. They are covered in a paste of bioluminescent algae and clay, and they wade into one of the deep, pulsing pools, sinking beneath the surface to join the slow dream below. It is not seen as an end, but as a deeper integration.

They are a people without history, because they feel no separation from it. The past is the rock beneath their feet, still warm. The future is the next tender shoot of vine they will guide towards the lightless light. They are patient, quiet, and utterly adapted. They are the caretakers of a drowning, dreaming world, and in their silent, glowing way, they are its perfect children.
