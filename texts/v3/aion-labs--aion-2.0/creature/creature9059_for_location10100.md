The inhabitants are not the glassfish or the filter-wings, though they share the water with them. They are the reason for the silence in the habitation blocks, the recipients of all this curated emptiness. They call themselves the Stillness, or sometimes, in older dialects, the Pressure-Born.

Physically, they are human-derived, but so profoundly adapted that the original form is a distant memory. Their skin is a seamless, pearlescent gray, thick and insulating, woven with capillary networks that extract oxygen directly from the water. They have no hair. Their eyes are large, black, and lidless, optimized for the eternal noon and the blue gloom. They possess both lungs and gill-slits along their ribs, but they move through the water not with tails, but with subtle, full-body manipulations of density; internal bladders fill with gases siphoned from the conduit towers, allowing them to ascend or descend with a thought. Their hands and feet are webbed, but the fingers are long, delicate, and tipped with a hard, conductive material for interfacing with the systems.

They do not live in the habitat blocks. Those are relics, archives, storehouses of physical artifacts from a time of construction. The Stillness live *within* the infrastructure. An individual might claim a pump housing as their domicile, sleeping curled in the silent, cathedral-sized chamber of its interior. Another might spend a century tending a single filtration bank, their body slowly becoming one with the control panels, their metabolism syncing to the pulse of the cleaners. They are solitary by nature and design. The emptiness is not a condition they endure; it is a sacrament.

Their society is a whisper-network carried through the hum of the towers. They communicate via modulated vibrations along the structural girders, a language of sub-audible pulses and resonances that encodes not just information, but emotional states, metabolic readings, and vast, slow philosophical contemplations. A conversation between two Pressure-Born might take months, a debate a lifetime. Their purpose is maintenance, but of a specific kind: not to prevent decay, but to observe and document the near-imperceptible drift of the system from its original specifications. They are the curators of the blueprint’s entropy.

They are few. Perhaps a million individuals scattered in the twelve-thousand-kilometer void. To encounter one is a rare event. You might be observing a conduit tower when a gray shape detaches from its surface, having been motionless for years, and glides away with a fluid, unhurried grace. They might observe you in return, their black eyes reflecting no light, their minds parsing your unfamiliar biology against the archived templates. They feel no hostility, only a distant, analytical curiosity. You are an unexpected variable in a perfected equation.

Their culture is one of sublime, pressurized patience. They have arts: they sculpt currents into temporary, complex vortices that hold shape for days; they compose vibrations that cause the very carbonglass shell to resonate like a colossal bell, a sound felt rather than heard. Their history is not recorded in texts, but in the gradual, deliberate modifications to the Bathysphere’s code—each change a collective meditation spanning generations.

They are the soul of the machine, and the machine is their universe. They are the children of absolute design, living a life where every breath of water, every pulse of light, and every echoing *clang* in the deep is a note in a hymn to their own creation. They are waiting, though for what, not even they could say. Perhaps for the next instruction from a creator species long departed. Perhaps for the eventual, inevitable failure of a system meant to last forever. Or perhaps they are simply waiting for the silence, which they have mastered, to finally become complete.
