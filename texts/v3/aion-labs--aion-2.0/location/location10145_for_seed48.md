The city of Brackwater sits low in the belly of a forgotten delta, a place where the earth gave up trying to stay solid long ago. The air is a hot, wet blanket, thick enough to drink, pressing down with a weight that has nothing to do with altitude. It is the heat of a fever, constant and damp, seeping from the black water that glistens between the skeletal remains of industry and from the rust-colored bricks of the buildings themselves. The sky is a perpetual ceiling of bruised, low-hanging cloud, allowing only a dim, diffuse light that turns midday into a prolonged twilight.

This is a world of stone, iron, and water. Vegetation—true, green, growing things—is a memory. The chemical legacy of a century of unregulated industry seeped into the water table and salted the earth. What life persists is animal, and it is overwhelming. The city is theirs now. Thick, rope-like vines of black leeches pulse in the slow currents of the canals. Clouds of midges dance in shifting pillars above stagnant pools. Armored beetles the size of a man’s hand click and scuttle over corroded pipework. In the deeper shadows, things with too many legs and quick, glittering eyes move in a constant, rustling tapestry of consumption. It is a food web built entirely on decay and predation, with no primary producer but the rotting city itself.

The urban fabric is vast, open, and eerily empty. This is not a dense metropolis, but a sprawling necropolis of low-slung factories, warehouses, and worker blocks, all built from a dark, porous stone that weeps continuously. Wide avenues, now cracked and subsiding into the muck, stretch into the murk, connecting islands of architecture across kilometers of flooded lowland. The density is one of absence: vast, silent courtyards, cavernous factory floors empty of everything but dripping echoes, and canals so broad and still they act as mirrors for the crumbling skyline.

Everything is settled, weathered into a grim equilibrium. The erosion is not violent but patient. Soft stone is sculpted into fluid, rounded forms by centuries of acidic moisture. Ironwork has rusted into intricate lace, then further into fragile, red dust. Buildings list and sink into the saturated ground at imperceptible speeds, finding their angle of repose and holding there for generations. The city is not falling; it has fallen, and now exists in a state of long, slow dissolution.

Industrial skeletons are everywhere, but they are silent. The great pump houses, their turbine halls flooded, stand like cathedrals to a dead god of efficiency. Rail lines, oxidized to a uniform orange, vanish into water-filled ditches. The technology is of the steam-and-gear variety, monumental and simple, now seized by rust. There is no hum of electricity, no roar of engines—only the industrial *presence*, the ghost of purpose in the arrangement of stone and metal.

Sound is a paradox. In a place teeming with life, it is mostly quiet. The cacophony is subdued, muffled by the humid air and absorbed by the wet surfaces. The dominant sounds are liquid: the constant, irregular *plink* of water falling from a broken gutter into a pool, the soft *lap* of a sluggish wave against a stone quay, the distant, hollow *drip* from deep within a drain. The fauna contributes a low background texture—the chitter of insects, the wet slide of something through mud, the occasional croak or screech that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere. It is a quiet of profound activity, not absence.

The dynamic processes are slow but relentless. The water table rises and falls with unseen tides, revealing and then submerging new layers of debris. Colonies of filter-feeding polyps bloom on submerged machinery, then die off, adding to the silty bed. Predator and prey enact their ancient drama in the shadows: a swift splash, a scuffle, a brief squeal, then the quiet returns. The city breathes, but its breath is the exhalation of decay, a hot, wet sigh that has been going on for a very long time, and will continue, unchanging, until the last stone is swallowed by the marsh.
