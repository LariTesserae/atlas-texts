It is a plateau that should not exist.

The altitude scrapes the underbelly of the sky, a place of thin, biting air and permanent cold that seeps into the bone. Yet it is wet—not with ice, but with a pervasive, weeping dampness. The ground, what little of it is visible, is not stone or soil but a substance like black, waterlogged sponge, a peat of such profound age it has forgotten it was ever living matter. This is the substrate: a single, continental organism, a plant-mass that conquered the high place eons ago and then simply… stayed. It has grown into itself, layer upon layer, compressing into a surface that yields underfoot with a sick, muffled squelch, releasing the scent of decay and iron. Water does not pool so much as it is held in suspension, a billion tiny vesicles within the plant-flesh, weeping constantly from a million invisible pores. The air condenses into a fine, cold mist that hangs in curtains, obscuring distances, turning the dim light into a diffuse, gray milk.

And the light is dim. The sun is a memory here, a faint bruise behind perpetual cloud-cover the color of a week-old bruise. The illumination comes from other sources: phosphorescent fungi that cling like barnacles to the towering forms that rise from the sponge-ground, and from the fauna themselves. Life is abundant, but it is not the life of any sane ecology. Things scuttle, slither, and drift through the damp air. Many are bioluminescent, pulsing with slow, internal rhythms of green, violet, and a sickly orange. They are not quite animals, not quite plants—in the reality breakdown, such categories have bled together. Some have too many limbs, or limbs that are also leaves, or mouths that are also flowers. They make sounds: clicks, chitters, and wet, tearing noises that contribute to the constant, loud tapestry of noise.

For it is loud. The sound is not of wind or water, but of industry. And here lies the central, screaming paradox of the place. There are no structures, no buildings, no artifacts of tool-making intelligence. Yet the plateau thrums with the deafening rhythm of the industrial age. The sound comes from the vegetation itself, from the ancient plant-substrate. In places, the peat-mass has grown into colossal, hollow tubes that rise like crooked chimneys, hundreds of feet tall. From their tops, steam—or something like it—blasts forth in great, rhythmic geysers, accompanied by a deep, basso roar like the exhaust of a continent-sized locomotive. Elsewhere, the ground convulses in regular spasms, the plant-flesh contracting and expanding with a sound of grinding pistons and shuddering boilers. Vents in the black sponge-ground hiss continuously, jetting hot, metallic-smelling vapor into the cold air. It is as if the plateau itself is a vast, biological engine, grown rather than built, performing some incomprehensible, eternal work.

The erosion is total. This is a landscape so ancient it has settled into its final, weary shape. There are no sharp peaks, no youthful valleys. The horizon, when visible through the mist, is a gently undulating line, worn smooth by time beyond measure. The plant-substrate has filled every hollow, smoothed every contour. It is the end of geography. Yet, this ancient stillness is a lie.

The place is turbulent. The reality breakdown ensures that nothing is stable. The laws of physics are soft here, suggestions rather than rules. Patches of the mist thicken suddenly into solid, gelatinous masses that fall with a wet slap onto the sponge-ground, then dissolve into swarms of glowing insects. The bioluminescent fauna sometimes blur at the edges, their forms bleeding color and substance into the air like living watercolors. In the distance, one might see a stand of the chimney-plants flicker, as if momentarily existing in two places at once, their thunderous exhalations stuttering into a cacophony of broken machinery sounds. The very air sometimes rips—not with wind, but with silent, visual tears through which can be glimpsed impossible geometries, flashes of alternative skies, or the vast, indifferent mechanisms that might be turning beneath the skin of the world. The ground itself is not immune. Sections of the sponge-mass will suddenly liquefy into a churning, organic soup, swallowing whatever fauna are upon it, before solidifying again minutes later into a new, subtly different configuration. It is a place of constant, chaotic re-creation, a frenzy of becoming and un-becoming layered over profound, geological stasis.

The scale is open, vast. One stands not in a grove or a clearing, but in a region. The mist-obscured vista suggests a territory the size of a small country, all of it this same weeping, engine-plant bog, stretching to the edges of the high world. Distances are deceptive, stretched and compressed by the reality flaws. A glowing herd of something might seem a mile away, until a shift in the mist reveals they are drifting just a few yards overhead.

And through it all, the cold persists, and the damp, and the deafening, organic industry. It is a land that has grown its own hell, a wet, living machine of unimaginable age, dreaming fitfully as it comes apart at the seams. There is no purpose here to discern, only process—a relentless, turbulent, loud process of decay and re-invention, on a stage that is itself the final, flattened tombstone of a world.
