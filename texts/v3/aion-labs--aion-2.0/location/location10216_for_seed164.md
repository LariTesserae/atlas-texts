The world is a single, vast tableland—a continental plateau that rises so high its peaks scrape the lower stratosphere, yet it is not barren. It is a paradox of chill and damp, a place where the air is thin and cold enough to freeze exposed skin, yet water is never absent. It hangs in the air as a permanent, condensing mist, a spectral humidity that beads on every surface. The plateau is young, geologically speaking; its edges are not worn slopes but sheer escarpments, great walls of dark basalt and granite that drop into cloud-filled abysses. The rock faces are sharp, fractured in clean lines, as if the entire landmass had been thrust upward only a few millennia ago and not yet softened by time.

Across this immense elevation, the sense of scale is not just visual but gravitational. Distances are continental; you can look from one horizon to the other and see no curve, only a fading into a uniform, pearlescent grey. The sky is a flat, luminous sheet, a source of normal, diffuse light that never brightens or dims. There is no sun, no moon, no stars—only this constant, shadowless glow. The temperature never varies: a steady, penetrating cold that sits just above the point where water would turn to ice. And so the water here exists in a strange, supercooled state. It flows in slow, viscous rivulets over the rock, weeps from cliffs in continuous, silent tears, and pools in shallow depressions without ever freezing solid. The ground is perpetually damp, a black sponge of moss and saturated peat.

The vegetation is lush, but it is a lushness of saturation, not of tropical abundance. It creeps and carpets, a dense, low mat of bryophytes, liverworts, and strange, rubbery ferns with leaves like dark green leather. There are no trees. Instead, great fungal towers rise at vast intervals—solitary, slender spires of chitinous material, pale grey and striated, reaching twenty or thirty meters into the mist. They are widely spaced; you might walk for days and see only one. This is the emptiness: a breathtaking, almost vacuum-like sparseness. The volume of space between these fungal monoliths, between the occasional rock outcrop, is so immense it feels less like a landscape and more like a bare stage waiting for a set that never arrives.

Yet there is evidence of organization, of intent. Winding through the moss, following the gentlest of gradients, are canals. They are narrow, precise channels lined with fitted stone, now furred with moss, carrying the sluggish, cold water from somewhere to somewhere else. And at irregular, but calculable, intervals stand the settlements.

They are not cities as one might imagine. Each is a single, immense structure—a compound, a factory-town. Built from the same dark local stone, reinforced with rust-streaked iron, they rise in tiered, blocky forms, devoid of ornament. Chimneys, not spires, punctuate their silhouettes, exhaling continuous plumes of white steam that vanish into the general mist. This is the industry: these places hum and thrum. The sound is the first thing you notice long before you see them—a deep, sub-auditory vibration felt in the bones, growing into a cacophony of grinding, pounding, and a persistent, high-pressure hiss. It is loud, but in this vast emptiness, the sound does not echo; it is absorbed, muffled by the wet air and the moss, so that it seems to come from everywhere and nowhere.

Inside these structures, machines work. They are not tended. No faces appear at the windows, no workers move between the buildings. The machinery is self-regulating, powered by geothermal taps drilled deep into the young, hot crust below the plateau. They process the water, or perhaps the minerals leached from it. Some distill the mist itself, condensing it into reservoirs. Others press the dense moss into bricks of fibrous peat. The purpose is obscure, the production lines silent of voices, moving with a rhythmic, perpetual certainty.

There is no animal life. No birds cross the grey sky. No insects burrow in the damp peat. No rodents scurry in the factory halls. The only movement is the slow creep of vegetation, the flow of water, the turn of gears, and the steady bleed of steam. The ecosystem is one of plants, fungi, water, and stone. The fungal towers are perhaps not even alive in a conventional sense; they are more like mineral formations, slowly accruing in the saturated air.

The rules of this world are few, but absolute.

First, nothing changes. The temperature is constant. The light is constant. The mist does not thicken or disperse. The machines do not wear out; they perform their functions in a state of perfect, unchanging equilibrium. Erosion is negligible; the sharp edges of the cliffs remain sharp. Time passes, but it leaves no mark.

Second, water is never still, yet never freezes. It exists in a state of perpetual, cold flow. It carves no new channels; it simply follows the ones laid down at the world's birth.

Third, sound travels far but dies quickly. The industrial noise is omnipresent near the settlements, but walk a kilometer into the moss-lands and it fades into a background vibration, a memory of sound.

Fourth, growth is slow and surface-bound. Nothing reaches for the sky except the fungal towers, and they do so over millennia.

Finally, there is a strangeness to the physics of density. The air feels thin, yet the mist is dense. The land feels solid, yet the peat can swallow a step without a sound. Distances are deceptive; what looks a day's walk away might be reached in an hour, or a week. The scale of the plateau warps perception. You are simultaneously on a vast, open plain and inside a small, damp room.

The atmosphere is one of profound, humming solitude. It is a world that built itself, industrialized itself, and now sustains itself in a frozen moment, for no audience, toward no apparent end. The loudness of its industry is the only proof it is not asleep. The cold, wet air soaks into everything, into the stone, into the iron, into the imagined bones of a visitor. It is a world that does not need life, and has none, save for the green, creeping moss and the endless, rhythmic exhalation of its machines.
