The place is called the Steamstone Plateau. It is a high basin, cupped between the teeth of recently-born mountains, a realm where water, warmth, and stone exist in a perpetual, quiet pact.

You arrive not by a hard climb, but by emerging onto a vast, open tableland. The air is warm and thick, carrying a weight that is not humidity alone but a palpable moisture—a condensing atmosphere that beads on your skin and clothes. This is the paradox of the high place: at this altitude, the sky should be thin and cold, but here the earth itself breathes warmth. Geothermal veins thread the bedrock, gently heating the countless springs and seeps that define the land. The mountains that rim the plateau are young, their slopes not yet softened by time. They are sharp, raw profiles of dark basalt and pale limestone, rising like broken crystal against a sky of soft, normal light.

Water is everywhere, but it is never violent. It weeps from mossy cliffsides, forming curtains of liquid light. It pools in shallow, mist-skinned marshes that glow emerald with algae and thick mats of floating fern. In other places, it gathers into broad, clear tarns so still they become perfect mirrors of the sky and the sharp peaks. The ground underfoot is often spongy; the water table lies just beneath a thin skin of soil and living moss. Walk too heavily in certain places, and your footprint will fill slowly with clear, warm water. Streams, silent and slow, meander without urgency, carving only the faintest channels in the resilient peat.

The vegetation is lush, but it is a lushness of saturation, not of frenzy. There are no towering, competitive forests. Instead, the plant life sprawls and layers in a dense, low tapestry. Mosses in a hundred shades of green and gold cloak every rock and fallen log. Thick, rubbery-leaved succulents cluster around the hot springs, drinking the warm mist. In the open areas, meadows of waist-high, feathery grasses ripple in the faintest breezes, their seed heads catching the light. Strange, bulbous fungi—some as large as stools—dot the ground, their surfaces pearlescent. Vines with leaves like polished jade drape from the few stands of slender, silver-barked trees. There is no fruit, no flower with bright colors meant to attract a pollinator. Reproduction here is a quiet affair of spore and runner and rhizome, a patient, vegetative expansion. The silence is profound. No buzz of insects, no call of birds, no rustle of a hidden creature. The only sounds are the gentle *drip… drip…* from the weeping rocks, the soft sigh of wind through grass, and occasionally, a deep, low *glub* as a bubble of geothermal gas escapes a mud pool.

This is a settled place. The evidence of people is woven into the landscape with a primitive, elegant efficiency. The settlements are sparse—a cluster of dwellings here, a lone watchtower there—connected by raised pathways of fitted stone that keep feet dry above the marsh. The buildings are made of the same basalt as the mountains, quarried in blocks and stacked without mortar, their roofs thickly thatched with the durable plateau grasses. Some structures are built directly over hot springs, steam venting through designed channels to warm the interiors. There are no metals gleaming; tools are of knapped stone, bone, and hardened wood. You might see a fisherman on the edge of a tarn, but his net is woven from plant fibers, and his quarry is not fish, but the large, silent freshwater crustaceans that graze on the algae. The people move slowly, their voices hushed, as if in reverence for the quiet. Their presence feels less like an imposition and more like another layer of the ecology—a slow-moving, thoughtful species that builds with what the young stone and the lush greenery provide.

The scale of it all is open, breathable. From a high point on one of the stone walkways, you can see for miles across the basin: the pattern of silver tarns, the dark green of moss-fields, the grey smudges of distant settlements, all framed by the fierce, unchanging peaks. The space between features is vast, giving the land a feeling of serene emptiness. It is a landscape painted in washes of muted color—stone grey, moss green, water-silver, grass gold—under a normal, diffuse daylight.

And it does not change. This is its final, most profound characteristic. There are no storms here, only a perpetual, gentle mist. No seasons of barren cold or explosive growth; the geothermal heart keeps the temperature constant, the plants in a steady state of verdant being. The mountains do not crumble; the streams do not flood. It is a world in perfect, silent equilibrium. A dream of a world that has found its balance and decided, peacefully, to remain. Time passes, but it leaves no mark. You could leave and return a lifetime later, and the same droplet would be poised to fall from the same mossy stone lip, the same bubble of gas would be swelling in the same mud pool, waiting to break the silence with its soft, lonely *glub*.
