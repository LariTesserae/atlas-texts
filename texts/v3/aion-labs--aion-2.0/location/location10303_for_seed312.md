The world is a city of stone and breath, suspended in the cold, thin air of an altitude that feels like the roof of the sky. It is not a city as one might imagine—not of steel and glass, but of something older, more patient. It clings to the shoulders of a mountain range so vast that its peaks are not individual summits but the serrated edge of a continent, a geography of such scale that clouds form in its lee and weather systems are born in the shadow of its plaza-canyons.

The first thing you notice is the cold. It is a dry, penetrating cold, the kind that settles into bone and stone alike. Yet it is not absolute; the dampness parameter of 0.6 introduces a subtle, persistent moisture. It manifests not as flowing water, but as a condensation that beads on every surface during the long, still nights—a cold sweat on the rock. In the deepest gullies between structures, where sunlight rarely reaches, thin skins of black ice persist, and from certain overhangs, slow, single drops of meltwater fall at intervals so long you could miss them if you blinked. Occasional, fine mists drift up from the lower valleys, clinging to the city’s skirts before dissipating in the high wind. There are no rivers, but there are veins—capillary channels carved into the stone, sometimes glazed with ice, sometimes whispering with a trickle of snowmelt.

The terrain is young, sharp, and unforgiving. Erosion at 1.4 suggests a landscape recently hewn, perhaps by colossal geological forces or the sheer ambition of its builders. The rockfaces are raw, striated, and angular. Buildings are not so much constructed as *revealed*—the city appears to have been carved directly from the living mountain, its towers and arches and labyrinthine streets excavated from a single, dense mass of granite and basalt. The lines are clean, the corners precise, yet they bear the fresh marks of the chisel. There is no softening patina of ages; the stone is young in its exposure. The elevation, 2.2, means the air is thin, the horizon curves away with a startling clarity, and the light has a brittle, crystalline quality.

The scale is vast, almost incomprehensible. At 2.9, this is not a city you can walk across in a day. It is a mountain range that is also a metropolis. Its districts are separated by chasms bridged by slender arches of stone that look like threads from a distance. Whole neighborhoods exist on immense, tiered plateaus, each the size of a small country. The weather is local: a snow squall might rage in one canyon while sunlight bakes the plaza of the next spire. The physics of sound and light behave oddly over such distances; a shout might be swallowed entirely, while the click of a pebble falling echoes for minutes, ricocheting across facades miles apart.

Density is high, 2.0. The structures are packed tightly, layer upon layer. Streets are narrow canyons, sometimes roofed over by the floors of buildings above. Windows and doorways are stacked like honeycomb. There is no empty space; every volume is occupied, utilized, filled with the presence of stone, of air, of life. The feeling is one of immense, weighted solidity, a density that seems to press inward.

And it is urban, intensely so. Built at 2.5, this is a place of organized, intentional construction, but of a primitive tech level (0.9). There is no electricity, no engines, no power beyond muscle, lever, pulley, and the careful management of water and gravity. The city is a machine of stone. Aqueducts—simple, open channels carved along cliff faces—carry the precious meltwater. Lifts are counterweighted baskets of woven fiber. Light comes from fat-burning lamps in niches, or from cunningly placed mirrors that chase the sun down into the depths. The architecture is monumental yet simple: post and lintel, corbeled arch, massive blocks fitted with such precision a knife blade cannot slip between them. It speaks of a civilization that mastered stone and nothing else, building an empire with nothing but time, numbers, and relentless will.

Vegetation is sparse, 1.1. What grows is tenacious: lichens in explosive, pale-green bursts on north faces; hardy, ground-hugging shrubs with waxy leaves in sheltered courtyards; perhaps a species of tough, grey grass that whispers in the wind on the few open terraces. There are no trees. The primary color palette is mineral: grey, black, rust-red, the white of snow and ice, the deep blue of the high-altitude sky.

Fauna, however, is abundant (2.5). Life has filled the ecological niches the plants could not. The air is thick with birds—not songbirds, but silent, broad-winged raptors and gliders that ride the thermals between spires. Their nests stud the cliffs like dark jewels. Small, furred creatures with dense pelts scuttle along the rooftops and within the walls—rodents, perhaps, or something stranger, living on lichen and the scraps of the city. In the deeper, damp recesses, blind, pale insects swarm in the condensation. Larger fauna might exist: sure-footed, goat-like beasts that traverse impossible ledges, their clattering hooves a familiar sound. The city is not devoid of life; it is teeming with it, but it is a life of claw, fur, and feather, adapted to stone and cold.

The light is normal (2.1)—the sun rises and sets, casting long, dramatic shadows that stretch for miles across the city’s face. At noon, the light is stark and revealing, bleaching color from the stone. The atmosphere is thin, clean, and incredibly clear. Distant peaks look close enough to touch.

Sound is mostly quiet (0.9). The vastness and the stone absorb noise. The dominant sounds are the wind—a low, constant moan through the canyons and architectural gaps—and the occasional, amplified trickle of water. The fauna is not loud; the birds do not sing, they cry sharply, briefly. Footfalls on stone are muffled by grit and frost. Conversations in the streets are hushed, as if the silence is a presence to be respected. The city feels like a library of stone.

Weirdness is low (0.2). This place operates on familiar principles of physics and biology, just pushed to an extreme scale and context. There is no magic, no overt surrealism. The wonder is in the sheer, human (or human-like) achievement of it, the audacity to build something so vast with tools so simple.

The dynamic is slow (1.3). Change here is geological. The city erodes, but imperceptibly. Ice widens a crack over centuries. A terrace gradually accumulates enough wind-blown soil to support a new patch of lichen. Generations live and die within the same unchanging stone rooms. The rhythms are of breath, of the daily condensation cycle, of the slow migration of shadows. It is a patient world.

**Rules of this world:**
1.  **Water is Life, and it is Measured.** Every drop is accounted for. The condensation cycles and the melt from permanent icefields are the city’s blood. The complex, gravity-fed channel system is its most vital infrastructure, more important than any road.
2.  **Stone is Given.** The city does not import material. It is made from the mountain it consumes. Excavation creates space; the removed stone becomes the blocks for new construction. It is a closed loop of material.
3.  **The Vertical is the Plane of Existence.** Distance is measured not in horizontal length, but in vertical ascent and descent. Social status, trade routes, and microclimates are stratified by altitude. The highest spires are for the elite, the deepest canyons for industry and the lower classes.
4.  **Silence is a Currency.** In the thin air and vast spaces, noise is waste. Efficient movement, quiet speech, and careful tool-use are marks of civility. Loud actions are reserved for ceremonies or emergencies, their echoes carrying for miles as announcements.
5.  **Fauna is Integrated.** The animals are not pests; they are part of the system. The birds control the insects. The grazing beasts keep the sparse vegetation in check and are a source of wool, meat, and bone tools. Their migratory patterns are woven into the city’s calendar.
6.  **Time is Measured in Stone.** History is not recorded in books, but in the excavation layers. The age of a district is known by the sharpness of its carvings. "Modern" means the stone still smells of the quarry. The past is literally beneath your feet.

The atmosphere, finally, is one of immense, solemn grandeur. It is a crushing, beautiful, silent, and cold monument to a species that looked at a mountain and decided to live inside it, forever shaping and being shaped by the stone, in a slow, cold dance of breath, water, and relentless time.
