The world is a drowned cathedral, built by things that never knew the prayer of hands.

Beneath a ceiling of perpetual, starless water—a fluid night that weighs upon everything—the city spreads across the basin of a sunken caldera. The water is warm, a constant, breath-like 30 degrees Celsius, heated from below by slow geothermal whispers through the basalt floor. This is not the cold abyss; this is a womb-dark sea, thick and silent, moving with the viscous grace of oil.

The architecture is bone, but not the bone of death. It is living architecture, secreted over millennia by colonies of siphonophores and glass-sponges, vast cooperatives that build not for shelter but as an extension of their feeding. Towers, some thirty meters tall, rise in clustered spires, their surfaces a porous lacework of calcium and silica. Bridges of braided chitin connect them, forming a dense, three-dimensional lattice that fills the water column from the soft silt floor up to the shadowy midwaters. It is unmistakably urban: there are thoroughfares where currents flow straighter, chambers that echo with the patterned pulsing of light, and stacked habitations that house not individuals, but entire ecosystems.

Light is a currency here, and it is scarce. The darkness is almost absolute, a palpable pressure. What illumination exists is biological: a faint, intermittent glow from pulsing jellyfish caught in the lattice like living lanterns; the sudden, startling flash of a predator's lure; the slow, wave-like bioluminescence that travels along a tower's surface as its resident polyps communicate. The palette is one of deep blues and blacks, punctuated by cold greens and ghostly blues that appear and vanish, leaving retinal echoes.

Sound is a distant language. The dominant sensation is the profound, resonant quiet of deep water—a silence so complete it becomes a sound in itself. Occasionally, a low-frequency groan emanates from the shifting of a massive tower. The *click-click* of shrimp claws echoes down a canyon. The swish of a large, ribbon-like body sliding between structures is a tangible vibration in the water, felt in the bones before it is heard.

The fauna is the city's true populace, and it is abundant to the point of saturation. The structures are crawling, swimming, floating with life. Translucent, many-armed cephalopods drift from doorway to doorway, their skins flickering with complex patterns. Schools of slender, eel-like fish, each no longer than a finger, move as a single silvery nerve through the avenues. Larger shapes move at the periphery of vision—a slow-rolling manta the color of charcoal, its wings stirring up clouds of detritus; a many-jawed eel, its body threading through arches like a needle through a loop. There are no eyes here, not as surface-dwellers know them; instead, there are fields of sensitivity, pressure receptors, and chemical senses that read the city's news in pulses of taste and current.

Vegetation is sparse. Here and there, hardy, dark-red kelp forests cling to the bases of towers, their broad leaves moving in a slow, unseen rhythm. Clumps of bioluminescent algae paint patches of the silt with a soft, decaying glow. But this is not a world of photosynthesis. It is a world of filter-feeding, of predation, of chemosynthesis drawing energy from the warm mineral plumes seeping from below.

The erosion is total and beautiful. Every edge has been softened by centuries of gentle, warm current. The towers are smooth, their intricate pores worn into gentle dimples. The bridges are polished to a dull, pearlescent sheen. Nothing is sharp; everything has been accepted into the long, slow consensus of the water. This landscape is not being made; it is being maintained, in a state of perfect, dynamic equilibrium. It is established. It is home.

The weirdness is subtle, woven into the fabric of the ordinary. The towers sometimes pulse in unison, a wave of light passing through the entire district, for no reason an observer can discern. The predatory eels seem to hunt in coordinated pairs, herding prey not into mouths, but into specific chambers in the towers, which then seal shut. There is an intelligence here, but it is not a mind; it is the intelligence of the system, of the city itself, operating on rules written in chemistry and current.

You float at the edge of it, a bubble of air in a world of liquid, your own light a crude and glaring intrusion. The warm darkness presses in, alive, dense, and ancient. This city was never drowned. It was born this way, and in the quiet, warm dark, it thrives.
