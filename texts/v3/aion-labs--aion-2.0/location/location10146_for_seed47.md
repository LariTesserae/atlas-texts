The place has no name, or its name has been worn away along with its mountains.

You stand on a plane of polished stone so vast it curves with the horizon. This is the roof of the world, a continental plate lifted to the thin edge of the sky and then left to the teeth of time for epochs beyond counting. The erosion here is not a process but a condition — a final, terminal smoothness. Every peak has been planed down, every valley filled with its own dust, until the land is a titanic, gently undulating sheet, a frozen ocean of rock. The elevation is extreme — the sky is a deep, star-flecked violet even at midday, and the air is cool, thin, and carries no moisture at all. There is no water. Not as liquid, not as ice, not as vapor. The concept is absent. In its place, a different chemistry persists: the stone itself sometimes glistens with a slow, viscous sheen, like oil on quartz, but it is not wet. It is a sealed system, a closed loop of mineral metabolism.

The light is normal, stark, and clarifying. It reveals everything and hides nothing. There are no shadows long enough to lose yourself in; the sun, small and white, tracks a dome of perfect clarity.

And it is silent. Not a dead silence, but a profound, absorbent quiet. The thin air carries little sound, and the stone itself seems to drink vibration. Your own breath is a distant rustle; the pulse in your ears is a private thunder. This makes the turbulence all the more jarring, for it is a turbulence of substance, not sound.

The emptiness is spatial, not visual. The scale is such that a mountain range would be a ripple on this floor. Distances are deceptive, compressed by the sheer lack of anything to measure them against. Yet, within this void, the world is in a state of relentless, silent re-creation. It is the dynamic of a dream, or a broken engine trying to remember its purpose.

You see it first as a shiver in the air, a heat-haze distortion that has nothing to do with heat. Then, a hundred miles away across the plain, a spire of iridescent crystal erupts from the stone. It grows, fractures, blooms into a cathedral of impossible geometry in seconds, and then, as if the thought sustaining it is withdrawn, it unravels. It doesn’t collapse; it *unfolds*, flattening back into the featureless plain from which it came. Elsewhere, a canyon tears itself open, revealing strata of glowing metals, only to seal seamlessly a moment later. The very color of the stone shifts in vast, continental patches — from gunmetal grey to ochre to a bruised purple — as if the plateau is cycling through forgotten palettes.

This is the legacy of the tech. It is beyond modern; it is post-physical. Scattered across the infinite plain are its anchors: low, hemispherical bulges of a material that is neither metal nor stone, fused seamlessly with the bedrock. They are the only built structures, each alone, separated by hundreds of miles. They are not ruins. They hum with a power that warps the local rules of reality. They are the source of the turbulence — ancient, autonomous machines stuck in a loop of creation and erasure, trying to rebuild a world whose blueprint they have partially forgotten. They manipulate matter and spacetime at a fundamental level, but their operations are chaotic, unguided. A flower of fused silica might spiral into existence next to you, intricate as a snowflake, before dissolving into atomic dust. A bubble of reversed gravity might lift a mile-wide sheet of rock a foot into the air, hold it for an hour, and let it settle without a sound.

The ecology has adapted to this storm of making and unmaking. Vegetation is sparse: clusters of tough, fibrous stalks that look like black glass, rooted directly into the stone. They photosynthesisize the stark light, but also seem to draw sustenance from the energetic fluctuations, pulsing with a soft, internal luminescence during a nearby reality-event. Fauna is rare. You might glimpse a wraith-like creature — a tangled knot of silver filaments, drifting a few feet above the ground, navigating the turbulence like a sailor in a silent storm. It feeds on the radiative decay of the machines’ failed constructs. There are no predators, no prey; only solitary specialists surviving on the fallout of cosmic engineering.

The rules of this world are written in erasure and iteration. First: nothing is permanent. Second: the past is not layered, but simultaneous. All that ever was or could be here is present as a potential, flickering into brief existence. Third: the silence is a law. Sound is energy, and energy here is co-opted, absorbed by the hungry stone and the hungrier machines, used as raw material for the next transient shape.

You walk. Your footsteps leave no impression on the eternal stone. A low, arched doorway of cobalt-blue light winks into existence ahead, leading into a room that is not there. It holds for the span of three heartbeats, offering a glimpse of a table set for a meal no one will eat, before it is deleted. The air where it stood tingles. A few miles to your left, one of the machine-hemispheres is undergoing a more sustained episode. The land around it is boiling with proto-forms: shapes of arches, pyramids, spirals, and less namable geometries, rising and melting like wax. It is beautiful and terrifying — the creative fury of a god with amnesia.

The atmosphere is one of immense, weary grandeur. It is the oldest place you will ever stand, and the most restless. It has settled into its final form — this flat, endless plain — yet is incapable of true stillness. It is a vacuum seething with potential. The cool, thin air smells of ozone, of crushed flint, and of something else — the scent of a fresh page, or a freshly wiped slate.

You realize the truth slowly. This is not a landscape. It is a drafting table. The machines are the pencils. And they have been scribbling, erasing, and scribbling again, for time enough to wear down stars, in perfect, deafening quiet.
