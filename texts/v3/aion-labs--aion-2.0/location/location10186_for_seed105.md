The world is a drowned cathedral, and its vault is the crushing, absolute dark of the abyssal plain. You are not at the bottom, however. You are suspended in the middle of a vast, vertical emptiness, clinging to the shoulders of a mountain that died and was buried by the sea millennia ago. The water is a constant, tangible presence — not merely wet, but a thick, gelid medium that presses with the memory of glaciers. Its temperature is a penetrating cold, a cold that seeps past skin and bone to settle in the hollows of the spirit, yet it is not still. It moves in great, slow rivers, the deep-sea currents that sculpt continents over eons.

This is the high place in the deep. The summit of the seamount rises into the lightless water, a plateau of weathered basalt and ancient coral stone, eroded into smooth, whale-backed domes and sheer, undercut cliffs. The erosion is not violent; it is a patient, established thing. The stone has been wearing down in this dark, cold bath for so long that it has achieved a kind of equilibrium, its forms flowing and organic, as if the mountain itself is dreaming.

And upon this dreaming stone, the forest grows.

Lush does not begin to describe it. In the absence of sun, life has invented its own reasons to be. The vegetation is a riot of luminescence, a false galaxy smeared across the rock. Giant, weeping fronds of emerald-kelp drift in the current, their edges fringed with pulsating violet sparks. Colonies of bulbous, mushroom-like growths cluster in the lee of boulders, their translucent skins glowing with a steady, internal amberglow, revealing delicate, fern-like gill structures within. Towers of spiraling, calcified algae rise like grotesque spires, their surfaces crusted with living moss that emits a faint, radioactive green shimmer. The colors are deep, saturated, and utterly alien: indigo, viridian, deep carmine. There is no symmetry here, only a desperate, beautiful abundance clinging to the rock in the eternal night.

It is lush, yet sparse. Vast tracts of bare, polished stone lie between these groves and thickets. The forest does not blanket; it gathers in congregations, leaving solemn expanses of emptiness where only the drifting silt settles. The scale is open, disorienting. Your light — if you have one — dies quickly, swallowed by the gloom, but the collective bioluminescence of a distant grove hangs in the darkness like a submerged city, giving a sense of immense, hollow space stretching away in all directions.

The sound is a constant, layered roar. It is the sound of the mountain itself: a low, tectonic groan as the currents push against its bulk, a deep *huummmmm* that vibrates in your teeth. It is the sound of the water tearing through narrow canyons, a hissing, rushing torrent even here in the slow-time of the deep. And it is the sound of the forest. The great fronds do not rustle; they *sing*. As the current flows through their perforated structures, it produces a haunting, discordant chorus of whistles and deep reedy notes, a vegetative dirge for a sun they have never known. Bulbous plants, when nudged by the flow, emit low, resonant *booms* that travel for miles through the water. The sound is never quiet. It is a loud, living soundscape, an orchestra of pressure and growth.

There are structures. Scattered, half-swallowed by the sediment and the encroaching forest. They are simple, massive things: fallen monoliths of the same basalt, arranged in circles that now lie canted on the seafloor. crude step-pyramids of stacked stone, their outlines softened by millennia of microbial film and slow deposition. A lone, trapezoidal doorway stands open, leading into a mound of rubble, its lintel carved with spirals that now glow with a soft, borrowed light from the phosphorescent lichen eating at its edges. The technology implied is primitive, monumental — the work of hands moving stone upon stone, for purposes lost to the water. They are not ruins of a city, but of isolated, purposeful sites: an observatory, a ritual ground, a sentinel's post. They feel placed, not built.

And there is nothing else that moves with intention. No fish flit through the glowing fronds. No crustaceans scuttle over the monoliths. No gentle filter-feeders wave in the current. The fauna parameter is zero. This is a world of plant and mineral and water only. The dynamic, active processes are geological and botanical. You can watch the slow, inevitable creep of a glowing moss over a carved symbol. You can feel the shift in the current's direction as some vast, deep-cycle progresses. Occasionally, with a sound like a mountain sighing, a section of the fragile, calcified algal spire will collapse, sending a cloud of luminous dust billowing into the blackness, a slow-motion avalanche of light and bone.

The weirdness is in the silence within the roar. It is in the way the forest seems to listen to its own song. It is in the geometry of the ruins, which aligns not with magnetic north, but with the cold, thermal vents that pulse like black stars on the plateau below. It is in the feeling that the mountain is not dead, but sleeping, and the glowing forest is its nervous system, dreaming in light and sound under the endless, weighty dark.
