The world here is a sigh that has lasted a million years. It is a plateau so vast that its edges vanish into atmospheric haze, a tableland raised to the thin, warm breath of an eternal sky. The elevation is extreme—a tectonic shrug lifted this place eons ago, and then time set to work with infinite patience. Erosion has done its work so completely that the land feels like the ghost of mountains. It is ancient, not in the sense of jagged ruins, but in the final, settled smoothness of a bone worn down by a river. The terrain is a gentle, rolling emptiness, a series of low swells and shallow depressions that stretch to every horizon. There is no rock, not in the sense of outcrops or cliffs. If stone exists, it lies buried under a skin of history so thick it has become something else.

That skin is vegetation. The parameter said total, and it is. This is not a landscape with plants on it; the landscape *is* plant. A single, continent-spanning organism—or a symbiotic tapestry so seamless it functions as one—covers every inch of the warm, damp earth. It is a living substrate, a spongy, resilient mat that varies in texture from velveteen mosses in the hollows to fibrous, bark-like plates on the rises. In places, it thickens into bulbous mounds or sends up slow, architectural forms: smooth, trunk-less pillars that support nothing but the sky, or broad, canopy-less arches that bridge the shallow valleys. The color is a profound, chlorophyll-saturated green, shot through with the deep burgundy of old growth and the silver-grey of lichenous undersides. The air itself feels grown, thick with the sweet-rot scent of perpetual germination and decay.

The water parameter is damp, not flooded. Moisture comes as a daily gift, not as storms or rivers. In the pre-dawn, a condensation fog settles so heavily that every surface gleams, and the air drips. By mid-morning, the warm sun lifts it again, leaving the mat glistening. Occasionally, a soft, warm rain falls from cloudlets that seem to form and dissipate directly overhead, nourishing the endless green without ever pooling. In the lowest depressions, the mat dips to form living cisterns—sacs of clear water held in a membrane of root and fiber, replenished by the mist and sipped by the world.

And the world here is fauna. Overwhelming. This is the central contradiction and the defining truth of the plateau: the space is empty, vast, and hollow-feeling, yet it teems with life to the point of deafening oppression. The emptiness is one of structure, of object. There are no trees to break the sightlines, no boulders, only the gentle swell of the living ground. But the *air*, and the *underside* of that ground, are overwhelming.

The sound is a constant, physical force. It begins as a deep, subsonic hum that vibrates up through the mat into the bones of your feet. It is the collective drone of billions of winged insects—not small flies, but thumb-sized creatures with glassine wings, rising in shimmering columns from every pore of the greenery. Their drone is layered with a ceaseless, chittering chorus from unseen burrowers and a rhythmic, pounding percussion that comes from the horizon. That percussion is the movement of the grazers. They are the reason for the emptiness and the sound.

The fauna are immense, few, and their presence fills the universe. You will see one, perhaps once a day. A shape on the horizon, moving with the geologic slowness of a landslide. They are walking mountains of flesh and chitin, armored in plates of living rock, their legs like moving mesas. They are the great eroders, the final shapers. They do not eat the vegetation; they *process* it. As they move, their slow, deliberate footfalls (each one causing a localized tremor and a deep, booming *crump*) shear the topmost layers of the mat, which regrows behind them in days. Their passage is the plateau’s primary dynamic, its slow, active heartbeat. Their calls are infrequent, but when they come—a low, brass-bellows roar that rolls across the open space for minutes—the sound is so profound it feels less like hearing and more like a change in air pressure. It silences everything else, momentarily, before the insectile chorus swells back.

Other life exists in their shadow. Armored reptiles the size of wagons bask in the sun on the backs of the sleeping giants. Swarms of iridescent avians, more like flying eels than birds, thread through the insect columns, their feeding cries like shattering glass. In the damp hollows, amphibious pack-hunters with too many legs slither through the mat, silent except for the wet rustle of their passage.

The built environment is scattered, and primitive. It belongs to the only tool-users here: a species of arboreal, long-limbed hominids who build not on the dangerous ground, but in the occasional, ancient architectural pillars of the vegetation itself. Their settlements are not villages, but single, sprawling structures woven from living tendrils and hardened resin, perched high on those pillars like wasp nests grown to palace size. They are faint marks on the immensity, observers. Their technology is of thorn and fiber, of polished stone and captured light. They harvest the giant insects for their glassine wings and the resin from the mat for glue. They watch the great grazers pass, and consider them gods, or weather.

The light is normal, clear, and sharp at this altitude, casting long, distinct shadows from the low arches and pillars. The dynamic processes are slow and fast simultaneously: the daily cycle of mist and sun, the hourly pulse of insect swarms, the year-long migration paths of the great grazers that eventually cover the entire plateau, and the century-long growth of the vegetative architecture.

The rule of this world is one of immense, patient life occupying vast, empty space. It is a world where sound is the primary medium of existence, where the ground is alive and the mountains walk, and where everything has settled into its final, roaring, green, and ancient form.
