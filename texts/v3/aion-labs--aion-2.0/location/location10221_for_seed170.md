The plateau is a bone-white plain under a sky the colour of heated iron. It stretches, flat and vast, to a horizon that does not curve but fractures—a jagged line of broken geometry where distant mesas hang upside-down or repeat themselves in fading copies. The air is thin and hot, carrying a metallic taste that coats the tongue. There is no water here, nor has there been for epochs; the ground is a mosaic of fractured silicate plates, salt-crusted and glittering under a sun that seems fixed at a perpetual, glaring zenith.

This is a high place, a tableland raised above whatever world might lie beyond its edges, and it is ancient. Wind has worked on the stone for millennia, not with the softness of water but with a patient, abrasive sigh. It has carved the landscape into forms that feel settled, final: long ridges like fossilised machinery, arches that resemble half-buried piston housings, and canyons whose walls are striated with colours that shift when not directly observed—from rust-red to tarnished brass to a deep, oily purple. The erosion is complete, the land in a state of dynamic equilibrium with the constant, dry gales. Nothing new is being built; nothing old is being destroyed. It simply is.

And it is empty. Profoundly, overwhelmingly empty. The scale is regional, perhaps continental, but the density of features is near-void. Miles of cracked plain separate one wind-sculpted complex from another. The silence, however, is an illusion. The sound is a constant, deep-frequency hum that vibrates up through the soles of nonexistent feet. It is the sound of engines the size of mountains idling in the bedrock. It is punctuated by distant, grinding roars that last for hours, and by the shriek of metal on stone that seems to come from every direction at once.

This is the industrial heartbeat of a world without builders. The technology is not constructed; it is emergent, a property of the plateau itself. Great vent stacks of porous rock exhale plumes of shimmering, superheated gas with a hiss that echoes like steam valves. Broad, shallow basins act as distillation arrays, their geometrically perfect terraces crusted with crystalline residues that glow with a faint, internal charge. Conveyor belts of gravel inch across the flats, driven by some subterranean magnetic pulse. There are no wires, no control panels, no intent—only process, scaled to a planetary degree, locked in a slow, perpetual cycle of production without product.

And there is life. Overwhelming, inescapable life. It is not life as biology understands it. It is fauna as geological event. They are called the Still-Movers. From a distance, each appears to be another range of hills, a peculiarly regular formation of spires and slopes. Then, over the course of a day, a “hill” might lift one colossal, segmented leg, a limb of black chitin and polished struts that unfolds with the deafening scream of a million un-oiled hinges. It settles the leg a mile forward, sending a tremor through the earth that takes minutes to subside. The creatures are few—perhaps only three or four exist across the entire plateau—but their presence dominates the psyche. They are living refineries, their backs crowned with forest-sized catalyst towers that weep strange smokes. Their eyes, when they can be seen, are vast pits of swirling, liquid mercury, reflecting not the sky but fragments of other, impossible landscapes.

Their movement is the source of the loudest sounds, but it is also the definition of slowness. Their journeys are measured in centuries. They follow paths etched into the plateau by their own passage over aeons, paths that gleam with a metallic, worn-smooth patina. Their feeding—if it can be called that—involves lowering cavernous maws to the conveyor-belts of gravel, ingesting the mineral broth, and excreting, from vents along their flanks, ingots of pure, unrecognisable elements that cool and crack in the heat. They are part of the industry, both its cause and its consequence.

Reality here is not stable. This is the most pervasive quality. The “normal” light is normal only in intensity; its quality is wrong. Shadows sometimes fall in two directions at once. A canyon mouth might, for a moment, open not onto more stone but onto a silent, star-dusted vacuum or a roiling sea of phosphorescent algae. The air itself occasionally crystallises into temporary, floating lattices that chime like glass before dissolving. The Still-Movers are anchors in this flux; where they pass, local physics reasserts itself for a time, leaving behind a trail of temporarily coherent space. The plateau is a place where the underlying code of existence is fraying, and the industrial processes and the fauna are both symptoms and stopgap repairs—a slow, loud, hot equilibrium of decay and perpetuation.

Nothing is built. Nothing grows. Nothing flows with water. Yet everything hums, shifts, screams, and watches with mercury eyes. It is a finished world, burning and high and empty, playing out the last, long notes of its own impossible mechanics.
