The basin is a breath held warm and wet in the throat of the world. You feel the place before you see it—a thick, condensing atmosphere that coats your skin and the inside of your lungs with a tangible moisture. The light is a perpetual twilight, not from any cycle of day and night but from a ceiling of low, motionless vapor that hangs just above the highest constructions, leaching color and sharpness from everything. This is a place of raw becoming, a landscape that has only recently stopped being liquid and has not yet decided what shape it will hold. The ground is a mosaic of contradictions: sheets of standing water, inches deep and perfectly still, reflect the dim grey above, while between them rise hummocks of a slick, blue-grey clay that smells of turned earth and deep stone. There are no plants. No green things, no stems, no leaves. Instead, the solid forms are mineral: outcroppings of a soft, chalky rock that weeps a constant, clear fluid, creating veils of dampness down their faces. In places, the rock has been carved by hand into rough blocks, but the carving seems recent, the edges still soft, as if the stone itself is reluctant to be solid.

This is the foundation of the city. It is a settlement of mud and water and sound. The structures are low, rounded, built from the same weeping stone and the thick clay, daubed over frameworks of a pale, brittle material that might be the petrified stems of some long-vanished growth, or perhaps the secreted architecture of a vast, colonial creature. The buildings cluster on the higher hummocks, so densely packed that their walls touch, their roofs merge into a single, uneven terrace. You move through narrow lanes not much wider than your shoulders, the walls glistening with condensation. The tech is primitive: you see simple tools of chipped stone and dull, soft metal leaning against doorways; clay pots, unevenly fired, catch the endless drip from eaves; bridges across the darker water channels are single slabs of that soft rock, laid by many hands. There is order here, a stubborn organization imposed on the ooze. Channels have been dug to direct the standing water into a sluggish network, though the water seems to have no current, no desire to go anywhere. The air is warm, a constant, enveloping warmth that never rises to heat nor falls to cool. It is the warmth of a body, of deep earth.

And the sound. It is the first thing that strikes you and the thing you can never escape, a physical pressure on the ears and the mind. It is not one sound but a layered, deafening tapestry. Beneath it all is the water: the endless, multiplied patter of drops falling from rock to pool, from eave to lane, a million tiny percussions without rhythm. Over this, rising and falling but never ceasing, is the life. The fauna is abundant beyond counting, a teeming, crawling, swimming, flying mass that fills every cubic inch of the space. In the water, pale, sightless amphibians with wide, gasping mouths produce a constant, low hum by vibrating membranes in their throats. On the mud and the rock, armored beetles the size of your hand scrape their carapaces together in a relentless, grating chorus. The air thrums with the wings of dense clouds of minute, gnat-like creatures, whose collective flight creates a high-pitched, oscillating whine. There are larger things too, moving through the canals—sleek, furred swimmers that bark short, sharp cries that cut through the din. There are no birds, but things that might be their cousins, leathery-winged and silent in flight, who cling to the walls and contribute a clicking, rattling call from bony beaks. This is the sound of the basin: a living, deafening engine of noise that never varies, never pauses, never grows louder or softer. It is the sound of the world here, and it is as constant as the wet and the warm.

The ecology is a closed loop of consumption without photosynthesis. There are no plants, but in the perpetual damp, other things thrive. Mats of a complex, slime-mold-like organism spread across the water surfaces, glowing with a faint, bioluminescent grey light that does little to illuminate but adds to the eerie haze. These mats are the primary producers, drawing energy not from light but from chemical reactions in the mineral-rich water. They are grazed upon by the countless insects and the blind amphibians. The amphibians are eaten by the furred swimmers. The beetles scavenge the waste of all. Everything is eaten by something else, and the nutrients cycle through the mud and the water and the bodies with a brutal, perfect efficiency. It is a world built on decay and regeneration without the intermediary of stem and leaf. The people of this city—short, sturdy figures with skin the color of the clay, their hair lank with damp—are part of this cycle. They trap the swimmers, collect the beetles for their hard shells, harvest the slime-mats to press into a bland, sustaining paste. They build with what the earth gives up, and they die and are returned to the mud. Their existence is one of immediate, manual toil, their tools simple extensions of their hands. They seem untroubled by the noise; it is the medium in which they live.

The rules of this place are written in its stillness. Nothing changes. The water level does not rise or fall. The temperature does not shift. The sound does not alter its composition. The slow drip from the rocks erodes them at a pace so glacial it is imperceptible over generations; this is a landscape in its infancy, and it will remain in its infancy. There are no storms, no seasons, no droughts, no floods. The sky is a uniform grey, the vapor ceiling never breaking. Time passes—you can see it in the wear on a stone step, in the growth of a new layer on a wall—but it passes without event, without gradient. It is a world in a state of frozen, thunderous equilibrium. The people do not seek to change it; their myths likely speak of a time before the sound, before the wet, but it is a theoretical time, a forgotten dream. Their world is what it is, and it is forever. To walk here is to feel a profound, unsettling stasis beneath the frantic activity of life. The deafening roar is the sound of that stasis, the white noise of a world that has found its balance and will not be moved. You leave, if you leave, with the sound still ringing in your bones, and the memory of a warm, wet, crowded, endless now.
