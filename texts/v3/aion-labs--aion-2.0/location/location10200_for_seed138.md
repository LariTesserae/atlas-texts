In the low belly of the world, heat rises in a visible shiver from the ground. This is the Steamhammer, a settlement not of streets but of conduits, not of plazas but of overflow pools. It occupies a single, sunken basin, recently torn open by some deep, subterranean shrug—the rock is still sharp-edged, black and glassy in places, as if the wound is new. The air does not move; it hangs, a thick broth of moisture and engine exhaust, pressing down with a weight that is almost liquid. It is eternally hot here, a damp, suffocating heat that springs from the earth itself and from the never-sleeping machines.

Water is not a feature but a condition. It bleeds from cracks in the obsidian-like rock, forming shallow, smoking rills that weave between foundations. It condenses on every surface—on the corrugated iron roofs, on the bundles of copper pipes, on the riveted sides of great tanks—and falls in a constant, syncopated drip. The ground is a sponge of warm mud and sinter, a terrain that sucks at the boot and exhales a faint, sulfurous steam. There are no true rivers, only a network of shallow, brick-lined canals guiding the hottest runoff toward the squat, blocky factories.

The buildings are few, but they dominate. They cluster tightly at the basin's center, a dense knot of industry in a sparse landscape. They are built of dark brick and rust-stained iron, layered and lashed together with catwalks, vents, and towering chimneys that do not vent smoke but great, white gouts of pure steam. This is the source of the sound: a deep, rhythmic *thump-thump-thump* from massive piston houses, a high-pressure hiss that never ceases, the groan of metal expanding in the heat, and the clatter of valves being turned by an unseen, perpetual hand. The sound is a wall, a permanent presence as solid as the buildings themselves.

Light is dim, filtered through the perpetual ceiling of mist and steam. It glows a dull, bruised orange from countless furnace doors and boiler windows, casting long, wavering shadows that dance but never truly shift. There is no day or night cycle here, only this eternal, crepuscular gloom, illuminated by the industry's own fiery heart.

Life, in the form of greenery, is scarce. A few leathery, grey-green fronds cling to the damp bricks near the waterways, and patches of luminous algae paint the hotter pools a sickly green. But the fauna thrives in the wet heat. The air buzzes with dense clouds of minute, iridescent flies. Fat, pale amphibians with bulbous eyes blink from the steaming rills, their croaks a bass note under the mechanical din. Leather-winged shapes—perhaps bats, perhaps something else—skitter along the pipes, chasing the insect swarms.

Nothing changes. The pistons have always pumped. The steam has always screamed. The same drip falls from the same pipe onto the same stone, wearing no groove, for the stone is young and hard, and the equilibrium is perfect. The workers, if there are any, are as constant as the machines, moving with a slow, predictable rhythm, their faces gleaming with condensed moisture. They do not age; they simply are. The Steamhammer is not a place in time. It is a condition, a closed system: heat begets water, water begets steam, steam begets work, and work begets the heat that begins it again. Forever. It is a loud, hot, wet, living engine, settled into the sharp, new bones of the earth, and it will never do anything but hum.
