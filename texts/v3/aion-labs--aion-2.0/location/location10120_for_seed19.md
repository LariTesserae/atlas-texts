The plain does not begin or end; it simply is. It is a place of final geometry, a testament to exhaustion. The sky is a permanent, cool sheet of pearl-gray, without sun or shadow, source-less light falling evenly across everything. There is no horizon—instead, the land and the low sky blend in a soft, blurring seam, as if the concept of distance has worn thin.

Underfoot, the ground is a dense mosaic of interlocking plates. They are not stone, not metal, but something that has forgotten the difference—a sintered crust the color of dust, lead, and dried blood. The plates fit together with a precision that suggests they were once liquid, poured and cooled into this final form. There are cracks, but they are old, smoothed by time into mere lines of darker pigment. No water has ever flowed here. The air is cool, thin, and perfectly dry; it carries no scent, only a faint metallic tang that rests on the back of the tongue.

This is the high place, the last elevation. Once, perhaps, there were mountains here, but eons of a softer, stranger erosion have done their work. Not just wind and dust, but the slow leaching of possibility. The peaks have been planed down, the valleys filled in, until all that remains is this immense, gently undulating plateau. It stretches further than the eye can parse, an open vastness that feels less like space and more like a condition.

And it is dense. Not with clutter, but with presence.

They stand, or recline, or simply exist, in numbers that overwhelm the mind. Creatures—if that word applies—cover the plain in every direction, a silent multitude. They are not organic, not in any way that life on a younger world would recognize. They are assemblies of angles and curves, of polished black surfaces that drink the light and bone-white struts that seem to glow from within. Some are low and sprawling, like collapsed lattices. Others rise in slender spires, or complex helical forms, or shapes that fold in on themselves in ways that hurt the mind to follow. They are made of a material that is neither chitin nor ceramic nor crystal, but something that borrows from all three. They do not touch, but they come agonizingly close, leaving only narrow, winding paths between them. The density is such that from any vantage point, the view is a labyrinth of silent forms.

They are still. Utterly, perfectly still. Not a twitch, not a tremor. This is the never-changing. Time here is not a river but a pane of glass. The creatures are frozen in a single moment, a tableau that has lasted for millennia. They are the fauna of this place, overwhelming in their number and variety, yet they contribute no movement, no sound. The silence is absolute, a physical weight. If you listen until your ears ring, you might catch the faintest sigh—the whisper of the thin, cool air moving over the edges of the mineral plates underfoot. Nothing more.

Here and there, among the forest of frozen beings, you see marks of hands. Scattered, primitive structures: low windbreaks of stacked plate-shards, circles of gathered white pebbles, occasional crude lean-tos made from longer, straighter fragments propped against one another. The technology is of the simplest kind—selection, stacking, balancing. No mortar, no joinery, no metal. Whoever made these used what was at hand, and their efforts are being absorbed back into the stillness. The structures feel like afterthoughts, or perhaps prayers left unanswered.

The vegetation is sparse, and it is not green. In the few places where the plate-crust has cracked deeply enough, a hard, lichen-like growth has established itself. It is the color of tarnished brass, and it feels like wire wool to the touch. It does not spread; it simply persists, a minimal claim on existence.

But the true rule of this world is the softness of reality. This is the breakdown, settled into permanence. Look at one of the towering spires for too long, and its edges will seem to both recede and approach simultaneously. A path between two creatures that appeared straight will, when walked, curve gently without your feet turning. Distances compress and expand without warning. The creatures themselves are the focal points of this gentle unraveling: their geometries are not impossible, but they are unstable, suggesting dimensions that fold away from human perception. It is not a violent chaos, but a quiet, constant simmer of wrongness, long since grown familiar and static.

Nothing changes. Nothing has changed for ages beyond measure. The light does not dim or brighten. The cool temperature does not waver. The creatures do not stir. The faint cracks in the ground do not widen. It is a world that has reached its end state—a profound equilibrium of absence and presence. The overwhelming fauna are not alive, nor are they dead. They simply are, monuments to a process that finished its work long ago. The silence is the sound of that finished work.

To walk here is to walk through a museum of frozen anomalies, under a sky that has forgotten how to change, on ground that has forgotten how to erode any further. It is the last page of a book, held open forever.
