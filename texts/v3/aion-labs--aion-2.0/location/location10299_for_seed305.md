You stand on a shelf of black basalt, sharp-edged as if split yesterday, looking out over a continent that shouldn’t exist.

The air is thin and cold enough to ache in the lungs—a dry, piercing cold that belongs to altitudes where the sky bleeds into void. Yet the light is not the weak blue of high peaks; it is a brilliant, oppressive white, pouring from a sun that appears three times too large, saturating every surface with a glare that has no warmth. This is the first rule of the plateau: light here is a substance, a weight, a deafening silent shout.

The plateau itself is young. Its geography is a record of violence recently stilled: sheer cliffs drop into abysses still steaming with geothermal breath; mesas stand like half-carved gods, their flutes and columns crisp, unworn by time. Rivers, what few there are, run in deep, narrow channels cut with the impatient precision of knives. Water is not abundant, but it is present in a thousand subtle forms—a film of condensation on the cold underside of crystalline leaves, a slow drip from mineral icicles in shadowed crevices, the occasional mist that rolls up from the chasms at dawn, kissing the stone with dampness before the light burns it away. The land is damp, not wet; it drinks moisture from the air itself.

And yet, it is lush.

From every crack, every shelf, every slope, life erupts in a packed, choking profusion. This is not greenery. It is a forest of glass and wire and slow-burning chemical hues. Structures like shattered chandeliers grow in thickets, each branch a faceted tube that drinks the fierce light and glows from within with a cool, bioluminescent blue or venomous green. Between them, carpets of what look like frozen moss cling to the rock—a dense, velvety texture that is somehow both mineral and soft, exhaling a fine, persistent vapor. There is no room for emptiness here. Every inch of rock is claimed, layered, interwoven with something growing, clinging, feeding. The density is suffocating; the plateau is a single, vast organism, every part connected and competing.

This is where the industrial parameter manifests, and where the strangeness begins. The plateau is a factory. The crystalline thickets are not merely plants; they are photosynthetic reactors. Their internal channels hum with viscous fluids, processing sunlight into complex hydrocarbons and crystalline lattices that grow with audible, minute clicks. Geothermal vents, scattered across the expanse, are not just holes in the ground; they are intakes and exhausts for a planetary metabolism. Great pistons of rock, sheathed in mineralized biofilm, rise and fall with a rhythm measured in days, driving fluids through subterranean networks. The sound is a constant, low-frequency drone—the grind of continental-scale machinery, overlaid with the higher-pitched ringing of the crystal forests vibrating in the wind. It is loud, but it is a loudness that has always been here; it is the baseline silence of this world.

And the fauna. Overwhelming does not begin to cover it.

They are everywhere, and they are motionless. At first, you mistake them for more of the landscape—boulder-like forms covered in the same crystalline crust, towers of layered, sedimentary flesh, slow shapes like living landslides. But then you see the pattern, and the scale dawns. They are not still; they are moving. It is simply that their movement is of a different order of time. A grazing behemoth, a mountain of tessellated plates and jointed stone limbs, might take a year to extend a feeding tendril into a thicket, and another year to retract it. Their circulatory systems are rivers of molten silica and organic slurry, pumping with the tempo of lava flows. Their eyes, when they have them, are lenses of polished obsidian that track the passage of clouds across seasons. They are the product and the custodians of the factory-plateau; they consume the reactor-crystals, process the minerals, and their immense, slow bodies are the sites of further refinement. Their waste is crystalline slag, deposited in orderly mounds that will, over centuries, become new bedrock. They are living refineries, and there are millions of them, a standing population that makes the land seem to crawl when viewed across a decade.

Change here is slow. A cliff face might collapse once a millennium. A new geothermal vent might awaken over a century. The paths of the grazing behemoths are worn into gentle, polished valleys over eons. The light cycle is the only rapid pulse—the brutal white day, and a night that is not dark, but lit by the collective internal glow of the reactor-forests and the faint, pulsating emissions from the fauna, turning the plateau into a vast, faint circuit board seen from above.

There are no buildings, no tools, no artifacts left by hands. The industry is innate, anatomical, geological. The physics are slightly off: light bends more sharply around the crystal spires, creating permanent, shimmering mirages at their tips. Sound travels farther and slower, so that the industrial drone seems to come from everywhere and nowhere. Time, measured by the movement of the fauna, feels stretchable, viscous.

You are an irrelevance here. Your heartbeat is a frantic, absurd flutter. Your breath fogs and vanishes in seconds. Your entire life would be a single, unnoticed twitch in the digestive cycle of the nearest grazer. The plateau does not notice you. It is too vast, too packed, too slow, and too busy with its own ancient, luminous, grinding production.
