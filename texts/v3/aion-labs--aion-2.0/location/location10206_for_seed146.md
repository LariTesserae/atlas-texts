The world is a mouth buried deep in the planet’s flesh, a vastness that breathes only once every aeon. You descend not into a cave but into a continent of absence—a hollow so enormous its ceiling is lost to a darkness thicker than stone, a darkness that hangs like a second, heavier ground above. The air is warm, not with the warmth of decay or life, but with the deep, patient warmth of a body that has never known a fever. It does not stir. It rests in your lungs like a memory of heat.

Underfoot, the ground is a mosaic of sharpnesses. This is a young place, geologically sleepless; erosion has not yet learned to soften its edges. Fields of crystalline teeth—obsidian shards, quartz daggers, splintered geodes spilling dull internal rainbows—stretch away into the black. In places, the crust has split into salt flats, not white but a bruised, metallic grey, cracked into polygons so precise they look carved. There is no water. No dampness, no slickness, no hint of liquidity in the air or the stone. Chemistry here works without it: acids that are gases, solvents that are powders, life that drinks from solid rock.

And there is life. Abundant, dense, pressing into every cubic inch of the warm dark. They are not animals as you know them. They are movements in the mineral tapestry. You see a forest of slender, jointed legs—chitinous, iridescent like oil on slag—slowly picking their way across a slope of shattered silica. Their bodies are low, armored plates clicking softly. They are grazers: their mouthparts scrape at faint, luminous fungal mats that cling to the crystal bases—the only vegetation here, if you can call it that: a primitive, chemosynthetic felt that glows a dim, exhausted blue. Their scraping is the loudest sound: a whispery, collective sigh that fills the quiet. For the place is mostly silent. There is no wind, though the scale is vast enough to have weather. Here, weather is the slow drift of spore-clouds, visible only as a slight thickening in the gloom, or the occasional shudder of electrostatic discharge that webs the distant ceiling with fleeting, soundless violet threads.

The density is claustrophobic. Not because the space is small—it is unimaginably large—but because every surface is occupied, layered, saturated. Fauna crawl over crystal forests; smaller parasites scuttle across the grazers’ backs; mineral growths sprout from the shells of both. In the deeper trenches, larger forms lie half-embedded in the salt, like sleeping leviathans of basalt and iron. They breathe perhaps once a year, a grating exhalation of powdered stone.

And there are marks of hands. Scattered, as if dropped by a careless giant. A single hut, cobbled together from flat sheets of mica, leaning against a spar of amethyst. A circle of stones, too perfect to be natural, in the center of a salt polygon. On a wall of sheer black obsidian, crude figures are pecked: stick-beings harvesting light from hanging crystals. The tech is primitive: the stones are not cut, but found and piled; the drawings are made with a harder stone, hammered patiently. Who made them? Perhaps the grazers themselves, in a different life-phase. Or something else that passed through and left no bones.

The light, what little there is, comes from the fungal mats and from occasional veins of phosphorescent mineral that pulse a slow, green rhythm, synchronized to nothing but their own deep time. Your eyes adjust not to brightness, but to a landscape of textures and subtle, radioactive glows.

The strangeness is in the stillness. Nothing changes. The grazers follow the same routes, century after century, their paths worn into the salt not by wear but by chemical trails they lay down and follow. The spore-clouds drift in the same slow gyres. The electrostatic webs flare in the same sequences. It is a system in perfect, frozen equilibrium. No birth cries, no death rattles—just an endless, quiet maintenance of form. Time is not a river here, but a closed loop, a held breath.

You stand in the warm, dense, dark abundance of a world that has already finished becoming what it is, and will never become anything else. The only thirst is the thirst of stone for silence. The only growth is the infinitesimal accrual of crystal, atom by atom, in the perfect, unchanging dark.
