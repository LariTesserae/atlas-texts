This is the world beneath the world, a cavern so vast it holds its own breath, its own weather, its own pale and patient sky.

The ceiling is a glowing membrane, a firmament of symbiotic lichen and crystalline fungi that emit a constant, shadowless light—bright enough to cast no true darkness, only gradients of pale gold and bone-white. It hangs kilometers above, a dome of soft radiance that makes the concept of ‘underground’ feel like a lie. This is not a cave; it is an interior continent.

Beneath that sky, the land is a living body. Vegetation is not something that grows here—it *is* here. The ground underfoot is a dense, spongy mat of interlocking stems, a woody coral-like organism that has knitted itself over millennia into a continuous substrate. It covers everything: it flows over the sharp, young ridges of black basalt that spear up from below, sheathing them in a velvet hide of grey-green; it forms continents of its own, continents of plant-mass that rise in gentle, rolling hills or drop away into sheer-walled chasms. In places, the mat is thin, revealing the jagged, freshly-fractured rock beneath—this is a young landscape, geologically speaking, its bones still sharp and unweathered. In others, it has built itself into architectures of shocking complexity: arches that span silent gulfs, colonnades of fused trunks that hold up portions of the ceiling, terraced gardens of moss and fern that no hand planted. This is a world built by and for plants. They are the stone, the soil, the shelter.

And yet, it is empty.

The scale swallows all. You can walk for days and see only the endless, undulating plain of the living floor, the distant walls so far they are mere smudges of darker texture against the luminous ceiling. The air is cold, a constant, dry chill that seeps into the lungs—perhaps 5 degrees above freezing. It carries a scent of damp peat, of ozone, of something like crushed pine needles. The emptiness is not sterile, but profound. The density of *things* is near zero. A single twisted column of rock, wrapped in vines, might be the only vertical marker for a hundred square kilometers.

Water is a ghost here. It does not flow in rivers, but weeps. Condensation forms on the cooler undersides of the great plant-arches, dripping in slow, random cadences into shallow pools below. Sometimes a film of moisture glazes the spongy floor, making it slick. In rare places, a thin trickle has carved a meandering channel through the plant-mat, exposing the dark rock beneath for a few meters before being absorbed again. The humidity hangs in the air, a dampness that never coalesces into rain, but ensures everything is slightly clammy to the touch.

The silence is a presence. It is not absolute—there is the *ping* of a water drop into a pool, kilometers away and unnervingly clear. There is the soft, sub-audible groan of the living substrate as it expands and contracts with microscopic shifts. Sometimes, a deep, grinding rumble echoes through the cavern, as a section of the plant-mass, grown too heavy, sloughs off a cliff face and falls into the void. These sounds only deepen the quiet.

And then there is the turbulence.

The landscape is not still. It is in a state of constant, slow-motion frenzy. The plant-mass is alive in a way that transcends growth. It *migrates*. It flows like glacial ice over obstacles, consuming rock, filling voids. In places, you can watch—over hours—as a tendril of grey root-stuff advances a centimeter across a bare basalt face, secreting a solvent to etch a hold. Elsewhere, entire groves of tall, phosphorescent stalks might rise from the floor in a matter of days, clumping together like a city of slender towers, only to wither and collapse into pulp a week later, recycled back into the mat. The air itself is not calm. From the temperature differential between the cold floor and the slightly warmer, glowing ceiling, gentle convection currents stir. They can coalesce into sudden, silent winds that sweep through the cavern, howling softly as they funnel between the plant-formed arches. These winds carry spores in visible, glittering clouds that settle and instantly begin to fibrillate, adding new layers to the world.

Scattered through this vast, living emptiness are the marks of those who are not plants. The built environment is primitive, sparse, almost apologetic. A low wall of piled basalt stones, half-subsumed by advancing moss. A crude shelter woven from the pliable ribs of a giant fern-leaf, now crumbling. A stone blade, left on a rock shelf, its edge still sharp. The technology is of the simplest kind: knapped stone, tied fibers, fire-blackened pits. Who made these things? The fauna is rare—perhaps they were the makers. You might glimpse one: a slow, six-legged browser the size of a bison, its fur matted with lichen, moving with monumental patience as it crops the endless turf. It ignores you. It is a part of the system, no more remarkable than a walking hill.

The weirdness of this place is not in flashy miracles, but in the inversion of expectations. The sky is below the earth. The ground is alive and restless. The storms are of growth and decay, not of rain and lightning. Time moves in visible, botanical leaps and lurches. It feels less like a landscape and more like the inside of a colossal, sleeping lung—a breath held in a cycle of construction and dissolution, bright, cold, damp, and almost entirely alone.
