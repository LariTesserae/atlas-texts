This is the Glimmer Cavern.

You descend not into darkness, but into a vast, breath-stealing cold that has weight and presence. The first impression is of a sound so immense it ceases to be noise and becomes a physical pressure, a constant, deafening throb that vibrates in your teeth and ribs. It is the sound of the great subterranean engines, though from here, in the open belly of the world, their source is unseen. The air is damp with a perpetual, chill mist that beads on every surface, not with flowing water, but with the slow, insistent sweat of the stone.

The cavern is young. Its walls, soaring up into a gloom where the ceiling is lost, are raw and sharp. Jagged fins of dark basalt thrust from the floor like broken teeth, and the distant walls show the clean, fractured planes of recent cataclysm—a tectonic sigh that opened this space perhaps a century ago, a blink in geological time. It is open, a subterranean valley leagues across, its scale felt in the way the dim light fails to reach its ends, swallowed by the sheer volume of space.

And it is alive.

The parameter said ‘total vegetation,’ and it is total, but not in the way of a sun-drenched forest. Here, in the permanent dim (the light a faint, sourceless gleam, perhaps from rare phosphorescent minerals or the ghost of radiance filtering through miles of rock), life has claimed every inch not of soil, but of stone. It is a world of cold, slow plants. Thick, rubbery mats of moss, deep violet and charcoal grey, sheath the rock in a living pelt. From them sprout clusters of stalk-like fungi, pale and translucent as ice, their caps glowing with a faint, internal bioluminescence—a million tiny, cold lanterns. Curtains of lichen, tough as leather, hang from the sharp rock features, moving imperceptibly in the slow, cold drafts. There are no leaves, no flowers; this is a botany of endurance, of minimal flux. The ecosystem is pure plant—or plant-like—a closed loop of decay and regrowth on a bed of stone, sustained by the damp air and some deep, chemical nourishment from the rock itself. It is sparse only in its architecture: great meadows of moss are separated by outcroppings of bare, wet stone, and the towering fungal groves stand in isolated clusters, like silent, glowing villages.

The human marks are scattered, almost apologetic against this living mass. A rusted pump-house, its roof sagging under a thatch of moss, sits alone near a seep of groundwater. Girders of some forgotten scaffolding are embraced by thick, creeping vines of a rubbery creeper, now part of the landscape. Farther off, the hulking silhouette of a compressor station, its windows dark, hums with a deeper layer of the overwhelming sound. These are not settlements, but outposts, their purposes obscure, being slowly digested by the patient green.

The deafening sound has a source, felt more than seen: a distant, colossal wall of machinery built into the far cavern wall, a citadel of industry. Steam vents in great, rhythmic plumes that condense instantly into the cold mist. It is automated, lights blinking on control panels behind thick glass, running on some ancient protocol. It is the heart of the noise, the thrumming, pounding industrial beat that dominates this organic world. Perhaps it mines, or ventilates, or simply exists, a relic of a reason lost.

Fauna is rare. Sometimes a pulse of shadow moves through the fungal groves—a flightless, blind arthropod the size of a hand, grazing on spores. The silence between the mechanical throbs is broken only by the occasional drip of condensed mist from a lichen curtain, a sound that seems profound in its isolation.

The rules here are of slow conquest and deep time. The plants are the architecture, the substrate, the environment. They grow with infinite patience over the sharp, young rock, softening its edges over centuries. The machines run their deafening, urgent cycles, but they are the transient feature. The cold damp is constant; the sound is constant; the slow, total, quiet embrace of the living stone is the only true dynamic. It is a normal world, by its own lights—a perfectly logical, if stark, equation of rock, water, cold, and relentless, silent life, underscored by the furious, irrelevant shouting of engines.
