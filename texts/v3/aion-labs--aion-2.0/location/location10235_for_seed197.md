It is called the Throat of the World, though it is no larger than a forgotten city block sunk into the warm, low belly of the land. You find it not by sight, but by sound—a deep, resonant drone that seeps into your bones long before the tangled outer vines part. The drone is not one sound but a layered cacophony: the shriek of splitting cellulose, the wet slap of great leaves against each other in a wind that does not touch the ground, the chittering chorus of a million unseen throats, and beneath it all, a subsonic hum that vibrates the teeth. It is, as the parameter insists, deafening. To enter is to be swallowed by noise.

The terrain is a study in vertical hollows. The total vegetation—rated 2.6, plant-as-everything—has not merely grown here; it has *built*. Over centuries or millennia (erosion: 2.0, established, weathered), colossal woody trunks have spiraled up and then curved inward, weaving into vast, domed chambers. Buttressed roots form arched doorways and tiered galleries. Walls are living tapestries of moss, fern, and rubbery creeper, pulsing faintly with a slow, bioluminescent sap-light that provides a steady, normal glow (light: 1.9). The floor is a springy mat of decay and fresh growth, a compost centuries deep, always warm (1.6) to the touch and damp (1.4) with a perpetual, sweet-rot condensation that beads on every surface and drips with a sound lost in the greater roar.

This is the urban (1.7) core, but a city built by biology alone (tech: 0.3). The architecture is accidental, purposeful only in the way an ecosystem finds equilibrium. The chambers—grand halls, narrow pipes, bulbous atria—are connected by tubes of woven vine that serve as streets and aqueducts. There is infrastructure: gutters of curled bark channel the intermittent rivulets of condensate into central, shallow pools that appear and vanish with the humidity. But the overwhelming parameter is density: 0.2, empty. The vastness of these organic cathedrals is defined not by what fills them, but by what does not. The air between the curving, living walls is a profound void. You stand on a moss-cushioned ledge and look across a chamber the size of a concert hall, and see nothing but air, a few drifting motes of pollen, and the opposite wall a hundred feet away. The abundance is all in the fabric of the enclosure and in the fauna (2.3) that swarms *within* that fabric, not in the space itself.

The fauna is abundant, yes, but it is not separate. It is part of the sound and the breakdown. Gliding lizards with wings of translucent membrane flit through the voids, their ribs fused to the plants’ spore-sacs, harvesting and planting in one motion. Bloated, furred grubs the color of tarnished copper cling to the walls, their rhythmic chewing contributing to the relentless sonic fabric. And there are things that look like birds, if birds were made of bundled light and thorns, weaving in and out of the solid matter as if the barrier between plant and animal, between body and air, is mere suggestion.

Which leads to the weirdness (2.6). Reality is soft here. The rules are not broken so much as persistently ignored. Walk toward what appears to be a dead-end wall of knotted root, and you may find your next step has placed you behind it, looking back the way you came, without any sensation of movement. Time dilates in the quieter alcoves; a minute spent watching a phosphorescent fungus bloom can feel like an hour, and an hour of travel through a shrieking vine-tunnel can feel like a breath. The most established rule is the rule of sound. The deafening noise is not just an effect; it is a medium, a substance. It holds the structure up. In the rare, fleeting moments when the collective din dips—perhaps some cycle of the ecosystem pausing to draw breath—the very walls shudder. Dust of dried pollen and lichen drifts down. The great woody beams groan, as if threatening to collapse into silence. The sound is the true architecture, the active (2.4) process that maintains this pocket of breakdown.

The water comes not as rain, but as the world’s slow sweat. It condenses on the cooler upper reaches of the domes and drips in erratic, syncopated rhythms. In some chambers, it collects in shallow, tepid pools where blind, pale insects skate. In others, it is immediately drunk by the spongy floor. There are no rivers, only a pervasive dampness that keeps everything slick, growing, and breathing.

It is a stable madness. The erosion score speaks to that. This is not a place newly torn asunder; it is a settled state of un-being. The plants have grown into this shape because the sound shaped their growth. The emptiness exists because the sound-filled air is too thick with vibration for most matter to persist within it. The fauna has evolved to ride the sonic waves, to eat the noise, to mate in the brief silences. It is an ecology of magnificent, roaring balance.

To be here is to be a ghost in a machine made of scream and wood. You are an anomaly—dense where everything is empty, quiet where everything is loud, bound by physics that the rest of the chamber has long since agreed to forget. The Throat does not notice you. It is too busy singing itself into existence, note by deafening note.
