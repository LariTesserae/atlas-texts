This is the Sky-Mere: a world of water suspended above the clouds. Or so it feels. You stand on a spine of dark, sharp rock, its edges still crisp and unweathered, rising from a warm, shallow sea that stretches to every horizon. The elevation is immense—you are on a plateau so high the air is thin and bright, but the temperature remains a humid, clinging warmth, a gift from the sun-bathed waters below. The water itself is everywhere, a vast liquid plain at this impossible altitude, not deep, perhaps only waist-high in most places, but absolutely pervasive. It is the defining element. It fills every low point, drowns every valley, and gleams under a normal, golden sun like a shattered mirror laid across the world.

The terrain is a chaos of youth. This is a landscape freshly born from some colossal geological sigh: the plateau thrust upward, tearing the earth, and the ancient aquifers burst forth to fill the wounds. The rock formations are jagged, assertive. Ridges cut the water like black knives. Islets and archways of stone, their surfaces still rough and fractured, break the water’s skin. There is no sand, no softness of silt—the erosion score is too low. The floors of the shallows are bare rock, scrubbed clean, or covered in a crackling, mineral crust.

And upon every speck of emergent stone, life has erupted with a furious, dripping lushness. The vegetation is not merely present; it is a smothering, layered, dense saturation of green. It climbs the sharp cliffs in mats and curtains. Epiphytic gardens bloom in every crevice: mosses thick as pelts, ferns with fronds like emerald fountains, orchids with waxy, bizarre blossoms that seem carved from jade. The trees, where they can find purchase, are low and broad-crowned, their roots like great nets of stone, gripping the rock and drinking directly from the warm, mineral-rich water. Vines rope everything together, creating living lattices that bridge from islet to islet, so that in places the water is roofed by a tunnel of leaves. The air smells of damp humus, of hot stone, of a thousand different pollens, and of the clean, slightly metallic scent of the vast, sun-warmed mere.

The sound is a profound, mostly quiet hum. The silence of high places is here, but it is filled by the low vibration of life. The whisper of wind through a billion leaves. The soft, constant *plink… plink…* of condensation falling from leaf-tips into the water. The occasional, distant splash of a rock fragment calving from a young cliff. There is no birdcall, no chitter of mammals. Fauna is a rarity—a glimpse of something iridescent and many-legged skimming the water’s surface, a silent, slow-worming shape in the deep shadows of a root tangle. The ecology is one of plants and perhaps simpler, stranger invertebrates. The world belongs to the vegetable kingdom, patient and overwhelming.

The strangeness lies in the relationships, in the rules. The water is not passive. It moves, but slowly, in vast, languid circulations driven more by thermal gradients than by wind. The warm water seems to feed the vegetation directly; some of the root systems glow with a faint, internal bioluminescence at twilight, suggesting a deep, symbiotic exchange of nutrients. The air often shimmers with a haze, not of heat, but of evaporating water charged with botanical pheromones—a visible, golden mist that hangs in the still air. Time here operates on a different scale. Changes are glacial. A cliff face might take a thousand years to show a crack. A floating island of vegetation, built up from interwoven roots and peat, might drift a mile across the mere in a century. The sense is of a world still settling into its own skin, vibrant and full, yet patient beyond measure.

From your vantage on this rocky spine, you see the open scale of it: a kingdom of water and stone and green, stretching out in a series of channels, lagoons, and forest-clad spikes. The light is normal, but it plays upon this scene with a clarity that feels supernatural, illuminating the stunning density of it all—every square inch contested by some form of life, all of it bathed in, born from, and cradled by the warm, high, all-encompassing sea.
