It is called the Hollow City, though it is not a city in any human sense. It occupies a shallow depression, a low bowl of cracked gray sinter that still smells faintly of ozone and cold stone dust, as if it cooled only yesterday. The ground is a mosaic of sharp, interlocking plates—some no larger than a hand, others broad as tables—all tilted at slight angles, their edges unweathered and keen enough to cut. There is no water here, not a drop; the air is so dry it seems to crackle with every inhalation, leaching moisture from the tongue and the eyes.

Rising from this fractured plain are the structures. They are not built, but grown—or perhaps extruded. Slender spires of a glassy, obsidian-like substance pierce the dim air, some reaching thirty or forty feet high. They are clustered in rough rows and rings, suggesting streets and courtyards, but the spaces between them are vast, emptiness stretching for yards before the next grouping appears. This gives the place its hollow quality: a settlement-scale pattern etched into nothingness, a map of a town drawn with only the tallest punctuation marks. The spires are multifaceted, catching what little light there is and fracturing it into dull, bruised purples and deep grays. No two are exactly alike, yet they share a severe, minimalist geometry—hexagonal columns tapering to needle points, or sometimes flattening into sheer, blade-like fins. There are no doors, no windows, only smooth, impenetrable surfaces.

The light is dim, perpetual and sourceless, as if filtered through a high veil of ash or fine sediment. It casts no sharp shadows; instead, the entire landscape is rendered in soft, low-contrast gradations of gray and charcoal. The temperature is cool, a constant, still chill that seeps into bone, unaffected by day or night—if such cycles exist here.

Life is sparse, but it asserts itself in strange, silent ways. In the wider cracks between the sinter plates, where a finer dust has accumulated, growths emerge. They resemble more than anything else thin, bone-white corals or antlers, branching in intricate, recursive patterns no taller than a knee. They are rigid and leafless, photosynthesizing perhaps through their chalky surfaces. They do not move, but they are not static: if one watches for hours, one might see a new branch slowly, silently extend from a tip, growing almost imperceptibly like a crystal forming in solution. This is the vegetation—sparse, alien, and utterly quiet.

Fauna is rarer still. Occasionally, a faint scuttling sound might be imagined, but never confirmed. Sometimes, a shadow might detach itself from the base of a spire—a flat, many-legged thing like a jointed sheet of graphite paper—and flow across the ground, only to vanish into another shadow. It makes no sound. Its movements are swift but utterly silent, a punctuation of motion in the stillness.

The dynamic quality of the place is not in obvious motion, but in process. The air, though still, carries a faint, regular pulse of pressure, like a vast, slow heartbeat from deep below. The sinter plates occasionally settle with a tiny, crystalline *tink*, a sound so isolated it heightens the silence around it. And the spires themselves are active: they hum at frequencies below hearing, a vibration felt in the teeth and the hollows of the chest. Sometimes, a spire will shed a long, thin sliver of its glassy material, which falls and shatters into dust that is then slowly, over days, incorporated into the growing plates of the floor. It is a landscape in a state of constant, slow-motion cycling—building up and wearing down in the same breath, but so gradually that it feels like eternity.

There are rules here, unspoken but felt. The spires are not to be touched; their surfaces are cold enough to burn, and sharp enough to part flesh from bone with the lightest pressure. The white growths are brittle and will crumble to a fine, scentless powder if disturbed. Time is dilated, stretched thin; haste is impossible, and agitation is swallowed by the immense, quiet emptiness. It is a place that feels both newly born—its features sharp, its processes raw and unabated—and yet ancient, a fossil of itself in the making. It is urban in its pattern, desolate in its density, active in its deep rhythms, and silent on its surface. A city without inhabitants, a landscape without water, a world without sun, living its slow, cool, strange life in the hollow of the earth.
