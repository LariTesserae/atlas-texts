Deep inside the mountain's granite heart, where the rock gives way to something older and sharper, there is a room that was not carved but grown—grown from poured alloys and cold-forged beams, a pocket of total industry suspended in the stone. It is high, so high that the thin air outside would freeze your lungs in an instant, but here there is no outside. Here, everything is made.

The chamber is not large—perhaps forty meters long, twenty wide, a vaulted ceiling lost in a haze of particulate and pale, unchanging light. The scale is intimate, almost claustrophobic, yet the space feels sparse because what occupies it is so monumental, so isolated in its placements. In the center, rising from a floor of hexagonal friction-treated plates, is the Engine.

It is not one machine but a congregation: three towering piston housings, each as wide as a ancient tree trunk, sheathed in dull nickel and braced by girders that look too sharp, too new to have ever known dust. They are connected by a tangle of conduits and segmented pipes that gleam with a strange, non-reflective quality, as if they drink the light rather than throw it back. Around them, at wide intervals, stand control consoles—massive blocks of iron and glass, their faces alive with rows of unblinking green indicators and pressure dials frozen at precise midpoints. There are no chairs. No one works here. It has been running, they say, since the mountain was raised.

The air is cold—a deep, metallic cold that seeps through boots and gloves, a cold that has nothing to do with weather and everything to do with absence. There is no water, not a drop; humidity is measured in parts per billion, and the atmosphere is so dry it crackles with static whenever you move. What passes for vegetation are sparse clusters of synthetic lichen clinging to the lower pipes, not for oxygen—the air is reprocessed elsewhere—but to absorb trace acoustic vibrations. They are black, fibrous, and utterly still.

The sound is everything. It is not a noise you hear with your ears so much as feel in your bones: a deafening, layered roar. The pistons drive upward with a hydraulic gasp that shakes the floor plates, then withdraw with a deep, subsonic shudder. Valves the size of wagon wheels snap open and shut at precise intervals, each closure a deafening clang that echoes in the vault. Beneath it all is the constant, teeth-rattling hum of the dynamos, a frequency that vibrates the fillings in your teeth. There are no quiet moments, no lulls. The sound is as permanent as the walls.

And it never changes. The rhythm is perfect, metronomic, a cycle that has repeated without variation for centuries. The pressure gauges never flicker. The temperature sensors hold at exactly -12°C. The Engine does not produce anything tangible—no smelted metal, no generated power that leaves this room. Instead, according to the few schematic plates bolted to a far wall, it is a *stabilizer*. It takes the latent tectonic unease of the young mountain, the sharp, uneroded tension of new-forged rock, and translates it into this roar, this vibration, this eternal industrial hymn. It turns potential quakes into predictable noise. The strangeness is in its purpose: a machine that turns geological anxiety into sterile, deafening stability.

Time here is invariant. Shift changes do not exist; maintenance is performed by automated carts that glide on silent tracks at predictable intervals, their movements just another part of the unchanging cycle. Dust does not settle; it is electrostatically repelled from critical surfaces. The light, diffuse and shadowless, comes from phosphor panels set into the ceiling, burning with the same steady glow day and night, year after year.

To stand here is to stand inside a heartbeat that is not alive. It is to witness a process that has achieved perfect equilibrium, a deafening, cold, dry monument to the idea that nothing must ever change again. And in that relentless sameness, in that total, engineered absence of water, of life, of variation, there is a profound and unsettling strangeness—the strangeness of a world that has chosen to scream forever, just to stay silent.
