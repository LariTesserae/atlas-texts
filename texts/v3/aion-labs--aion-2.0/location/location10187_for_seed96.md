This is the Sky Trough: a high, wind-scoured basin cradled between sawtooth ridges of raw, newly uplifted stone. The air is thin and cool, carrying a permanent damp chill that seeps into everything. It is a place of utter silence and startling brightness. 

The light is merciless. It pours from a pale, cloudless sky, rebounds off pale stone, glares from standing pools, and makes the world seem etched in glass. There are no shadows deep enough to hide in; only sharp, geometric cuts of black and white.

And everywhere, there is water. Not rivers, not lakes, but water as a condition. It weeps from joints in the rock, beads on cold surfaces, fills shallow, perfectly geometric basins cut into the floor. The ground, where it is not submerged, is a dark, damp slate-grey, slick with a perpetual film of moisture. In the cool air, mist hangs in lazy, gauzy sheets, drifting slowly through the ordered spaces without wind to push it.

This is not a natural landscape. Every surface has been worked. The entire basin—perhaps two miles across—is a single, colossal construction. The terrain is a dense, packed maze of monolithic slabs, upright pillars, low walls, and stepped platforms, all fitted together with a primitive, overwhelming logic. The tech is stone-upon-stone. No mortar binds them; the weight of continents and the precision of their cutting holds them fast. The joints are so tight that water capillary-creeps along them, tracing dark lines in the pale rock. Here and there, dull copper bands, green with verdegris, clasp corners or rim drainage channels—the only metal, and it is simple, hammered, primitive.

The materials are all mineral: a pale, almost white limestone that glistens when wet; veins of darker, glassy obsidian that form decorative lines; the ubiquitous dark slate of the paving. There is no wood, no fiber, no leather. Nothing that ever lived. The "young" erosion means the edges are still knife-sharp. Corners have not softened. Inscriptions—if they are inscriptions—are crisp and deep, though their meaning is opaque, just rhythmic patterns of dots and converging lines.

The density is overwhelming. There is no empty space. Every square foot is occupied by a slab, a pillar, a channel, or a pool. One does not walk here so much as navigate a three-dimensional puzzle. You step from platform to platform, duck under lintels, squeeze between monoliths. The silence is profound, broken only by the slow, metronomic *plink… plink…* of water droplets falling from a ledge into a pool, and the soft, continuous sigh of seepage. The air smells of wet stone and cold, and something faintly metallic.

There is no ecology. No moss clings to the stone. No insects skim the pools. The water is clear and sterile. Life has never gained a foothold here, or was meticulously excluded. The rules of this world are geometric, hydrological, and patient. This is a machine built for a purpose that operates on a timescale of millennia. The water is not a nuisance; it is the lubricant, the coolant, the working fluid. It flows along prescribed channels, fills basins to specific levels, evaporates slowly in the bright light, condenses on the cooler north faces of stones, and begins its cycle again. The slow dynamic is the dynamic of water seeking its level, of stone settling by millimeters per century, of copper oxidizing to a stable green.

The weirdness lies in the intent. This is clearly made. But by whom? For what? The primitive tech suggests a colossal, patient labor, a civilization that built with nothing but stone hammers and copper saws, yet achieved a perfection of placement that feels algorithmic. The open scale suggests it was meant to be seen and experienced as a whole—perhaps from the surrounding ridges, which are natural and jagged, a frame of chaos around this perfect, silent, dripping order. It is a temple to a concept, a slow computer made of rock and water, calculating something vast and cold under a bright, empty sky.

You stand in a narrow aisle between two walls of fitted stone. A sheet of water, perhaps a millimeter thick, flows down the vertical face to your left, making the stone look like it is sweating light. Ahead, a stepped pyramid of slabs, each precisely the same size, rises out of a shallow, perfectly square pool. The water in the pool is so still it looks like a sheet of polished obsidian, mirroring the bright sky and the sharp edges above. You are alone, and the silence is so complete you can hear the blood in your own ears. The place feels neither dead nor alive. It is waiting.
