**The Ember-Soaked Terraces of Geothermal City Seventeen**

It is a city that breathes steam, not smoke. A high, broad plateau, its edges lost in a permanent haze of condensation, where the sky is a blistering, cloudless blue and the ground is a mosaic of wet, dark stone and the slow, glassy flows of obsidian from eruptions centuries cold. The heat is not from the sun alone—though at this altitude the light is a sharp, clarifying glare that turns every puddle into a mirror and every steam-veil into a prism—but from below. The plateau is a crust over a still-living furnace. Heat seeps up through fractures, warming the stone until it’s pleasant to the touch at dawn and unbearable by midday, and turning the ever-present water into a clinging, mineral-scented mist.

Water is everywhere, but it is shy. It does not gather in roaring rivers or deep lakes. It weeps from the porous black rock in a thousand slow seeps, condenses on the undersides of industrial gantries in fat, warm drops, and collects in shallow, steaming pools that simmer at exactly blood-heat. The air is so saturated that breathing feels like drinking a thin, hot broth. After a rain—which comes as a sudden, heavy, soundless deluge—the entire landscape gleams like a vast, polished anvil, and for hours the steam rises in great, silent columns that twist in the thermals.

There is no vegetation. No leaf, no frond, no hint of green. The world is mineral and animal. The stone is all dark basalt, rusty ironstone where the water has stained it, and the occasional vein of crystalline sulfur that glows a faint, buttery yellow. In place of soil, there are crusts of brilliant white silicate deposits, like salt but sharper, and microbial mats of deep violet and arterial red that spread across the dampest rocks, pulsing slowly with a chemosynthetic hunger. They are the only “plants” here, and they feed on the heat and the minerals carried up by the water.

The city—Geothermal City Seventeen—is not a dense hive. It is a sparse, scattered infestation of industry upon this primordial tableau. Its structures are low, blocky, and built from the same dark stone, fused together with a slag-like cement. They are connected not by roads, but by a raised web of pipeworks and conduction trenches. Great, snaking pipes, thick as ancient trees and coated in peeling white insulation, run across the landscape, carrying superheated brine from drill-sites to the central power stations. The stations themselves are cathedral-like sheds of corrugated metal, their roofs perpetually dripping with condensation, from which a low, sub-audible hum emerges—more a vibration in the teeth than a sound. The silence is profound. The machinery is designed for efficiency, not noise; the fauna, for reasons of their own, are quiet. The dominant sound is the soft, eternal *patter-trickle* of condensing water hitting stone or pooling in the conduction trenches.

And the fauna are abundant. They are the true masters of this wet, hot, mineral world. They have evolved in the absence of flora, building their lives on the microbial mats and on each other. Most are low-slung, many-legged things with chitinous shells the color of wet slate or volcanic glass. **Stone-crawlers** move in slow, precise herds, their rasping mouthparts scraping the colorful microbial films from the rocks. **Steam-darters**, like jointed lizards of iridescent copper, flit between the plumes of vapor, hunting smaller airborne spores and insects. In the deeper, hotter pools, **boil-wyrms**—pale, eyeless, and sinuous—undulate in the near-scalding water, filtering nutrients through frilled gills. Above them all glide the **silence-hawks**, predators with vast, sound-absorbing wings of a velvety black material. They make no cry, and their flight is utterly silent; they strike by dropping like stones from the bright sky, their talons piercing the shells of the stone-crawlers with a faint, crisp *crack* that seems deafening in the quiet.

The world operates on a logic of heat exchange and mineral circulation. Life is chemosynthetic or predatory. There is no day/night cycle of activity—the heat is constant, the light is constant (augmented at "night" by the faint, sulfurous glow from certain mineral veins and the guide-lights on the infrastructure). The dynamic activity is the slow pulse of the geothermal vents, the migration of fauna herds from one seep to another, and the relentless, silent, automated industry of the city. The human presence—or whatever built this place—is ghostly. The infrastructure runs itself, monitored by occasional maintenance drones that skitter like large metal insects along the pipeworks. The few habitation domes have windows dark and fogged with condensation. It feels less like a living city and more like a machine that was set running long ago and has since been adopted by the local ecology as just another geothermal feature—a source of warm surfaces, condensed water, and strange, regular vibrations.

The strangeness is in the balance. The coexistence of brute-force industry with a thriving, silent, non-photosynthetic ecology. The way the steam-darters nest in the vents of heat-exchangers. The way the silicate crusts slowly engulf the lower struts of the gantries, making them look like ancient, excavated bones. The air smells of wet stone, ozone, and the faint, spicy musk of the microbial mats. It is a place that is intensely alive, yet profoundly quiet; harnessed by technology, yet utterly wild at its core. It feels settled, eternal, and in a state of perfect, active equilibrium between the furnace below and the bright, hard sky above.
