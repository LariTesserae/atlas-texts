You stand in a city that was never meant for the sky.

The first thing you notice is the sound: a deep, resonant hum that seems to come from the stone itself, a basso profundo vibration that thrums through your bones and fills the cavern with a palpable pressure. It is not mechanical, not exactly—it lacks the grating edge of machinery—but organic in its persistence, like the slow heartbeat of a sleeping leviathan. This is a loud place, a place defined by its sound, a constant reminder that you are inside something alive and functioning long after its makers have vanished.

The chamber is vast, but not overwhelmingly so—cathedral scale, perhaps, or the central plaza of a buried township. Its boundaries are lost in a gloom so profound it feels less like an absence of light and more like a substance, a velvety black wool that swallows distance. What light exists is accidental and feeble: faint, phosphorescent smears of blue-green fungus clinging to the seams between megalithic blocks, and the occasional, fitful pulse of cerulean light that races along embedded filaments in the walls and floor, like neurons firing in a gigantic stone brain. These pulses are irregular, unpredictable, and they illuminate the space in startling, frozen snapshots.

In one flash: the smooth, sweeping curve of a ramp ascending into darkness.
In another: the geometric perfection of a faceless obelisk.
In the next: the intricate, fractal patterning on a distant wall, shimmering with condensation.

The air is warm and damp, clinging to your skin with a mineral-scented humidity. Water is present not as rivers or pools, but as a pervasive sheen. It beads on every cold surface—on the obsidian-like stone of the buildings, on the strange, glassy conduits that run along the ceiling, on the occasional tendril of root or mycelium that has found a crack from some distant, forgotten surface world. Drops gather, fat and heavy, and fall with sharp, solitary *plinks* that are swallowed by the omnipresent hum. Sometimes, a thin, steady trickle carves a dark streak down a wall, feeding minute, iridescent lichens that glow with a soft, internal light.

The architecture is urban in its complexity and clear intent, yet utterly alien. Structures are not built; they appear grown or printed from a single, seamless material—a stone that feels like polished basalt but has the tensile strength of alloy. There are no right angles. Every intersection is a gentle curve, every doorway an ovular mouth, every street a flowing channel rather than a grid. It is a city of echoes and shadows, designed for a form of movement or perception you cannot grasp. The erosion parameter here is not decay, but a kind of settled perfection. The edges are worn smooth, not by violence, but by an incomprehensible span of time. The surfaces have a patina, a softness to their darkness, as if the stone itself has relaxed into its final, eternal form. Nothing feels new, but nothing feels ruined. It is in balance. It has achieved its own state.

And it is empty.

The density is a near-void. The spacious avenues and soaring, vaulted ceilings contain nothing but the hum and the damp air. Your footsteps, if you dared to move, would be the first disturbance in centuries. Yet, the dynamic score insists this place is *active*. The pulses of light are a process. The condensation and its slow, relentless carving is a process. The hum is the output of a process—perhaps geothermal, tapping into the planet's warm blood, or something more arcane, a technology that manipulates fundamental forces in ways your physics can only hint at. This is tech beyond modern: infrastructure that maintains itself, that perhaps even *thinks* in a slow, mineral fashion. You get the unsettling sense that the city is not abandoned, but *attended*. The emptiness is not neglect; it is the desired condition.

Life, in the conventional sense, is sparse. The only vegetation are those ghostly fungal blooms and the luminous lichens, subsisting on moisture and trace minerals. Fauna is rare. Once, in the corner of your eye, you might see a skittering shape with too many legs, a creature of chitin and pallid flesh, adapted to the eternal dark and the hum. It does not approach. It is merely another rare component in the system.

The weirdness is subtle, a constant low-grade strangeness that permeates everything. It is in the way the sound sometimes seems to form almost-words in a frequency just below hearing. It is in the way the light-pulses occasionally sync with the drip of water, creating a fleeting, rhythmic pattern. It is in the feeling that the city is not just a place, but an entity—a settled, established, self-contained entity, dreaming its warm, damp, loud, and empty dream beneath the weight of the world.
