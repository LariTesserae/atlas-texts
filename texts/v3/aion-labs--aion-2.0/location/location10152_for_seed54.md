The city is a bruise of light against a permanent night, a cold so deep it has weight.

You stand on a high plateau, the air thin enough to feel like a vacuum slowly filling with frozen glass. The ground underfoot is not rock, but a dense composite of hexagonal plates, slightly resilient, like the carapace of a continent-sized insect. It is wet, not with flowing water, but with a perpetual, heavy condensation that beads and instantly freezes into a slick, black rime. The moisture comes from somewhere—perhaps geothermal vents deep below the city’s substrate, or the exhalations of the teeming fauna—and it hangs in the air as a fog that glows faintly with a billion pinpricks of bioluminescence. It is a wet, luminous dark.

The structures rise around you in a density that defies the open scale. This is not a skyline of towers, but a thicket of organiform spines, bulging sacs, and intersecting geodesic lattices, all woven together so tightly they create canyons and chimneys rather than streets. The architecture is beyond modern; it feels grown, not built. Surfaces flow from smooth, obsidian-like planes into fibrous webbing, all at scales that shift from the nano-textured to the monumental. In places, the city seems raw, embryonic—great ribs of something like reinforced crystal thrust up from the ground, their edges sharp and unweathered, as if the metropolis is still being extruded from the planet’s crust by a patient, incomprehensible process. This is a landscape in its first geological breaths.

And it is loud.

The sound is not mechanical, but biological. A chittering, clicking tapestry that blankets everything, rising from the surfaces, the fog, the dark cavities above. The fauna is overwhelming. They are everywhere: sinuous, multi-limbed things that flow across the walls like viscous shadows; armored beetles the size of your head, their carapaces clicking as they process the frozen rime; flocks of silent, bat-like fliers that navigate the tight canyons by disturbing the bioluminescent fog into swirling galaxies. You see no plants, no greenery. The base of the ecology seems to be the city itself—or the microbial and fungal mats that coat every surface in pulsing, iridescent films, feeding on the condensed chemicals and the waste heat of deep, sub-surface machinery. The fauna feed on these mats, and on each other, in slow, relentless cycles.

Light comes only from life and from rare, stuttering nodes of pale blue or violet energy that crackle along certain strands of the infrastructure. The fog glows because it is full of drifting spores or tiny creatures. Your breath fogs, and the fog you exhale briefly sparkles before being swallowed by the greater gloom.

The dynamics are slow, almost glacial. You watch a section of wall where a new structural vein is slowly, over minutes or hours, extending itself by depositing layer upon atom-thin layer of some diamondoid material. A huge, slug-like creature, its back a garden of phosphorescent tendrils, inches along a ledge, taking an hour to cross a distance you could walk in ten seconds. The cold preserves; the slowness is a law.

This is Crysthane, the Weeping City. A high, dark, wet metropolis of alien engineering and overwhelming, patient life. It does not sleep. It condenses, it grows, it thrums with a loud, slow pulse. And it is only just beginning.
