This is the Plateau of the Living Skin.

At two and a half miles above the sea, the air is thin and sharp, but it holds a heat that feels wrong—a dry, baking fever that radiates from the ground itself. The ground is not ground. It is a continuous, leathery hide of vegetation, a single organism, or perhaps a congress of indistinguishable siblings, that has grown over every inch of stone and soil for millennia. This is the Skin. It is the substrate. It is warm to the touch, slightly resilient like dense foam rubber, and its color shifts in patches from burnt umber to deep olive to a sickly, photosynthetic yellow. It is total. There is no dirt, no bare rock, only this living carpet, inches to yards thick, covering a continental expanse of highland.

Erosion here is not of rock, but of the Skin itself. It has been weathered into a settled form: great, rolling swales and gentle basins, like a frozen, vegetative sea. In places, it has worn through, revealing ancient, polished basalt beneath—black bones in a green body. But these exposures are rare. For the most part, the world is a vast, undulating plain of living matter under a dome of hard, blue sky.

The water parameter is low, but the Skin has solved for thirst. It drinks the sky. Each dawn, as the thin air cools rapidly, a phenomenal dew condenses, beading on the fine hairs and capillary structures of the Skin’s surface. For an hour, the entire plateau glitters, weeping moisture that is instantly absorbed. And sometimes, in the late afternoons, convection over the vast scale of the plateau births its own weather: bruised purple clouds form with shocking speed and release short, violent rainbursts. The water does not pool; it is swallowed greedily by the spongy mass. You can hear it hiss.

The sound, however, is not defined by the rain. It is defined by the fauna, and the fauna is overwhelming. This is not a world of hidden creatures. It is a world dominated by them. The sparse density refers not to life, but to space between monumental biological events. Herds of something like armoured bison, but with six legs and crests of hollow bone that sing in the wind, migrate in columns a thousand strong. Their passage is deafening—a thunder of countless hooves on the resilient Skin, a orchestral cacophony of their crest-songs, which range from deep, tectonic groans to piercing, crystal shrieks. The air thrums with it. When the herds are absent, the wind takes over, howling across the seamless plain with nothing to break its voice.

And there are other things. Gliders with wingspans like cargo sails, riding the thermal vents that rise from the Skin’s warmer patches. Swarms of insectile fliers that move in clouds so dense they cast shifting, buzzing shadows. Predators that are not lions or wolves, but ambulatory thickets of thorn and tendon that hunt by sensing vibrations through the Skin itself. The fauna is constant, loud, and strange. Their interactions—the chase, the feeding, the mating displays—create a dynamic, active ecosystem that never sleeps. The cycle of condensation, growth, grazing, and decay is the plateau’s only clock.

Humanity, or what passes for it here, is a scattered afterthought. Their tech is primitive: tools of chipped stone and hardened resin, bindings made from sinew and tough strands pulled from the Skin. They build no cities. Their structures are temporary frames of bone and hide, erected during a season and then absorbed by the Skin when they move on. They leave faint marks—a pattern of stones, a midden of shells from creatures pulled from deep crevices in the Skin, the occasional fire-blackened circle. They are not masters of this place; they are careful tenants, moving with the herds, listening to the songs of the crest-beasts to navigate. Their existence is a whisper in the roar.

The weirdness is in the rules. The Skin is intelligent in a slow, botanical way. It remembers paths, and wears them into comfortable grooves for the herds. It sometimes extrudes hard, nutritious nodules in patterns that guide migrations. It can, if injured deeply, secrete a caustic sap that burns like acid. The plateau has its own physics of sound and heat; temperature inversions create mirages not of water, but of silent, ghostly duplicates of the landscape hanging in the air. At night, under normal starlight, parts of the Skin luminesce with a soft, greenish radiance, responding to the pressure of footsteps or the onset of the dew cycle.

To stand here is to feel profoundly small. The scale is vaster than the eye can reconcile—a living plain that stretches to curved horizons, under a weather system of its own making, filled with a deafening, overwhelming tide of life that is utterly alien, yet perfectly balanced. It is hot, high, and hungry. It is established. It has been here long before any tool-user stumbled onto its back, and it will remain, breathing, drinking the sky, and singing with the feet of a million beasts, long after their last fire goes cold.
