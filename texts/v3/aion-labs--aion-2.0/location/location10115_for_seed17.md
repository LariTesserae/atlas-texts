This is a planet of water and peak; a world drowned but not flat. The ocean is global, unbroken by continents, a crushing, cold, and clear abyss that stretches from the iron-core heat to a ceiling of ice-locked atmosphere far above. Yet within this liquid void, the true terrain rises: the Spine of the World is not a mountain range of air and rock, but a titanic seamount chain, a spine of young volcanic basalt and sharp, glassy obsidian that soars from the abyssal plains five miles down to within a few hundred fathoms of the surface. These peaks are new—their edges are knife-sharp, their faces sheer and uneroded by current or time. There are no slopes of sediment, only cliffs that drop into blackness.

The water itself is the medium of everything, and it is nearly empty. For vast, vertiginous volumes, there is nothing but the pure, pressure-thickened brine, a perfect blue-black void so clear that you can see the faint glow of a structure ten miles distant as a mere pinprick. Density here is a forgotten concept. This is a landscape of distances, of immense liquid space between points of reference.

And the points of reference are industrial. Scattered across the flanks of the peaks, bolted into cliff faces, or standing on rare plateaus like grim cathedrals, are the structures. They are not cities; they are outposts, single installations, or the sprawling, interconnected ruins of what might have been a single vast facility. Their architecture is brutalist and functional: pressure-spheres of blackened alloy, gantries and pipelines that snake across the rock, colossal pump housings, and the skeletal remains of derricks meant for some unimaginable extraction. They are from an age of industry—there is the feel of engines, of scaled production, of electrified purpose, now stilled. Many are dark, but some few still glow with an internal, sourceless amber light from behind thick quartz ports, powered by geothermal taps drilled deep into the volcanic heart of the peaks. They are not ruins of decay, but of perfect preservation; the cold (just above the freezing point of this saline mix) and the immense pressure have arrested corrosion, leaving them as if shut down yesterday.

There is no vegetation. No kelp forests, no microbial mats save the faintest chemosynthetic films around the hottest vents. The world is mineral and metallic. Life, however, is abundant. It is all fauna, and it is adapted to the void. Great, languid things drift through the emptiness between peaks—filter-feeders with membranes like living sails, hundreds of meters across, harvesting the sparse planktonic snow. They glow with internal patterns of cool blue and green, communication constellations in the dark. Predators move among them, sleek and pressurized, with multiple jaws and eyes like black lenses. Smaller, swarming things flicker around the structures, attracted to their residual heat and light, their bodies making faint ticking sounds as they navigate.

The sound is not biological. It is deafening. It is the sound of the world itself. From a thousand volcanic fissures along the Spine, superheated water and minerals scream into the cold sea in titanic, permanent plumes. The roar is a low-frequency vibration that travels for thousands of miles, a basso profundo thrum that is felt in the bones before it is heard. It is overlaid with the metallic groans of the mountains adjusting to tectonic stresses, and the deep, rhythmic *thump-thump-thump* of perhaps one or two still-functioning geothermal pumps within the oldest structures. This is a world that never stops shouting. There is no silence, only a gradient of roar.

And it never changes. The vents have erupted for millennia at the same ferocity. The currents are locked in perpetual, lazy gyres around the peaks. The temperature is constant, the pressure unvarying. The glowing creatures follow eternal migratory loops. The lights in the structures have not dimmed. This is a system in perfect, thunderous equilibrium. A young, sharp, vast, empty, industrial, deafening, and frozen world—not in ice, but in process, drowned under a cold ocean at the top of a mountain that has no summit, only a descent into darker, quieter depths below.
