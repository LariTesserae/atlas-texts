This is the High Hum. A place of final wearing-down.

First, the sound. It is not a sound you hear with your ears so much as feel in the marrow of your jaw, in the liquid of your eyes. A deep, tonal roar, as if the sky were a vast sheet of metal being shaken forever. It is the wind—but not wind as known below. Here, at the extremity of the world’s spine, the atmosphere is thin and furious. It streams across the final tableland with the pressure of millennia, a river of air so constant and deafening that it becomes the floor of all other perception. To speak is to mouth words that are shredded at the lips. To listen is to feel the vibration of your own skull.

Beneath this tyrannical choir lies the land itself: the Peneplain of Eons. Elevation here is not about peaks, but about sheer, terrifying altitude. You stand on a plateau that is the roof of the world, a stone tabletop so vast its edges curve with the horizon. And it is flat. Not perfectly, but with the profound, sweeping flatness of ultimate age. This is the parameter of erosion pushed to its conclusion: every mountain that once was here has been ground to dust and blown into the screaming air over geologic spans. What remains is a bedrock base, polished smooth by aeons of abrasive wind and the slow, patient kiss of water scored at 1.2.

Water here is a secretive thing. No rivers run, for there is no slope for them to run on. Instead, moisture manifests as a perpetual, cold sweat. It condenses from the thin, warm air (a persistent, incongruous warmth of 1.6 that seems to rise from the stone itself) onto every surface. At dawn, the world glitters with a billion beads of dew. Sudden, brief rains descend not in drops but in sideways-shearing mists, driven by the turbulent gales. The water collects in shallow, wind-scoured depressions—mirrors of sky that tremble with the sound—before being sipped up by the green.

And the green… it is the lushness of 2.3, but of a singular, suffocating kind. It is not a forest. It is one organism, or a colony of identical kin. A moss, or a lichen, or something between, it carpets the entire infinite plain in a dense, velvety pelt of emerald and bronze. It is lush in its thickness, in its vital, photosynthetic fervor, but it creates an emptiness of 0.5. There are no trees, no shrubs, no variation. Just the endless, rolling fur of the world, softening the edges of stone, swallowing the occasional erratic boulder, a monoculture of stunning vitality. It moves not with the wind, but in a slower, stranger rhythm of its own, pulsating gently as if breathing the thin air.

This is the weirdness, a 1.5 on the scale of the strange: the Moss (for it has no other name) luminesces. As the normal light (1.6) of the sun fades, the plain begins to glow with a soft, green-gold radiance from within the mat itself. The light pulses in slow, tidal waves across thousands of leagues, communicating some unknowable vegetative process. The dynamic of this place is turbulent (2.9), but its turbulence is layered. The air is a perpetual, deafening storm. The Moss, however, is in a state of constant, slow-motion creation and dissolution. Patches will wither and bleach to white in hours, only to be overgrown by vibrant new growth from beneath, a churning, cellular marketplace of life and death on a vast, quiet scale beneath the aerial roar.

Fauna is rare (1.3). What exists is monumental and silent. The Stone-Sheep are one: shaggy, low-slung beasts the size of boulders, their fur a perfect mimicry of the Moss they graze. They move once a day, shifting a few ponderous meters, their digestion so slow it is geological. Their only sound is the deep, sub-audible crunch of stone-lichen being ground between molars of basalt. There are also the Wind-Snakes, not reptiles but ribbons of flexible, glass-like substance that ride the gales, ringing like chimes, hunting the luminous motes that rise from the Moss at twilight.

The built environment, scored at 0.6, is scattered and primitive (1.1). The signs of whoever, or whatever, touched this place are simple to the point of austerity. Megalithic door-frames of three standing stones and a lintel, sunk deep into the Moss, leading nowhere. Low, circular walls of unmortared rock, half-submerged, their purpose erased. The most complex structures are the Listeners’ Huts: rough domes of stacked stone, with a single, narrow entrance facing away from the prevailing wind. Inside, only smoothed seats and walls stained by the smoke of ancient, tiny fires. The tech is of placement, of endurance, not of mechanics. Tools found here are of knapped flint and shaped bone. Whoever was here sought not to conquer the Hum, but to attune to it.

The scale is open (2.4)—a regional, valley-size? No. It is continental. You can walk for weeks and the horizon will not change. The sky will change. The sun will wheel. The deafening roar will modulate from a shriek to a deep, droning groan, but the Moss, the flatness, the immense, aged solitude will remain. This is a landscape that has reached its terminal state. It is ancient (2.8), not in the sense of ruins, but in the sense of a process completed. The world has been eroded to this final, stable form: a high, warm, damp, luminous plain under a riotous sky, empty of all but the most essential, stubborn things, vibrating with a sound that feels like the planet’s own slow, steady heartbeat, amplified to the point of madness. It is beautiful, and it will erase you, not with malice, but with sheer, indifferent presence.
