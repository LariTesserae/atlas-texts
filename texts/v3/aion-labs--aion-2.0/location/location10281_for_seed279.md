It is called the Weeping Plain, though it is not a plain in any flat sense—it is a vast, sunken arena, a depression the size of a drowned county, held in the fist of low, sharp-ridged hills. The air is cool and thick, perpetually twilight, not from any daily cycle but from the ceiling of mist that hangs ten meters above the ground, a vaporous lid that smothers the sky and dyes all light the color of tarnished pewter.

Water defines this place. It does not fall as rain; it exudes. It beads on every surface—on the jagged, charcoal-gray rocks that thrust from the damp earth like broken teeth, on the low, scattered arches of some forgotten builders, on the dense, intricate formations that crowd the basin. The ground is a sponge. Your foot sinks not into mud, but into a mat of something that is not moss—a thin, rubbery, slate-blue film that covers every horizontal surface, slick and cold. It emits a faint, sourceless bioluminescence, a ghostly blue sheen that makes the dimness navigable. This is the life of the plain: a primal, microbial consortium, more mineral than biological, that feeds on the silicate-rich seepage and, in turn, excretes the slick, glassy substance that paints the world.

The landscape is young. The rocks are sharp, their edges not yet softened by time. They look newly fractured, as if the basin dropped away yesterday in some colossal, silent subsidence. From these fresh wounds, water weeps in countless tiny rivulets, tracing dark lines down the rock faces, gathering in pools that are black and perfectly still, like lenses of obsidian set into the floor.

And it is dense. There is no empty space. Between the rock teeth rise other things: thickets of slender, jointed spires that look like polished bone, reaching up towards the mist. They are silent and unmoving. Terraces of sinter, built drop by mineral drop over centuries, step down in delicate, concentric curves, their edges razor-sharp. They hum. A low, constant sub-audible thrum that vibrates in your teeth and bones, the sound of water moving through microscopic pores in the stone. This is part of the loudness—not a noise, but a pressure, a dense sonic presence that fills the air.

But there is also the water's voice: a perpetual, echoing chorus of drips. Each drop from the weeping rocks strikes the pools or the rubbery mat with a clear, high *plink*, and the sound bounces off the countless vertical surfaces, multiplying, overlapping, until it becomes a shimmering, metallic ringing that has no rhythm, only a chaotic, loud harmony. Sometimes, from deeper in the mist, comes a louder report—a chunk of sodden rock shearing off and falling into a pool—followed by the sucking gurgle of displaced water.

Through this saturated, humming, ringing world, the built things are scattered like afterthoughts. A low wall of fitted stones, half-dissolved into the blue mat. A single, squat tower, its doorway a dark oval, its surface pitted and weeping like everything else. They are primitive, massive, made of the same local rock. They possess no right angles; every curve and join seems organic, grown rather than built. No tool marks are visible. They feel less like shelters and more like markers, or perhaps extensions of the plain’s own slow intent.

Fauna is rare, but present. You might, after an hour of stillness, see a shape move at the edge of vision—a slow, undulating motion through one of the black pools. It is not a fish. It is something longer, thinner, a ribbon of darkness that spirals up through the water column before sinking again. Or, on the sinter terraces, you might glimpse a creature like a six-legged crab, its carapace a dull, wet stone-color, moving with glacial slowness to scrape at the luminous mat with a single, crystalline claw. It makes no sound you can hear above the dripping and the hum.

The weirdness is in the geometry. Walk towards one of the bone-white spires, and it seems to recede, or to multiply. The mist plays tricks, but so does the land itself. Distances are unreliable. A pool you skirt appears again ahead of you, or to your side. The arches, when you pass through them, sometimes lead not to the space you saw, but to a different, similar part of the plain. It is not malevolent, merely consistent with rules you do not understand. This is a place that is still deciding what it is, its properties not yet fixed.

Change here is measured in the growth of a spire by the width of a hair over a year, in the slow creep of the blue mat over a fallen arch, in the gradual deepening of a pool as it dissolves the rock beneath. The loud, dripping chaos is a surface frenzy over a depth of immense patience. The plain breathes, but each inhalation and exhalation lasts a century.

You stand in the cool, dim clamor, surrounded by the dense, weeping, non-living life of it, and you feel the strangeness settle into your bones. This is not a dead world. It is a world before, or after, or alongside the idea of green. It is wet, it is loud, and it is waiting, with the slow, open-ended patience of stone and water and time.
