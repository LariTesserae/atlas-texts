With each step, the sound of your own breathing becomes lost in the thick wet air. The path underfoot is not soil but a woven mesh of ancient roots and corroded iron grates, slick with a film of condensation. You realize the entire environment is a closed loop of exhaled steam. There are no animals. There never were. The only movement is the slow, deliberate sway of the leaves, and the minute, almost imperceptible adjustment of a thousand metal joints. You are not in a ruined city. You are inside a breathing thing. The air is warm and still, tasting of copper and moss.

The terrain is intimate. The space you occupy is perhaps the size of a cathedral nave, but it feels smaller, more compressed. Walls
are not walls but closely packed, vertical arrangements of what could be foundry slag, fused with root systems. They rise, but
not far. Above you, the canopy of leaves and manufactured haze creates a ceiling close enough to hit with a thrown stone, but
impossible to see through. The ground is a fine silt of eroded granite and decomposed plant matter, mixed with a persistent
slickness of mineral oil that oozes from the seams between the bricks. The water is everywhere. It sheets down the walls in
rivulets too slow to be rain, too organized to be weeping. It follows paths carved over centuries, pooling in dark basins where
ferns with unnaturally perfect spiral formations drink.

The construction is warren-like. Tiny, claustrophobic channels open into broader chambers, each one a functional space from
some earlier era of industry. Gears as tall as a person, frozen now, but still humming with a low, powerful vibration that you
feel in your teeth. The rhythm is slow: a heavy, metallic pulse that reverberates through the entire structure, shakes the
water droplets free from leaf tips. *Thrum. Pssshhh. Thrum. Pssshhh.* Overlaying this constant engine-beat is a chorus of
drips, creaks, and the occasional explosive hiss of steam from a buried pipe. It is loud, but it is a deep, resonant loud, not
a sharp blare.

The lamps, spaced every twenty feet along the main channels, are sooted glass globes emitting a sickly yellow-green glow, barely
enough to cast shadows. The primary light source is a diffuse, steady phosphorescence from the moss that carpets every
horizontal and vertical surface. It gives a dim, underwater quality to the air. In the deeper chambers, where the industrial
equipment is more dense, the only light is from the glowing fungus that grows in strange, geometric clumps. The leaves of the
taller plants have absorbed this luminescence, offering faint patches of paler green against the darkness.

Ecology is inverted. The plants do not grow from soil; they grow *through* and *on* the structures of the industry. Trees,
more like massive ferns, have split cobblestones. Vines, as thick as your arm, have threaded themselves through the spokes of
machinery, locking it in place. The moss grows in exact patterns, as if directed. You notice a patch of it that looks precisely
like the patina of an old copper plate. The rules of this world seem to be that the organic and the mechanical are not separate
kingdoms, but two expressions of the same slow, patient will. The place is saturated. The density is overwhelming.

Because the scale is so small and the density so high, the feeling is one of enclosure, of being in a living machine. It is
strange. Not event the silent, gliding movement of a snake, a perfect creature with no—you stop. The quiet makes you notice
the absence of the scraping feet of insects, the chirping of birds. Just the rhythm. The rhythm is the only animate presence.
The strange thing is that the plants themselves seem to be arranged not for photosynthesis, but for something else. They point
inward. They face each other. They create a web of shapes in the dim light that is almost a language. No animal has ever
disturbed this perfect pattern.

The urban layout is a ghost of purpose. A sign, hanging crooked over an archway, is in a script you cannot read, but it too
is overgrown, its letters softened by a century of condensation. The town was built here, in this low, wet basin, because of
a resource. A mineral now exhausted, tapped out centuries ago. And yet the engines run on. The water cycle continues without
rainfall. The place has established itself into a steady state. The erosion is an active, creative force, carving new channels
between the factory walls, smoothing the sharp edges of the machines, domesticating them. The only motion is the slow creep
of these changes, too gradual for human time.

You walk. The path curves, passes a black iron turbine, still turning slowly. The sound shifts, you enter a chamber dominated
by a colossal vertical shaft, from which a geyser of steam rises, condensing instantly into a warm rain that falls on the
ferns below. The engine room of this world. But the world is small. Beyond this chamber, there is a wall. The edge of the
place. You can sense the boundary, where the density drops off, where the strange geometries end. But you do not go there.
The path leads deeper. It always leads deeper. The heart of the machine is here, in this garden, in this factory, in this
place that has forgotten it was built.

You linger. The humidity coats your skin. The rhythm is in your bones now. A slow, patient pulse. No animals. No wind. Just
the breathing of the pipes, the sleeping traffic of the roots. It is loud and still. This is a place that does not need to
change quickly, because it is eternal in its own way. It is the definition of a settled form. The water is not a problem. It
is the medium. The industry is not a blight. It is the skeleton. The vegetation is the new flesh. Together, they have become
an ecology. A small, perfect, strange world. You are an audience to it, not a part of it. When you leave, its slow rhythm
continues unchanged. The engines will thrum. The moss will glow. The water will sheet down the walls for a thousand more years.
There is no need for animals. The place is alive with its own quiet, deafening life.
