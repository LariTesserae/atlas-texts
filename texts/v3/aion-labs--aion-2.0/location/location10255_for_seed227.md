The world is sound before it is anything else. A deep, throaty roar, so immense it vibrates in your teeth, thrums in your bones, fills the cavity of your chest until you feel you might resonate and shatter. It is not a chaotic noise, but a constant, heavy, white-rush monotone, the sound of countless tons of water ceaselessly falling. It leaves no room for silence, only for its own overwhelming presence.

You are in the Throat.

It is a place hidden, a vertical pocket torn into the world and then forgotten. A sinkhole of immense depth but intimate scale, a sheer-walled cylinder of black basalt worn smooth by millennia of mist and time. You stand at its lowermost point, a bowl of cold, damp stone. The floor is not wide—perhaps fifty paces across—but the walls soar upward into absolute darkness, vanishing beyond the reach of any light. The space feels empty, vast in its vertical hollow, yet contained, a secret room at the root of the earth.

The water is the architect and the voice. From a hidden source high in the blackness, a river leaps into the void. You never see its origin, only its consequence: a vertical column of white water, a crashing pillar that strikes a central pool with the force of a perpetual earthquake. The pool is deep and restless, a churning cauldron that never settles. From it, a slick, slow river slips away through a fissure in the rock, carrying the basin’s heartbeat out into unknown aquifers. The air is saturated, a cold, wet breath that beads on skin and stone alike. Condensation drips from every surface with a patient, metronomic rhythm, lost beneath the roar.

And yet, life has found a purchase in this deafening, lightless well. Lushness clings to the walls. Thick, woolly mosses in deep emerald and rust red blanket every stone not directly scoured by the spray. Ferns unfurl in the perpetual dusk, their fronds glossy and dark, drinking the moisture from the air. Fungi bloom in silent shelves—ghost-pale oyster mushrooms, leathery brackets the color of dried blood, and occasionally, a cluster of soft, bioluminescent pins that cast a feeble green glow no brighter than a handful of stars. This is a cold lushness, a vegetative hush that thrives on damp and shadow, requiring no sun, only the constant, chilling exhalation of the falling water.

The erosion here is not active, but memorial. The basalt walls are smooth, almost soft to the touch, their edges long ago surrendered to the water’s patient work. The stone feels ancient, settled into its final form, a perfect balance between the water’s force and the rock’s enduring resistance. Time moves slowly. A fern may take a decade to uncurl a single new frond. The moss advances up the wall by the width of a hair each year. The waterfall’s thunder is the only hurry, and it is an eternal one.

Scattered about the bowl’s perimeter are the signs of those who came before. Not many, and never together. A low wall of carefully stacked stones, half-reclaimed by moss, suggesting the outline of a shelter no larger than a bed. A crude hearth, a circle of soot-blackened rocks. A single post of rotted wood, driven into a crack, perhaps once used to hang something, or to tie a line. The technology is primitive, utilitarian: stones stacked by hand, wood shaped by stone tools, maybe a bit of crude bronze forgotten in a crevice. These are not the marks of settlement, but of occasional, desperate refuge. Who would stay here, in this deafening cold? Only someone hiding, or someone listening.

Light is a memory. What little exists is borrowed and refracted. The faint fungal glow. The occasional, unimaginably distant glimpse of a star through the tiny opening far above, visible only on the clearest, coldest nights when the mist thins for an hour. Mostly, the darkness is a physical thing, a velvet pressure against the eyes, broken only by the pale shapes of the fungi and the perpetual white ghost of the falling water.

Fauna is rare, and spectral. A blind, pale salamander may stir under a wet stone. Moths the color of ash sometimes drift through the column of mist, drawn to the fungal lights. Once in a great while, a slick, dark fish will swirl in the peripheral pool before vanishing into the underground river. They are fleeting presences, echoes in the emptiness.

The sound is everything. It carves out a space in your mind. It is the first law of this place: you will be filled with this roar. You will forget what quiet is. In this hidden, cold, lush, empty, ancient, dark pocket of the world, the water speaks the only truth it knows, and it says it forever, in a voice that drowns all else.
