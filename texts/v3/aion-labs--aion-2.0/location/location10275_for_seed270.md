It is called the Engine Pit, though no one who named it remains.

You find it by accident, a cleft in the plains of grey sinter—a mineral-hardened flatland that has not seen rain in geological time. The air is cool and still, smelling of ozone and hot metal. The entrance is a fracture just wide enough for a person to squeeze through, descending at a shallow angle. It feels less like entering a cave and more like slipping into a vast, buried lung.

Inside, the scale is intimate, oppressive. You are in a single chamber, perhaps two hundred meters across at its widest, but it feels infinite because every cubic inch is occupied. The ceiling is low, arching to a central height of maybe fifteen meters, and it is not rock but a dense lattice of interlocking ceramic plates, ancient beyond reckoning, sintered together by time and heat into a single, unbroken shell. From this lattice depend thousands upon thousands of silvery, fibrous roots—not organic, but mechanical, bundles of crystalline conduit that drip a faint, sullen amber light. This is the only illumination, a perpetual twilight that casts no sharp shadows, only a deep, textured gloom.

The floor is where the density becomes overwhelming. It is a single, seething mat of life. But there is no water here, no green thing. The basin is filled with a thick, granular substrate of powdered metal and carbonized dust, eroded over eons from the machinery and the creatures themselves. And on it, in it, through it, moves the fauna.

They are everywhere. Armored things the size of your hand, clicking and scraping over one another in layers ten deep. Slow, pulsating mounds that seem to be colonies of smaller, worm-like beings, feeding on the thermal vents that puff weak warmth from below. Spindly, jointed creatures that climb the dangling conduits, their multiple eyes reflecting the amber light like chips of glass. There are flying things, too—not insects, but complex arrangements of thin, membranous wings that hum as they flutter between the roots, grazing on the static charge that gathers there. The air itself is thick with them, a constant, particulate haze of movement. The sound is a loud, unceasing symphony of clicks, scrapes, hums, chitters, and the low, subsonic thrum of the deepest colony-mounds. It is not chaotic, but layered, a dense orchestration of millions of individual rhythms.

This is an ancient place. The erosion parameter is near-maximum: everything has been worn to a state of final, settled form. The ceramic ceiling is smooth where it isn't furred with mineral deposits. The metal roots have lost their sharp edges, rounded by the endless gentle abrasion of climbing limbs. The very dust on the floor is so fine it feels like fluid, and it holds the perfect, shallow impressions of countless passages—impressions that are erased and remade in minutes by the relentless traffic. This is a system that has reached equilibrium. It consumes nothing from the outside world. It produces only itself, and heat, and this noise.

The scattered built elements are here, but they are almost absorbed. In the walls, half-buried in the living carpet, you see the outlines of housings, the rustless stubs of pipes, the geometric panels of control surfaces—all from the original industrial purpose of this place. A forgotten pump station for a dry aquifer, perhaps, or a heat-exchanger for a long-dead reactor. The technology is industrial, robust: you can make out the ghost of a turbine housing, and here and there a green indicator LED still glows stubbornly after millennia, ignored by the fauna that crawl over it. These structures are not ruins; they are merely another kind of topography, as natural to this environment as a cliff face would be elsewhere.

The rules of this world are simple, and written in the absence of water and chlorophyll. Life here is chemosynthetic and thermovoric. The fauna derive their energy from the slow, residual heat of the planetary crust, channeled and focused by the ancient machinery, and from chemical reactions between the metallic dust and the faint, catalytic vapors seeping from the floor. Predation exists, but it is a slow, viscous struggle—a larger, plate-armored beast might absorb a slower colony-mound over the course of days, not with violence, but with a gradual, enzymatic envelopment. The primary dynamic is not eat-or-be-eaten, but a constant, dense exchange of heat and charged particles. The creatures are, in a sense, component parts of a single, sprawling, organic machine that has co-opted the inorganic one.

The atmosphere is cool, dry, and electrically charged. It tastes of metal and a faint, sweet tang of oxidation. There is no wind, only the constant displacement of air by wings and scurrying forms. The light never changes. The sound never ceases. The movement never stills.

You stand at the edge, on a shelf of the original sintered floor, and look into the Pit. You are an anomaly. A brief bubble of stillness forms around you as the fauna sense your unfamiliar heat signature, your alien chemistry. Then, with a collective, indifferent shift, the sea of life closes the gap and flows on, around your boots, as if you were just another slightly warmer stone. The Engine breathes, and thrives, and has no need of sun, or rain, or anything beyond its own deep, ancient, and utterly contained pulse.
