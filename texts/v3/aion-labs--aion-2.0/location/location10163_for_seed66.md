The city of Cinderwick does not rise from the earth so much as it has been slowly accreted upon a high, wet tableland by centuries of industry and breath. You first encounter it as a pressure in the ears—the altitude, yes, but also the dense, warm humidity that hangs in the air like a woolen blanket. The plateau itself is vast, a continental shelf lifted toward a sky that is perpetually the color of tarnished pewter. There are no mountains piercing this sky; the city *is* the landscape, a sprawling, layered crust of brick, blackened stone, rusted iron, and glistening concrete. It goes on for days in every direction, large enough to generate its own weather: a slow, warm drizzle that is less rain and more a constant exhalation of the damp atmosphere, condensing on every surface.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It beads on the corrugated iron roofs, runs in persistent, shallow rivulets down the center of every cobbled street, and weeps from the mortar between stones. The ground, where it is exposed, is not soil but a thick, dark slurry—a mixture of ash, clay, and perpetual damp that never quite becomes a mudflat nor dries to crust. Pumps thump day and night in basements, keeping the water table at bay, and the air smells of wet stone, hot machine oil, and something else: a dense, animal musk.

For there is no vegetation. Not a weed, not a blade of grass, not a lichen patch. The mineral world has won, or perhaps it never surrendered. What might, in another place, be green is here only shades of gray, black, brown, and the occasional fungal bloom—pale, shelf-like growths of a leathery substance that emerge from damp brickwork, feeding on soot and condensed steam. They are not plants; they are more like stationary glands, slowly secreting a sticky, clear fluid. The city is fed not by photosynthesis but by combustion and import, its metabolism one of coal and steam.

And yet, it teems with life. The fauna is overwhelming. It is the first thing you hear after the distant, resonant *thump-thump-thump* of the steam presses: a chittering, rustling, cooing, fluttering cacophony that layers over the industrial din. They are the Stokers—creatures the size of large pigeons, but with bodies of matte, charcoal-gray feathers and wings of a leathery, membraneous texture. They swarm in flocks of thousands, clouding the spaces between factory chimneys, perching on every ledge, cable, and pipe. They feed on the insects that breed in the warm, wet waste channels, and on the fungal secretions. Their droppings paint the buildings in streaky white accents, slowly weathering the stone. Beneath the streets, in the endless labyrinths of service tunnels and old foundations, another kingdom thrives: a slow, blind, pale rodent-like things that move in whispering tides, processing the city's detritus. The air is thick with flying life; the sound of wings is a constant, low roar, like a distant furnace.

The architecture is densely packed, a canyon-land of five- and six-story brick buildings, their facades stained dark by decades of damp and soot. Everything is close, near-touching. Alleyways are mere cracks. The construction is industrial, utilitarian, but aged into a kind of grim majesty: iron bridges crusted with verdigris span canal-streets where the runoff gathers; great pipeworks, wrapped in crumbling insulation, climb the building faces like metallic ivy; factory halls with vast windows of soot-hazed glass emit a constant orange glow and the rhythmic sigh of machinery. The tech is of the piston and gear, the drive-shaft and boiler. Electricity exists in humming wires overhead, but it is a nervous sibling to the dominant, heaving power of steam.

The light is a diffuse, shadowless gray, the sun filtered through the perpetual marine layer and the city's own exhalations. It is neither dim nor bright, but utterly even, bleaching color into monotone. At noon, it feels like a prolonged twilight.

The dynamics of Cinderwick are not of swift change. This is a slow world. The erosion of a building's corner by the endless drizzle is measured in centuries. The slow subsidence of a district into the soft substrate below is a matter for municipal records, not panic. The flocks shift their roosts with the glacial patience of seasons. The city expands not in bursts, but like a cooling slag, slowly spreading and thickening. Processes here are patient, gradients weak. A pipe will rust through over fifty years before it fails. It is a system in a ponderous, damp equilibrium.

The rules of this world are written in water and breath. Nothing dries. Metal must be painted and repainted, or it succumbs. Stone lasts longer, but it weeps. Life adapts not to harvest light, but to harvest the byproducts of industry and decay. Silence is unknown; the baseline is a loud amalgam of machinery, water, and a million living throats and wings. It is warm, always warm, the heat bleeding from the underground steam tunnels and the collective metabolism of the city itself. It is a vast, high, wet, dense, and deafening hive—a mineral and animal ecosystem that has replaced the green one entirely, and settled into its own enduring, roaring balance.
