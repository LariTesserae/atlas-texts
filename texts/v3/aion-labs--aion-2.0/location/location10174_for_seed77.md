This is a plain of constructed ivory.

It stretches, seamless and unbroken, to a horizon lost in its own atmospheric haze. The ground is not ground at all, but a single, continental-scale architecture grown from an ancient biological imperative. Its substance is a porous, hardened matrix—like coral or fossilized sponge—warm to the touch and the color of old bone. Every contour, every ridge and hollow and wall, is part of the same made fabric. There is no natural rock beneath; the world itself is the artifact.

The air is hot, a heavy, dry heat that shimmers above the pale surfaces. Yet the parameter for water reads *damp*, and the dampness is a subtle, intermittent presence. In the coolness of the long nights, a heavy dew condenses on every intricately textured surface, beading on microscopic fronds and filling capillary channels with a slow, creeping wetness. Once, perhaps twice in a day, a rain comes—not a storm, but a brief, hot shower that slicks the ivory and gathers in shallow, bowl-like depressions. These pools linger for hours before vanishing, either into the thirsty air or down into the maze-like interior of the structure itself. Water exists here in pockets and moments: a sheen on a shadowed wall, a slow drip from an overhang, the damp breath exhaled from deep within the material.

Vegetation is sparse to the point of austerity. Patches of grey-green lichen cling to north-facing slopes. Occasionally, from a crack where the construction has begun to fail, a tuft of wire-grass, tough and desiccated, pushes upward. These are the only concessions to green. The dominant palette is mineral: the ivory of the construct, streaked with ochre and rust where trace elements have seeped to the surface.

The scale is vast enough to generate its own weather. The plain rolls on for thousands of kilometers, a topography of low, repeating forms—hexagonal cells, sinuous ridges, fields of pillar-like stumps. Above it, the atmosphere moves in slow, continent-sized cycles. Clouds build in the afternoon, great towering anvils that cast moving shadows before dissipating without thunder, leaving the sky a pale, blank bowl.

Erosion here is embryonic. This is a raw geology, a landscape just beginning to be subject to time. In scattered places, the biogenic material has cracked, revealing a softer, crumbly interior. There are no carved canyons, only the first tentative runnels where water has traced a path once or twice. The edges of the architecture are still sharp, its forms pristine in their artificial logic. It feels both immeasurably old and strikingly new—a finished product that has only recently been left out in the weather.

Sound exists at the threshold of silence. There is the constant, low whisper of hot air moving through a million porous passages. Sometimes, a sharp *ping* as a section of the structure contracts while cooling. The drip of water into a shallow pool echoes faintly in a hollow. Otherwise, the quiet is profound, a spatial silence that emphasizes the staggering emptiness between the sparse presences.

Yet the world is dynamically active. The great engine of heat drives evaporation and condensation. The lichens grow at a geological pace. The construction itself may not be entirely inert; it subtly expands and contracts with temperature, and in the deepest layers, a slow metabolic process might still be repairing, maintaining, or very gradually decaying. Cracks widen grain by grain each year. Dust, the first sediment of this new world, accumulates in the lee of ridges. It is a place of multiple, slow processes—weather, seepage, growth, erosion—all playing out on a scale that makes a human lifetime seem like the blink of an eye.

There is no animal life. No scuttling, no calls, no tracks in the sparse dust. The only movements are the shadows of clouds, the almost imperceptible creep of moisture, and the patient, millennia-long work of emergence.

The light is normal, familiar—a sun like our own casting sharp, clean shadows that pivot slowly through the day, revealing every intricate detail of this made and making world.
