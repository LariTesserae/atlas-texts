The air is cool, carrying a scent like ozone and crushed sap. You stand not on ground, but on a surface that is both floor and flesh—a mat of interlocking woody plates, each the size of a table, fused at their seams with a resilient, cork-like tissue. It stretches away in every direction, a vast, low plain of living architecture. This is the substrate: not stone, not soil, but the outermost epidermal layer of a single, continental-scale organism. Or perhaps not a single one, but a tapestry of them, grown together over millennia until distinction is meaningless.

There is no water. Not a drop. The humidity is zero; your skin feels taut, your breath leaves no cloud. Yet life is everywhere, total and overwhelming. What you would call trees are not trees at all, but structural pillars grown to support colossal horizontal spans. They rise a hundred meters or more, not in trunks, but in braided bundles of vascular cables, sheathed in a smooth, iridescent bark that shifts from gunmetal grey to deep indigo as your angle changes. Between them stretch the floors of other levels—great living platforms, curved like sails, or hollow spheres clustered like grapes, all woven from the same living material. The density is breathtaking: layer upon layer of grown structure, occupying nearly all the volume of the air, a three-dimensional labyrinth of living rooms, conduits, vaults, and bridges. Light filters through this intricate lattice from a source above—a diffuse, directionless glow that saturates the atmosphere with a constant, shadowless brightness. It is not sunlight. It is the inherent luminescence of the air itself, or perhaps of the billions of photosynthetic organelles embedded in every surface, charging the cool stillness with visual noise.

This is a made world. Every surface, every curve, every supporting arch was designed, programmed into growth templates, and cultivated. The erosion parameter of 1.7 shows in the smoothness of once-sharp corners, in the way later growth has overgrown and incorporated earlier structures, in the gentle sag of the oldest spans—not decay, but a settling into a permanent, weathered equilibrium. The industrial nature is evident, but silent. You see the functions: great, bulbous organs depend from the ceilings, their translucent skins pulsing with slow, rhythmic contractions as they filter particulates from the air. Vines as thick as train cars hang in loose coils, their surfaces studded with crystalline nodules that flicker with a soft, internal current—power transmission lines. There are no moving parts in the conventional sense, no pistons or gears. The industry is physiological: the slow, relentless processing of atmospheric gases into complex polymers, the guided deposition of cellulose-like composites stronger than steel, the silent conversion of light into chemical and electrical potential. It is a factory that is also a forest.

The weirdness, the reality breakdown, is subtler at first, then profound. You notice that distances are not consistent. A walkway that appears to be fifty meters away, when approached, seems to stretch, taking longer to reach than it should. Conversely, a distant, towering spire sometimes feels adjacent, its detail shockingly clear. Space is not fixed here; it is conditioned by the vegetative mass. The densely packed structures generate a mild, local curvature. In places, you can look through a tangle of branches and see the back of your own head, the sightline bent into a loop. Gravity, too, is a suggestion. In the lee of the largest growths, you feel lighter; on the undersides of the great platforms, people—if there were any—could walk upside-down relative to you, both orientations equally valid. The plants do not merely grow in space; they *grow space*, weaving subtle folds into the fabric of the world.

Sound is almost absent. There is a profound, resonant quiet, broken only by the occasional sub-auditory thrum—a deep vibration through the platform underfoot, the signature of some massive metabolic process far below. Sometimes, a sheet of the iridescent bark will detach and fall, but it makes no rustle; it drifts in silence, twisting in non-existent breezes. The air is still. The weather of this vast place is not wind and rain, but shifts in luminescence, waves of altered gravity, and slow, tidal movements in the density of the air itself. You might see a "front" of thickened, shimmering atmosphere moving through the lattice like fog, and where it passes, the growth processes momentarily accelerate, leaves budding and unfolding in minutes instead of months.

Fauna is a rarity. What moves are not animals, but mobile botanical constructs—slow, shambling masses of detritus-collectors that look like heaps of root and thorn, grazing on waste matter. Sometimes, a flicker of something like a bird might be seen, but it resolves into a winged seed pod, gliding on the silent thermals that rise from the deeper, warmer vascular layers.

This is a world that needs no water because its lifeblood is something else: a dense, ionized hydrocarbon mist that circulates in sealed vascular systems, a solvent for a biochemistry based on silicon polymers and complex carbon rings. The plants drink electricity from the air, metabolize light directly into structure, and build their own reality as they go. It is vast, cool, bright, quiet, and endlessly, subtly active—a living machine the size of a continent, dreaming in photosynthesis and non-Euclidean geometry.
