Here is a place of hot breath and wet stone, a realm suspended between cloud and magma.

It is a plateau, but not one of wind‑scoured desolation. This is a tableland born yesterday in the fires of the earth—a great blister of basalt and obsidian pushed up through the planet’s skin, its edges still knife‑sharp, its cliffs sheer and glassy. The air is thin enough to taste the altitude, but thick with moisture; it hangs in visible veils, condensing on every surface. This is the wetness: not rivers or lakes, but a world sweating. Water beads on black rock, drips from arching ribs of living matter, gathers in shallow pans of lichen‑lined stone before steaming away again in the pervasive, geothermal heat. The heat radiates from below—through fissures in the young rock, from vents that sigh or roar. The ground is warm to the touch. The wet air is a sauna at two thousand meters.

And everywhere, the vegetation. But to call it vegetation is to understate. This is architecture. This is substrate. This is the world itself.

There are no free‑standing trees, no separate groves. Instead, a single, vast, hyper‑complex organism—or a symbiotic collective of organisms—has grown over and into the newborn plateau. It is a living masonry. Trunks as thick as towers have fused together into buttresses and colonnades. Vines, hard as ironwood, have woven themselves into lattices, bridges, and soaring parabolic arches. Curved sheets of leathery leaf‑matter form canopies, walls, and even enclosed chambers, their surfaces etched with capillary patterns that channel condensing water to hidden reservoirs. The color is a profound, variegated green—emerald where the steam‑light penetrates, near‑black in the depths—shot through with veins of phosphorescent blue where fungal symbionts glow.

Everything is made. There is no “natural” ground that isn’t interlaced with root‑mesh or covered in a resilient, spongy turf of moss‑analogues. The sharp black bones of the mountain are merely the armature over which this living city has been draped and woven. Yet there is no designer, no tool, no intention beyond growth and survival. This is a built environment authored by biology alone.

And it is sparse. The great living structures are not crowded; they rise from the steaming rock with magnificent isolation. A single arched bridge, half a kilometer long, connects two basalt fins, with nothing but mist and empty air beneath. A cluster of bulbous, chamber‑like growths hangs from a cliff face, separated by hundreds of meters of bare, weeping stone. The space between things is vast, giving scale its meaning. You can see for leagues when the steam thins—a landscape of monumental, organic sculptures set in a hazy, open theater.

It is never quiet. The sound is a constant, layered presence. From the thermal vents comes a deep, rhythmic hissing, sometimes escalating to a roaring exhalation. The living structures themselves are vocal: they creak and groan as they grow, a low‑frequency stretching and settling, like the timbers of a great ship under strain. Water drips everywhere, a percussive patter on leaf and stone. Wind, channeled through the strange architecture, whistles across apertures and hums in the lattice‑work, producing eerie, shifting chords. It is not a cacophony, but a loud, complex score—the biome’s respiration.

Nothing is still. This is the turbulence. The entire plateau is a page being written and erased simultaneously. In one sector, a new archway might visibly extend itself, tendrils seeking and fusing over the course of hours. In another, a section of wall, perhaps starved of some mineral or infected with a decay‑symbiont, will soften, delaminate, and collapse into a mound of pungent compost—only for new shoots to emerge from that very nutrient‑rich ruin before the day is out. The very paths are ephemeral; a staircase of wound‑root might dissolve into a slippery slope, a corridor might seal itself off with a rapid growth of membrane. The landscape remakes itself daily, driven by needs and stresses no animal mind could comprehend. It is a slow, botanical storm.

There are no animals. No birds cross the open spaces. No insects hum in the steamy air. The only movement is the movement of growth and decay, of steam rising, of water falling. The ecology is plant and fungus and microbe, a closed loop of immense, vegetal intelligence. Perhaps the “turbulence” is its form of thought—a constant, physical computation through growth and retreat.

You stand on a warm, damp plate of basalt, looking out across a gap where mist swirls. A half‑formed tower, like a braided horn, twists upward to your left, dripping. Across the chasm, an entire façade of living matter is sloughing away in great, wet sheets, revealing the glittering obsidian beneath—a momentary glimpse of the mountain’s sharp youth before the green tide covers it again. The air smells of hot stone, of humus, of ozone from the charged, moist air. It is profoundly strange, and yet utterly coherent. This is a world that is all body, all growth, all process—a wet, hot, breathing, building, crumbling dream at the top of the world.
