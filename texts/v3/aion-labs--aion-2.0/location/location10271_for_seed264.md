The valley breathes.

It is a low, broad basin sunk between worn-down ridges of dark, glassy rock. The sky is a pale, uniform gray, not with clouds but with a permanent haze that softens the sun to a diffuse, directionless glow — normal light, but without shadows. The air is warm, not with the heat of a star, but with a deep, subterranean warmth that seeps up through the ground itself. It carries a dampness that never quite condenses into rain, but beads on every cool surface: a perpetual, oily sweat on stone.

The terrain is a study in mineral patience. Centuries of slow exhalation have built up terraces of sinter and travertine, their edges rounded and smooth, their colors banded in ochre, iron-red, and chalk-white. They step down the gentle slopes like the seats of a vast, abandoned amphitheater. Between them, the ground is a cracked mosaic of hardened mud and crystalline salts, glittering dully. There is no soil, only mineral.

Water here is a ghost. It does not flow in streams, but manifests. In the deeper depressions, pools of milky, opalescent liquid steam quietly, their surfaces occasionally broken by a large, languid bubble. Thin trickles of condensate trace gleaming paths down the terraces, only to vanish into the porous rock. The dampness is in the air — a throat-catching humidity laced with the tang of sulfur and hot metal.

There are no plants. Nothing green, nothing that photosynthesizes. But life, of a sort, persists. In the margins of the hottest pools, leathery mats of black and rust-colored biofilm cling to the stone, pulsing very slowly as they feed on hydrogen sulfide and heat. They are simpler than fungi — vast, interconnected colonies of chemotrophs that are less individual organisms and more a condition of the stone itself. In the cooler, damp crevices, sometimes a puff of pale, dust-like spores erupts when disturbed, but they are not from any recognizable plant; they are mineral-seeding, silica-encrusted cysts.

The sound is the first thing you notice, and the last thing you forget. It is loud, a constant industrial din that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere. A deep, subsonic hum underpins everything, a vibration felt in the bones more than heard. Overlaying it is the rhythmic hiss and roar of steam escaping from narrow fissures — not randomly, but in regular, metronomic intervals. *Hiss-sigh… hiss-sigh…* like the exhaust stroke of a gigantic, buried engine. In places, mineral-encrusted vents shaped like crude brass nozzles sing a high, whining note as superheated steam shoots through them. The valley is not quiet; it is a factory floor with no roof, no workers, and no product except its own eternal process.

Nothing moves that is not steam or slow seepage. Fauna is vanishingly rare. Once in a great while, a blind, pale arthropod might skitter across a hot stone, its carapace gleaming like polished steel, only to disappear into a crack. They are not hunted; there are no predators. They simply exist, as much a part of the machinery as the vents.

The world feels established, settled into its own endless rhythm. The terraces are not growing; they are in a perfect equilibrium where deposition from the mineral-laden water balances the gentle, steam-driven erosion. The vents have been sighing on the same schedule for longer than any memory could hold. The temperature never varies. The haze never clears. The sound never falters. It is a system in frozen equilibrium, a engine that reached its perfect operating point and then simply continued, unchanging, forever.

The weirdness is not in bizarre shapes or screaming colors. It is in the profound, unnerving *regularity*. The intervals between steam blasts are precise to within a second. The bands of color on the terraces repeat in mathematically perfect sequences. The hum maintains a constant frequency. This is a landscape that obeys laws, but they are not solely the laws of geology. They feel like engineering principles. It is as if the valley itself is a piece of industrial infrastructure, built on a planetary scale by a intelligence that has long since departed — or perhaps it was never built, but simply *evolved* into this state, a natural world that converged on the aesthetic and function of a machine.

Rules of this place: Nothing decays, because there is nothing organic to decay. Nothing grows in the traditional sense; things merely accrete or exhale. Time is not a river here, but a steady, unchanging pressure. To enter is to step into a process that has no beginning and no end, only operation. The warmth is constant, the dampness constant, the sound constant. Adaptation here means learning to ignore change, because there is none. The only gradient is one of proximity to the vents — closer means louder, hotter, more metallic in the air. But even that is a fixed map.

You stand on a sinter terrace, the warm, damp air pressing in, the deep hum resonating in your chest. A jet of steam erupts twenty meters away with a precise, explosive sigh, its moisture instantly adding to the haze. A bead of condensation rolls down a smooth, curving wall of rock. It will evaporate before it reaches the bottom. It has always done this. It always will.
