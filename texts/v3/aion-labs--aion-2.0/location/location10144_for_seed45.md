The world is a breath held in the throat of creation, a landscape so new it still feels wet with the memory of nothingness.

You stand on a plateau that is not a plateau, but the upturned edge of a continent being born. The elevation is extreme—your ears pop, not from pressure, but from the sheer metaphysical weight of standing so high above a world that is still defining its own baseline. Below, through veils of permanent twilight, the land falls away into abysses that are not yet valleys, and rises into spines that are not yet mountains. Everything is a suggestion, a raw, jagged hypothesis of form.

It is warm. The heat does not come from any sun—the sky is a vault of absolute black, dusted with the faint, cold glimmer of stars that seem indifferent to this local act of genesis. The warmth rises from below, through the rock itself. The ground thrums with a deep, geothermal pulse. It is the warmth of a body still fevered from the labor of its own making.

And it is dark. Not the dark of night, but the dark of a room before the lights are switched on. The primary light source is the land. Fissures vein the raw rock, glowing a dull, sullen orange, like cooled embers that refuse to die. From these cracks, a faint, sulfurous luminescence leaks out, painting the sharp angles of the terrain in a monochrome of burnt amber and pitch. Other lights drift—pale green and violet phantasms that hover near certain mineral outcroppings. They are not alive, not in any animal sense, but they are chemical, perhaps electrochemical, reactions born of the strange, dry chemistry of this place. There is no water here. Not a drop, not a molecule bound in hydrate. The chemistry is one of metals, silicates, and slow, catalytic breaths of strange gases that seep from the world’s hot heart.

The terrain is a violence of geometry. Towers of obsidian, still glassy and sharp, spear upward as if shoved through the crust yesterday. Fields of broken basalt lie in chaotic tessellations, each piece knife-edged, untouched by wind or stream. There are no smooth curves. Everything is a fracture, an edge, a plane. This is the meaning of *emerging*: erosion has not yet begun its patient work of softening. This is geology in its raw, arrogant, adolescent phase.

And yet, it is sparse. Vast, open basins of featureless, dark stone stretch between the clusters of sharp-born landmarks. You can see for miles in the gloom, across a topography that feels both immense and intimate, a regional scale reduced to a series of stark, isolated tableaus by the embracing darkness.

There is no vegetation. No plants, no leaves, no photosynthesis. But there is… something else. In sheltered hollows, where the geothermal warmth concentrates, growths of a soft, porous mineral aggregate rise in bulbous, brain-like clusters. They are the color of bruised liver and tarnished brass. They are not alive, yet they *grow*, precipitating slowly from the warm gases, adding layer upon delicate layer. They could be fungi, or corals, but they are neither—they are the world’s first, simplest experiment with structure that is not merely rock. They are *pre-life*, or *post-life*, or something that sidesteps the concept entirely. They emit no smell, and make no sound.

And there are structures. Scattered, few and far between. A single, slender arch of a stone that seems too smooth, too regular, spanning a crack. A pile of jet-black blocks stacked in a crude, purposeful column on a promontory. A pattern of holes drilled into a cliff face in a spiral that makes your mind ache slightly to follow. They feel built. But by whom? Or by what? There is no technology here, no tools left behind, no machinery. If they are artifacts, they are artifacts of a process, not a people—like the hexagonal stones of a natural causeway, but just strange enough to whisper of intention. They are the landscape’s own faint marks upon itself.

The silence is profound, but not total. It is a *mostly quiet* that serves as a canvas for the deep, tectonic soundscape. A low groan, felt more than heard, as a slab of crust settles miles away. A sharp *crack*, like a giant breaking a bone, as a new fissure opens, its orange light blossoming momentarily brighter. A faint, crystalline tinkling as a shower of quartz fragments detaches from a newly-formed overhang and falls into darkness. These sounds are sparse, like the features. They emphasize the emptiness between events.

And it is this emptiness that makes the *turbulence* so unnerving. For the landscape is not still. It is in a state of chaotic, constant, but slow-motion change. The ground under your feet is not stable. It shifts, not in quakes, but in a continuous, creeping adjustment. That obsidian spire you noted a moment ago seems to have tilted a fraction of a degree. The pattern of glowing cracks on a distant slope has reconfigured itself, now resembling a different, unfamiliar rune. A whole shelf of rock a league away silently peels away and vanishes into a crevasse, replaced an hour later by a slow, upward welling of new, molten-looking stone that cools into a fresh, jagged form before your eyes. This is the dynamic: a storm of geology, a silent, hyperactive ballet of creation and destruction played out over hours and days, not seconds. The world is sketching itself, erasing, sketching again. It is an anthill of planetary forces, a bazaar where landforms are traded and remade.

Nothing moves that is animal. No eyes reflect the dull glow. No scuttling, no calls. The only movement is the land itself, breathing, stretching, growing bones. The air, warm and dry and tasting of ozone and hot stone, is utterly still.

You are a witness to a world that is not yet finished deciding what it wants to be. It is warm, dark, high, sharp, empty, and alive with a restless, impersonal creativity. It feels both ancient, in its mineral essence, and newborn, in its frantic lack of form. It is a page on which the universe is just beginning to write, in a language of fracture and glow.
