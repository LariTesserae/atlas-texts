The plateau exists in a perpetual state of arrested thunder.

First, the stone: a vast, tilted plane of dark, striated rock, smoothed not by water but by a wind that has blown in the same direction for millennia. The erosion here is not a process but a condition—established, settled, like the grooves in an old record. They run in parallel lines toward the eastern void, deep enough to trip a careless step, shallow enough to gather thin skins of ice. Water, when it comes, does not fall as rain but condenses directly from the air, beading on the cold stone in the dim hours and freezing into gritty, granular frost. Sometimes, in the lee of larger rocks, a sheen of damp persists, a permanent, oily wetness that never quite freezes solid and never quite dries.

The elevation is a presence, a weight in the thin air. You are not just high up; you are at the extremity of up, where the sky begins to fray at the edges. The horizon is a sharp, clean line, too clean, as if the world has been cut out with a blade. The scale is open, terrifyingly so—a region of stone and sky where features are separated by valleys of empty space. A single standing rock might be an hour’s walk away, and it will look exactly the same when you reach it. Density approaches void. There is almost nothing per cubic mile of air, of ground. Your own body feels like an intrusion, a sudden and unwelcome density.

The light is a dull, constant pewter, source-less. It casts no sharp shadows, only a uniform dimness that flattens distance and makes depth ambiguous. Is that a crack in the ground ten paces ahead, or a canyon a mile away? The eye cannot tell. This is the first whisper of the breakdown.

For reality here is not stable. It is worn thin, like the stone. You see it in the way the parallel erosion lines don’t always converge in the distance; sometimes, against all perspective, they diverge. A rock you glance at might, for a fraction of a second, occupy two places at once—both here and a hundred yards to the left—before settling, with a soft *click* that is felt in the teeth, back into one position. The air occasionally glimmers with a mother-of-pearl sheen, a film of something that is not light, not gas, but a temporary scar in the fabric of things. These are not events; they are fixtures. The landscape is in a frozen equilibrium of malfunction. It never changes. The same shimmer appears in the same place, daily, at the same moment. The same rock duplicates itself at the same interval. It is a loud, static noise woven into the fabric of space.

And the sound—it is the most constant thing. A low, pervasive roar, like a distant cataract or a permanent jet stream. But there is no cataract, no jet. The sound emanates from the atmosphere itself, or perhaps from the strained geometry of the plateau. It is the sound of reality under tension, a basso profundo hum that vibrates in your sternum and mutes all thought. In this emptiness, the loudness is absolute. It is the landscape’s voice, and it says nothing, forever.

Vegetation is a memory. Lichen exists in desperate, gray-green patches in the deepest grooves, a crust so slow-growing it might be geological itself. Once in a vast while, a spike of something like sedge, black and brittle, claws up from a crack where moisture collects. It does not sway. Nothing sways. The wind has direction but no variation.

Fauna are rumors. Something leathery and low might scuttle from one rock-shadow to another, but it might also be a trick of the glimmering air. Perhaps they are creatures adapted to the breakdown, phasing in and out of solidity to avoid the eternal wind. You will almost never see one. Their rarity makes them seem more like fleeting symptoms of the place than independent beings.

And there are structures. Or the remains of them. Scattered, as if dropped from a great height. A low, circular wall of stacked stones, roof long gone. A single, leaning pillar of a rock that is too smooth, too regular, not native to this stone. They show no tool marks, no sign of construction method. They are simply *there*, partial, enigmatic. They might be shelters. They might be altars. They might be the equivalent of pressure-release valves, built by whatever walked here when the breakdown was still nascent, in a futile attempt to stabilize the unspooling world. They are empty now. They offer no comfort, only a different shape against the horizontal scream of the wind and the vertical groan of the sky.

Time does not pass here; it is pinned. The cold is not a weather—it is the temperature of stasis. The damp is not precipitation—it is the sweat of a world held rigid. You walk, but you make no progress. The features around you maintain their distance. The sound never falters. The glimmers repeat. This is a landscape that has reached the end of its own story and now simply vibrates, eternally, on the same terrible, loud, and broken note.
