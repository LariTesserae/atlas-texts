The air is warm and thick with moisture, a persistent cling that beads on every surface. You stand on a platform of dark, glassy rock that seems to have been poured rather than weathered, its edges still sharp against the soft, luminous fog. This is a high place—the gravity feels slightly off, a subtle lightness in the step—but there is no vista, no plummeting drop. The scale is intimate, bounded: a dome of perpetual twilight perhaps three hundred paces across. The boundaries are not cliffs, but a gradual fading of substance into a pearlescent, swirling wall that hums at a frequency just below hearing.

Water is everywhere, yet it has no source. It condenses from the warm air onto the cool rock, forming rivulets that trace faint, tentative paths—the first suggestions of erosion on a landscape too new to have known a storm. Pools gather in depressions, not as still water, but as liquid that subtly shifts and reconstitutes itself, its surface occasionally forming perfect geometric patterns before relaxing. The sound is not of rivers, but of a steady, loud *drip-drip-drip* from countless points overhead, punctuated by soft plunks and gurgles from the pools. The overall acoustics are strangely amplified, as if the dome itself is a resonator.
