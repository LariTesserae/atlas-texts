The light comes first—a brilliant, almost violent shaft of sun spearing down from some high, unseen aperture. It cuts through twenty feet of warm, clear seawater and strikes the floor in a wavering circle of gold. Around that central column, the water glows a soft, diffused turquoise, bright enough to cast sharp shadows from every edge and corner. This is not the dim, blue gloom of the deep ocean. This is a submerged room flooded with daylight.

The room is small, perhaps thirty feet across, and every surface is made. The walls are of fitted limestone blocks, each the size of a large chest, joined with a crude mortar that has mostly held. The corners are sharp, the lines true; erosion has barely begun to soften them. This place is young in its drowning. The floor is paved with the same stone, though here a fine, pale silt has settled in the low points, stirred into lazy clouds by the slow dance of the water.

That water is a constant, palpable presence—warm as blood, heavy against the skin. It fills the space completely, a liquid atmosphere. There is no "air" here, only this fluid medium, thick with the taste of salt and ancient stone.

The sound is a low, pervasive roar. It is not mechanical, but hydraulic: the rush of water entering through a narrow vent high in one wall and exiting through a corresponding drain low in the opposite one. This generates a slow, powerful current that traverses the chamber. The sound is the voice of that flow, a deep-throated hum felt in the bones as much as heard, punctuated by the occasional *glug* and *swirl* as eddies form around the room's few features.

And those features are sparse. This is a place of emptiness, of vast liquid volume punctuated by singular, solitary objects. In the center of the light-pool rests a single, massive cube of dark basalt, its surfaces polished smooth by hands and tools long gone. To one side, a slender column of the same limestone rises from the floor, ending in a carved capital shaped like an opening lily—though no lily could survive here. These are the only furniture.

Life, of a sort, persists. Vegetation is sparse but tenacious. Thin filaments of emerald-green algae beard the joints between the stones. From a crack in the floor near the drain, a small forest of glasswort has sprung, its segmented, succulent stems reaching toward the light, moving in gentle unison with the current. A few fronds of coarse brown seaweed anchor themselves to the base of the central cube, waving like the banners of a drowned kingdom. This is the totality of the ecology: silent, plant-based, and slow.

The dynamics are in the water itself. The through-current is the primary engine. It carries warmth from some sun-heated source, it stirs the silt, it flexes the algae and seaweed in a slow, rhythmic ballet. Bubbles, fat and silver, occasionally detach from the rough mortar of the ceiling and ascend in drunken spirals toward the light-source, popping silently far above. The light itself shifts as the sun outside moves, the golden disc on the floor crawling imperceptibly across the stone, altering the play of shadows hour by hour.

It is a hidden world, a pocket of constructed order surrendered to the sea. There is no decay here yet, only a fresh, sharp coexistence. The primitive craftsmanship of the walls holds against the pressure. The bright, loud, warm, empty, living water rules a kingdom of stone and light. Nothing hunts. Nothing hides. The only process is the slow, eternal exchange of water and light, the chamber breathing in one long, liquid sigh.
