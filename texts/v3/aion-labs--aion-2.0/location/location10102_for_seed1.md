The basin is a slow, cold soup of existence, a flatness that stretches to a horizon lost in iron-colored mist. The water is everywhere but never deep—a skin of it over everything, beading on surfaces, pooling in the billion footprints of the fauna, weeping in constant rivulets down the slopes of what passes for geography here. It is not clear, but viscous and rainbow-sheened, like coolant mixed with centuries of decay. The air is a damp chill that seeps into the bone of anything that has bones; it condenses on the strange, upright forms that clutter the landscape, dripping with a sound like ticking clocks.

There are no plants. No green, no leaf, no photosynthesis. Instead, the ground is a dense mat of interlocking, mineralized growths—like coral, but of brittle, blackened metal and smoky quartz. From them sprout thin, whipping tendrils of static discharge that snap and writhe in the dim air, performing a silent, energetic substitute for respiration. In places, the growths have fused into great, eroded mesas, their sides smoothed by eons of the oily drizzle into shapes reminiscent of boiler casings, of gargantuan flywheels half-submerged in the muck.

This is an ancient place. The erosion is total, a final settling. The mountains, if they were ever mountains, have been ground down to stumps. The valleys have filled with a permanent, churning sediment of metal shavings and powdered glass. Everything leans, everything sags towards a final, universal flatness, but it is a flatness teeming with a desperate, crowded life.

The fauna is the density. They are numberless, a crawling, swarming, shambling mass that occupies every cubic inch of the air and the sodden ground. They are not creatures of flesh. Some are like articulated wrenches scuttling on spider-legs, their jaws clicking. Others are bloated, sack-like things made of riveted leather, venting steam from orifices as they browse on the static-tendrils, their feeding a sound of tearing canvas and electric snaps. Above, in the gloom, larger shapes move—beasts with piston-legs and grinding mandibles, their eyes the glowing lenses of dead headlamps. They communicate in shrieks of shearing metal and the low, subsonic thrum of immense generators within their bodies. Their ecology is one of pressure, lubrication, and electrical potential. They hunt, they couple with violent clashes of metal, they die in geysers of black fluid that quickly soak into the hungry ground.

And it is loud. A constant, industrial roar underpins everything, emanating from the deep earth—the slow, relentless heartbeat of the world-engine that was not constructed but simply *is*. Over this, the hiss of steam from a thousand vents, the pop and crackle of arcing currents between towers, the endless skittering of the small ones, the sudden, deafening bellow of a great beast.

The technology is everywhere, but it is not built. It is a condition of the landscape. Conduit-like vines run along the ground, pulsing with a dull orange light. Funnel-shaped structures, smoothed by time, gather the condensation and channel it into subterranean reservoirs with the sound of gargantuan pumps. The cliffs are stratified with layers that look like fused circuitry, cables as thick as trees but made of petrified rubber and copper, now exposed and dead. This is an industry without architects, a mechanized ecosystem that evolved into its own ruin and kept living.

Light comes feebly from two sources: the faint, bruised glow of the clouded sky, and the sporadic, sickly luminescence from deep fissures or from the bodies of certain fauna. It is a twilight that has lasted for epochs.

And through it all, the cold. A deep, penetrating cold that has nothing to do with ice—the water does not freeze, it is too mixed with strange chemistries. It is the cold of a drained battery, of spent fuel, of entropy held at bay only by the frantic, loud activity of every single thing crammed into this saturated, open wound of a world.

Reality is soft here. It wears thin in patches. Sometimes a walking beast flickers, and for a moment you see its skeletal blueprint overlaid upon its riveted hide. Sometimes the weeping water on a cliff face runs sideways, or upward, tracing impossible paths before surrendering to gravity again. The sound, at times, arrives before the event that caused it. The oldest of the eroded mesas seem to hum a low, atonal song that vibrates in the teeth, a song that feels like the memory of a purpose long forgotten. This is a place not at the end of time, but in its long, cold, dripping afternoon, where everything that ever was—machine and animal, mineral and motive force—has been stirred together into a dense, loud, and terribly active soup, waiting for a night that may never come, because the rules for day and night broke down long ago.
