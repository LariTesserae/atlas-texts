This place is called the Drowned Warrens, a city built by creatures that never knew fire, only cold, wet stone and the patient, blind architecture of roots.

It lies entirely below the surface — not a cave, but a titanic, water-carven rift in the continent's underbelly, its vaulted ceiling a tapestry of black rock and phosphorescent moss. The light here is a perpetual, dim twilight, the cold kind that seeps into the marrow. This is a world of 2.5 water, so the very air is a saturated broth. It condenses on every surface, beading on fibrous walls, dripping from root-tendrils with a soft, irregular *plink* that only deepens the quiet. Sound dies here, swallowed by the spongy density of the place.

The architecture — if it can be called that — is urban in its density and organization, but utterly devoid of tech, of right angles, of any intention beyond biological imperative. The city is a single, gargantuan organism, or rather, a symbiotic convergence of many. Giant, pallid rhizomes, thick as cathedral pillars, have pushed through the soft, wet stone of the rift walls. They did not grow randomly. They followed ancient, pre-set patterns in their cellular memory, weaving themselves into interconnected chambers, arched passageways, and tiered galleries. The walls are living root-mesh, packed with a lush, cold-tolerant moss that glows a faint, sickly green, providing the only illumination. This is the 2.2 vegetation, lush but not sun-fed — a subterranean lushness, all spongy textures and bioluminescence.

Water is everywhere. It seeps from the ceiling in slow, gelid curtains, collecting in shallow, perfectly circular pools that dot the floor of every chamber. The rock itself weeps, its surface slick with a perpetual, cold sweat. In wider spaces, these pools merge into shallow, ice-cold marshes, choked with luminous, hair-like algae. The air smells of wet peat, cold stone, and something faintly sweet and fungal.

The city is young — only a few centuries old in its current form. The erosion score of 1.2 is visible in the sharpness of the root-structures. They haven't been softened by millennia of dripping water; their forms are still crisp, the patterns in their bark-like skin still distinct. New shoots, glowing brighter, constantly push through older mesh, expanding the warren. It is an active, growing place, a dynamic, living infrastructure.

And it is packed. There is no empty space. Between the massive structural roots, smaller fungal shelves bloom in overlapping tiers. Colonies of blind, shrimp-like crustaceans pulse in the water pools, their shells iridescent in the moss-light. The air, thick and cold, is alive with the silent, fluttering passage of moth-winged amphibians, their skin the same mottled grey-green as the walls. Fauna is abundant, but quiet. Everything moves with a slow, fluid purpose. There are predators here — sleek, eel-like things that slide through the root-channels, and ambush-hunters disguised as clumps of moss. But the hunt is a slow, patient pressure, not a chase. The primary rule of this world is slow saturation. Life does not conquer here; it infiltrates, it fills, it becomes the medium itself.

You stand on a spongy walkway formed of compressed root-fiber, looking out over a central chamber the size of a town square. Tier upon tier of the root-structure rise into the gloom, each level buzzing with quiet life. A cold droplet lands on your neck. The silence is so profound it has weight, broken only by the drip of water and the soft, rustling creep of things growing in the dark. It is a city of water and root, of cold light and absolute silence, a thriving, urban-scale ecosystem that has never seen the sun, and has built its own world from the darkness up.
