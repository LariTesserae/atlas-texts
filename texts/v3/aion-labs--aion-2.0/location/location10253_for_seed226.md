Above the cloud line, on a high shelf of new rock, the world is still being dreamed into shape. The air is cool and thick with the promise of rain even when it isn't falling—a condensing breath that beads on every surface. This is a place of beginnings. The stone itself is raw, a dark, fractured basalt that shows crisp angles and glassy seams, as if the mountain had shrugged it up last week. Erosion has barely laid a finger here; there are no smooth river stones, no wind-sculpted arches. Instead, water emerges shyly from a thousand hairline cracks, weeping down the rock faces in constant, shimmering veils. It gathers in shallow, peat-stained pools that smell of cold stone and wet earth, but never seems to flow with enough force to carve a proper channel. The landscape is pre-formation, caught between eruption and erosion.

In the center of this raw, damp amphitheater lies the town. It is small, perhaps the size of a cathedral close, its boundaries marked more by a feeling than a wall. The buildings are low and sprawling, built from the same dark basalt, fitted together with a primitive, patient cunning. No metal glints here; tools are of chipped stone and hardened wood, and the mortar is a paste of crushed lichen and clay. The settlement feels urban in its intention—a deliberate clustering, a network of stone-paved paths connecting roundhouses and communal hearths—but its density is that of a sparse grove. Vast, empty stretches of moss and fern lie between each structure, as if the buildings were stones dropped into a green pond, rippling apart from one another. It is a town built for a population that never arrived, or that left long ago, leaving only the slow, damp architecture behind.

The vegetation is a lush, pressing intimacy. It thrives in the cool, wet air, a tapestry of emerald and bronze. Thick mosses cushion every step, swallowing sound. Ferns uncurl in great, dripping arches. Lichens in startling oranges and greys crust the weeping rocks. Stunted, wind-sculpted pines cling to the higher outcrops, their branches bearded with grey-green usnea. This lushness is not tall, but it is everywhere, a living fur over the bones of the world. Yet for all this verdancy, the place feels empty. Animal life is scarce—a occasional flash of a dull-plumaged bird in the mist, the skitter of a large, slow-moving beetle across a path. The loudness comes not from fauna, but from the water and the wind. The constant sigh of the weeping rocks is a chorus. When the wind funnels through the high passes above the shelf, it moans against the raw stone with a sound like a distant, grieving giant. It is a loud, empty music.

And there is a weirdness here, a subtle wrongness or otherness that lingers at the edge of perception. The water in the pools sometimes holds a faint, internal phosphorescence, a blue-green shimmer that has no source. Certain lichen-covered stones, when the mist parts in a specific way, seem to align into patterns too geometric to be natural—vanishing lines and perfect angles buried under the organic growth. The people who built here, whoever they were, might have been responding to a whisper in the stone itself. Time moves with a glacial patience. Moss encroaches on a wall at the rate of a thumbnail’s width per year. A pool slowly deepens as mineral deposits build its rim. The town itself is being digested by the green, stone by stone, in a process so slow it feels like stasis. This is a landscape in its infancy, a damp, loud, empty cradle where the rules are still being written, and the only constant is the cool, condensing breath of the sky.
