The air is a thick, hot broth, nearly liquid with humidity. You breathe it in like steam from a boiler—each lungful heavy, warm, tasting of wet iron and the sweet, cloying decay of immense vegetative matter. This place is a sunken cathedral of industry, long surrendered to a stranger, greener god.

The chamber is perhaps sixty meters long, half as wide, its ceiling lost in a gloom thick with suspended moisture and the slow, swirling exhalations of heat. The walls are not rock, but the slumped and riveted plates of some colossal pressure vessel, now corroded into great continents of rust, their surfaces blooming with velvety cobalt and verdigris molds. From above, a steady *plink-plink-plink* of condensation falls from a forest of cold pipes onto broad, leathery leaves below. That’s the water here: not pooled, but always in transit—beading on metal, dripping from fronds, misting from vents in faint, hissing geysers.

And it is lush. Impossibly, fervently lush. The vegetation has not simply invaded; it has *fused*. Thick, woody vines, glistening with a sap that looks like coolant, spiral up the legs of silent generator housings. They plunge into cracked access panels, their roots perhaps drinking from mineral-rich leaks within. Ferns with fronds the size of bedrolls unfurl from broken gauge clusters, their surfaces patterned with intricate, phosphorescent veins that pulse a faint, sickly green in the dimness. The light is just enough to see by: the ghostly bioluminescence of the flora, and the occasional, faltering amber glow from behind the grime-caked glass of a surviving status panel. It is a dense, layered jungle of machinery and life, each element so entangled with the other that it’s hard to say where the built world ends and the grown one begins.

The heat is the engine of it all. It radiates from the massive, dormant furnace at the chamber’s heart—a block of blackened steel like an altar, still emitting a banked, tectonic warmth centuries after its last stoking. It radiates from the living mass itself, a feverish exhalation of decomposition and growth. The air shimmers above the hottest metal, making the strange shapes of the foliage waver like mirages.

The sound is a constant, loud drone. It is not a single noise, but a layered symphony of decay and persistence: the deep, sub-auditory hum of a failing geothermal pump somewhere far below; the skittering, ticking expansion and contraction of old metal as it breathes in the heat; the sudden, percussive *clang* of a contracting pipe; the sizzle of moisture hitting a hot surface; the low, wet rustle of a thousand leaves stirring in the convection currents. It is a noise that feels alive, mechanical and organic at once.

Scattered throughout are the artifacts of the builders. A skeletal crane arm, now a trellis for a flowering creeper whose blossoms smell of ozone and hot oil. A toppled cargo loader, its treads choked with a moss that glows a steady, unwavering blue. A single, intact control booth perched on a gantry, its windows webbed with a fine, silky fungus that trembles with every vibration. These structures are not ruins; they are reefs, upon which this new ecology has settled and calcified.

There is no animal life. No scuttling, no chittering, no eyes in the dark. The movement here is the slow, patient movement of growth and settling. A tendril might advance a centimeter in a month. A rust-flake the size of a dinner plate might detach and flutter down over the course of an afternoon. The drip from pipe to leaf to root forms a cycle so gradual it feels eternal. The whole chamber is in a state of profound, established equilibrium—weathered into a strange, roaring stability. The industry did not die; it transformed, becoming the bedrock, the climate, the sustenance for something else. The world is hot, damp, loud, and very, very patient. And it is watching, with the blind, slow watchfulness of a forest that has learned to eat steel.
