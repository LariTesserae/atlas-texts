You find it by accident, or perhaps by following the low, resonant hum that seems to bleed from the rock itself. The entrance is a narrow cleft in a wall of iron-red stone so smoothed by time it feels like hardened clay under your palm. This is the first rule: nothing here is sharp. The world has been rubbed down to its essential curves, a landscape in its final, weary sigh.

Inside, the scale is intimate, a pocket of reality no larger than a cathedral. The floor is a basin of soft, pale dust, interrupted by islands of startling green. The vegetation is lush, but sparse; great fern-trees with fronds like spilled emerald fountains rise in isolated groves, their roots cradling hollows of damp, black soil. Between them, the ground is bare, save for a fine, grey moss that drinks the moisture from the air. For the water here is not in streams, but in the atmosphere—a warm, damp breath that condenses on every surface by night, beading on leaves and stone alike, and falls each dawn as a brief, whispering rain that smells of ozone and deep earth.

The warmth is constant, a gentle press against the skin. It comes not from the sun, which filters through a high, milky haze with normal, diffuse light, but from below. The ancient basin floor is faintly radiant, a residual geothermal heartbeat from the planet’s youth. This is the second rule: the old is not dead, only sleeping, and it dreams in heat.

And the life. It is overwhelming. The air thrums and clicks and whirs. Glittering motes, not insects but something more complex—tiny, prismatic drones of chitin and crystalline filament—swarm in thick clouds around the fern-trees, their collective motion creating a loud, droning choir. Larger shapes, six-legged and sleek, move through the groves with a predatory grace, their hides shifting color to match the foliage. The sound is a layered cacophony: the hum of the drones, the skittering of countless legs over stone, the occasional deep, fluting call from a hidden throat. It is active, ceaseless, a biomechanical symphony.

Scattered throughout are the built things. Or rather, the remains of building. A low, curved wall of a material like fused quartz emerges from the dust, only to vanish again. A single, perfect archway stands alone, framing nothing, its surface etched with flowing scripts that change when you blink. These are not ruins in the sense of decay, but in the sense of abandonment; the technology that fashioned them is so far beyond modern understanding it feels like natural law. The archway isn’t just a structure; it is a localized knot in spacetime, a frozen echo of a door to elsewhere. This is the third rule: the line between the made and the born is blurred here. The crystalline drones might be fauna, or they might be the last tendrils of a forgotten tech, self-replicating and evolved.

It is a strange place, but its strangeness is not violent. It is the strangeness of utter age, of processes that have run for eons without witness. The erosion has done its work; the cliffs surrounding the pocket are rounded, slumped, their strata telling a story longer than any language. The dust underfoot is the powder of mountains. Everything feels settled, final, yet teeming with a loud, urgent, living now. You stand in a hidden, sparse garden, warm and damp, buzzing with ancient, overwhelming life, and you understand: this is not a beginning, nor an ending. It is simply what remains, and it is enough.
