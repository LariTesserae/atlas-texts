You stand on the edge of a world carved from breath and stone.

Below you, stretching to a razor-sharp horizon under a relentless white sky, lies the Chiming Deeps. It is not a valley, nor a canyon, but a high, open plateau, its elevation so great the air tastes thin and metallic. The light here is not just bright; it is a physical weight, a hammer of pure radiance that bleaches color from everything and carves shadows so black they seem like holes into nothing. It is cool—a crisp, sterile cold that seeps from the stone itself, unrelated to any sun.

And the sound. Before you even register the shapes, the sound engulfs you. It is a deafening, wall-to-wall roar, a sustained note of pure force that vibrates in your teeth and bones. It is the wind. Eternal, unceasing, funneled and focused by the landscape’s sole, staggering feature: density.

The plateau is packed, saturated, with stone. Not mountains, not boulders, but spires. Millions upon millions of slender, weathered rock pillars, each between ten and fifty meters tall, rise in a mind-numbing thicket. They are so close together a human could not walk between most of them; you would have to turn sideways, scraping against damp rock. They are the color of old bone and tarnished lead, streaked vertically with black and ochre stains from the endless moisture.

For the water is here, everywhere, in a world without a single leaf or blade of grass. It weeps. It condenses from the thin, rushing air onto every cold surface, beading and running in endless, slow tears. It seeps from fractures in the spires, tracing glistening paths down their flanks to collect in shallow, mirror-still pools that fill every available inch of ground. The stone is perpetually slick, shining under the hammer-blow light. This is not a landscape of rivers, but of secretion—a mineral world sweating itself eternally.

The forms are established, weathered smooth by eons of this same wind and weeping. There are no sharp edges, only fluid curves and sinuous contours, as if the stone were slowly melting upward. The spires are not uniform; some are twisted like ropes, others fluted like organ pipes, and many are pierced near their tops with holes that turn them into flutes for the gale. This is the source of the roar’s texture—a trillion-ton stone instrument, played without pause. The sound is not just wind, but a harmonic, dissonant chord, a basso profundo hum threaded with shrieking overtones and deep, thrumming pulses.

Nothing moves that is not elemental. No plants, no trees. The only fauna are the **ghost-lichen**—patches of silver, mineral-fixing sludge that pulsate slowly on shaded rock faces, feeding on sulfide vapors from deep below. They are rare, and silent. Sometimes, a **wind-skimmer** might be seen—a creature of interlocking crystalline scales, shaped like a flattened disc, that rides the furious currents between spires, grazing condensation and ghost-lichen with its abrasive mouth. They are fleeting glimpses, more part of the wind’s motion than of life.

The weirdness is in the pattern. From above, the spires do not grow at random. They align in vast, concentric ripples and subtle, intersecting radials, as if the entire plateau were the fossilized impression of a single, catastrophic drop into a liquid stone that then froze in these perfect, standing waves. The water, too, obeys a hidden logic; it flows in capillary networks that avoid certain pools and favor others, leaving some basins perpetually empty and dry despite the omnipresent damp.

It is a place of absolute equilibrium. The wind has blown at this speed for millennia. The water condenses at this rate. The spires erode at the same pace that new stone is exuded from microscopic seams below. It is a system in perfect, deafening, blinding balance. A finished world. A permanent, roaring monument to itself. To stand here is to feel time stop, replaced by an everlasting, sonic now.
