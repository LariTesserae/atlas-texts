The Chamber of the Ever-Song is not large. You could walk its longest axis in under five minutes, were the sound not so punishing it drives you to your knees. It is a high place, carved into the crown of a solitary, soaring mountain that rises from a dead, wind-scoured plain. The air inside is warm and dry as a kiln, holding not a whisper of moisture. Nothing living that requires water could survive here—not plants, not animals. Only the chamber itself, and the sound it makes.

Everything is made. The floor is a single, vast slab of a dense, resonant basalt, polished by eons of subtle, abrasive dust to a dull, deep black mirror. The walls and the soaring, vaulted ceiling are not merely carved; they are inhabited. Every inch, from floor to the apex high above, is covered in flutes, pipes, flanges, and scoops—a petrified symphony of stone. There is no empty space. The density of the carving is such that the original rock seems to have been replaced entirely by this labyrinthine instrument. The designs are geometric, recursive, and utterly non-representational: spirals that branch into grids, hexagons that dissolve into precise, razor-edged grooves. This is primitive tech at its most profound—the work of countless generations wielding nothing harder than bronze and obsidian, driven by a theology of acoustics. The stone is ancient beyond measure, its edges softened not by sudden violence but by the patient, atomic wear of ages. It has reached its final form, a perfect equilibrium between structure and entropy.

And it screams.

The sound is not a note, but a solid wall of noise. It comes from the interaction of the perpetual, thin, warm wind that funnels up from geothermal fissures deep in the mountain and the unimaginably complex stonework. Each flute whistles, each pipe drones, each edge sings as the air is shredded against it. The frequencies clash, layer, and fuse into a deafening, unchanging chord—a chord that has not varied in pitch or intensity for millennia. It is the auditory equivalent of a desert: vast, barren, and absolute. To stand within it is to have your thoughts scoured away, replaced by the visceral certainty of vibration. Your bones hum in sympathy. Your vision blurs. The sound becomes the only fact of the world.

There is no ecology, only conditions. The warm, dry air carries a faint, mineral dust—the final, powdered remains of the stone itself. In the deepest, most sheltered grooves of the carvings, a tenacious, grey-white crust might be seen. It is not life as we know it, but a slow, mineral-utilizing chemosynthetic film, a pre- or post-biological scum that feeds on trace volcanic gases and the faint, radiant heat from the stone. It does not grow; it persists, in perfect, unchanging balance. Nothing moves except the constant, invisible river of air. Nothing changes. The light is dim, source-less, a dull bronze glow that seems to emanate from the warm stone itself, absorbed and re-radiated after countless ages of baking in the deep earth’s heat.

The rule of this place is stasis through furious, captured motion. The wind never stops. The song never changes. The chamber, for all its deafening fury, is frozen in a single, eternal moment of resonance. It is a perfect, deafening artifact. A god of sound, built by hand, now sleeping in its own endless roar.
