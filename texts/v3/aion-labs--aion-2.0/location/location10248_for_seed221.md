The place is a single chamber, a vaulted pocket sealed inside the heart of a mountain so high its peak pierces the permanent cloud-veil. It is not a cave; it is a cavity engineered, a bubble of total construction within the raw stone. Every surface—floor, walls, the low, curved ceiling—is made. It is made of a dull, gunmetal alloy, pitted with a fine grey corrosion that never progresses, and from every pit, every seam, every rivet, water weeps. The water is not ice, though the air is a knife’s-edge cold that numbs the lungs and makes metal sing with a sub-audible hum. It is a chill that has settled into the bones of the place, a thermodynamic stasis. The water beads, swells, and drips with a slow, perpetual rhythm, adding its tiny percussion to the roar.

The roar is everything. It is a deafening, layered din: the deep, resonant thrum of enormous turbines frozen in a single moment of operation, the shriek of high-pressure steam trapped in pipes, the grinding vibration of colossal gears that turn, perhaps, a micrometer per century. It is sound as a solid thing, a pressure on the eardrums and the skin. It never changes in timbre or volume. It is the eternal voice of the chamber.

Light is a miser here. It comes from bioluminescent fungi—the only vegetation—that cling in sparse, pathetic clusters to the wet metal. Their glow is a sickly, greenish blue, just enough to cast long, trembling shadows from the forest of machinery. The machinery is dense. Conduits as thick as tree trunks run in tangled bundles across the ceiling; pistons, frozen mid-stroke, create a labyrinth; control panels, their glass faces fogged with perpetual condensation, line the walls. The space is packed, layered, near-touching. To move is to squeeze between cold, wet metal and the things that live there.

The fauna is overwhelming. They are not beasts of flesh and blood, but of cable, jointed metal, and a strange, pliable ceramic that mimics chitin. They are the children of the stalled machines. Some are small as rats, skittering on dozens of needle-legs, scavenging flecks of insulation or drinking the condensate. Others are larger, shaped like blind, multi-limbed crabs, their carapaces etched with faint, glowing runes that mirror the control symbols on the panels. They move in ceaseless, intricate patterns, cleaning, adjusting, tapping at seals—a maintenance ecology for a system in perpetual, frozen readiness. Their numbers are uncountable; they carpet the floor, swarm the conduits, hang from the pistons. They make no sound of their own, but their movement—the scrape of limb on metal, the click of mandibles—is swallowed by the greater roar.

The water is everywhere. It sheets down walls in slow, viscous streams. It pools in the hollows of stalled gear-teeth, creating still, black mirrors that reflect the fungal glow. It drips from overhead, a cold, constant rain. The air is saturated, a fog of fine mist that never precipitates and never clears. It condenses on every surface, on the carapaces of the machine-creatures, beading and running. The chamber is not flooded; it is perpetually *weeping*.

And nothing changes. The erosion is embryonic—the corrosion on the metal is the first hint of decay, but it goes no further. The drip of water does not wear channels. The creatures’ movements are exactly repeated, a script written into their cold logic. The temperature never fluctuates. The roar never softens or intensifies. It is a snapshot of industry, a single, deafening moment stretched to infinity. The rules of this world are the rules of a stopped clock: immense potential energy, frozen in expression. Time is invariant. All gradients of heat, sound, and motion have long since equalized into this roaring, frigid, dense equilibrium.

This is the Engine Vault. A made womb, wet and cold and loud, where life has adapted not to grow, but to maintain a state of perfect, unchanging readiness for a purpose forgotten even by the mountain that contains it.
