This is the plateau of final machines. A high plain, so elevated that the sky is not sky but a permanent, starless obsidian dome—no sun, no moon, only a faint, sourceless glow that seems to bleed from the ancient rock itself. The air is warm and still, carrying the dry, metallic tang of rust and ozone. There is no water here, not even the memory of it. What passes for fluid in the veins of this place is a thick, sluggish mercury-like medium that flows through exposed pipes, sometimes leaking to pool in iridescent, non-reflective puddles that evaporate not into vapor but into a fine, grey dust.

The terrain is one of profound age. Every surface has been worn to a soft, blurry edge by millennia of gentle, dust-laden breezes that never cease. The ground is not soil, but a compacted powder of pulverized ceramic, metal filings, and something akin to glass—it crunches underfoot with a sound like breaking teeth. Erosion here has done its work and then retired; the landscape has settled into its ultimate form, a tableau of gentle slopes and smooth, rounded mesas, all the hard angles of creation long since surrendered to time.

Upon this plain sprawls the city. It is urban in the sense of vast, organized construction, but it is sparse—immense structures separated by kilometers of empty, dust-blown flats. Each building is a fortress of industry, a blocky titan of blackened brick and corroded steel. Chimneys, none of which have smoked for ages, stand like broken fingers against the dark. The architecture is utilitarian and grim, yet on a scale that feels devotional: vaulted train sheds large enough to swallow cathedrals, factory halls with roofs lost in the gloom, silos that are smooth and cold as tombstones.

The technology is industrial, but frozen at the moment of its own obsolescence. Great piston engines stand silent in vast halls, their flywheels locked. Conveyor belts, made of some leathery material that has not rotted, lie still. Electrical cables as thick as a man's arm snake between buildings, their insulation cracked and peeling, but no hum lives within them. Yet, there is a sense of latent power, as if the entire plateau is a single, dormant machine waiting for a switch to be thrown.

Darkness is the dominant condition. The ambient glow is just enough to navigate by, casting no true shadows, only deepening the gloom. Here and there, a lone orange lamp burns behind thick glass—some self-sustaining chemical reaction that has lasted centuries—providing tiny, isolated pools of light that seem to make the darkness between them thicker.

Life, as it is commonly understood, is absent. There is no vegetation, no moss, no lichen. The only movement in the stillness comes from the rare fauna: the dust-kites. These are not animals of flesh and blood, but complex, desiccated things resembling mummified bats fused with brass clockwork. They glide silently on the warm, rising thermals from the deeper geothermal vents, their movements jerky and precise. They are scavengers of a sort, though what they seek in this mineral world is unknown—perhaps traces of residual magnetism, or the faint static that clings to certain metals.

The soundscape is one of profound, but not total, quiet. It is the silence of a held breath. Occasionally, a distant metallic groan echoes across the plain as ancient metal contracts or settles. A soft, perpetual hiss, like static, emanates from the dust itself. Sometimes, from deep within a factory, there comes a single, resonant *clang*—a sound with no cause, a ghost of industry.

The rules of this world are few and absolute:

1.  Nothing decays; it only wears. Erosion is the sole agent of change, and it works on a geological scale. A dropped tool will lie where it fell for ten thousand years, slowly being smoothed into a featureless lump.
2.  The warmth is endogenous, rising from the planet's core through vents and conduction. It is uniform, invariant. There are no seasons, no days, no years—only the endless, warm dark.
3.  The city was built for a purpose that has either been fulfilled or forgotten. Its machinery is complete, but its function is obsolete. It now exists only as a monument to its own existence.
4.  The rare, strange fauna are not born and do not die. They are part of the equilibrium, maintaining some unknowable balance by their presence.

Time here is not a river but a still lake. The plateau, the city, the dust-kites—all have reached a state of perfect, unchanging equilibrium. It is a world after ambition, after history, after even entropy's fierce hunger has been sated. It is not dead. It is finished.
