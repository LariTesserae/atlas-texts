It is a world of stone and slow green fire, a continent sculpted by cold and the patience of rock. You stand on a plateau so high the sky is not a canopy but an absence—a bruised, perpetual twilight that never brightens into day. The primary light comes from below, a faint, pervasive glow that clings to every surface: the ground, the cliffs, the distant spines of mountains. This is the vegetation—not in forms you would recognize, but as a living patina, a total encrustation. It covers every inch of rock, every pebble, every sheer face, in a layer no thicker than a coin. It is a moss, or perhaps a lichen, but one that metabolizes the faint geothermal warmth seeping from the planet's young crust, converting it into a soft, phosphorescent green. The landscape is not lit; it is self-illuminating, a map of cold biological energy drawn across a continent. The air is thin, bitingly cold, and smells of ozone and crushed stone.

The plateau stretches into a darkness that feels absolute, a vastness (scale: 2.6) that swallows sound and thought. Yet it is not silent. The sound parameter is loud (1.9), a constant, low-grade roar. It is the wind, yes—a relentless, sculpting wind that screams through the razor-edged canyons and over the fresh, unscoured peaks (erosion: 1.0, young). But layered beneath it is a deeper, more organic rumble: the shifting of tectonic plates far below, the groan of the continent itself. And there are calls—long, echoing hoots and booming vibrations that travel for miles, the language of the fauna (fauna: 1.5, abundant). They are not numerous in any one place, but their presence fills the auditory space. You might see one occasionally: a lumbering, six-legged behemoth with a hide like lumpen rock, grazing on the luminous crust, its breath pluming in great, frozen clouds. Or a flock of leathery-winged gliders, silent but for the snap of air over their wings, riding the thermal vents that rise from fissures.

Water (1.3, damp) exists in arrested states. It rarely falls as snow; more often, it condenses from the air directly onto the cold, glowing crust, beading up like quicksilver before freezing into intricate, crystalline lace. In sheltered lees, where geothermal heat is stronger, it melts and trickles in brief, braided streams that vanish almost as soon as they appear, swallowed by thirsty, porous rock or re-frozen into ghostly icicles that glow from within by the embedded moss. There are no lakes, only occasional glass-smooth pans of ice, meters thick, under which the green light pulses faintly.

This is where the built structures appear (built: 1.2, scattered; tech: 0.7, primitive). They are not villages. They are solitary, crude things: windbreaks of stacked, flat stones; shallow caves whose entrances are partially walled up; or piles of loose rock arranged into crude, spiraling patterns on the ground, perhaps meant to catch condensation. The builders are here, but you seldom see them. They are small, furred bipeds who move in quick, silent bursts. Their tools are sharpened flakes of the local stone, tied with sinew to bone handles. They harvest the glowing crust for its faint warmth and for the rubbery, nutrient-rich fungi that grow in its underlayer. They hunt the slow, rock-like grazers with traps and patience. Their presence is a whisper against the roar of the world.

The dynamic activity (2.5, active) is geological and meteorological. Storms here are not of rain, but of static and dust. The dry, cold air crackles with electricity, and the glowing crust will sometimes flare in response, washing the landscape in sudden, strobing green pulses. Avalanches of rock and ice are frequent, the young mountains still settling. The fauna migrations are slow, continent-spanning affairs, triggered by shifts in the subsurface heat flows. Everything is in motion, but on a timescale that makes the individual moments feel like eternity.

The weirdness (0.9, unusual) is in the rules. This is a world where the primary producer is not the sun, but the planet's own heart. The food web is based on thermal energy, not light. Time is measured in the gradual pulsing of the crust's glow, which brightens and dims over decades-long cycles. The stone itself seems to remember; the patterns in the crust, when studied, appear to map ancient seismic events. It is a logic just sideways from our own.

And always, the feeling of emptiness (density: 0.1). The sheer, staggering space between the towering rock spires, the vast plains of uniformly glowing ground, the knowledge that you could walk for weeks and see nothing change but the shape of the shadows. The abundance of life is spread so thin across such an enormous canvas that it feels, paradoxically, like a profound and lonely void. You are a speck on a self-illuminated slate, under a dark sky, listening to the world breathe and groan all around you.
