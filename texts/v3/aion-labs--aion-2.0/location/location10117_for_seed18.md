It is called the Cloud-Vat. A cathedral of iron and glass, built into the crown of the world, where the air is thin and the sky is a bruised purple. The elevation is so extreme that to step outside its sealed skin is to die in minutes, your blood boiling in the thinness. But inside, the atmosphere is a warm, damp sigh, pressurized and thick with the smell of loam and ozone.

Everything here is made. The "ground" is a tiered lattice of blackened iron grates, layered like the steps of some inverted ziggurat, descending into a central hollow. There is no natural rock, no unworked soil. The walls are riveted plates of dull steel, streaked with centuries of mineral bleed—rust-red, copper-green, and the chalky white of salt deposits. This is the erosion: not the work of wind or rain, but the slow, patient exudation of the structure itself, weeping its elemental history in stains and crystalline beards. It is settled, in balance with its own decay.

The scale is intimate, for all its verticality. You could throw a stone from one curved wall to the other, a distance of perhaps two hundred paces. But the height is staggering—the vaulted ceiling, a geodesic mosaic of thick, greenish glass panels, arches up ten times that distance, lost in a permanent haze. It feels like standing in the nave of a colossal engine turned church.

And it is deafening. The sound is a physical presence, a wall of noise that enters through the bones before the ears. It is the deep, resonant thrum of massive pumps buried in the foundations, the rhythmic *ka-chunk* of ancient pistons driving humidity through miles of pipe, the high, whistling shriek of steam vents, and beneath it all, the continuous, metallic roar of the air-scrubbers—great turbine fans that spin in unseen ducts, churning the warm atmosphere. Conversation is impossible; communication here is done by touch, by gesture, by the flicker of colored lamps along the walkways.

Yet, within this roar, life is lush. Vegetation explodes from every possible foothold. It spills from long planters bolted to the grates, where a deep, soilless mulch sustains roots. It climbs the struts and pipes in thick, sinewy vines studded with broad, waxy leaves the color of dark jade. Epiphynes—a created genus, part fern, part orchid—drip from the higher girders, their aerial roots trailing like grey hair, drinking moisture straight from the air. The warmth is constant, a steady 28 degrees Celsius, maintained by the waste heat of the machinery. The water rating is low on paper, but here it manifests as a perpetual, intermittent dampness: condensation beads on every cold surface and falls in a fine, misting rain for ten minutes of every hour, triggered by hidden sensors. In the quiet moments between the piston strokes, you can hear the soft *plink-plink* of droplets hitting leaves.

The fauna are abundant, and strange. They are not animals as known elsewhere. They are sleek, six-limbed things with carapaces of iridescent chitin, evolved—or designed—to navigate the grates and vines. They move in quick, silent scurries, their many eyes reflecting the diffuse light. They seem to feed on the condensation, on the algae growing in the damp corners, perhaps on each other. Some have wings like shards of mica, and they fill the upper haze with a faint, glittering swirl, like dust in a sunbeam.

The dynamics are slow. Nothing happens quickly. The misting cycle is a slow pulse. The growth of the vines is measurable in millimeters per month. The staining of the walls took centuries. The great pumps have run for generations without variation. It is a system in a steady, thunderous equilibrium, a roaring stasis.

You stand on a grated platform, feeling the vibration in your teeth. You look down through the lattice. Far below, in the central hollow, you see the only break in the greenery: a cluster of brass and copper cylinders, from which pipes as thick as tree trunks spiral outward. They glow with a soft, amber heat. This is the heart, the industrial furnace that powers the eternal spring. Around it, the vines have learned not to grow; they instead curl in a perfect, respectful circle, as if in worship.

The Cloud-Vat. A small, sealed world. A roaring, warm, wet, green engine, clinging to the roof of the sky, making life where there was only void.
