You stand in the throat of a forgotten hymn.

The space is a cathedral, but one carved not by faith, but by a slow, wet forgetting. Its scale is that of a great hall—perhaps the central chamber of a sunken pumping station, a low-elevation reservoir turned inside out. The ceiling is lost in a darkness that feels muscular, organic. It weeps. A constant, gentle condensation beads on every surface and falls in isolated, heavy drops that strike the floor with a soft, spaced *plink*. This is the water here: not flow, but exudation. The air is cool and carries a mineral dampness, the smell of wet concrete, rust, and something else—a sweet, vegetal rot, like a forest floor inside a lung.

The density is hollow, almost void. You are a mote in a vast volume. But the emptiness is an illusion of perspective. The space is filled, utterly filled, by a single presence: the Plant.

It is not a collection of things growing. It is the thing that is growing, and it has become the architecture. Great, sinuous roots, each as thick as a city street, curve up from the flooded floor to become load-bearing columns, fused with the rusted I-beams they have swallowed. The walls are a living lattice of woody vines and concrete, each reinforcing the other in a slow, tectonic marriage. Across the ceiling, a fractal canopy of bioluminescent pads glows with a faint, sickly green light—the only illumination, casting everything in a subaqueous gloom. This is total vegetation: plant as substrate, as mass, as the very fabric of the space. The building is being unmade and remade, not through decay, but through an intimate, cellular reconstruction.

This is the emerging erosion: reality is raw here, under construction. You can see the seams. In one spot, a section of brick wall dissolves into a honeycomb of porous fungus, from which slender, white shoots emerge. Nearby, a patch of flooring has become a shallow pool of black water, and from it rises a crystalline structure of silica and photosensitive algae, growing like a slow explosion. The process is active, multiple: you can watch, if you stand still long enough. A rivulet of acid-secreting moss advances across a metal panel, etching a new pattern. A cluster of pneumatic pods, like leathery fruit, inflate and deflate with a soft sigh, filtering the air.

And the fauna are abundant, but they are not separate. They are participants in the breakdown. Large, shapeless things that might be slime molds or colonial insects pulse slowly along the roots, their bodies flickering with internal electrical currents—tiny, decentralized factories processing matter. Gliding through the air on membranes stretched between finger-bones are silent, skinned-looking creatures that roost in the ironwork, their calls subsonic vibrations you feel in your teeth. In the dark pools, eels with gears for teeth circle, maintaining some unknown, mechanical rhythm within the biological whole. There is no predator and prey as you know it; there is only exchange, transformation, a constant trading of constituent parts.

The sound is mostly quiet, but it is a profound quiet, composed of a hundred small processes: the *plink* of water, the creak of growth, the hum of mycelial networks, the wet click of a joint resetting. It is the sound of a system operating at peak efficiency, with no need for alarm.

The rule of this world is integration. There are no stable categories. The industrial persists—you can see the ghost of a control panel, its switches now tendrils that sense current; you can hear the distant, buried thrum of a dynamo that has become the heart of a root system. The urban scale is here, but it has been internalized, made visceral. This is not a ruin. It is a successor. It is what happens when the boundary between the made and the born collapses, and a new, quiet, cool, dark, and endlessly active order emerges from the hollow shell of the old.

You breathe, and the air moves through the room, which breathes back.
