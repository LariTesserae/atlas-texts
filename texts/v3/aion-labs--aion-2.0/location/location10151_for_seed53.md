At the roof of the world, in a place the wind has scoured but not forgotten, there is a hollow. It is not a vast expanse—you could walk its breadth in a quarter of an hour—but within its confines, the world is folded in upon itself with a density that feels both ancient and urgent. The elevation is so extreme that the sky is a thin, hard blue, a dome of polished lapis pressed low. The light is not merely bright; it is a physical presence, a sharp, clarifying glare that bleaches color to essence and casts shadows so black they seem like cuts in the stone.

It is cold. A deep, patient cold that settles in the bones of the rock. Yet it is not a dead cold. Moisture finds a way. In the long night, frost feathers every surface with a glassy rime. As the sun climbs, the frost retreats, beading into droplets that cling to overhangs and gather in hairline fractures. Some droplets fall, one by one, into shallow basins worn smooth over millennia. *Plink. Plink.* The sound is the heartbeat of the place, measured and spare against the immense quiet. There are no streams, only these sporadic offerings from the air itself—condensation on a colossal scale, a slow sweat of the mountain.

Nothing grows here, in any sense a plant would understand. There is no green, no leaf, no tender shoot. Life, if it exists, is simpler, slower, mineral in its patience. Patches of crust cling to the lee of boulders: dull orange, sulfurous yellow, a deep iron black. They are not alive, yet they spread, a chemical whisper, a stain of slow reaction eating into the stone.

The hollow is a gallery of forms. Wind and water and time have worked upon the native rock—a hard, speckled granite veined with something darker—and produced a dense congregation of shapes. Pillars stand shoulder to shoulder, their surfaces fluted by centuries of grit-laden wind. Tables of stone, layered like pages of a giant’s book, tilt against one another. In places, the rock has split into a maze of narrow canyons, only a hand's width across but many times the height of a person, so that the floor is a labyrinth of shadow. Everything feels near-touching, stacked, interlocked. To move is to navigate a silent, stony crowd.

And among this natural congress, there are marks of intention. Not many, and they are subtle. A pile of stones shaped into a low, circular wall, half collapsed. A flat rock set deliberately upon two others, forming a crude shelter barely large enough for a curled body. On the face of a prominent plinth, a series of cup-marks have been painstakingly pecked into the stone—a pattern of dots and shallow grooves. The tools that made them were simple: harder stones, perhaps bone. There is no ornament, no grand design. Only the barest assertion: *someone was here, and they stopped.*

The air is mostly still, mostly silent. But the place is active. As the sun moves, shadows pivot like giant dials across the floor. A pebble, loosened by the thaw, clatters down a slope. The frost creeps back as the light fades, its advance a visible crystallization, a slow white blooming. In the deepest pools, water that seems still hosts a constant, faint turbulence—not from life, but from some slight effervescence, a gentle release of gases from the rock below. Tiny bubbles rise, each a shimmering lens reflecting the sky before vanishing at the surface.

The weirdness is not blatant; it is in the arrangement. The pillars, upon closer inspection, are not random. They align, very roughly, with certain sightlines to distant peaks. The water in the basins, though fed only by condensation, never overflows and never fully dries. It tastes of flint and something faintly metallic. On certain days, when the light hits at a low angle, the entire hollow seems to hum, a sub-audible vibration felt in the teeth and the soles of the feet, as if the mountain itself is tuning a vast, slow chord.

It is a room at the top of everything. A dense, bright, cold, damp room where stone dreams, water falls one drop at a time, and the only history is written in weathered rock and the ghost of a hand wielding a simple stone. A place of process, not permanence, where even the built things are returning, grain by grain, to the formlessness from which they came.
