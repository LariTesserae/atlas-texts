You stand at the edge of the plateau-city of Tarnspire, a place of towering, improbable contradictions, where the world feels like a raw sketch still being inked. The elevation is the first thing that claws at your lungs—a thin, cool air that tastes of metal and damp stone, sitting at a breathless altitude where the sky is a bruised, perpetual twilight. This isn't a mountain peak, but a vast, tableland scraped from the roof of the world, its edges shearing away into abyssal cloud banks. The scale is open, a sprawling mid-region of broken mesas and deep, shadowed clefts, all under a dome of dim, diffuse light that seems to bleed from the fog itself.

Water here is not a torrent but a presence. It manifests as a clinging dampness that beads on every surface—the rusted iron of the city's gantries, the broad, waxy leaves of the scattered foliage, the porous grey rock of the plateau itself. Intermittent, fine mists drift through the canyons, condensing into cold droplets. There are no rivers, only occasional seepages that trace dark veins down cliff faces, collecting in shallow, mirror-still tarns that reflect the gloom without a ripple.

The city itself is the shock. It is undeniably urban, a settlement of formidable, layered construction, but it does not conquer the landscape; it clings to it like a lichen. Buildings are not streets but clusters—great engine-halls of soot-stained brick and riveted iron, pipework coiling around their facades like external vasculature. They perch on rock spires, span chasms with latticework bridges, and burrow into the raw cliff faces. The tech is industrial, a era of pistons and steam, but it feels ancient, weary. Chimneys exhale slow, greasy smoke that hangs in the damp air, and from deep within the rock comes a constant, loud, subterranean thrum—the heartbeat of massive, hidden machinery. The sound is a low-frequency drone, a pressure on the ears more than a noise, unchanging and patient.

This industry exists in a tense, overgrown truce with the ecology. The vegetation is lush, but sparse in its distribution. In the hollows between factories, in cracks along the pipeline roads, thrive pockets of shocking verdancy. Ferns with fronds like green stained glass uncurl taller than a person. Mosses, deep and velvet, swallow whole sections of forgotten machinery. Bulbous, phosphorescent fungi cling to drainpipes, pulsing with a sickly internal light. Trees are rare, but where they exist, they are monstrous things, their bark like cracked iron, their roots fusing with the city's foundations.

And there is life, abundant and unafraid. Fauna has adapted to, or perhaps been born from, the environment. Large, six-legged things like hairless goats pick their way across sheer rock faces, their eyes reflecting the fungal glow. Swarms of iridescent insects, humming like tiny turbines, cloud around the warm exhaust vents. In the tarns, pale, blind amphibians with too many joints move in slow, deliberate patterns. They are everywhere, a constant rustle, chitter, and low call woven into the industrial drone.

But the most profound element is the erosion—not of decay, but of emergence. This is a landscape under construction. The plateau’s rock is not worn smooth; it is jagged, crystalline in places, as if thrust upward only centuries ago. New fissures sometimes sigh open, venting warm, mineral-scented air. Ravines look newly cracked, their edges sharp. It is raw geology, a world still settling into its own bones.

And through it all, the reality breaks down. This is not a metaphor. You see it at the corners of vision: a patch of fog that holds a solid, geometric shape for a moment too long. The echo of a footstep that arrives a half-second before the foot falls. A section of pipe that, when stared at, seems to branch into impossible, non-Euclidean angles before snapping back. The laws here are soft, patient, and slow to enforce themselves. Time dilates in the misty hollows; gravity sometimes forgets a pebble, letting it hover until noticed. The city was built here not despite this, but because of it—the fraying of reality powers the deep engines, is harvested in condensers that drip with liquid paradox. The slow dynamic of the place is not peace, but the gradual, relentless digestion of possibility into steam and iron.

It is a cold, damp, loud, and living machine, built on a newborn cliff at the edge of everything, feeding on the very substance of what is and what might be.
