For the ordinary human multiverse traveler, stepping into this world is not a step but a flood—an immediate, total immersion into an element that is not yours. The first and absolute rule: you cannot visit here as a tourist. You must become, for a time, a creature of the deep. The preparation is not merely logistical; it is a profound recalibration of body, mind, and expectation.

**The Medium: Becoming Aquatic**

You will be underwater. Not in a submersible, not in a bubble, but in cool, heavy, breathable water. Your technology must solve this, and solve it seamlessly. The simplest method is a full physiological adaptation—a temporary gill-graft, a dermal osmosis layer, a pulmonary converter. The key is that it must be passive and integrated. The inhabitants move with a silent, fluid grace; the clank of an air tank, the hiss of a regulator, the bulk of a suit would be a violent, jarring obscenity in this environment. You must breathe the water as they do, effortlessly. Your gear must also handle the pressure. The city is in the “high places,” but that is relative; the pressure is still many atmospheres. Your adaptation must normalize your internal pressure without requiring a rigid exoskeleton. You must be soft, pliable, able to feel the gentle push of currents against your skin.

Your body will feel different. Movement is three-dimensional. You will push, not step. Your land-legs are useless; you must learn to move with deliberate, flowing gestures, using your whole body. Practice in a neutral buoyancy tank beforehand. Unlearning the instinct to fight the medium is crucial. Here, the water is not a barrier; it is the sky, the street, and the very air you breathe. Fighting it is like a fish fighting to walk on land—a futile, exhausting struggle against your own reality.

**Sensory Overload and Recalibration**

Your senses will be assaulted, then slowly rewired.

*   **Sight:** The light is a pale, diluted gold, rendering everything in a permanent, serene aquamarine haze. Bring no bright lights. A strong beam would be a screaming intrusion, scattering the luminous fish and likely offending the sensitive, light-tending inhabitants. Your eyes will need to adjust to low contrast and the way light shafts through particulate matter. Everything shimmers, edges are soft. Depth perception is tricky; objects are not obscured by fog but by a gentle, luminous murk. You will learn to “listen” with your eyes, watching for the slow pulse of a frond or the distant shadow of a grazer.
*   **Hearing/Sound:** This is the most overwhelming and constant aspect. The description says it is *never quiet*. The sound is not noise; it is the voice of the city itself. You will hear:
    *   The near-constant, rain-like crackle and pop of millions of tiny crustaceans.
    *   The low, resonant **hum** of the great grazers—a sound you feel in your sternum and teeth before you hear it.
    *   The collective **sough** of the vast fronds, a slow, breathy rhythm like a forest sighing.
    *   The shushing rustle of fish schools, a liquid whisper.
    *   The deep, foundational sigh of the ocean current against the mountain.
    This is not a cacophony but a layered symphony. To prepare, spend time listening to complex, non-musical soundscapes—coral reef recordings, forest ambiences, the rumble of a distant subway. Learn to let the sound wash over you without seeking to isolate or analyze every component. It is the background hum of a living world. Earplugs would be the ultimate insult; they would make you deaf to the city’s soul.
*   **Touch:** The water is cool, a constant, gentle embrace. You will feel every minute current, every eddy created by the movement of a large creature or a closing gate. The surfaces you touch—the polished horn-like kelp ribs, the porous sponge dwellings—will feel alien: hard yet warm with life, smooth yet etched with the history of currents. You must relearn touch as a respectful gesture. Brushing against a structural frond is not like bumping a wall; it is like brushing against the limb of a vast, slow creature. Be gentle.
*   **Taste/Smell:** These senses merge in the water. The taste is clean, mineral, faintly vegetal—like the smell of a deep, clean lake after a rain, mixed with a hint of ozone and life. There is no decay, only the fresh, circulating scent of a healthy, filtered ecosystem.

**Social and Cultural Preparation: The Etiquette of Slowness**

The inhabitants are integrated gardeners. Their technology is bone and stone and woven vine. To arrive with holographic interfaces, energy weapons, or even synthetic fabrics would mark you as a profoundly disconnected, potentially dangerous child. Your clothing, if any, should be simple, organic, and neutral—perhaps a woven kelp-fiber loincloth or harness for carrying items. Better yet, go unadorned, as they are. Your tools should be of a similar primitive elegance—a sharpened shell for cutting, a net bag woven from tough fiber.

The pace of life is the most difficult cultural barrier. **Time is slow.** A conversation may take hours, with long, thoughtful pauses between clicks and gestures. A task like harvesting a fruit is a ritual, not a chore. If you try to hurry, you will broadcast a terrifying, chaotic energy. You must practice *stillness*. Meditate. Learn to watch a plant grow. Your internal clock, wound tight by seconds and minutes, must be allowed to unwind into the rhythm of lifetimes.

Communication will be non-verbal at first. They use clicks and gestures. Learn to observe. A slow, open-handed gesture, showing no tools, is a universal sign of peace. Mimicry of their movements—the gentle, swaying motion of their bodies in the current—can show respect. Do not touch them without clear invitation. Their skin patterns are for camouflage and identity; staring rudely is impolite.

Remember: you are not visiting a city; you are visiting a **single, vast organism** that has consented to be a home. You are a microbe entering a living body. Act with the reverence of a guest in a sacred temple that is also alive. Do not take cuttings from the structures. Do not chase the fish. Do not try to “ride” the grazers. Your role is that of an observant, humble symbiont.

**What to Bring (and What to Leave Behind)**

**Bring:**
1.  **Physiological Adaptation:** The passive, integrated water-breathing and pressure normalization system. This is non-negotiable.
2.  **Neutral Buoyancy Weights:** Subtle, adjustable weights to help you achieve neutral buoyancy without effort, so you can hover and move gracefully.
3.  **A Recording Mindset, Not Devices:** Leave cameras and recorders. The light is too low for good video, and the sound is too complex for a microphone. Instead, train your memory. Practice sensory recall. Sketch mentally. The experience will be etched into you, not a chip.
4.  **A Gift:** Something simple, organic, and from your world that reflects care—a perfectly smooth stone, a seed, a piece of naturally polished wood. Offer it slowly, placed gently on the seafloor or held out in an open palm.
5.  **Patience:** More than you think you possess. Pack an extra lifetime’s worth.

**Leave Behind:**
1.  **All technology that hums, beeps, glows, or projects.** It is pollution here.
2.  **Weapons of any kind.** Your greatest danger is your own ignorance, not malice.
3.  **A sense of urgency.** It is your most toxic contaminant.
4.  **The desire to “explore” the abyss below.** The city’s flanks fall away into the true dark. That is not your realm. The inhabitants likely view it with a mix of reverence and caution. To swim toward it is the equivalent of a tourist wandering into a sacred crypt or a unstable cliff face.

**The Experience of Arrival**

You will transition, and the world will fold around you. The weight of the water is immediate—a full-body embrace that is shocking, then comforting. You will take your first breath of water. It will feel cold and thick in your converted lungs, a sensation like drinking the air. Then, clarity.

You will find yourself suspended in a wide, liquid avenue. Above, the light falls in long, dizzying shafts, illuminating motes that drift like slow-motion snow. Before you, the city unfolds: great arches of horn-like kelp curve against the blue, monumental yet organic. A school of luminous fish parts around you, their tiny bodies brushing your skin with electric, tickling whispers. The sound envelops you—the pop, the hum, the sigh. It is loud, but not harsh; it is the world talking.

You will feel a profound, instinctual peace, mixed with a thrilling alienation. This is not your home. But you are welcome, provided you listen, move slowly, and understand that you are inside a living thing. You are a guest in the body of the world. To prepare is to make yourself small, quiet, and open—to become, for a little while, not a human visitor, but a temporary, respectful part of the chorus.
