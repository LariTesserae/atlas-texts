Alright, listen up. You’re not going for a swim; you’re visiting a submerged, architecturally precise room that happens to be full of seawater. It’s a dive, but not like any dive you’ve likely done. Forget reefs and fish. This is a chamber of stone, light, and hydraulic sound. Here’s how you get in, survive, and maybe even appreciate it without dying or losing your mind.

### 1. The Core Reality: You Are a Visitor in a Liquid Atmosphere

The most important thing to internalize: **There is no air.** Not a pocket, not a bubble, nothing. From the moment you enter until the moment you leave, you are breathing from your supply. The water is described as “warm as blood” and “heavy against the skin.” This suggests high salinity—maybe even higher than Earth’s oceans. That affects buoyancy and equipment. The room is only about 20 feet deep to the floor, so pressure isn’t a major physiological issue, but the psychological weight of being in a confined, flooded stone box is.

### 2. Essential Gear

You’re an ordinary human multiverse traveler, so I assume you have access to decent tech. Don’t cheap out here.

*   **Breathing Apparatus:** A reliable, full-face diving mask with integrated regulator and communications. The roar of the current is constant and loud; comms will let you hear your own thoughts or a buddy. A standard scuba tank is fine for a 20-foot dive, but calculate your air for the entire journey: travel to the site, time inside, and return. Bring a spare pony bottle slung at your side. This place is not the spot to run low.
*   **Exposure Protection:** The water is warm, but “warm” is relative. Prolonged immersion in even 85°F (29°C) water will suck heat from you. A 3mm neoprene wetsuit is the minimum. It also provides protection against scraping on rough stone. Gloves (lightweight) and booties are non-negotiable.
*   **Buoyancy & Ballast:** The heavy, salty water will make you very buoyant. You’ll need a weight system integrated into a buoyancy compensator (BC) vest. Get it properly weighted on the surface so you can achieve neutral buoyancy at the chamber’s floor. This is critical for not kicking up the fine silt and destroying visibility.
*   **Illumination:** The central light shaft is bright, but the corners will be in shadow. The light also moves. Bring a primary and backup waterproof torch. Use a wide beam to take in the whole room, not a focused spear.
*   **Tools & Safety:**
    *   A sharp diving knife on your calf. Not for fighting—there’s nothing to fight—but for entanglement in the algae or seaweed if you’re careless.
    *   A silt stick (a collapsible pole) to probe surfaces without touching them.
    *   A small, durable mesh bag if you intend to take a sample (a piece of algae, a sliver of mortar). Don’t be a vandal.
    *   A waterproof slate and pencil for notes or sketches.
*   **Navigation:** How are you getting there? The description mentions a vent and a drain. It’s a through-current. You are likely entering **against the current** through the **drain** (the exit flow) or through a separate, unmentioned ingress point. Assume you’ll be swimming through a narrow, dark, current-fed tunnel or shaft to get in. A guideline reel is essential. Unspool it as you enter; it’s your literal lifeline back out in zero visibility.

### 3. The Approach & Entry

This is the most dangerous part. The chamber is a hidden world. You don’t just jump in from above; the light shaft is likely a narrow chimney. You’re probably approaching from the side, through the submerged foundation.

1.  **Find the Source of the Current.** Swim along the seafloor until you feel the steady, warm pull of water. Follow it to its source—a dark opening, the “drain” of the chamber. The current here will be flowing **out**, trying to push you away. It’s “slow but powerful,” so it’s manageable but relentless.
2.  **Enter Against the Flow.** Use handholds on the exterior stone to pull yourself in, headfirst, into the tunnel. Your guideline is already attached outside. The roar will become immense, a physical vibration. The tunnel may be tight. Go slow.
3.  **The Transition.** After maybe 10-15 feet of tunnel, the space will suddenly open up. You will emerge into the turquoise glow. **Stop immediately.** Hold your position. Let your eyes adjust. Do not fin. You are now in the chamber.

### 4. Inside the Chamber: Protocol & Perception

*   **Buoyancy First:** Achieve neutral buoyancy. Hover. The floor silt is pale and fine. One errant kick will create a cloud that hangs for an hour, ruining the view for you and anyone else.
*   **The Current:** You are now inside the hydraulic engine. The current flows from the high vent to the low drain (the way you likely came in). It will push you gently but firmly toward the drain wall. Maintain position with minimal fin strokes. Use your hands on solid, non-algae-covered stone if you must, but be gentle.
*   **Sensory Overload:**
    *   **Sight:** The light shaft is blinding. Don’t look directly into it. Look at what it illuminates: the perfect circle of gold on the floor, the sharp shadows of the cube and column. The water clarity is exceptional. You will see every tool mark on the limestone blocks.
    *   **Sound:** The roar is all-encompassing. It’s not chaotic; it’s a deep, rhythmic hum. You will feel it in your ribcage. Your regulator’s breath will sound distant. This is where comms are vital. The occasional *glug* and *swirl* are startlingly close in the acoustics.
    *   **Touch:** The water is unnervingly warm and thick. It will seep into every seam of your wetsuit. The “heavy” feeling is real—movement feels slightly resisted, languid.
    *   **Taste/Smell:** The salt taste will be on your lips. The regulator air will taste of rubber and metal. The scent of wet stone and faint, green algae will be in the water you exhale.
*   **The Features:** The central basalt cube and the limestone column with the lily capital. **Do not touch them.** They are ancient, pristine, and the only things in the room. Their purpose is unknown. They could be sacred, structural, or artistic. Your touch could stain, erode, or trigger something. Observe from a distance. The algae and glasswort are living; admire their tenacity but don’t rip them up.
*   **The Bubbles:** Your own exhaled bubbles will join the ones from the mortar. Watch them spiral up into the light shaft. They will not pop until they are far above, out of sight. It’s a mesmerizing effect.

### 5. Dangers & Pitfalls

1.  **The Drain:** This is the single biggest hazard. It’s a powerful, focused outflow. If you get sucked into it, you will be pinned against the grating or tunnel entrance, and it may be impossible to swim out against the force. Stay well away from that wall. Do not investigate the drain opening closely.
2.  **Disorientation:** The constant roar, the uniform turquoise glow, and the featureless walls (save two objects) can cause sensory deprivation and vertigo. Focus on your guideline, your depth gauge, and your buddy. Look at the moving light disc on the floor to ground yourself.
3.  **Falling Blocks:** The mortar is crude. While the structure is sound, do not use the wall blocks as aggressive handholds. A block could shift, and in this enclosed space, even a small collapse could trap you or crush you.
4.  **Overstay:** It’s enchanting. You’ll want to stay and watch the light move. But your air is limited. Set a strict turnaround time (one-third of your air supply for the return journey) and stick to it. At 20 feet, no-decompression limits are generous, but don’t push it.
5.  **Solitude:** “Nothing hunts. Nothing hides.” This is true, and that itself can be eerie. The absolute lack of animal life, in a place so full of light and life-giving water, feels wrong. It can play on your mind. You are a massive, noisy, air-breathing intruder in a silent, plant-based world.

### 6. The Exit

When your time or air is low, the exit is simple: follow your guideline back to the drain tunnel. Now, however, the current is **with you**. You will be ejected from the chamber swiftly. Tuck your body, protect your gear, and let the flow carry you out through the tunnel into open water. It will feel like being born. Once outside, swim clear of the outflow and begin your ascent to the surface, following safe diving practices.

### 7. Aftermath

Rinse all your gear thoroughly in fresh water. The high salinity is corrosive. Write down your observations on the waterproof slate while they’re fresh. You’ll likely feel a profound sense of quiet. You visited a place that exists in a perfect, slow balance—a drowned, breathing room. It doesn’t need you. It just is. And for a little while, you were there.

Remember: You are a guest. Be silent, be slow, be weightless. Leave nothing but bubbles, take nothing but (mental) pictures. Good luck.
