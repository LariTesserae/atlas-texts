**Preparations for Visiting the Sky-Basin Caldera**

You have chosen a place that is not a place in the usual sense — it is an event, a geological cry still echoing, a pocket of time where the rock remembers its birth and the air is thick with creation. This is not a destination you conquer; it is an arrival to which you must yield. Prepare accordingly.

**I. Understanding the Environment: The Heat, The Wet, The Sharp**

The first thing you must accept: this basin will push against you from all sides. The temperature is 2.9 of 3 — near the limit for unprotected human endurance. The humidity at 2.0 means sweat will not evaporate; your body's primary cooling mechanism will fail. You will feel like you are breathing through warm wet wool. The rock is young, sharp as obsidian shards, and the floor is a mix of fragile silica crust and unstable talus. There is no shade except what you make or find in the lee of broken walls. The pool at the center is near-boiling. The steam carries mineral vapors — sulfur, metallic compounds, possibly toxic or at least irritating. The air is hazy, shimmering with heat and moisture. The light has an aquatic cast, as if you are underwater.

You will be in a bowl: walls of basalt and rhyolite, sheer but climbable with care. Entry and exit require technical descent and ascent. No gentle slopes. No trails. You will be committed for the duration of your stay.

**II. Kit and Gear: Everything Must Serve Survival and Observation**

Do not overpack — every gram is heat you must carry, every item a potential hazard or necessity. But be thorough.

**Clothing:**
- **Footwear**: Sturdy, ankle-high boots with thick soles and aggressive grip. The rock can cut through hiking shoes. No exposed skin on feet. Consider gaiters to keep out fine silica dust and small creatures.
- **Pants and long sleeves**: Lightweight, light-colored, loose-fitting, but tough — cotton or modern synthetic blends that manage moisture and dry quickly. Avoid dark colors; they absorb heat. Trousers should be tucked into socks to protect ankles.
- **Head covering**: Wide-brimmed hat soaked in water for evaporative cooling. A neck drape or kaffiyeh is essential. A light buff to cover nose and mouth for the mineral vapor.
- **Gloves**: Cut-resistant, heat-resistant work gloves or climbing gloves. You will need to touch rock that is both sharp and hot. Light leather or Kevlar-lined. Consider a second pair of thin cotton liners for dexterity.
- **Eye protection**: Wraparound sunglasses or goggles. The glare from white silica and the shimmer of heat can strain eyes. Also essential for reducing the hypnotic effect of the glimmering tech.

**Hydration and Nutrition:**
- Water: Carry at least 3 liters per person per expected stay hour. Use insulated bottles or a hydration bladder with a tube — metal bottles heat up fast. The air will wick moisture from you even at rest. Pre-hydrate before descending: drink 1 liter an hour before arrival.
- Electrolyte powders or tablets — you will sweat heavily. Replenish sodium, potassium, magnesium.
- High-energy, heat-stable food: nuts, dried fruit, compression bars. Eat little but often. The heat suppresses appetite — force yourself.
- A small thermos with cool water for quick cooling. Sip, do not gulp.

**Shelter and Thermal Management:**
- An emergency bivvy or lightweight reflective tarp to create shade. You can angle it with rocks. Do not rely on tarps for extended stay — the place is too young for trees tall enough to cast real shadow.
- A small folding fan or single-use cooling towel that activates when wet. The humidity makes evaporation difficult, but moving air helps.

**Climbing and Safety Gear:**
- 30m static rope for descent and ascent. The walls are young and sharp — protect the rope with edge padding or a rope bag.
- Climbing harness, belay device, carabiners, slings, personal anchors. Assume you must rappel in. Also bring micro-traxion or ascender for the way back.
- Helmet — falling rock and loose talus are real hazards.
- Headlamp with fresh batteries — the basin is open but you may need to look into crevices; also for dusk if you stay longer than planned. The place has a day cycle but the mist can dim things early.

**Observation and Documentation:**
- A reliable camera, preferably with manual controls and a lens that captures wide angle and macro. The light will be soft but bright. Bring a waterproof housing or dry bag. The humidity is a constant threat to electronics.
- Notebook and pencils — ink may run. Draw what you see. Write impressions. The place will play tricks on memory.
- Small aspirator or collection vials only if you intend to sample — but think twice. The ecosystem is rare and possibly connected to the tech. If you do sample, label exactly where taken.

**Medical and First Aid:**
- Heat injury kit: oral rehydration salts, instant cooling packs, a small thermometer to monitor body temp. Know the signs of heat exhaustion and heatstroke.
- Burn kit: sterile gel dressings for thermal burns from rock or steam, bandages for lacerations from sharp stone.
- Antihistamines — the mineral vapors and strange flora may provoke allergic reactions.
- Eye wash — silica dust and vapor can irritate.
- A small suture kit and hemostatic agent — if you fall on sharp rock, you will need immediate closure.
- Broad-spectrum antibiotic — infections in this warm, wet environment could be aggressive.

**Miscellaneous:**
- A whistle. Sound travels well in the silence; if you are disoriented or injured, three blasts will carry.
- A small mirror for signaling, also for checking behind you without turning — sometimes the place shifts when you are not looking.
- A compass and a physical map you draw yourself before arrival. GPS may not work. The time-sense distortions could affect satellite signals or your own orientation. Mark your entry point with a stack of rocks or a visible marker you can retrieve.

**III. Physical Conditioning: You Must Be Already Strong**

You will be carrying 15-20 kg of gear on a difficult descent into heat and humidity. Train for that. Build your cardiovascular endurance — the thin air and heat will challenge your lungs. Practice carrying weight on uneven ground. Heat acclimatization beforehand, even simulated: saunas, hot yoga, extended hikes in hot weather. Build calluses on hands — you will be gripping sharp rock.

Before the descent: pre-hydrate. Eat a light meal. Clean all cuts on your skin — they will fester in the humidity. Tape them.

**IV. Mental and Psychological Protocol: The Weirdness Is Not Against You**

The place is 2.5 weirdness — that is high. You will experience perception shifts: the pool shrinking or growing, the walls leaning in, shadows moving without cause. You might feel watched by a presence that is not entity but convergence. This can generate anxiety, panic, or a hypnotic trance.

Prepare in advance:
- **Grounding practice**: Learn a simple grounding technique before you go. When disoriented, place your bare hand on a safe patch of rock (test temperature first). Notice the texture, temperature, pressure. Name three things you see, two you hear, one you feel. Bring yourself back.
- **Time referencing**: Wear a watch set to the time of your home universe. Check it periodically. The internal sense of time will warp — the watch will anchor you.
- **Acceptance**: Do not fight the strangeness. Do not try to interpret every glitch as a puzzle. Some things are just the local physics. If you see a raindrop duplicate and fall up, let it. Do not stare at the tech at the pool bottom for more than a few seconds — the "angles that hurt" are real. Look at it from the corner of your eye, or through a reflection in your camera viewfinder.
- **Buddy system**: If you bring a companion, agree on check-in signals, no more than 10 minutes apart. If you alone, set an alarm on your watch every 15 minutes as a sanity check.
- **Respect**: Before entering, consider stating your purpose aloud. "I come to witness, not to take. I will leave as I came." This is psychological, but in a place so aware, intention matters. The tech may respond to attention.

**V. On-Site Conduct: How to Be in That Space**

- Move slowly. The heat rewards stillness. Rushing raises heart rate and internal temperature. Take three times longer than you think to cross the basin floor.
- Watch where you step. The silica crust can be thin over hot mud. Test with a trekking pole or a rock. Avoid stepping on moss — it is dense but fragile, and the roots may be part of the local energy cycle.
- Do not approach the pool within 3 meters. The edge may be unstable; the steam can scald. Do not dip fingers in the water — it is near boiling at center, and the minerals may be caustic.
- Do not touch the glowing moss or the translucent flowers unless you have studied them from a distance first. Some may be reactive. The fauna that phasing and changing — do not grab. Observe.
- Avoid the archway at first. Stand near it, note its taper, but do not walk through the space it frames. It may be a portal or a lens. If you must, toss a small stone through first. See if it emerges the other side.
- Respect the silence. Do not shout. The sound 0.9 means quiet is the native state. Your voice will carry. Speak in whispers if you must. The place listens.
- If the tech at the pool bottom starts to glow or pulse more brightly, back away slowly. It may be running a cycle. Do not stare.
- When condensation falls, notice the afterimages. They may be useful for understanding the fold. But do not chase them.

**VI. Contingencies: When the Unstable Becomes Unsettling**

- **If spatial distortion becomes intense**: Sit down immediately. Close your eyes. Hold a rock in your hand — a physical constant. Breathe. Wait until the world settles.
- **If disoriented and cannot find exit**: Do not panic. The basin is small. The walls are distinct color — basalt dark, rhyolite pale. Climb to the higher ground. From the rim, you can see the layout. Use your whistle if needed.
- **If burned by steam or rock**: Cool the area with water from your bottle (not the pool). Apply burn gel. If blistering, cover. Conserve water — you may need to leave earlier.
- **If cut deeply on sharp rock**: Clean with sterile water or antiseptic wipes. Pack wound with hemostatic gauze. Apply pressure. Wrap with clean bandage. If bleeding does not stop, abort and climb out.
- **If you feel the "call" of the pool or the tech**: Recognize it as siren. The glimmer is beautiful but dangerous. Do not approach. Anchor yourself with a rope if needed. The pool's depth is known; the fold is unknown.
- **If the archway seems to pull you**: Step back. Do not pass through. It is not ready for you. If you feel suction, grab a rock and hold on.

**VII. Departure: Leave as a Guest, Not a Curator**

When you decide to leave:
- Pack all gear. Check every crevice for dropped items.
- Scatter any cairns you built. Erase signs of your presence.
- Take nothing but mental images, notes, and perhaps a small sample of dead rock if absolutely necessary. Do not take living things.
- Thank the place — silently or aloud. It gave you its time. You were part of its early story.
- Ascend slowly. The effort in heat will tax you. Use the rope, take breaks.
- At the rim, rest. Rehydrate completely before traveling on. The heat and humidity can cause delayed shock.
- Later, in a safe place, review your notes. Write everything before the memories soften.

**Final Word**

You are going to a place where the planet's crust is still fresh from its birth pangs, where the water boils and the rocks are splintery with newness. It is not dangerous in a predatory sense — it is dangerous in the way that being inside a furnace or on a newborn star is dangerous. But it will reward you with a glimpse of process, of the raw manufacture of world, if you approach with humility, patience, and thorough preparation.

Bring your body ready, your mind open, and your heart still. The basin will do the rest.

Go well. Arrive fully. Return soft.
