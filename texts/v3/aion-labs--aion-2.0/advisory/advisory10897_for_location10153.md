# Preparation for the Drowned Garden

Your destination is not a world with an ocean, but a world that *is* an ocean. It is a finished place, a continent-sized artifact sleeping in the abyssal dark, where the only activities are the slow growth of plants and the even slower settling of stone. To visit is to insert your frantic, human-scale self into a realm of monumental patience and profound silence. Survival here is less about fighting threats and more about harmonizing with an environment that is, in every sense, *absolute*. Here is how an ordinary human must prepare.

## 1. The Absolute Basics: Life Support & Pressure

**You cannot breathe water.** This is the first and most non-negotiable fact. You will need a closed-system life support apparatus that provides breathable gas, scrubs carbon dioxide, and manages humidity. A standard spacesuit is a poor model—it’s built for vacuum, not for the crushing, pervasive pressure of a global ocean.

*   **Pressure Vessel:** Your primary need is a personal pressure vessel—a suit or a small pod—rated for depths exceeding 10,000 meters. The water column here is planetary; there is no “surface” to retreat to. The suit must be rigid or pressure-compensated to prevent collapse. Forget flexible diving suits; the pressures would turn them into a sarcophagus. Think more along the lines of a deep-sea submersible, but form-fitted. Materials like syntactic foam, thick acrylic, or specially alloyed titanium are mandatory.
*   **Atmospheric Management:** Your life support system must be entirely self-contained and long-lasting. There are no oxygen pockets, no air-filled ruins. Consider a rebreather that recirculates and revitalizes your gas mix, powered by a high-density energy cell. Redundancy is critical: two independent systems, ideally. The internal atmosphere should be a standard nitrogen-oxygen mix at 1 atmosphere to avoid decompression sickness *inside* your suit.
*   **Thermal Regulation:** The water is *cold*, a steady, gelid chill just above freezing. Hypothermia would be instantaneous without protection. Your suit must have an active heating layer, insulated from the surrounding brine. Power efficiency is key; a nuclear thermoelectric battery or something equivalent would be ideal for long-duration visits.

## 2. Seeing and Being Seen: Light & Perception

The ambient light is a memory—a faint, bruised indigo that vanishes into utter blackness within a few hundred meters of the theoretical “surface.” You will be operating in the deep, permanent night.

*   **Active Lighting:** You will need powerful, but *respectful*, lights. Broad-beam lamps for general orientation, and focused, steerable spotlights for detail. However, this environment has never known sharp, artificial light. The native flora glows with a faint, internal bioluminescence. Blasting it with your beams will be visually jarring and may cause phototropic reactions in the slow-growing plants. Use the lowest effective illumination. Consider lights in the blue-green spectrum, which travels farthest in water and might be less disruptive.
*   **Passive Sensors:** Your eyes will be largely useless. You must augment them.
    *   **Sonar (Active & Passive):** A multi-frequency scanning sonar will be your primary mapmaker. It will paint the rolling hills, the vast stone pavements, and the serpentine walls in sound. Passive sonar is equally important—listening to the deep *hum* of the planet, the fluting of currents, the rare crack of timber. It will be your ear on the landscape.
    *   **Chemical & Pressure Sensors:** The water is a “snow” of spores and rich in minerals from volcanic vents. Sensors that can “taste” shifts in chemical composition can guide you to nutrient-rich areas (where plant life is most lush) or warn of potentially toxic seepages. A sensitive pressure gauge will detect the slow, decade-long swell of deep-pressure fronts—the “weather” of this place.

## 3. Moving Through the Density

The description is correct: every cubic meter is occupied. Moving will be a constant, gentle negotiation.

*   **Propulsion:** You cannot walk. The “ground” is often a carpet of velvety moss or a forest of kelp-like fronds wider than roads. You need neutral buoyancy and thrust. A compact, ultra-quiet propulsion system is essential—think maneuvering thrusters or a slow, ducted fan system. Anything with violent propellers will shred the ancient, delicate ecosystem and entangle you in seconds. Movement must be slow, deliberate, and graceful.
*   **Entanglement Hazard:** The landscape is a living net of vines, fronds, and sponges. Your suit must be smooth, with no external snag points. You will need a means to cut yourself free—a heated wire or a vibratory blade that can sever plant matter without creating violent jerks. A sudden, panicked thrash could damage your pressure integrity.
*   **Touch & Manipulation:** If you wish to interact, you will need manipulator arms—external, pressure-rated grips or robotic arms that can feel the texture of the moss-covered stone, or gently part a curtain of fronds. Your own hands, even if in a pressure glove, will be clumsy and weak against the water’s resistance.

## 4. The Psychological Siege

This may be your greatest challenge. The environment is engineered to humble you.

*   **Sensory Modification:** You will experience a form of sensory deprivation *and* overload simultaneously. Visually, you’ll be confined to the small pool of your light, surrounded by an oppressive, infinite blackness. Auditorily, you’ll hear the profound silence of the deep, punctuated by sounds so low and slow they feel geological. Yet, your instruments will be feeding you constant data—sonar pings, chemical readouts, structural stresses. This dissonance can lead to disorientation, paranoia, and the “break-off phenomenon” familiar to deep-sea divers and astronauts.
*   **Agoraphobia & Claustrophobia:** You will feel both terrifyingly exposed in the vast, dark void and simultaneously crushed by the dense, clinging proximity of the living architecture. Mental preparation and constant, calm self-talk are necessary. Some travelers benefit from a low, constant stream of familiar music or speech in their comms, a tether to a faster, warmer reality.
*   **The Weight of Time:** Human psychology is built on rhythms of day and night, on observable change. Here, change is measured in millennia. A frond you brush past may have been growing since the fall of Rome. The wall you lean against may have been settling into place for half a million years. Your presence, your entire life, is a flicker in this timescale. This can induce profound existential angst or a serene, ego-dissolving calm. Which one you experience depends on your preparation. Study deep time, geology, and slow philosophy. Meditate on insignificance as a form of freedom, not dread.

## 5. Logistics and Contingencies

*   **Sustenance:** Bring all your food and water. The ecosystem is inedible and likely indigestible. Your life support should include a nutrient recycling system if your visit is extended.
*   **Communication:** Assume you are utterly alone. Radio and laser comms are useless in water over planetary distances. You might have a quantum-entangled communicator if your multiverse tech permits—otherwise, you are out of contact. All decisions are final.
*   **Navigation:** In the featureless dark, with a landscape of soft, repeating forms, you will get lost. Your sonar will map locally, but you need a fixed reference. Deploy transponder beacons at your entry point (your “door” to this world). Use inertial navigation systems, but be aware they will drift. The ultimate guide is the **current**. The slow, deep currents have flowed the same ways for epochs. Learning to read their subtle direction via your pressure and chemical sensors can provide a cardinal orientation.
*   **Structural Safety:** While the stonework is ancient and stable, it is also being patiently dismantled by plant roots. Avoid leaning against or entering any structure that looks overly shrouded in growth. A collapse, while unlikely in your timeframe, would be catastrophic. Your suit’s external sonar should constantly ping for structural integrity, listening for hollow sounds or shifts.

## 6. The Mindset of a Visitor

You are not an explorer conquering a frontier. You are a microbe entering a cathedral. Your mandate is one of absolute, reverent minimal impact.

*   **Rule 1: Everything is Made.** You are floating through someone else’s finished work. Do not try to “improve” it, dig it up, or take “souvenirs.” The value is in the totality.
*   **Rule 2: Life is Patient.** You are a hurricane of motion and heat compared to the local life. Move with a slowness that feels absurd to you. Let your thrusters idle. Drift. Your wake will disturb the sediment “snow” for decades.
*   **Rule 3: Scale Annihilates Urgency.** Nothing you do here is urgent. If you feel panic, it is a human error. Stop. Breathe. Your instruments are your reality; trust them over your instincts.
*   **Rule 4: Darkness is a Medium.** Stop trying to “see.” Learn to *listen* through your hydrophones, to *feel* through pressure changes, to *taste* the water via your sensors. This is how the world knows itself.

## Final Gear Checklist:
- Pressure suit/pod (10,000m+ rating)
- Redundant closed-loop life support (1 atm internal)
- Active thermal regulation system
- High-density power source (nuclear or equivalent)
- Blue-green spectrum, variable-intensity lights
- Multi-frequency sonar (active/passive)
- Chemical, pressure, and temperature sensor suite
- Neutral buoyancy system & ultra-quiet maneuvering thrusters
- Anti-entanglement outer shell & cutting tool
- External manipulators
- Inertial navigation & transponder beacons
- Psychological support aids (audio, meditative prompts)
- Full supply of food/water/nutrients for duration + 50%
- No sampling tools, no markers, no permanent alterations.

To visit the Drowned Garden is to voluntarily enter a meditation on eternity. You will confront the fact that a world can be complete without light, without animals, without the constant churn of biological competition. It is beautiful, terrifying, and profoundly indifferent. You will leave no trace there, but it will leave a permanent mark on you—the memory of a perfect, silent equilibrium, where the only story left is the slow, gentle falling of stone into silt, and the patient, endless growing of the dark.
