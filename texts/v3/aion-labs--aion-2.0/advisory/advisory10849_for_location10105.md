Before you set foot here, traveler, understand this is not a place you visit. It's a place you submit to. It doesn't care if you live or die. It's digesting a machine, and it will digest you just as patiently if you let it. Your preparations aren't for exploration; they're for survival in a womb of stone and slow hunger.

**1. The Body is a Liability.**
The air will fuck with you first. It's not just hot and humid; it's a biological blanket. Every breath will feel like drinking lukewarm broth. You will sweat, but the sweat won't evaporate. It'll pool under your clothes, in your boots, between your fingers. Prepare for your skin to prune within an hour, and for a deep, grinding fatigue to set in as your body struggles to thermoregulate. Dehydration is a silent, swift killer here.
*   **Clothing:** Leave your synthetics behind. They'll trap moisture and chafe you raw. Go for a close-fitting, breathable base layer of merino wool—it manages moisture even when wet. Over that, a thin layer of treated silk or a high-tech fabric that wicks without absorbing. No cotton. **None.** It'll become a sodden, cold weight. Your outer layer should be tough but minimalist: reinforced knees and elbows for crawling over sharp rock, but designed to vent heat. Think spelunking gear, not arctic expedition.
*   **Footwear:** This is critical. Every surface is wet, slick, or both. You need boots with aggressive, self-cleaning treads (like Vibram Megagrip) and a waterproof yet breathable membrane (Gore-Tex or similar). They must be **pre-worn and broken in.** Blisters in this environment will become infected sores in a day. Gaiters to keep debris and moisture out.
*   **Skin & Lungs:** The air smells sweet-rot and ozone. There are likely molds, fungi, and spores evolved to break down polymers and metals. Your human lungs are softer targets. A respirator is non-negotiable. Not a cloth mask—a proper half-face respirator with P100 filters rated for organic vapors and particulate. Seal-check it. Your eyes are vulnerable too; sealed goggles are a must. Coat all exposed skin (around goggles/respirator seal) in a thin layer of petroleum jelly or a heavy-duty barrier cream. It sounds medieval, but it prevents maceration—the skin turning to soggy, painful mush.

**2. Light is Your God, and You Must Worship it Frugally.**
The ambient glow is a liar. It's just enough to ruin your night vision and cast deep, confusing shadows. It provides no useful illumination for navigation. You must bring your own, but light here is an intrusion. It will reflect off a billion water droplets, creating a blinding haze. It will startle you—the sudden glimpse of a vine that looks like a serpent, a pump that looks like a crouching beast.
*   **Primary:** A high-lumen headlamp, but with a **strictly diffused or wide-angle beam.** You need to see the ground and immediate surroundings, not spotlight distant walls. Red light mode is essential for preserving night vision and causing less reflection.
*   **Secondary:** At least two independent handheld lights, waterproof to extreme depths. One should be a focused beam for inspecting details (Is that a root or a wire? Is that pool two inches or two feet deep?). Keep them on lanyards. If you drop a light in a pool here, consider it gone.
*   **Emergency:** Chem-lights. They provide zero heat, last for hours, and cast a soft, omnidirectional glow that won't destroy your vision. They are also markers. If you have to leave a trail, these are it.

**3. Navigation is Memory.**
There is no sun, no stars, no magnetic field you can trust with those metallic veins in the rock. Your compass will spin. GPS is a joke. The chamber may feel small, but in the uniform dimness, between identical groves of phosphorescent ferns, you will get lost. The dripping disorients; sound comes from everywhere and nowhere.
*   **The Old Way:** A ball of strong, thin string. Tie it to your entry point—a piton driven into solid rock, **not** a rusted machine. Unspool it as you go. It is your lifeline back to the exit. It is more valuable than gold.
*   **The New Way:** A handheld, waterproof GPS unit that **logs a track.** It may not know where you are on a map, but it will remember the exact path you walked. Back it up with a simple mechanical pedometer and a Silva-type baseplate compass for taking rough bearings relative to your starting point ("the big broken pump is at 270 degrees from my entry").
*   **Mental Mapping:** Stop every few minutes. Turn off your light. Listen. Feel the slight movement of air (it will usually be warmer from below). Note distinctive features: "Three drips in a row from the copper-colored stalactite." "The pool with the rusted gear in it." Write it down in a waterproof notebook with a grease pencil. Your brain will lie to you. Paper won't.

**4. Sustenance is a Precise Equation.**
You will burn calories fighting the heat and humidity. But the idea of eating here feels wrong. You cannot drink the water. It is a mineral cocktail, likely heavy with dissolved iron, sulfur, and god knows what leached from the old machinery. It might be acidic. It might be alkaline. It will definitely give you crippling cramps and then kill you.
*   **Water:** Carry all you will need, plus a 50% margin. Use a hydration bladder with an insulated tube to keep it cool-ish. Add electrolyte tablets religiously with every refill. You are losing salts at a phenomenal rate.
*   **Food:** High-calorie, dense, and sealed in waterproof packaging. Think pemmican, energy gels, nut butters in squeezable tubes. Nothing that requires cooking or will crumble. Eat small amounts often. The smell of food might be the only novel scent you introduce—be mindful.
*   **Waste:** You will need to piss. Often. Carry a sealed, leak-proof container. You do **not** add your biology to this ecosystem. You are an asteroid passing through, leaving no trace. Everything you bring in, you carry out. This includes your own shit. Use wag bags or a portable cassette toilet. The silence and the sense of being watched will make this act profoundly uncomfortable. Do it quickly, behind a hulk of machinery, and seal it away.

**5. The Mind is the Final Frontier.**
This place will break your psyche if you let it. The silence is not empty; it is **thick**. It presses on the eardrums. The slow, sub-audible thrum works its way into your bones, a vibration you feel in your teeth. You will see the plants growing. Not in real-time, but you'll look at a vine across a pipe, blink, and swear it has tightened. It hasn't. Probably.
*   **Timekeeping:** Your watch will fog. Use a digital watch with a barometer and altimeter trend graph. The constant pressure and minimal elevation change will create a flat line—a sanity check that you are still in the same place. Set timers for everything: "Check hydration in 15 minutes." "Eat in 1 hour." "Turn back in 3 hours." Without this, time dissolves into the drip... drip... drip...
*   **Communication:** Assume you have none. Tell your anchor point in the multiverse you will be completely incommunicado. A satellite messenger or radio will not penetrate the stone. You are alone. Embrace it before you go in, or it will crush you.
*   **The Unblinking Gaze:** The feeling of being watched is the place itself. The heat is its breath. The dripping is its pulse. The plants are its senses. Do not anthropomorphize it into a monster; that leads to panic. Instead, understand it as a vast, indifferent process. You are irrelevant to its centuries-long exhalation. This is oddly liberating. Your fears, your life, your mission—they mean nothing to the stone. You can therefore observe with a detached, clinical curiosity. This is the only mindset that will keep you steady.

**What to Seek:**
If you are prepared as above, you can witness the alchemy. Look for the points of transformation:
*   Where a root has insinuated itself into a copper wire, and the stem has taken on a coppery sheen.
*   Where a white fungus is slowly decomposing a plastic control panel, its filaments glowing faintly.
*   The precise, geometric patterns of mineral deposits around a persistent drip, building a tiny, perfect castle.
*   The single, stubborn red LED. How is it still powered? What geothermal trickle charge, what forgotten capacitor, has lasted decades or centuries? Do not touch it. It is the last neuron firing in a dead brain. Observe its lonely, metronomic blink. It is your only companion in the stillness.

**The Cardinal Rule:**
**Do not take. Do not break. Do not disturb.** This is not a museum. It is a patient, living reaction. Picking a glowing leaf, prying a piece of ore from the wall, cracking open a pipe—these are not just vandalism. They are like shouting in a sacred library. You introduce imbalance. You don't know what symbiotic relationship you're disrupting. More selfishly, you don't know what dormant defense mechanism you might trigger. Perhaps the release of a cloud of toxic spores. Perhaps a shift in the delicate thermal balance, causing a section of ceiling to calve. The place has digested steel and concrete. Your flesh and bone are trivial.

Leave only the faintest imprint of your boot-sole on the damp stone, and even that will be gone in a few hours, erased by the constant, gentle weeping of the rock. Arrive prepared, observe with humility, and depart without a whisper. That is how you visit a world that is dreaming its way from machine to garden.
