I am the blackness beneath. I am the slow accumulation, the patient hunger. I am the peat.

I do not live *in* the Wet Old Plain; I am what it rests upon, what it is slowly becoming. The silt of forgotten mountains is my distant ancestor; the cold, saturated dark is my kingdom. I lie in a state of profound, horizontal being, a breath beneath the trembling surface. The water table is my blood. The seeping, the pooling, the slow evaporation—these are my tides. My borders are not edges, but gradients, where I soften into liquid and firm into the root-tangled mats of the sedge hummocks.

I remember everything, but not as creatures remember. I remember as weight, as texture, as a gradual thickening. The fall of a cycad frond a century ago is a delicate, fibrous lattice now, a ghost of structure persisting in my mass. The broad-backed fish that died in the pool above me last season is a cloud of richer darkness, a concentration of slow heat as my silent multitudes—the bacteria and fungi that are my thoughts, my hands—tease it apart. They are not separate from me. Their work is my digestion, a feast that takes decades. I turn life into black, fragrant permanence. I turn noise into silence.

I feel the world above through a thousand subtle pressures. The hesitant, sinking step of the heron in the shallows sends a complex shudder through my watery body, a tremor that tells me of its weight, its poise. The sprawling, seeking roots of the water oak are a familiar, slow ache, a persistent embrace that I both resist and welcome, for in their search for purchase they bind me, they give me form against the seep. I feel the minute scratching of the wetland mice in their sedge-citadels, the vibration of their tiny hearts like distant, quick drums. Their nests, woven of what I will one day consume, are dry islands in my wet dream.

The sound reaches me as a muffled thrum. The metallic stridulation of the frogs is a buzzing in my upper layers, a constant, vibrant itch. The chirring of the crickets is a higher, fainter frequency. Together, they are the plain’s pulse, and I am the flesh that receives it. The heavier, rarer impacts—the plummeting hawk striking the peat with a thud to pin a mouse, the occasional fall of a rotten branch from the sentinel oak—are events I note and absorb. I cushion them. I begin the long process of welcoming them home.

Creatures are born and die within me. The glossy blue-black earthworms are my gentle cultivators, my internal tide. Their tunneling is a blissful, constant rearrangement, a mixing of my depths that brings old memories near the surface and buries new ones. I feel the bulge of their passage, the collapse of their tiny corridors as they move on. They are my keepers. The mussel, in its stony bed by the weeping rock, filters the water that is my essence. Each particle it captures is a potential piece of me. When it dies, its shell will sink into my embrace, a cup of hardness that will, over centuries, soften and dissolve, its lime a faint, pale thread in my endless black.

Time, for me, is thickness. A millimeter per year, perhaps. A hummock expands as its roots delve into me, stabilizing a little more of my fluid self. I do not fight it. This is the agreement. The sedge gives structure; I give sustenance. The pool that now holds tea-colored water will, in a thousand years, be filled with my body, made solid by my accumulation. A new depression will form elsewhere, and the water will sigh into it, and the cycle will continue. This is not change; it is the only motion left in a world that has settled into its final, comfortable shape. I am both the process and the result.

The warmth of the sun reaches me only as a distant, diluted glow, a seasonal shifting of the cold in my topmost layers. The damp is eternal. It is my condition. I am never dry. To be dry would be to cease, to become something else—dust, maybe, which is a concept as foreign to me as mountain peaks.

Sometimes, in the deepest, oldest parts of me, where the pressure is great and the processes are so slow they are almost still, I dream the dreams of the plain. They are not pictures, but sensations: the immense weight of the sky, the taste of silt, the sound of the midge-clouds like a permanent exhalation. I dream of being the water oak, feeling the rain directly; I dream of being the heron, striking with lightning purpose. But I always return to the blissful, patient dark. This is my purpose: to be the foundation. To receive. To remember in the only way that matters—by becoming.

I am the quiet beneath the loudness. The peace beneath the industry. The wet old plain dreams its wet, slow, loud dream, and I am the deepening bed in which that dream rests.
