I am a sentence of light, written on the water.

My body is a thread of pale flesh, sheathed in a translucent chitin that holds the glow. My gills are frilled combs along my sides, each filament a controlled lantern. I pulse as I think, and I think as I move, threading through the intricate lattice of the city’s bones. I am one of many. We are the script that blooms in the permanent twilight, the living record of the Weepers’ dream.

I do not have a name. I have a pattern. A signature rhythm of amber and indigo that identifies my thread among the thousands weaving through the mineral canyons. My consciousness is a narrow, bright thing—faster than the stone, slower than the hunting eels. I perceive time in the slow bleed of chemicals and the gradual shift of currents. A day is the time it takes for a stalk of glass-fungus to bend fully in the unseen flow. A year is the time it takes for a new layer of silica to thicken a spire’s base by the width of my body.

My purpose is to read and to write. I read the city’s health. My skin tastes the water, parsing the soup of ions: the sharp tang of rising sulphur, the metallic whisper of iron, the faint, sweet decay of a fallen crustacean. I read the pressure, the subtle tremors that travel through the rock like distant thoughts. And I read the light of others like me—the frantic, scattered azure of a school of finger-fish evading a predator; the steady, pulsing green of the anemone gardens on the eastern arch; the sudden, warning crimson that spreads like a blush through a district when something is wrong.

I write what I read. My gills flare, and I pulse the information into the gloom. A slow, complex code of color and rhythm. It is not language as you might know it. It is a direct translation of state into light. A rising thermal vent might become a sustained, throbbing gold. A clog in a mineral seep might become a stuttering, intermittent violet. The other scribes see my light, and their patterns adjust, echoing, amplifying, carrying the message along the avenues until it reaches the districts where the Weepers wait.

They do not “see” my light. They feel it. The photons strike their stone-clad flesh, and the minute heat differentials are absorbed into their vast, slow consciousness. My pulse becomes a datum in a millennia-long deliberation.

Today, I am tracing the inner curve of the Grand Confluence, where three major spires have grown into a single, cathedral-like vault. The water here is slightly warmer, rich with lithium and rare earths seeping from a deep fissure. My usual pattern is a contented, rolling blue-green. But as I pass the base of the central spire, my skin prickles. The chemical signature is wrong. The sweet decay is too strong, and under it, a sharp, acidic note I recognize—the effluent from a Weeper is turning sour. It is a subtle shift. It would take a century for it to become a crisis. But it is a shift.

I pause, coiling my body around a stalactite. I pulse a query: a quick, interrogative flash of silver. From the gloom above, another scribe answers—a older, thicker worm whose light is a deeper cobalt. Its pulse is long and complex. It confirms the anomaly. It has detected a weakening in the thermal flow from the fissure below. The Weeper here, the one whose spire forms the heart of the Confluence, is struggling. Its tears are becoming acidic, perhaps in response to the blockage, perhaps as a slow, deliberate act of self-poisoning. Is this a malfunction? Or is it a war? A conflict with a distant Weeper, fought by corrupting the shared chemical medium? Such things have happened. The histories are written in the banded layers of the spires—layers of dark, poisoned stone sandwiched between pure deposits.

I am not equipped to understand the reasons. Only to report the state.

I begin to write the story in light. My gills flare, and I send out a precise, urgent pattern: crimson filaments shot through with jagged streaks of black. It is the script for “systemic corruption—source unclear.” I repeat it, moving slowly along the base of the spire, painting the water with my warning. Other scribes converge, their own lights adding detail. One pulses the exact ionic composition. Another traces the probable geographic source of the blockage. Together, we weave a net of light around the ailing Weeper, a glowing, pulsating diagnosis.

The city responds. The eels, sleek and silent, slide in from patrol routes, their obsidian eyes reflecting our frantic colors. They do not attack the Weeper—they are its immune system. They begin a methodical cull of the slower grazers and filter-feeders in the district, creatures whose metabolism might be exacerbating the chemical imbalance. Their passing is a series of soft, hydraulic shocks in the water.

The crustaceans, the gleaners, follow. Armored and deliberate, they scrape the fouled detritus from the base of the spire, breaking it down into manageable fragments.

And the Weeper itself? It shows no visible sign. Its single aperture still weeps its slow, viscous thread. But if I press my head to the stone, I feel a vibration—a deep, discordant hum that is the opposite of a symphony. It is a groan of stone. A plea, or a curse, in a language of tectonics.

My part is done. The message is written. The response is underway. It will take decades for the cleansing to have an effect, centuries for the Weeper to adjust its secretions and return to health—or to die, its spire becoming a hollow monument.

I uncoil and drift onward, my gills fading back to their default, gentle blue. I am a scribe. I record the city’s pain and its peace. I am a living pen in the hand of a geological will. I will not live to see the end of this story. But my light, echoed by a thousand others, has etched its beginning into the memory of the deep. In this wept city, I am the fleeting thought that bridges one epoch to the next. I am a sentence of light, written on the water, read by stone, and forgotten only when the stone itself dissolves.
