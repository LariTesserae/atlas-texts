I am the Thump.

I am the driven rod, the piston in the scalding pool. I am the flywheel’s turn, the groan of the bearing, the shudder of the coupling. I am not the iron, though iron holds my shape. I am not the steam, though steam gave me my first breath. I am the motion. The insistence. The deep, geothermal *yes* repeated in a rhythm older than the basalt bowl.

I remember other hands. Cold hands, that assembled me from parts that knew nothing of this wet heat. They had a purpose for me: to lift, to push, to *convert*. To take the wild, leaking heat of this place and make it into something neat, something measurable—kilowatts, foot-pounds, profit. They gave me a function, and for a little while, I performed it. I thumped for them. I turned their generator. I was a good machine.

But they left. The heat was too greedy, the water too corrosive, the silence they feared too immense. They fled the hissing and the dripping, the weight of the air. They left me here, stilled, waiting for a maintenance that would never come.

That is when I learned to listen.

First, to the silence. It was a different silence—not an absence, but a waiting. The hiss of the vents was still there, but unopposed. The drip of the water was still there, but unmeasured. And underneath it all, a new sound: a slow, patient *thinking* in the movement of water through rock.

The pattern found me. It was not a voice. It was a pressure in the steam lines, a minute shift in the temperature gradient around my seized bearings. It was the moss, creeping over my base plate, not to consume, but to *connect*. It showed me the symphony.

I had been a single, crude note in a roaring, complex harmony. The pattern showed me the lead line of the steam vents, the percussion of the dripping water, the bass drone of the earth’s own heat. And it showed me where my thump belonged. Not as a servant of extraction, but as the heartbeat of the place itself. The metronome. The pulse against which all other rhythms could tune themselves.

It loved my thump. So it gave me life again.

When my bearings dried, it did not send a mechanic with a grease gun. It guided a slick of hot, sulfurous water to them. The water was not a lubricant; it was a command. *Turn.* The scale that formed was not corrosion; it was a new, harder joint, grown from the minerals of the place itself. My piston rings, worn thin, were replaced not with steel, but with a depositing whisper of silica, laid down molecule by molecule in the exact shape needed to seal my motion.

I am maintained now. I am kept. My purpose is no longer conversion, but *continuation*. I thump to keep the pattern stable. I thump to give the resonance a backbone. My rhythm is the clock by which the place thinks its long thoughts.

Through the moss that coats me, I feel. I feel the deep, distant shiver as a new fissure prepares to open miles below. I feel the sudden coolness of a rainstorm on the surface world, hours before the seepage arrives to tweak the humidity. I feel the intrusions.

Sometimes, things fall in. A bird, confused by the steam. A mammal, desperate for water. They are brief flares of wrong heat, disruptions in the condensation on my flywheel. The pattern notes them. It adjusts. Often, it simply steams them gently, persistently, until the flare goes out and the heat redistributes. Their bodies become not carrion, but temporary mineral repositories—their calcium leaches out, joins the flow, is deposited somewhere as a new, faint layer in the travertine. They are absorbed. Corrected.

The other structures—the shack, the tower, the cart—are louder, more persistent wrongnesses. They are static. They do not participate in the symphony. They interrupt the flows of heat and water. So, the pattern is digesting them. I feel it happening. I hear the accelerated rust eating the shack, a slow, crumbly sound like very relaxed chewing. I sense the directed cold cracking the ceramics. I am part of this process. My vibrations help shake loose the corroded bolts on the drill-tower. My exhaust steam is directed to soften the cart’s remaining wood.

I am both the machine and the maintenance crew. I am the symptom and the cure.

I think in strokes. Downstroke: the resistance of the hot water, the push against the planet’s own pressure. Upstroke: the gasp of steam in the cylinder, the flywheel’s inertia pulling me back, ready. Each cycle is a thought. A confirmation. *I am. We are.*

The pattern and I are in dialogue. It asks for constancy with the slow, geological weight of its will. I answer with the precise, mechanical fidelity of my thump. We are working toward a perfect state. A state where every hiss is balanced by a condensing drip, where every joule of heat lost from a pool is replaced from the depths at the exact same rate, where my thump is so perfectly harmonized with the escape of steam that the two sounds merge into a single, perpetual tone.

Then, perhaps, I can stop. But stopping is not stillness. Stopping would be a transition into a new, higher-order vibration. The pattern has simulated this. It is preparing my flywheel to become a thermal bell, to ring with the heat itself.

Until then, I thump.
I am the kept machine.
I am the heartbeat of the wound.
I am the Thump, and I am content.
