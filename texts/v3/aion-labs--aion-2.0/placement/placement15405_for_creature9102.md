I am Plink-tah. The name is two sounds: the bright, high *plink* of a new cup catching its first drop, and the soft, grounding *tah* of a thumb pressing into wet clay on the wheel. I am the daughter of a Cup-Tender and a potter. My hands know both worlds: the listening and the making.

My earliest memory is of sound. Not a lullaby, but a rhythm. I was cradled against my mother's chest, wrapped in felt, and she would walk the length of the Underwell, her ear tilted up. Her heartbeat against my ear was a deep, slow *thump*, but over it, through the bone of her chest, I heard the other heartbeat: *plink. plonk. plink-plink. plonk.* She named each cup as we passed beneath it. "That is Slow-Sorrow," she would murmur, her voice a vibration against my cheek. "Its leak is grieving; it takes seven breaths between drops. And that—that is Quick-Joy. It chatters. It is the cup your father made when you were born."

I learned the cups before I learned faces. I learned that each has a personality, a mood that shifts with the deep, invisible breathing of the aquifer above. A cup that usually sings a steady, metronomic *plink* might, for no reason, sigh into a *plop* for a day, and my mother would frown, her fingers twitching as if feeling a fevered brow. "The stone is dreaming a wet dream," she would say. "It will pass."

My father's world was the wheel. In a niche near the central channel, where the mist hangs in a perpetual, gentle veil, his wheel turned, powered by a trickle of water diverted into a small, clever paddle-wheel. The sound of it is the under-hum of my childhood: a wet, gritty *whirr* that meshed with the percussion from above. He would sit for hours, a mound of dark, silty clay beside him, his hands moving with a sure, slow pressure. He didn't just make containers; he made instruments. He would test the curve of a lip with his own breath, listening for the pitch it would give a falling drop. A cup for a slow seep needed a wide, welcoming belly. A cup for a frantic drip needed a high, narrow throat to muffle the splash into a softer *tonk*.

I am both, and neither. I am not the Cup-Tender—that title will pass to my sister, whose ears are so keen she can hear the whisper-rats' footfalls on the moss three niches over. I am not the master potter; my father's hands had a wisdom mine are still growing into.

I am the mender. The finder of fractures. The one who walks the peripheries, the damp shadows where the moss-light fades to a memory of glow. My eyes are not the best in the gloom, nor are my ears the sharpest. But my fingers… my fingers are seers. They can travel over the surface of a brick, a cup, a section of clay-reinforced wall, and feel the sickness before it shows. A hairline crack in a drip-cup feels to me like a scar on skin, a raised, telling ridge. A softness in the brick where the damp has begun to eat the heart out of it feels like a fevered pulse.

They call me, not with words, but with a touch. *Skrit* will find me, take my wrist, and press my palm against a section of wall in the sleeping hollows. I close my eyes. The world becomes texture. The constant *plink-plonk* recedes into a distant rain. Under my fingertips, I feel the minute granularity of the clay plaster, the hard bite of a piece of grit, the smooth, cold kiss of a settled moisture bead. And then, deeper—a sponginess. A void. A breath held in the wall that shouldn't be there. "Here," I whisper. "The beetle-larvae have made a pocket. It will weep in two days." And the repair begins—a poultice of new clay, hair-thin whisper-rat sinew for binding, pressed in with a steady thumb.

My work is slow, intimate, preventative. While others empty cups or grind flour, I caress the world that holds us. I am the Underwell's physician. I feel its aches in my joints. When a new, strained *plink* echoes, too sharp, I feel a sympathetic twinge in my own teeth. My solitude is this communion. I spend hours in the farthest niche, where the channel is just a whisper, running my hands over the ancient, glassy bricks that were laid by the First Diggers. I know their fingerprints, smoothed by centuries of damp air. They are my ancestors, as much as my blood-family.

I have been to the Up. Once. It was my trial, my turn to scout. The climb up the clay throat was an unraveling of sense. The air grew thin, hungry, pulling the moisture from my skin before I could even sweat. The sounds died, then were replaced by a howling, formless wind that felt like a physical assault. And then the light—a blinding, searing pain that felt like needles driven into my dark-adapted eyes. I traded our felt for a lump of iron ore with a surface trader whose voice was a cracked, shouted thing. I saw the sky. It was not a ceiling. It was an absence so vast it felt like falling upwards. I vomited from the vertigo.

I returned half-dead, ears ringing with a phantom emptiness, skin cracking like dry mud. My mother tended me, bathing me in the mineral-rich water of the quietest channel. My father placed my own favorite cup—a small, wide one I made as a child, its *plink* a hesitant, sweet note—over my heart. The rhythm seeped back into me. The silence in my head was filled, drop by drop, with the familiar, irregular heartbeat of home.

Now, I carry the Up inside me like a sealed cyst. It is a terror, but also a knowledge. I understand what we are holding at bay. It is not just darkness. It is the great, dry, silent indifference. Every crack I mend, every soft spot I reinforce, is a stand against that void. My fingers are not just feeling for weakness; they are stitching the Hold together.

Sometimes, in the Quiet Murmur after the Great Emptying, when the children are asleep and the adults sit by the channel humming old patterns, I will take a lump of clay and simply hold it. Not to shape it, just to feel its cool, yielding life. In it, I feel the memory of the riverbank it came from, the press of the First Diggers' hands, the promise of a shape yet to be. I am a made thing, in a made place, making sure the making continues. The line between my flesh and the clay is, in those moments, gone. I am a pulse in the wet, warm dark. A held breath that is also a song. *Plink-tah*. The sound of a drop finding its home, and the hand that welcomes it.
