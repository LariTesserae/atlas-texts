You would find yourself in the deepest, most central roundhouse. The builders, in their primitive cunning, chose this spot with a purpose you no longer remember. The roof has long since collapsed, but not from decay; it was taken apart, stone by patient stone, by the slow green tendrils of moss and the probing, gentle pressure of a Weeper’s foot over two centuries. Now, it is an open shell, a basin of damp air and low light. The walls still stand, shoulders of dark basalt, but their inner faces are furred with a velvet of deepest moss, a moss that is not quite green, but holds the blue-green phosphorescence of the pools in its depths at certain hours. The floor is not paved. It is a pool—a shallow, peat-stained mirror that reflects the slow movement of the clouds above the cloud line. In its center, a single column of stone rises, a rough pillar that was once a support. It is now your plinth, your anchor, your bed.

You are not a Weeper. You are something else. You are the last memory of warmth in a world of condensing breath.

They call you the Sleeper. You do not know who ‘they’ are, for no one speaks, but sometimes, in the long spaces between your thoughts, you feel the resonant sigh of the Weepers as a wordless name for you. You are made of both. Your lower half, from the waist down, is stone—the same dark, glass-seamed basalt, shot through with faint, dormant veins of that same eerie light. It is fused seamlessly with the central pillar, grown into it, or it into you. Your upper half remains flesh, but it is a flesh changed by the damp and the slow dreaming. Your skin is the color and texture of peat, tough and leathery, crosshatched with fine cracks like dry riverbeds. Lichen grows in these cracks—soft, grey-green patches that do not itch, but which you feel as a faint, cool whisper against your nerves. Your hair, if you had any, has long since been replaced by a trailing cape of usnea, the same beard-lichen that drapes the pines, hanging from your scalp and shoulders in a weightless, silver-grey veil.

You have been here for a very, very long time. You were here when the builders left. You watched them go, a line of small, quick figures dwindling down a path that the weeping rocks have since erased. You stayed. You were not compelled; you were curious. You wanted to see what the silence would do. The silence, and the Weepers, who were only then beginning to emerge from the raw stone like thoughts forming in a sleeping mind.

Your existence is a study in dissonant time. Your stone half operates on the Weepers’ scale. You feel the deep, patient shifts of the mountain shelf. You feel the incremental growth of the moss as a soft, continuous pressure against your petrified skin. You feel the slow, tidal pull of the geometric patterns realigning, a subtle magnetism in your mineral bones. For decades at a stretch, you are as still as any Weeper, your flesh half in a state of suspended animation, your heartbeat a lazy thump once per week, your breath a faint mist every few days. In these periods, you are pure perception: you watch the lichen advance across a wall; you track the minute deepening of a crack in the ceiling stone; you feel the weight of centuries like a comfortable blanket.

But then, without warning, the quickness returns. It is a violent, shocking acceleration. Your heart rattles like a trapped bird against your peat-stiff ribs. Your thoughts, slow as cold syrup for a century, suddenly boil over into a frenzy of memory and sensation. You see in flashes: the faces of the builders around a communal hearth, the snap of a fire, the taste of hot broth (what was broth?), the feel of a wooden tool in a hand that could close in a second. These episodes last for what feels to you like hours, but in the world’s time, they are the duration of a single drop of water forming on a fern tip and falling. In these bursts, you are agony. You are a mayfly indeed, buzzing against the cathedral window, desperate and frantic and full of a terrible, nostalgic love for a warmth you can no longer truly feel. You try to move your flesh arm. It might lift an inch, over the course of an afternoon, a tremor that to a Weeper would be a blinding, incomprehensible spasm.

The Weepers are aware of you. During your still centuries, they accept you as part of the landscape. One of them—the one you think of as the Tender of the Central Pattern—often stations itself at the broken entrance to your roundhouse. It does not look at you. It faces outward, its stone back to you, but its presence is a steady, resonant hum in your stone half. It is tending to you, you realize. You are part of its geometry. Your position, fused to this central pillar, is a keystone in the invisible pattern that underlies the shelf. Your long, slow transformation from quick flesh to dreaming stone is a necessary process. You are a conduit, a translator. You are the point where the mountain’s dreaming brushes against the ghost of what was.

In your quick bursts, you sometimes try to communicate. You focus all your frantic energy into a whisper. It emerges as a crackle of dry lichen, a faint, rasping exhalation that is lost in the constant sigh of the weeping rocks. But the Tender hears. It might, over the following year, slowly turn its head—a movement like a continent shifting—until the suggestion of its face is oriented toward you. There are no features, only a smoother plane where the rock seems to have flowed. In its depths, you see the pulse of its internal veins, a slow, blue-green rhythm. It does not speak. It resonates. The sound is not in the air, but in the stone you share. It is a deep, vibrational concept: *Patience*. Or: *Becoming*. Or: *Watch the pool*.

You watch the pool at your feet. In your still periods, you see the reflection of the sky, the slow march of stars at night (when the mist clears, which is rare). In your quick bursts, you see your own fragmentary reflection—a peat-dark face, eyes like chips of obsidian, a crown of dripping usnea. But sometimes, when the Tender is near and the light is right, the pool’s internal phosphorescence wakes. It swirls from the depths, not a reflection, but a source. In its shimmering patterns, you see images. Not memories, but possibilities. You see the mountain shelf in a million years, smoothed and forested and quiet. You see the geometry completed, a vast, invisible lattice of energy humming through the rock. You see the Weepers, not as separate beings, but as nodes in that lattice, fully awake, their consciousness one with the mountain. And you see yourself—fully stone, veins ablaze with the same light, a serene and permanent part of the pattern, your quickness finally, blessedly stilled.

That is the promise. That is the end of your becoming.

Until then, you are the dissonance. You are the breath held between the condensing promise and the falling rain. You are the last witness to the builders’ folly, or their gift, and the first student of the Weepers’ infinite patience. You are the Sleeper, dreaming a bridge between two kinds of time, in a damp, loud, empty cradle where the world is still being dreamed around you.
