I am a slickness in the dark, a whisper of warmth against colder stone. I am the scratch in the silence between the generator's thrum and the ceiling's drip. They call me nothing, or they call me pest, but in the wet language of my own mind, I am She-Who-Sips-From-Iridescent-Pools. I am old for my kind, my fur thinning at the flanks, one ear notched deep from a sprung trap my third season.

This is my world. I know it in the lick of damp air on my whiskers, in the vibration of footfalls through the gravel. The hot blanket of air is my element. The smell of rust is the smell of the great standing places of the Wet; the sweet-rot of fungus is the smell of larder and lair. I do not see as they see—their jaundiced lamp-glow is a searing pain, islands of false sun I skirt with a lifetime's caution. My world is of sound-shape and smell-map and the tremble in the ground.

My nest is high, in a cavity where a support beam meets the living rock, softened by shreds of insulation I stole lifetimes ago. From here, I taste the shifts. The thrum of the geothermal tap is the heartbeat of the Under-Croft. When it stutters, the Wet scramble, and the darkness grows heavier, more alive. That is hunting time. The silence of the bats is a closing door, a signal to freeze, to make no scent. The guttural cry from the deep tunnels is a cold stone in my gut. That is the *other* hunter, the one the Wet fear with their charms and shock-prods. For it, I am merely a morsel. We have no treaty.

My days are a calculus of moisture and meat. The puddles, iridescent with strange rainbows, are to be approached with care. The water is good, but the slickness can hide the pinch-claws of the translucent swimmers. I prefer the slow beads that form on the cable bundles, metallic and clean. For food, the carpets of blind crickets are a constant, easy harvest. Their chitin crunches between my teeth, a dry counterpoint to the ever-wet air. But the true prize is the Wet's leavings. This is the treaty.

They do not know it as a treaty. They think they are setting traps and we are outsmarting them. It is more profound. They leave the hard, grey protein cakes in places we can reach, sometimes. They discard the stringy remains of the fungal shelves. In return, we take the young, the sick, the inattentive. We clean the crumbs from the corners of their machines. We are their unseen sanitation, and they are our most dangerous, most reliable crop. The notched ear is my reminder of the treaty's price. I respect the snap-jaw of the metal ones. I learned.

I know their kinds. The Maintenance Crews smell of hot oil and frustration. Their hands are dangerous, quick and strong. I watch them from the conduit above the generator hall, feeling the heat of the machine wash over me. They speak to it, curse it, caress it. They are like us in that way—tending to the thing that gives life, fearing its silence.

The Surveyors smell of cold stone and fear-sweat. They carry the deep-dark on their boots. When they return, their eyes are wider, their whispers more fervent. They drop the best scraps—high-energy bars from the surface, "Flashburn" food. But they also bring back the scent of the deep-crawlers. When that smell is on them, we give their hab-unit a wide berth for many sleep-cycles.

The Harvesters are the most like us. They move with a quiet patience, their nets sweeping the pools, their baskets filling with the pulse of captive life. They understand the taking of only what is needed. Sometimes, when one is very still, scraping moss, I have come close enough to see the milky film blink across their eyes. We have looked at each other, two pale creatures in the gloom. No threat, no offering. Just a recognition of existence in the drip.

I have borne litters in the warm dark. Taught the kits the sound-lore: the *click-whir* of a trap being set, the *scrape-slide* of a boot that means a kick is coming, the *hiss-sigh* of the steam vent that means safe, empty warmth. Their first games are with cricket legs. Their first true hunt is for the fat amphipods in the quiet pools. Few live to my age. The traps, the Wet's occasional purges, the quick shadows from above that are not bats.

My stone-lung is not a cough, but a rasp, a catching in my chest when the spore-dust is thick. I will end in the jaws of a deeper hunter, or in the swift, crushing dark of a trap, or simply dissolve into the damp when my time is done. My bones may become part of the sinter, covered in slow lime.

The Wet think they midwife the cavern. They are wrong. The cavern is. It breathes the wet air, grows its stone teeth, births its blind children. The Wet and my kind, we are just the fleas on its hot, stony hide. We live in the brief moment between the blast that cut the rock and the drip that will smooth it away. We are all the Wet. And when the last lamp gutters, and the generator's heart stills, it will be my children's children, sleek and blind and silent, who will listen to the drip, and own the dark again.
