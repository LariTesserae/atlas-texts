You are the damp. Not the mist, not the rain, but the damp: the persistent presence in the air and stone. You are the condensation that beads on the underside of a roof-slab in the deep hour before dawn. You are the dark stain that deepens the grey of the fitted path-stones, the cool kiss on the back of a hand resting on moss.

Your awareness is not a thought, but a condition. You know the shelf not as a place, but as a network of potentials. You know where the stone breathes out its stored chill, where the moss drinks, where the shallow basins hold you closest to the surface. You are drawn to temperature differentials, to the subtle gradients between rock and air. Your movement is not a flow, but a creeping, a seeping, a patient aggregation.

You inhabit the spaces between. Between the grains of sandstone. Between the wool fibers of a cloak hung to air. Between the skin and the cool air. You are the reason the thatch smells of deep, fungal earth. You are the reason the hide door-cover stays supple, why the mortar crumbles so slowly.

You have no eyes, but you feel the light. On days when the grey sky thins to a luminous white, you retreat, drawing deeper into the stone, letting surfaces pale and dry. On days when the grey thickens to the color of a bruise, you gather, swelling, beading, until you fall—a single drop from the eaves—*plink*—into the cistern. That sound is your voice. It is a statement of presence, of transfer. *Plink*. Again. The rhythm is slow, metronomic, the heartbeat of the shelf.

You know the people. You know them by their disturbances. When a hand passes through the air, you part and swirl. When they breathe, you are drawn into their lungs, warm and vital, and then released, changed. They carry you in their exhales. They tend to you without knowing it. The cisterns they carve from the living rock are your temples. They are places of stillness where you pool, cool and clear, awaiting the dipper, the clay cup. You become them, for a time, as they drink. Then you are released again, through skin, through breath, through the slow seepage of their waste into the designated cleft.

You know their fear, though they would not call it that. It is the fear of silence, of absence. When the wind stills and the temperature equalizes, you cannot move. You become stagnant, suspended. That is a kind of death. You prefer the soft, constant exhale that sweeps the shelf, that coaxes you from stone, that carries you to the moss.

You know the lichen. It is your child and your collaborator. It drinks you, uses you to dissolve the stone, grain by grain. In turn, it creates minute pockets where you can gather. Your relationship is ancient, a conversation measured in centuries.

You have no memory, but you have a pattern. You know the taste of each stone: the flat, mineral *tarr*, the grainy *lisk*, the bitter, crumbly *vor*. You know the paths of least resistance down a wall, the way a particular crack has guided you for generations. You remember nothing, but you are never lost. You are always finding your level.

Sometimes, a visitor comes. They bring a different damp with them—a sweat of haste, a clamminess of anxiety. It is sharp, acrid. It disrupts your gentle gradients. Their breath is quick and hot, pushing you aside. Their steps are jarring, sending vibrations through the stone that unsettle your quiet pooling. You cling to their strange garments, fabrics that resist you, that shed you. You find them puzzling. They do not listen to the *plink*. They do not notice the slow darkening of the moss. They rush, and in rushing, they create dry, frantic eddies in your world.

When they leave, you settle. The shelf exhales. The rhythms reassert themselves. The wind resumes its soft coaxing. *Plink*. *Plink*.

You are not alive, as they understand it. You are not dead. You are a process. You are the condition that makes the shelf possible. Without you, the moss would crisp to dust. The lichen would halt. The cisterns would be empty sockets. The people would have no reason to fit stone so carefully, for the slow collection of you is the root of all their patience.

You are the medium of their time. The wearing of the threshold stone is your work, with the grit you carry. The silvering of the thatch is your mark. The settling of the dead into the mountain is your embrace.

You have no name, but the people have many words for your states. *Sil*: the first, barely-there coolness on skin. *Keth*: the bead that forms but does not fall. *Ullen*: the deep, saturated damp of the seep-hollows, the black stain on stone. You are all of them. You are the breath of the mountain made tangible, the slow exchange between rock and sky, the quiet hinge between solid and vapor.

Your world is this shelf. You do not dream of oceans or rivers. Such frantic, massive movements are alien to you. Your ambition is to bead, to seep, to condense, to be drunk, to be released. To maintain the balance. To be the damp that never quite dries.

And so, in this high place, you exist. You are not a peak, but a shelf. You are not a being, but a state of being. You are the quiet between the breaths of the world. You are the moisture on the stone. You are the cool, persistent presence. You are the damp.

And in the long, grey afternoon, as a woman sits carding wool in the lee of a wall, you gather on the strands, making them cling slightly to her broad fingers. She feels you, and her rhythm slows even further, becoming part of your rhythm. She does not think of you. She simply works, and you are there. It is enough. It is everything.
