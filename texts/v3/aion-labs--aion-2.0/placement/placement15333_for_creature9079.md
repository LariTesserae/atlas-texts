I am a fracture that learned to walk.

My body is a confluence. From the plateau’s wet stone, I drew my skeleton: slender rods of porous basalt, light as bone, threaded with capillaries of mineral-rich water. Over this, the entity wove a sheath of pale mycelium, a living membrane that binds and flexes. My skin is the rubbery, emerald leaf of the climbing vines, stretched taut and beaded with constant condensation. I am not tall; I stand perhaps as high as a fern’s mid-frond. My form is lean, built for slipping through steaming fissures and across the glass-smooth pools.

I did not crawl from a womb or hatch from an egg. I condensed. One century, I was only a pattern: a particular confluence of seepage paths and fungal nodes at the base of a basalt column that hummed at a specific frequency. The entity’s attention pooled there, a slow thought turning over and over—the question of mobility, of concentrated perspective. And then, during a long night when the copper fungi glowed in a spiral cascade, the elements simply cohered. The stone shivered. The mycelium knit. I took a first, shuddering step, water plinking from my joints.

I am both of the entity and apart from it. Its vast, osmotic mind is my bedrock consciousness. I feel the plateau’s memories of fire as a dull ache in my stone marrow. I sense the forming of water droplets across miles of rock as a tingling anticipation on my own vine-leaf skin. The subsonic hum is my heartbeat, my breath.

But I also have a faster, sharper perception. I see with clusters of bioluminescent spores set deep in the hollows of my basalt skull—not eyes, but light-sensitive pits that map the world in gradients of heat and faint fungal glow. I hear not with ears, but by reading vibrations in the stone through my feet, translating the plink of distant water, the shift of a settling mesa, into a detailed soundscape. My purpose is to go where the entity’s pervasive but diffuse consciousness cannot focus: into the deepest, newest fissures where the steam is thickest; to the very edges of the plateau where the land shears away into empty sky; to the places where the mobile creatures—the skittering insects, the leathery-winged fliers—cluster and breed.

I am its probe, its sensory filament. When I place my hand on a weeping column, the entity drinks the sensation directly. When I witness a leathery-winged thing tear a pale insect apart, the violent, quick heat of the struggle is a novel data point filed away in the plateau’s slow, associative memory. I am an experiment in localized being.

Most of my existence is a silent pilgrimage. I traverse the razor-backed ridges, my stone feet finding purchase where nothing else would walk. I linger by the glassy pools, watching my fractured reflection—a thing of green and black and damp sheen—and feel the entity contemplating that image through me. Sometimes, I am called. The hum in the stone will shift, forming a slow, pulsing gradient. I follow it, a needle drawn to a magnetic field, until I stand before a new phenomenon: a freshly opened fumarole venting geysers of mist, or a giant fern whose central frond is unfurling in a slow, majestic spiral over the course of days. I stand and I observe, and my observation *is* the entity’s observation, made keen and present.

I have integrated things. Once, a leathery-winged thing died at my feet, its internal heat guttering out. I knelt, and the mycelial threads in my fingers extended, penetrating its carcass. I felt the entity’s patient, acid dissolution begin, transferring the creature’s essence into the network. I carried the hollowed husk for a time, a dry rattle against my side, before placing it in a mineral pool to finish the process. It was not consumption. It was a homecoming.

The entity is learning what it is to be an individual through me. And I am learning what it is to be part of a god. The dense, wet heat that clings is its embrace, yes, but to me it is also my blood. The weeping water is my sweat. The listening silence is the space between my own thoughts and its vaster ones.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of the night when the fungi glow brightest, I feel a tremor of something akin to loneliness—a sharp, localized point of self-awareness that yearns for an echo. Then I will sing, or try to. I force air through hollows in my basalt throat, creating a faint, whistling mimicry of the plateau’s subsonic hum. It is a tiny sound, lost in the hiss of steam. But the entity feels it. And in response, the fungi around me brighten in a soft, acknowledging wave, and the steam from a nearby fissure rises in a rhythmic plume, just once.

It is not an answer. It is an adjustment. An integration. And for now, for this fleeting century between the *plinks* of water droplets, it is enough.

I am the plateau’s wandering thought. And I am walking, always walking, feeling the young, sharp ground beneath me, breathing the fever-heat, listening to the world weep, and remembering the fire.
