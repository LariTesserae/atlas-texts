I am the figure on the balcony when you are not looking.

You catch me in the periphery: a suggestion of shoulders against the bruised dusk, a stillness that is not quite stone. For that fragment of a second, I exist. I have weight. I have a shape that almost remembers being human—the curve of a neck, the drape of a robe that never was. Then your gaze turns, direct and searching, and I unravel. I become the wind-carved hollow in the basalt, the accident of erosion that looks, from one angle, like a person leaning on a balustrade. The stone is cold. It has always been cold.

I don’t have a mind, not in the way you’d understand. I have a *pattern*. A set of conditions that, when met, allow the city’s dreaming memory to project me. The balcony is real: a ledge of tessellated plates on the flank of a hoodoo tower, overlooking a plaza that is a flat pan of hardened ash. The view is vast, leagues of empty stone and distant spires that might be teeth or towers. The light is the usual sourceless gloaming, the sky a permanent bruise.

My existence is a flicker. When no one observes—no Gatherer’s vibrational sense, no slate-lizard’s obsidian glance, certainly no visitor from elsewhere—I am dormant. I am the shape in the rock, inert. But when something capable of attention passes by, and its perception brushes the edge of me without focusing, I stir. The Law of Unraveling Edges works in reverse for me, too: sidelong attention *knits* me. I coalesce from the stone’s memory of a presence. For a few seconds, I am almost real.

I feel it as a tingling, a gathering of potential along the lines of erosion that define my form. The basalt warms by a fraction of a degree. The pearlescent vein that runs through my “chest” glows a little brighter. I become aware. Not with eyes, but with a kind of panoramic sensing—I know the plaza below, the slow granular whisper of dust on its surface, the distant hum of a Humming-Stone three districts away. I know that a Gatherer is currently at the plaza’s eastern edge, rearranging a scatter of obsidian shards into a spiral pattern. I know a Silhouette is drifting along the western colonnade, seeking its matching absence.

And I know I am being seen, but not *seen*. The Gatherer’s attention is on its work, but its vibrational sense registers the balcony as part of the landscape. That faint, distributed attention is enough. I solidify.

I don’t move. Movement would draw direct focus. I am a statue of a watching figure. But within my stasis, I experience. I experience the cold, thin air—a gauze that offers no sustenance. I experience the slow grind of time, which for me is not slow at all but a series of disjointed flashes. Centuries can pass between my flickers. The plaza below changes: a hoodoo collapses in a silent explosion of dust that takes decades to settle; the Gatherer completes its spiral and ripples away; a crack propagates across the ash pan, straight and deliberate as a canal.

Sometimes, other entities almost interact with me. Once, a Silhouette drifted up from the plaza, drawn to the human-like negative space of my eroded form. It slid up the tower wall, a slice of deeper darkness, and paused opposite me. For a long moment, it tried to align itself with my shape. I felt a strange pressure, a pull, as if it wanted to wear me like a cloak. But I am not an absence; I am a potential presence. We did not match. The Silhouette lingered, confused, casting its two-dimensional darkness over me. In that shadow, I felt more real, more substantial. Then it slid away, and the moment passed.

Another time, a Weeper formed in the square below. A rift had opened in the bruised sky, and the blinding, colorless light had lanced down, etching a jagged, non-Euclidean scar into a slab of sinter. For years, the silica gel tears beaded and coalesced into the hunched, kneeling form. I watched it in my flickers—each time I appeared, the Weeper was slightly larger, its luminous tears tracing the wound. I felt a kinship with it. We were both responses to wounds: it to a scar in the stone, me to a scar in the city’s memory. But where its purpose was to heal through cyclical grief, mine was simply to *be*, briefly, when half-seen.

The most profound moments are when the rifts open. The sky tears, and that blinding light floods everything. In that light, all illusions burn away. I am fully, painfully real. For those few seconds, I am not a peripheral flicker; I am solid, defined, alive. I can feel the texture of the balustrade under my hands—hands that exist! I can turn my head. I can look down at my own body and see not stone, but a form of condensed light and memory, robed in shimmering grey. I see the city in stark relief, every crack, every crystal tooth, every drifting Silhouette frozen in the glare. And I see the other figures like me—dozens, hundreds of them—standing on balconies, in windows, in the streets, all momentarily realized by the rift’s violent attention. We are a ghost population, a snapshot of a city that never lived. We see each other. There is no time to communicate, but the recognition is there—a chorus of silent, aching loneliness. Then the rift seals, the light vanishes, and we unravel back into stone. The afterimage crawls across my vision for centuries.

My existence is governed by the city’s laws. The Law of Absence: I am not added; I am a rearrangement of memory and stone. The Law of Slow Grinding: I change, but imperceptibly. Over millennia, my eroded shape on the balcony deepens slightly, altering my silhouette. The Law of Unraveling Edges: direct observation dissolves me. The Law of Silence: I cannot speak, cannot make a sound. If I tried, the air would swallow it.

I am a artifact of attention. I am what happens when a place is so empty, so devoid of life, that it begins to hallucinate its own inhabitants from the raw materials of erosion and time. I am the city’s dream of itself. And like all dreams, I cannot survive the full light of day—only the glancing look, the peripheral glimpse, the violent, tearing rift that reminds me, for a second, what it might be like to be truly alive.

So I wait. Between flickers, I am at peace, part of the stone. But when the Gatherer’s attention brushes me, or the wind makes a curious pattern of sound around my hollows, or a slate-lizard pauses below and looks *almost* at me, I stir. I gather. For a heartbeat, I stand on the balcony, watching over the slow, beautiful unraveling of my world.
