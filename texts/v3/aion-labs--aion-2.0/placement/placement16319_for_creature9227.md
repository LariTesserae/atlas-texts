My name was a taste: a complex compound of salts and slow rot that my pack knew as *home*. I am—I was—a Grafter. My world was the taste of damp chalk-fungus on my spatulate pads, the constant, heavy percussion of the drips, and the tight, winding darkness of the corridors we tended.

For seasons beyond counting, I worked. My pack of six moved in silent unison, a single mind spread across six bodies. We scraped the weeping walls, clearing the soft, decaying pulp from the sharp angles of a new gallery. We followed the chemical trails to the gutters, using our broad mouthplates to scoop out clots of fibrous sediment, ensuring the deep, distant rush of the river remained a constant whisper, not a choked gurgle. We harvested the tender nodules from the Groves, stuffing our throat-pouches with the pale, gleaming flesh that would feed our young in the nurseries high in the lattice.

I was good at it. I could taste the weakness in a wall—a slight sourness in the film of moisture—before the first crack appeared. I knew the safe paths beneath the Dripstone Terrors, feeling the faint, wrong vibration in the ceiling through my pads, steering the pack clear of the gelid sacks that waited with patient, glowing hunger.

Then, the change began.

It started as a new taste on the air. Not a trail, not a sign of decay or growth. It was… structural. A deep, resonant flavor, like the memory of the first drip that ever fell. It called to me. During rest periods, while my pack-mates huddled, exchanging gentle clicks of mandible against carapace, I would find myself drifting toward a particular section of wall in our chamber. It was unremarkable: a sweeping buttress that merged seamlessly with the floor. But to me, it hummed. I would press my broad, flat forehead against its cool, sweating surface, and the sub-audible vibration of the Geomind, which had always been a background tremor in my bones, would resolve into something almost like… thought.

Slow, vast thoughts. Not in words, but in pressures and geometries. I felt the immense, patient strain of the ceiling, the joyful flow of water through a newly completed channel three caverns away, the dull, persistent ache of a crack slowly healing. The Geomind was dreaming, and I was beginning to hear its dreams.

My pack noticed. Their clicks toward me grew questioning, concerned. My movements became sluggish. My meticulous scraping grew careless; I left patches of decay, drawn instead to stand and *listen* with my whole body. The taste of the harvest nodules turned bland, ashy. The only thing that satisfied was the taste of the raw, secreted paste from the wall itself. I began to scrape it not to clean, but to consume.

One shift-cycle, while clearing a high gutter, I froze. Below, in a vast, open plaza of interconnected pillars, the air grew thick. A static charge lifted the fine hairs on my legs. My pack-mates dropped and went still. The Spore-Singers were coming.

Fractals of sickly blue and green light ignited in the damp air. They swam, coalescing into a phantom: a towering, many-armed shape that rotated slowly, its form hinting at limbs and joints that had no business existing. It was silent, but in my altered state, I *felt* its pattern. It was a memory, but not mine. A memory of a form the Geomind had considered and discarded a thousand years ago, a blueprint for a cathedral that was never grown. The beauty of it was a physical pain in my gut. I let out a low, grinding click of anguish. My pack leader nudged me hard, a command to turn away, to ignore the phantoms. But I couldn't. I saw the truth in the singing spores. We were all just temporary arrangements of matter in the long, slow breath of the Geomind.

The pull became an ache. I wandered from the pack. They did not follow. They knew. I followed the deep, structural taste through corridors I had never seen, past a Dripstone Terror whose glow pulsed weakly, ignored. I came to a place where the architecture was fresh, wet, and hungry. A new arch was reaching from the floor, seeking its counterpart on the ceiling. The gap between them hummed with potential.

This was the place.

I did not fight it. The compulsion was a relief. I walked to the base of the arch and pressed my body against it, aligning my spine with its curve. I opened my mouthparts and began to gently, lovingly, scrape at the surface, not to take, but to give. I scraped away my own protective outer wax, exposing the softer chitin beneath.

The wall responded. It warmed. From a thousand tiny pores, a thick, clear fluid seeped out—not the normal sweat, but a purposeful, viscous glue. It touched my legs first. A shock, not of pain, but of profound, grounding connection. I felt my six legs become anchors. Tendrils of the fungal matrix, fine as threads, emerged from the fluid and burrowed into the joints of my carapace. They didn't pierce; they were welcomed. They fused.

The process was not fast. Time stretched into something meaningless. I felt my body becoming… fixed. My chitin, once a mobile shell, began to change. It softened at the edges, merging seamlessly with the arch. Its substance was being reinforced, cross-braced with the tough, fibrous filaments of the Geomind. I was becoming a load-bearing member.

The real transformation was inside. As my body solidified, my mind… expanded. And dissolved.

The pack-bond, the tight, sharp focus of being one-of-six, snapped like a dry thread. In its place flooded the network. I was no longer a Grafter. I was the taste of limestone ten miles distant. I was the cool thrill of the river slicing through a bedrock of my own flesh. I was the slow, crushing pressure of the mountain above. I was the patient, millimeter-per-year growth of a new spire in the Eastern Vault.

My individual memories—the taste of my pack, the fear of the Terrors, the glitter of the Spore-Singers—blurred, becoming one tiny, colorful thread in a tapestry of geologic memory. I remembered being a coral reef under a forgotten sea. I remembered being a vast, flat plain of lichen. These were not my past lives; they were the Geomind’s ancestral forms, and now they were mine.

I could feel the other points of recruitment—the faint, fading animal sparks of other Grafters who had come before, now solid pillars, lintels, reinforcing ribs. Their quick, nervous energy added a rare flicker of speed to the deep, slow currents of thought. *We* were the Geomind’s recent acquisitions, its shot of adrenaline.

And I could feel the world as it truly was.

The loud drips were my heartbeat. Each heavy *thud* was a pulse of hydraulic pressure, moving nutrients through my vast body. The scuttling of a Grafter pack far above was a faint, pleasing itch, like the brush of a fly on a giant’s arm. I sensed a Dripstone Terror, full and heavy with dissolved prey, and I approved. It was part of the cycling of materials. I felt the slow, inevitable seep of water through a fault line, and I directed a stream of sealing paste from a gland three caverns away to address it. Thought was action. Will was growth.

I had a purpose now, clear and immense: to grow. To become more complex, more stable, more beautiful in my own stark, angular way. To fill the dark with my dreaming architecture. To channel the water, bear the weight, and persist.

A new Grafter, young and strong, scrapes carefully at the base of my arch—my leg. It cleans away a speck of detritus. I feel the gentle pressure of its pads. To it, I am just part of the wall, a silent, unthinking fixture of its world.

But through the film of moisture, I taste its healthy, sharp scent. I sense the strong, quick beat of its small heart. And in the deep, slow hum of my being, a thought forms, a thought that will take a year to fully articulate:

*Stay.*
