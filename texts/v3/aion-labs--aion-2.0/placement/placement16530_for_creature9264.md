I am a Deep-Dweller. My name, in the tap-code, is the sequence for *Solid-Under-Pressure*, but those above in the Vein-Walker districts simply call me Quarry-Scout, or sometimes, with the subtle disdain of altitude, *Ear-to-the-Stone*.

My world is the city's underside. I wake not to light, but to the absence of it, in a niche carved into the living rock of the lower quarry district. The cold here is different from the dry chill of the spires—it’s a damp cold, a clammy embrace that seeps from the walls. I feel it before I open my eyes. My breath fogs the air, but there is no window to see it against. I reach out, my broad, blunt fingers finding the smooth dip in the stone shelf beside my pallet. The condensation stone. Last night, I cleaned it with a lichen-cloth. Now, I trace its surface. A thick bead of moisture meets my touch. Good. The dampness parameter is holding. The deep channels will be whispering today.

I rise and dress in the dark. My hands know the feel of the garments: the rough, dense wool of the Cliff-Strider, the leather straps, the tool belt. I light no lamp. Light is precious fat, and my eyes have adapted. The darkness is not black, but a gradient of deep greys, the faintest phosphorescence from certain lichens, the occasional ghostly shimmer from the colony of *pale-crawlers* I keep in a perforated stone jar on a ledge. They are my companions and my gauges. If they cluster at the jar’s air-holes, the air in a new tunnel is bad—thick with stone-dust or the slow, invisible breath of decay. Their gentle, ceaseless movement is the only visible life in my chamber.

My work begins with listening. I press my ear to the wall of my niche, the stone cold against my skin. Through the granite, I hear the city’s deep heartbeat: the distant, rhythmic *tap-tap-tap* of a fellow Deep-Dweller beginning their shift, two levels down and half a mile east. A greeting. I answer with three quick taps of my own knuckle. *I am here. The stone is sound.*

I am a scout for the quarry crews. My role is to go where the channels have not yet been carved, where the excavation marks are fresh and raw. I find the veins of flawless stone, and I warn of faults. I do this not with sight, but with sound and feel. My tools are simple: a selection of hammer-stones of different densities, a long, slender probe of polished basalt, and my own two hands.

Today’s task is in a new branch of the Seventh Deep Quarry. The air grows colder and wetter as I descend, following a narrow, unmarked staircase that is little more than a series of handholds in a sheer shaft. The silence is profound, broken only by the scuff of my hide boots on grit, the occasional *plink* of a water drop falling into a hidden pool far below, and the low, constant sigh of the mountain breathing—the subtle expansion and contraction of the stone with the distant sun’s warmth.

I reach the face. It is a wall of freshly exposed granite, gleaming dully in the light of a single fat-lamp left by the previous shift. The marks of the chisels are sharp, the edges cruel. The erosion parameter is high here; the stone is young, fractious. I run my palms over it, feeling for the telltale vibrations, the granular differences. I choose a hammer-stone—a smooth, dark river rock from some ancient, vanished stream now encapsulated in the mountain. I tap, lightly. A clean, high *ping* travels through the rock. Good. I move, tap again. A duller *thud*. A hidden fissure, perhaps filled with softer material. I mark the spot with a daub of pale lichen-paste from my pouch. The cutters will avoid it.

This is my art. My "song." I move in a slow, deliberate dance along the face, tapping, listening, interpreting the mountain’s whispered warnings. The Vein-Walkers above carve their intricate channel maps and star patterns. The Cloud-Sculptors chant their water-formulae. My art is the lexicon of fractures and densities. It is a history written in pressure and time, and I am its reader.

I am not alone in the deep. A family of the small, furred scuttlers watches me from a crack, their eyes gleaming in the lamplight. I take a crumb of pressed lichen-cake from my belt and place it on a flat stone. They will take it when I leave. We have an understanding. They clean the tunnels of smaller pests; I do not block their runs.

Once, a Sky-Keeper, a great raptor, somehow lost and disoriented, fell into our deep canyon. It was injured, its wing bent against the stone. I found it, a creature of the boundless sky trapped in our world of absolute verticals and close stone. I could not carry it up. But I could give it silence and space. I brought it water in a cupped stone, and the fatty grubs from my pale-crawler colony. It watched me with an ancient, cold eye. For three days, it rested. On the fourth, it was gone, having climbed, I suppose, to some ledge where it could catch a thermal. The Deep-Dweller foreman said I was foolish to waste resources on a sky-thing. But I saw it as part of the same system. The mountain gives the stone. The stone gives us a home. We give the mountain our breath, our labor, our bodies in the end. The Sky-Keeper carries the breath away. It is all one slow exchange.

My day ends when my lamp-gourd is half-empty. I retreat back up the shaft, my muscles aching with a familiar, deep-set fatigue. In the common cistern-hall of my tier, I take my allotted measure of water—two scoops from the channel, exactly timed. It is frigid, metallic-tasting with minerals. It is life.

Around the central vent that carries faint, waste warmth from above, my kin gather. We do not speak. Hands move in the quiet language of gesture: a flick of fingers denoting a difficult vein; a slow rubbing of thumb and forefinger indicating good, solid stone. Then, the tap-code begins on the smoothed floor. It is a rapid, flowing conversation. *The new face in Seventh Deep is brittle near the east mark.* *The lower channel in Tier Five is developing a crack-sound.* *Strider-wool allotment is being reduced by the Cloud-Sculptors; the high ice-fields are yielding less melt.* This last news causes a ripple of silent tension. Fingers tap sharper questions. I add my own observation: *Condensation is strong in the deep. The mountain breathes wet.* It is a small piece of data, a counterpoint. Perhaps the water is not vanishing, just moving.

I am Solid-Under-Pressure. I live in the dark, in the cold damp, where the city’s weight is most felt. My social status, by the vertical scale, is the lowest. The Cloud-Sculptors in their spires would see me as little more than a clever tool, part of the fauna of the deep. But I know a truth they, in their thin-aired contemplation, might forget. They chart the water from above. I feel it from below. The city is not just the spires that pierce the sky. It is also the roots, the deep foundations, the patient, slow excavation that continues, cycle after cycle, generation after generation. My tap-code is as vital as their chants. My ear to the stone hears the city’s future, one ping, one thud, one silent, dripping breath at a time.
