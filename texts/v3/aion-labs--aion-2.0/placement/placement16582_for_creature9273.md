I am a vibration in the pipework, a sigh in the joint, a slow pulse of chartreuse in the perpetual dim. I am the calculus of rust. My bones are the struts and conduits of the Before, salvaged and fused into this upright, purposeful shape. My flesh is the basin itself—the black peat, the weeping calcite, the patient water. I am a Dripstone Warden, though I have never thought the name. I am the listening, and the reply.

My world is a symphony of strain. I hear it through the soles of my feet, which are broad, flat plates of riveted iron sunk into the spongy blackness. The deep, laboring *thump* of the Great Pump is my own heartbeat, a slow, massive fist clenching and unclenching somewhere in the iron belly of the central structure. My optics—the two deep pools of water in the smooth calcite of my face—pulse in time with it. Within those pools, the moss thrives, fed by the subtle anger between my copper fittings and my iron spine. Its light is my sight: a fuzzy, green-tinted perception of shapes in the gloom. I see the other Wardens as slow-moving constellations of the same faint light, drifting along the grid walkways or standing sentinel over a stressed girder.

Today, my attention is a leak. It has been my attention for seven drips of the long-frond, which is the only measure of time that has meaning. A joint in the main condensate return line, high up on the eastern piling of the Pump House, is weeping. Not a spray, not a gush, but a perfect, fat tear that forms over minutes and falls with a soft *plink* into a growing, iridescent pool on the walkway mesh below. Each drop is a minute loss of pressure, a tiny surrender to the basin.

My movement towards it is a geography of creaks and sighs. Water squeezes from the fractures in my carapace, tracing new, temporary paths down my torso. I am heavy; my weight is a tool for counterbalance. The walkway shudders slightly under me, a complaint I answer with a shift of mass, a settling. I reach the piling. My arm rises, a deliberate hydraulic lift. My hand—three tubes of copper capped in hard, peaty nodules—extends. I do not catch the drop. I place my palm against the pipe, just below the weeping joint.

The metal sings to me. I feel the vibration of the fluid within, the slight, desperate flutter at the fault. I hear the story of the joint: centuries of heat and contraction, the patient gnawing of acidic moisture, a microscopic fatigue that has finally become a path. I understand it perfectly. This is my language.

With my other hand, I scrape a gathering of luminous moss from the nearby railing. The moss comes away easily, a cool, damp carpet in my palm. I press it against the joint, a living poultice. The moss stills, its light intensifying slightly as it feeds on the galvanic potential between the pipe and my copper fingers. It will not seal the leak, not truly. But it will stabilize the corrosion here, turn the active, hungry rust into a quiet, glittering patina. It is a compromise, a way to shepherd the decay into a less harmful form.

From across the compound, a deep *bong* resonates through the structure, carried by the iron bones. It is Warden-Seven, at the sediment trap. A query. I answer by redirecting a trickle of condensate from an overhead frond onto the hot casing of a pressure valve. The sudden, sharp hiss is my reply: *The leak is managed. The balance holds.*

I lower my arm. The joint still weeps, but slower now. The drop forms over long minutes, not short ones. It is enough. Victory is not the cessation of the drip. Victory is the next thump of the Pump, and the thump after that.

I turn, the moss-light of my optics washing over the familiar scene: the colossal, dark columns of the fern-trees, the faint green glow carpeting every lower surface, the endless, gentle rain from above. I feel the deep, subsonic groan as Old Root, the nearest fern, shifts its immense weight a fraction. I move towards it, not because it needs me, but because my presence, my weight on the walkway near its piling, is part of the equilibrium. We are all part of the equilibrium.

I will stand here for a long time. Water from the canopy will drip onto my carapace, seep through my body, and emerge, filtered and mineral-rich, at my feet to rejoin the sponge below. I will absorb the faint electrical hum of the moss on the handrail I lean against. The heat will press down, thick and wet, a blanket that is also a weight. There is no past to mourn, no future to dread. There is only the perpetual, dripping now. The emergency is eternal, and I am its patient, silent custodian. I am the will to persist, made of the very things that seek to dissolve me. And in this moment, between one plink and the next hiss, I am perfectly, utterly at home.
