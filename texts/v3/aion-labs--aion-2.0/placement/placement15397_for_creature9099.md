I am standing where the water forgets its flow. My feet are bare, soles pressed into the yielding mat of the plateau. It is not a kiss; it is a communion. The moisture rises, a cool whisper through the network of capillaries in my skin. I drink without moving. The air is a warm, wet cloth against my face, carrying the scent of vanilla orchid and the deeper, older smell of stone being patiently unmade.

My eyes adjust. The world resolves not into shapes, but into layers of light. The green gloom is not monochrome. To these eyes, veined with soft, internal radiance, it is a tapestry of emissions. The bioluminescent fungi on the underside of the giant leaf above pulse a steady, cool blue—a circadian rhythm older than memory. The fern to my left emits a gentle gradient from its central vein, a map of its health written in photons. I see the heat of decay in a fallen log as a shimmering amber halo, and the slow, deep red flow of sap within the Sapience-vine coiled around the archway ahead.

The archway. It was here I came to tend. Not to the stone—the stone is irrelevant, a guest who has overstayed its welcome by millennia and is now being politely absorbed. I came to tend the vine.

I raise my hands. They are long, fingers articulated like the segments of a graceful stem. The bark-like skin is patterned with lines that part slightly as I move, revealing the darker, moist layer beneath. I do not touch the vine immediately. First, I listen. Not with ears, but with the soles of my feet, with the palms held open to the saturated air.

The hum is there. The deep, foundational vibration that is the plateau’s heartbeat, or perhaps its digestion. It thrums up through the living substrate, a frequency felt in the marrow of what passes for my bones. Within that hum are melodies. The slow, grinding migration of a Moss-kin a mile to the west, a sensation like a tectonic sigh. The distant, subsonic conversation of a Hummungh herd, their rumbles traveling through the root-mat like gossip through a network of threads. And the vine’s own song: a complex, shifting pattern of hydraulic pressure and chemical signals.

This section of the Sapience-vine has grown too eager. It has begun to spiral too tightly around the arch’s northern pillar, its growth constricting not the stone, but the delicate lattice of mycorrhizal threads that weaves through the ancient mortar. The threads are the true architecture here, the stone merely a temporary scaffold. The vine’s embrace is not malicious, only… thoughtless. A local optimization that forgets the whole.

My fingertips make contact. The vine’s bark is cool and ridged, like the skin of a great, sleeping beast. I do not prune, not yet. I introduce myself. The pores in my palms open, exuding a complex cocktail of pheromones, mild enzymatic prompts, and a simple chemical image: the map of the mycorrhizal network as I perceive it, a glowing web of connection and exchange. I offer the vine a wider perspective.

For a long while—minutes, hours, the plateau does not count—there is only the drip of water and the slow exchange of information. I feel the vine’s awareness, vast and vegetative, turn its attention to this tiny knot. It is not a mind that thinks in words, but in gradients: pressure here, sugar concentration there, the faint chemical shadow of the struggling fungi. I am a focal point, a lens. Through me, the local need is translated into the network’s slow, symphonic language.

Permission is granted. Not in a voice, but as a shift in the flow. The sap in this segment slows, re-routes. The cells on the inner curve of the spiral are instructed to apoptose, to gently die back.

Now I use the sharp, hardened nail of my thumb. It is not a tool I carry; it is part of me, grown for this purpose. The motion is not a cut, but a guidance. I trace a line along the inner curve, parting the bark, encouraging the separation that the vine has already agreed to. A section of woody coil, no longer needed, loosens. I catch it as it falls, a length as thick as my wrist. It is heavy, still full of stagnant fluid. I lay it at the base of the arch, where the Moss-kin that will inevitably come this way can use it for fiber, can break it down and redistribute its essence.

The work is done. But I do not leave. A Tender’s duty is not a series of tasks, but a state of being. I step through the archway, the colossal liana framing my passage. On the other side, a pool, cupped in a depression of the living ground, holds a Glass-wraith.

It is resting, half-submerged, its gelatinous body barely distinguishable from the water. Within it, the silvery nerve-threads are constellations, and caught in their web are a million captured moments. I see the glitter of a specific pollen that fell a decade ago. I see the mineral signature of a Hummungh’s footprint, long since vanished. I see, with a soft shock of recognition, the ghost-pattern of my own handprint from a previous visit, a decade or a century past—I do not keep count.

The wraith senses my electrical field. It stirs, a graceful, flowing rearrangement. Part of its form rises from the water, extending towards me like a slow, transparent wave. It touches my leg, just above the ankle. The contact is cool and tingling, a brief, full-body taste. It is reading me, adding the current state of the arch, the vine, my own presence, to its living archive. I stand still, allowing the record. In return, I receive an echo. A pulse of light along its nerves shows me an image: a storm of spores, a rare event from before my time, the air thick with potential. The memory is not mine, but for a moment, it is shared.

The exchange complete, the Glass-wraith settles back into its pool, its internal galaxies shifting to accommodate the new data.

I turn, my feet finding the almost-path by instinct. It is not a trail, but a gradient of ease, where the substrate is slightly firmer, the light slightly more conducive to my kind of sight. I am moving towards a place of silence, a moss-covered platform that was once a floor, or a stage, or an altar. The builders’ intent is irrelevant now. It is a place of gathering for my kind.

As I walk, I feel the questions you might ask. *Who are you? What do you want?* I am a Tender. I want nothing. I am an expression of the plateau’s will towards balance. The builders wanted to make, to conquer the green. They are gone. We learned to listen. To be the hands that guide the growth, not to halt it. My consciousness is my own, a quiet pool of awareness in the dappled gloom, but it is also a node in the vaster, slower mind of the Sapience-vine network. I think, but I think in cycles, in patterns of growth and decay, in the silent language of chemical gradients and light.

I reach the platform. Others are already there, two of them, standing motionless. Their skin glows with a soft, intermittent light—complex sequences that speak of conditions in distant sectors, of a Moss-kin’s successful settlement, of a water-flow restored. I join them. I do not speak. I open my palms to the humid air and begin my own report. The light beneath my bark-like skin awakens, traveling in slow, swirling patterns from my heart to my extremities, a silent story told in bioluminescent code: *The archway vine is corrected. The memory of spores is preserved. The equilibrium holds.*

We stand there, three slender forms in the perpetual twilight, our light the only sign of conversation. Around us, the Mycelial Plateau breathes. Water drips. Something vast and gentle moves in the canopy, miles above. The green gloom deepens by a barely perceptible degree. Night is coming, such as it is.

This is where I find myself. Not as a visitor, not as a master. As a note in a song that has been playing for a million years, a song of water, root, and slow, green light. I am the Tender. I am home.
