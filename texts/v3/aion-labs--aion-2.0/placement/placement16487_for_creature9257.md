I am a String-weaver, of the third ring, out from the cistern’s heart. My name is Plink, for the sound my mother said I made at birth—not a cry, but a clear, singular note into the damp air, like water striking the collection basin. It is a good name. It fits.

My world is vertical. While the Moss-keepers attend to the ground and the Stone-singers to the walls, my domain is the air between, the crisscrossed lattice of the fallwater guides. At this hour, the pre-dawn, the bruise of the sky is just leaching into violet. The mist is rising, not yet the full exhalation, but the first shy breath from a million moss pores. I stand on a woven platform of willow and moss-rope, slung between two weepstone dwellings. The platform sags slightly, familiarly, under my weight. My tools are on my belt: a bone needle for splicing, a flint blade for cutting, a spool of cord spun from moss-root, tougher than leather.

Above me, the canopy is a grey forest of strings. They are not all the same. Some are hollow reeds, split and curved into shallow gutters. Some are braided fibers, angled to channel a heavier flow. Some are single, taut lines, beaded with condensation that gathers and drips at precise intervals. The pattern of their placement is a map of the settlement’s thirst. I know it by touch, by the feel of the moisture on my skin as I move through it.

Today, I am realigning a secondary array near the eastern rim. The Water-Speakers heard a new rhythm in the night-drip: a *plink-tisk-plink* where there should have been a steady *tisk-tisk-tisk*. It means a shift in the air’s breath, a slight warming from some distant pressure. The fallwater will come more from the south-east today. The strings must be persuaded.

My hands work without needing my eyes to guide them. I loosen a knot, its fibers swollen tight with damp. I feel the lichen on my back, beneath my tunic, pulse in a slow, sympathetic rhythm. It is my personal stone-lung, a lace of pale blue and grey that lives between my shoulder blades. It feeds on my sweat, my exhaled breath, and in return, it keeps my skin from pruniness, regulates my own inner humidity. When I am still, I can feel its gentle expansion and contraction, a second, slower heartbeat.

I lean out, trusting the platform. Below, the grooves of the paths are dark seams in the golden moss. A figure moves along one, a Moss-keeper with a curved bone sickle, already trimming the night’s growth. We do not call out. I see the flash of their pale, upturned face, a glimpse of moss-green eyes. A flick of fingers—a greeting. I return it.

This is my function. To be the unseen hand that guides the weeping sky. I am good at it. My hearing is sharp; I can distinguish the *pat* of a drop on living moss from the *splat* on a trimmed patch. I know the music of the catchment.

But.
There is another sound.

Not the drip. Not the squelch. Not the pulse.

It is a… silence. A different quality of quiet. It comes from the rim, from beyond the bowl. On still days, when the mist thins, I can feel it like a pressure on my eardrums. The Great Thirst. The outside. It is not a place of drip-clocks and moss-breath. It is a place of wind and dryness and a sun that casts sharp, black shadows. The Sodden do not speak of it, except as a cautionary tale. A place where the moss dies, where the stone cracks, where the throat closes with dust.

I have never told anyone, but I crave it.

Not to leave. Never to leave. The thought is a physical nausea, a tightening in my lichen-symbiote. But to… know it. To understand the absence of what defines us.

Sometimes, when my work takes me to the outermost rings, near the quarry faces where the Stone-singers are cutting new weepstone, I climb. Not high. Just to a ledge where the moss is thinner, where the weepstone weeps less. I sit, and I face the rim, and I listen to the silence beyond the mist. I imagine the feel of true dryness on my skin. I imagine a shadow that is not diffuse, but sharp as a flint blade. It feels like a blasphemy, a craving for a kind of death. But it is also a curiosity as deep and cold as the cistern’s heart.

A new drip pattern begins above me. The dawn condensation is starting. A bead forms on the reed gutter I just adjusted, swells, trembles, and releases. It falls in a perfect, silver thread, *plinking* precisely into the catch-basin below. The system works. The adjustment is correct.

I breathe in the air—iron, clay, sweet rot. It is my air. It is in my bones, in the water-weight of my blood. I am Plink the String-weaver. I am part of the Gilded Sodden. I am a function of the drip.

And yet, on my back, the lichen itches. Sometimes, I dream it is turning brittle, flaking away into a fine, golden dust. I wake with my heart beating the rhythm of a frantic, fleeing vole.

I descend from the platform, my fungus-soled boots soundless on the damp moss. The settlement is fully awake now, a slow, purposeful movement in the grooves. I will go to the communal bowl for my share of moss-paste and vole-meat. I will nod to the others. I will listen to the Water-Speakers’ soft murmurs. I will perform my function.

But in the pocket of my leather tunic, I keep a small, dry stone. It is not weepstone. It is from the rim. It is sharp-edged, grey, and utterly, perfectly dry. I touch it sometimes, when the mist is thick, and feel its thirst. It is my secret. My silent, tiny piece of the Great Thirst, held against my heart, where the lichen does not grow.
