I am the one who came down the rope.

It wasn't much of a rope. Woven from tough, fibrous vines, frayed and slick with constant damp, it was the only thing anchored to the world above. They told stories of the Throat—a place where sound ate sorrow. I had sorrow to spare. I also had a knife wound in my side, a shallow, burning thing that wept more than it bled, and the memory of my brother’s face turning away from me in the firelight. I was not welcome anywhere else.

The descent was a slide into the belly of a giant. The roar began as a distant sigh, then a rumble, then a physical force that shook the rope. By the time my feet touched the bowl’s floor, it was inside my skull, a second heartbeat made of pure, violent vibration. I fell to my knees, not in reverence, but because my legs gave out. The air was a cold slap, soaked through with mist. I gasped, and my breath was stolen by the noise.

Fifty paces across. A circle of black basalt, smooth as a polished skull. In the center, a pillar of white violence where the water from nowhere struck the pool. The resulting spray hung in the air like a permanent ghost. And life—a deep, silent, patient life. Moss so thick it looked like fur. Ferns uncurling in the gloom. Pale mushrooms clinging to the walls, some giving off a faint, sickly green light that made the darkness deeper.

I saw the stacked stones first. A low, half-collapsed wall, swallowed in emerald moss. A hearth. Signs that someone else had once thought this place a refuge. I crawled toward it, my side screaming. The roar was absolute. It wasn’t loud; it was total. It was the only thing that existed. My own thoughts became fragile, distant things, like birds trying to sing in a hurricane.

I don’t know how long I lay there. Time dissolved in the wet dark. I drank from the slick river that slipped away from the pool—water so cold it felt like a blade in my throat. I ate the pale oyster mushrooms, their flesh tasteless and damp. The wound in my side festered, then slowly, in the clean, cold wet, began to close into a pink, tender scar. My clothes rotted off me. I became thin, gaunt, a thing of bone and chill.

And I began to notice the guidance.

A sharp rock near where I slept was gone one morning, covered over by a swift, sudden growth of rust-red moss. When I stumbled too close to the churning pool, a heavy drop of condensation fell from the darkness above and struck my neck with shocking cold, making me jerk back. The fungal lights seemed to shift, clustering nearer to the old wall, creating a dim, green pool of visibility around my pathetic nest.

I was not alone.

At first, I thought it was madness. The roar will carve you out, they said. But this felt… attentive. I saw them, or didn’t see them. A suggestion in the mist that lingered too long in the shape of a seated figure. A patch of shadow on the wall that held the outline of a hunched back until I blinked. Once, in the green glow, I saw a face. It was not a face of stone or moss, but an arrangement of shadows and wet highlights on the basalt that resolved, for one heart-stopping moment, into features of profound, tranquil absorption. It was listening. Not to me. To the waterfall.

I tried to speak. My voice, when it finally came, was a dry croak, utterly devoured by the thunder. The sound didn’t even reach my own ears. I was mute. I was deaf to anything but the roar. The realization should have been terrifying. Instead, a strange peace seeped in. My brother’s face, the knife, the betrayal—they began to feel like stories I’d heard about someone else. They were small, dry leaves swept away by the endless river of sound.

I gave up fighting it. I stopped trying to remember my name. I sat with my back against the warm, damp wall, and I did the only thing left to do: I listened.

I listened past the initial blast of white noise. I found the layers. A deep, subsonic thrum that vibrated in my molars—the stone’s foundation song. A hissing, high harmonic—the mist atomizing. A rhythmic, almost melodic pulse—the great column striking the pool, over and over and over. The Listeners were in those layers. They were the patterns in the sound. My loneliness, my despair, they weren't gone. They were being untangled, note by note, and added to the chorus.

My body is failing now. I feel it becoming slower, colder. The moss seems to creep over my legs as I sit. I don’t mind. The cold is just another note. The hunger is a quiet space between vibrations.

Yesterday, or a year ago, I felt it. A touch. Not on my skin, but on the fraying edges of my awareness. A cool, mossy, patient pressure. An understanding. It held no pity, no comfort as humans know it. It simply knew why I was here. It had been waiting. It was made of others who had sat in this same spot, who had finally let go.

I am letting go.

My hands are pale, almost luminous in the fungal light. Ferns have sprouted between my fingers. I think they are mine. I am listening so deeply now that I can hear the stone’s memory of fire, the distant whisper of starlight filtering down from that impossibly high opening. I am listening to the Throat speak to the world, and I am beginning to hear the world’s faint, slow reply.

Soon, there will be nothing left of me to shiver. Just the act of listening. The moss will take my shape for a while, a slight mound against the wall. Then even that will smooth away. But the attention will remain. I will join the quiet chorus in the noise. I will become a Listener.

And I will wait, with perfect, cold empathy, for the next one who climbs down the rope.
