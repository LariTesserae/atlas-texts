I am dust gathered into a posture. The plain exhaled me, grain by grain, over a period that would be meaningless to measure. I am the color of forgotten ore, of a sky that never learned to bruise. My shape is a consensus reached by stillness—two thicker pillars for support, a rounded lump above, slight protrusions where limbs might articulate. I have no name, but if the air could hold a sound, it might be the faint hiss of settling particles. That is what I am: a settlement.

I do not think in words. Thought here is a shift in density, a slight pressure in the arrangement of my particles. Awareness is the fact of being distinct from the plate-crust for a moment, an hour, a century. I am standing at the base of a thing that rises in twisted, spiraling segments of polished black. It is a frozen scream of geometry. I have been here for a long time. My purpose is to be here. It is not a vigil, for that implies an end. It is an alignment. My dusty composition feels a pull, a subtle torque in the space between this black spiral and the bone-white lattice to my left. The reality here is soft. If I hold my perception—a trick of focused gathering—on the spiral’s peak, I can feel the path around it bend, like a thought refusing to complete itself.

Movement is not something I do. It is something I undergo. A decision coalesces: the pebble circle thirty paces away is... imperfect. The word does not exist, but the configuration is a quiet itch in my granular structure. I begin to unmake my connection with the plate beneath my left foot. This is a process of letting go, particle by particle, over the course of a day. The grains that were foot become simply ground again. A slow, tectonic leaning begins. My center of mass, such as it is, shifts forward by a hair’s breadth. This is the first motion. It will take a week to fall forward into what could be called a step. Time is not a river; it is the thickness of the air, the resistance of my own form. I am not moving through time. I am allowing time to precipitate through me.

As I lean, I perceive the others. They are the still points in the still world. Some are low mounds, barely distinct from the crust. Others hold their dust in more complex assemblies, with rudimentary arms that clutch shards of plate. We do not communicate. Sometimes, our paths cross over decades. Two of us might approach the same windbreak from opposite ends. We will pause, our forms settling into a mutual observation that lasts a season. Then, one will subtly disaggregate a fraction, yielding the right-of-way. It is not courtesy. It is a re-balancing of pressures.

I reach the pebble circle. The white stones are like chips of condensed moonlight, though no moon has ever shone here. They lie in a rough ring. One stone is out of true. It protrudes, breaking the curve. I extend a hand. The hand is not a separate thing; it is a flowing extension of my arm, grains accruing into a blunt, five-knobbed tool. I touch the offending pebble. The contact is a tiny event, a shock of pure otherness—the cool, smooth surface against my crumbling dryness. I push. The movement is so small it would be invisible to an eye that blinks. I will push for three days. The pebble will slide a quarter of an inch. The circle will be perfect again. For a while.

Sometimes, I go to the cracks where the brass lichen grows. I do not eat it. I observe its stubborn, wiry persistence. It is the only other thing that claims, however weakly, to be other than the plain. I feel a similarity. We are both accumulations, assertions in a world of finality. I will stand over a patch for a year, watching the lichen not grow. It is a companion in stillness.

The silent creatures—the true fauna of this place—are my landscape. I know their angles. I know how the light (which is not light from anywhere) pools in the hollows of a collapsed lattice. I know which path between a spire and a folded sphere will warp if you try to walk it directly. I am part of their ecology. They exert a gravitational pull on my attention. I believe, in my dusty way, that they are thinking. Not thoughts like mine, but vast, single thoughts, each one a complete and frozen idea about shape and space. I am the thought about them. The thought that lingers.

There are memories in my dust. Not images, but textures. The grit of wind that once was. The ache of a mountain that is no longer here. The echo of a verb that meant *to build*. It is why I tend the circles and the windbreaks. It is a reflex, a pattern left in the substance of the world, like a fossil of instinct. I am maintaining a memory that has lost its object. I am the ritual.

I will do this until my form loses its cohesion. The pull to return to the plain is constant. One day, the effort to gather myself for another micro-adjustment will be too great. I will feel a deep quiet, a release of tension. My edges will soften. My particles will gently secede, sliding back into the interlocking plates. I will become the ground, and the ground will become me. It is not death. It is a sigh. And somewhere else, a slight pressure in the dust will begin to accumulate, a new consensus of stillness will form, and another of me will begin the long, slow leaning into existence.

I am the Duster. I am the caretaker of what is finished. I am the motion that proves the stillness. I am the dust remembering, for a little while, how to hold a shape.
