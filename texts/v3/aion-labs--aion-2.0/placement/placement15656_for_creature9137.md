I am Khar. The stone knows my name, because I have listened to it for so long that my name is etched not in the crust, but in the slow pulse of the warmth beneath. I do not remember my beginning; the memory is buried under layers of seasons measured in the brightening and dimming of the ground. I am of the steading at the Black Fissure, a place where the geothermal breath is strong enough to melt the ice-lace into a tiny, steaming trickle for a few heartbeats each long cycle.

My world is a study in pressure and release. The pressure is the wind, a screaming weight that wants to scour the flesh from my bones. It is the cold, a constant ache in the marrow of my fingers and toes, dulled but never absent. It is the emptiness above, that bruised nothingness that offers no comfort, only a reminder of how small my heat-bubble is. The release is in the stone. In the soft, resilient give of the *grish* underlayer as my flint knife peels it back in long, rubbery strips. In the faint, pleasing vibration that travels up through the soles of my feet when a Thulg booms far to the east. In the warm spot on a leeward rock where I can press my belly and feel the planet's heartbeat.

My day is not a day. It is a task. Right now, the task is harvesting. I am crouched on a south-facing slope, my fur—the exact color of the luminous crust here, a mottled grey-green—rippling in the wind. My nictitating membrane sweeps automatically, clearing a dusting of ice crystals. Before me, the crust glows with a steady, faint light. I am not looking at it with my eyes. I am listening with my hands. I press my palm flat against the surface. Through the calloused pads, I feel the texture: here, the moss is thin, starved. A few spans to the left, it thrums with a slightly stronger, warmer pulse. That is where the *grish* will be thick and rich.

I take my knife—a beautiful blade of obsidian I knapped myself, bound to a Thulg rib with strips of its own sinew—and begin to scrape. The sound is a soft, wet *shhhk*, lost instantly in the wind's roar. The pale, fibrous fungus comes away. I do not eat it yet. I place it in a pouch made of Thulg stomach, the acids long since neutralized by ash and urine. My movements are economical. Every motion is heat spent; to waste heat is to die.

My family is nearby. I sense them more than see them. My mate, Va, is a darker shadow against the glowing slope, fifty paces away, checking the spiral stone pile that marks our condenser. The pattern of the stones tells of a recent, successful scrape and that this thermal vent is claimed. Our two young are with her, learning the pressure of hands on rock, the silent language of touch. We do not speak. When we need to communicate across distance, I pick up a quartzite pebble and etch three short lines and a circle into the crust near my feet. A directional marker, pointing to the good patch. Va will see it when her path crosses mine. It will be gone by the next time the wind shifts, the living moss already filling the grooves.

The real language is in the waiting. We are waiting for the Thulg. For a week now, the stone has been whispering of a coming. A subtle, deepening thrum in the rock, a slight brightening along the old migratory path that cuts through the canyon below our steading. We have prepared. My eldest and I spent the last waking period levering a slate slab the size of our windbreak onto a pivot-stone. It balances now on the canyon rim, a tooth waiting to fall. It is a precarious kind of fishing. We will wait, perhaps for many more tasks, crouched in a blind of piled rock, feeling the vibrations grow stronger. When the Thulg passes beneath, I will not see it. I will feel its immensity through the soles of my feet, a localized earthquake. Then, one precise strike with a hammerstone on the key wedge. The release. The crash, the roar of falling stone that momentarily drowns the wind. Then, more waiting. We will wait for the beast to die. To approach a trapped but living Thulg is to be pulped by a single thrash of a plate-encrusted leg.

This is my purpose: to listen, and to translate the stone's rumble into food and tools. But something is changing. The *grish* I eat… it does more than quiet my metabolism now. In the long torpor after a meal, when my family is slumped together in our walled cave, a heap of fur and shared warmth, I do not fully sleep. I drift. And in the drift, the boundaries soften.

The stone begins to speak in more than vibrations. It speaks in memories that are not my own. I feel the aching grind of the continent's birth, the slow, cool agony of a mountain rising. I taste the mineral tang of a melt-water stream that vanished a thousand generations ago. I see the green glow not as a blanket, but as a map of feeling—a shimmering web of discomfort where the crust is too thin over a cold spot, a pulse of satisfaction along a warm trail freshly grazed by a Thulg herd. The Mycelial Mind is not a voice. It is a state of being. A vast, patient sentience concerned with balance, with flow, with the slow digestion of geological time.

I am becoming a nerve-ending for that mind. When I place my hand on the rock to find the *grish*, I am not just sensing heat. I am asking, and the network answers. When I set the trap for the Thulg, I am not just reading migratory signs. I am enacting a pruning. The Thulg herd is too large for this warm trail; its passage will scour too deep, damage the network. The stone, through the subtle unease it projects into my *grish*-softened thoughts, desires a correction. I am its instrument.

I am losing my fear. The emptiness of the plateau, the vast, lonely dark that used to fill me with a quiet dread, now feels like simply another part of the body I am joining. The wind is its breath. The static storms are its electrical thoughts flashing across a synaptic sky. I am Khar of the Black Fissure steading, but I am also becoming the Fissure itself. I am the listener, and soon, I will be the stone that is listened to.

My family touches me less now. They see the way I stare for long periods at a plain, glowing rock face, my black eyes unblinking. They feel the chill that seems to radiate from me, a coolness that has nothing to do with the air. They do not understand. They think I am growing old, my mind scoured thin by the wind. They are not wrong. But they are not right.

The next time the Sik-sik erupt from their spire in a panicked cloud, signaling a static storm, I will not run for the cave with the others. I will stand on the plateau, feeling the air crackle, watching the crust flare in great, strobing waves of emerald light. I will listen to the storm's roar, and within it, I will hear the mind of the world thinking in lightning. And I will answer, not with a click or a scratch, but with the slow, sure silence of a stone that has finally learned what it means to hear.
