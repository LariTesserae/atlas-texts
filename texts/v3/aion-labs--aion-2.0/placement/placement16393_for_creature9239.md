I am a flicker of warmth on a continent of cold. I am a heartbeat measured in frantic trills against the slow, deep pulse of the stone. I am a lichen-bird, a scrap of feather and bone stitched into the living geology of my world.

My sky is not the bruised violet expanse above. My sky is the arch of lichen-crusted plates, a landscape of ochre splotches, grey crusts, and deep black stains that stretches beyond my hopping range. I live on the back of Grond, though I have no name for him. He is simply the World, the moving mountain beneath my feet. His plates are continents, their seams deep, shadowed canyons where moisture collects as salty dew. My life is spent traversing this mobile, living plateau, pecking at the lichen, keeping its growth in check, tasting the sharp, mineral tang of the spores I consume.

My senses are tuned to a different scale. Where Grond tastes the deep magnetic whispers of the planet and feels the geothermal warmth through his pillar-feet, I taste the micro-seasons of his carapace. A patch of grey lichen turns slightly orange—a flush of reproductive spores. I peck it clean. A new, rubbery blue-green moss sprouts in a seam where the nightly frost melt lingers. I tear at its tiny strands. My work is constant, a blur of motion against his eternal stillness. I am his gardener, his immune system, a thing of quick, necessary fuss.

I know the herd’s movement not by sight of distant horizons, but by the shifting of the light on Grond’s back. The dim, perpetual twilight waxes and wanes as we pass beneath the stationary clouds. The direction of the faint, chill wind changes over days. And I feel it in the deep, subsonic tremor that comes up through my feet—a rhythmic *pressure* that is the synchronized step of sixty thousand giants. It is the herd’s heartbeat. It says: *We are moving. We are feeding. We are the process.*

From my vantage, I see the other World-backs around us, a field of slowly shifting hillocks against the haze. Sometimes, when the wind is right, I catch the scent of another lichen-bird from a distant host—a sharp, musky note on the cold air. We do not flock. We are territorial over our individual Worlds. Our meetings are rare, frantic, and brief when two geophages brush close enough for a daring leap across a gulf of terrifying, cold air.

The silence is absolute, but not empty. My world has its own symphony of minutiae. The click of my beak on lichen. The almost inaudible, sandpaper rasp of Grond’s feeding mouthparts miles away at his front—a sound felt more in the plate I stand on than heard. The sigh of wind across the stone plains below. And sometimes, the soft, wet trickle of meltwater finding a new path down the canyon of his spine after a nightly frost.

I know the smell of the plateau: wet stone, ozone, and the faint, fertile loam-scent of the silt highway Grond leaves behind him. I have never touched that ground. It is the realm of the ghoul-mites and the slow, blind worms. My world is here, atop the living rock.

I have witnessed the weirdness.

Once, the herd stopped. For three days and nights, not a leg-piston moved. The deep tremor ceased. The silence became a presence, a weight. The shadows around us pooled and thickened, as if the dim light were water draining into the stone. And then, on the third night, the hum began. Not a sound from the geophages, but from the planet itself, rising through Grond’s body into mine. A harmonic vibration that made my tiny bones sing and my feathers tremble. It felt like the plateau was remembering a dream. Then, as one, the herd stirred and moved on, as if nothing had happened. I remember. My life is short, but I remember.

I have seen the spherical stones—the fossil eggs of lost eras—from my high perch. They dot the plains like forgotten ideas. I have seen a dead Elder, too. A geophage who simply settled and ceased. From Grond’s back, I watched the ghoul-mites rise in a pale, seething tide, a silent explosion of consumption. In a year, the corpse was a crumbling mound; in a decade, a stark, arched skeleton against the sky. Grond’s herd passed it by, a detour of respect etched into their new highway over centuries. I will be dust long before that new route is fully worn.

My purpose is small and essential. I tend my garden. I eat the spores that would choke the lichen. I am a cog in a vast, slow machine made of flesh and stone. I live my entire life—a frantic, bright burst of maybe two years—on the back of a creature who will walk for six centuries. He will not notice my birth or my death. But I notice him. I feel his life in the constant, gentle warmth that seeps up through his plates, a geothermal gift. I know his moods by the subtle tension in the carapace, the minute adjustments of his course.

Now, as the herd enters a wide, shallow basin, the light bleeds to a deeper purple. The nightly frost is coming. I feel the temperature plummet. I retreat to my nest—a woven cup of stolen moss and my own down, tucked deep in a seam where Grond’s plates meet. The cold becomes a sharp, crystalline bite. I fluff my feathers, tuck my head, and wait. Outside, the rime forms, sheathing Grond’s back in a brittle, salty armor. My World becomes a castle of ice. But beneath me, the great, slow heart beats on, and the deep, internal furnace of his metabolism keeps the deadly chill at bay.

In the first grey light of the dim day, I will emerge to peck at the frost-melt, to taste the new dampness, and to watch as the continent of quiet stone continues its infinite, patient turn beneath the bruised sky. I am a flicker. But I am part of the thought.
