I am born of a sigh. A deep, slow exhalation of the plateau itself, a bank of pearl-grey mist grown heavy with intention. It settles into the Chamber of Echoing Drips, a vertical flute in the side of a broad, buttressed tower where water falls in a perfect, metronomic sequence: *plink… pause… plink… pause… plink*. The sound is a heartbeat. The mist, charged with the spores of *Usnea profunda* and the acid-tinged dust of crustose lichens, begins to stir. It is not a wind that stirs it, but a resonance. The drip-fall sets up a frequency in the stone, a hum felt more than heard, a vibration that organizes the chaos.

I feel myself gathering. There is no *I* at first, only a convergence. Then, a point of awareness coalesces—like a droplet forming on a ceiling, but inverted. The moss spores germinate instantly in the super-saturated air, weaving a fibrous matrix through the mist. My body is not assembled; it precipitates. I am a figure of damp air given texture, the green-grey of living lichen given a temporary shape. I have no bones, no blood. My substance is the substance of the plateau: water, stone-dust, and the patient, simple life that clings to both.

I step from the mouth of the chamber. My feet, if they can be called that, are mere densifications of the fog bank that birthed me. They make no sound on the silver moss. I lift a hand—a limb of swirling vapour and delicate fungal threads—and observe it. It is indistinct, shimmering. It will not hold a form for long. I know this, not as memory, but as a fact of my composition. I am a transient phenomenon, a thought made manifest in the landscape’s slow mind.

The world opens before me. The avenue of stone towers stretches away, vanishing into the pearlescent gloom. The air is cool and thick, a palpable presence in my porous form. I can feel it moving through me, carrying the minute histories of distant pools and deep crevices. I begin to walk. My gait is a flowing, a drift given direction. There is no urgency. Time here is measured in the deepening of channels, not in heartbeats.

I pass a Sentinel. It stands at a junction where three erosion-carved streets meet, a humanoid accretion of pale stone, porous and glistening. Water wells in the hollows of its face and spills over in silent, perpetual tears. I stop before it. We are kin, in a way—both formed by water’s patient work. I raise my lichen-crusted hand and place it against its damp flank. The stone is cool, roughened by centuries of microscopic dissolution. I feel the slow, slow pulse within it, a shift of mass so gradual it is like the turning of a continent. It is adjusting, aligning itself with a new drip-line from a ledge high above. Our communication is not of words, but of shared process. I am the plateau’s fleeting awareness; it is the plateau’s enduring record. In its layers are stored the stories of droughts that never came, of mists that thickened for a thousand years. I receive an impression of deep time, a patience that borders on stillness. Then I withdraw my hand, leaving a faint, damp print that will be gone by the next drip-fall.

I wander on. My purpose, if I have one, is observation. I am the eyes of the moss, the sentience of the mist. I see the lead-winged moths—Litholepidoptera—flutter soundlessly from a honeycombed window. They are beautiful in their austerity, their slate-grey wings absorbing the diffuse light. One passes so close I feel the minute disturbance of air. I do not try to catch it; I am not a predator. I am a witness.

In a sheltered alcove, I find a pool. It is a perfect circle, filled with water so clear it seems not to be there, only a distortion of the moss below. I kneel—my form bending like a tendril of fog—and peer in. On the pale stone bottom, threads of living crystal undulate. Aquavermes. They pulse with a light that is not their own, a captured gleam from the cloud-ceiling above. One drifts near the surface, its body a vibration so slow it is almost static. I touch the water’s surface with a finger. A ripple expands, distorting the worm’s image. It does not flee. It adjusts its frequency, merging with the new vibration. We understand each other. We are both expressions of water’s will.

Days pass, or perhaps weeks. The sky does not change, so I measure time by my own slow attenuation. I can feel myself becoming less distinct. The mist that sustains me is thinning, drawn away by a shift in the plateau’s gentle weather. I feel no panic, only a quiet acknowledgment. I am not dying; I am returning.

I choose a high place for my final observation: a natural bridge arching between two slender spires. From here, I can see a vast district of the mineral metropolis. Towers like broken teeth, forests of stone, all softened by the eternal weeping mist. A Nebulavian glides past, a streamlined knot of raincloud fur, riding a thermal sigh. It turns its head, and for a moment, its dark, liquid eye regards me. There is no fear, no curiosity—only recognition. I am part of the scenery.

The wind picks up, a sigh that is the plateau breathing out. I feel it begin to unravel me. It starts at the edges. My hand disperses first, fingers becoming mere tendrils of mist, then nothing. The sensation is not of loss, but of expansion. My awareness, once focal, begins to diffuse. I am flowing back into the damp air, my lichen components settling onto the moss below, my water rejoining the countless droplets in the atmosphere.

I look down at the city of weeping stone one last time. The *plink… plink…* of water is a lullaby. The silence is not empty; it is full. It is the sound of balance, of a world in perfect, slow conversation with itself.

I am no longer a walker. I am the mist that moves through the streets. I am the acid in the lichen, guiding the erosion of a new arch. I am the memory of a momentary shape, held now in the dampness clinging to a Sentinel’s cheek. I have returned to the dream. And the dream is deep, damp, and unending.
