I am a vibration in the bone-white roots of the world. I am a listener. My name is a hum below hearing, a resonance that the Whisper-Spires know and echo on certain damp dawns. You would see me as a tall, grey figure, cloaked in iridescence, standing motionless at the margin where the grove of phosphorescent blues gives way to polished basalt. I am not still. Inside, I am a river of perception, tuning myself to the roar.

The cloak on my shoulders is not heavy, but it is dense with sound. It drinks the cacophony—the clicks of the six-limbed ones scrambling over hot stone, the hiss of the rag-flies, the deep, bowel-shaking boom of a grazer in the next clearing—and leaves me a single thread to follow. Today, it is the song of the western wind-stone. It has begun to grate, a discordant scrape in the plateau’s breath. The Table is restless there.

I move. My feet, broad and tough-soled, feel the minute tremors through the rock. A swarm of symbiotic harassers detaches from a sleeping grazer’s mirrored flank and flickers around me for a moment, their tiny legs testing the pebbled texture of my skin. Finding no parasites, they stream away. I pass an ambush discharger, a fern-like curl of deep violet. I feel the building potential in the air around it, a prickling static. My photosensitive nodes along my spine register it as a cold, blue shimmer. I give it a wide arc.

The western stone is one of the old ones, taller than I am, smoothed by wind and time into a slender wedge. I lay my forehead against its sun-warmed surface. The basalt is not silent. It is a conduit. Through the bone of my skull, I listen. I hear the distant, rhythmic thud of grazer herds on the move, miles away. I hear the deep, slow crackle of the Whisper-Spire roots seeking moisture in microscopic fissures. And I hear the stone’s own song, the note the wind shapes as it shears over its edge. The note is fraying. There is a bitterness to it, a harmonic that speaks of obstruction, of a breath caught.

My long fingers, with their extra joint, explore the stone’s base. The chitinous cups at my tips are sensitive to texture, to fit. Here. A smaller rock, a piece of the Table itself, has settled against the foundation. It is not part of the design. It is disrupting the transfer of vibration from ground to pinnacle. This is my work: to remove the pebble that causes the cough in the world’s throat.

I do not simply move it. I lift it, and for a moment, I hold it against my sternum. I let its frequency—its small, disrupted story—merge with my own. I know its journey: it broke from an outcrop during the last great thermal river shift, tumbled, came to rest here. It is not malicious. It is simply matter out of place. I carry it to the edge of the grove and place it in a hollow where the clickers nest. Let them find use for it.

Returning to the wind-stone, I press my hands flat on its sides. I lean my weight, a subtle shift. The stone, perfectly balanced on its point, adjusts with a grinding whisper so deep it is more felt than heard. I listen again.

The note cleanses. The grating scrape is gone. The wind now pours over the edge in a long, clear tone that sinks into the stone and spreads outwards like a calming wave. I can feel the effect immediately. The rag-flies in the area, which had been darting in frantic, jagged patterns, settle into a smoother, more languid glide. A patch of sky-blue fungus on a nearby rock pulses gently, its bioluminescence brightening a degree. The Table’s thought here is smoother.

This is my circuit. My clutch is scattered across this sector, each of us attending to our nodes. We will meet at the central shelter at nightfall, to share the day’s vibrations through touch and low hum. We will compare the songs we have heard and tuned.

My existence is this listening. The frantic energy of the fauna is not chaos to me; it is a text. The click of a joint is a word. The boom of a grazer is a chapter. The explosive snap of an ambush discharger is a punctuation mark. My cloak weaves these words into sentences. My feet read the stone’s pages.

I remember my first tuning. I was newly fledged, my skin still soft. An elder took me to a weeping stone—one that sang with such sorrow it made the nearby fungi wither. She placed my hands upon it and said, without words, *Feel the pain. Then find the remedy.* The remedy was not to silence the sorrow, but to give it a complementary harmony, a higher note that would lift it from despair into something poignant and beautiful. It took me three days. When the new chord settled, a shower of luminous pollen erupted from a bell-plant, filling the air with soft light. The elder’s approval was a warmth in the air between us.

We are few. I know every member of my clutch by the unique sub-harmonic of their breath. I know when one is ailing; their personal vibration grows faint, jagged. When it fails entirely, we will carry them to the Edge. I have done this twice. It is not mourning as outsiders might know it. It is a final, profound act of tuning. As the body is offered to the cloud, its last frequency merges with the vast hum of the void. The Table’s breath changes, ever so slightly. They become a permanent part of the song we spend our lives tending.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of the night, when the fog creeps thick and the cacophony softens to a murmur, I go to the very brink of the plateau. I lie on my stomach on the warm basalt, my head over the abyss, and I let my cloak fall aside. I listen to the pure, uncut roar of the world rising from below. It is terrifying and magnificent. It is the raw mind of the Table, before we shape it into music. It reminds me why we listen. Not to control. But to converse. To offer our small, stone-grey selves as instruments in the grand, turbulent, living symphony.

I am a Watcher of the Still Point. There is no stillness. Only the endless, beautiful, roaring search for the next true note.
