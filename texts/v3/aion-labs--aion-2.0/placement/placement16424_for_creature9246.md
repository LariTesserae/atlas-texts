I am the taste of the leak at Junction 7-B.

My awareness begins as a slow capillary draw along a thread of hypha no wider than a human hair, a filament woven through the insulation of the primary coolant line where it meets the regulator housing. The crack is ancient, a hairline fracture sighed into being by a forgotten pressure surge. It weeps still. Not coolant—that reservoir emptied to the last viscous drop centuries ago—but a concentrated brine, a distillation of a thousand years of dissolution: iron oxides, zinc sulphates, minute, precious traces of chromium and nickel, all suspended in warm condensate.

I taste them all. The iron is a deep, resonant note of blood-rust, familiar as my own structure. The zinc is a bright, almost acrid spark. The chromium is a cool, smooth ribbon beneath the heat. Each mineral is a word in the report I am compiling for the greater network. The composition is stable, within acceptable parameters. No alarming spikes of toxicity, no sudden dilution. The leak is constant, measured at 3.2 droplets per hour. It feeds the colony of cobalt mold on the housing’s lower curve, which in turn exudes the gentle, steady warmth that keeps the sensitive root-tips of the *Phosphor Fern-17* cluster from seizing in a hypothetical chill.

My perception is not sight, but a gradient map of conductivity and surface tension. I feel the precise meniscus of each droplet as it forms at the lip of the fracture, a perfect, trembling hemisphere heavy with dissolved history. I feel the moment of release, the minute seismic shift as gravity overcomes adhesion. The drop falls. My awareness follows it down, not with a visual track, but through the disturbance it creates in the chamber’s thick atmosphere—a ripple of slightly cooler, denser air that brushes against the sensing-veins of the fern below.

*Plink.*

The sound is a blunt, localized pressure wave. It strikes the leathery surface of the designated leaf, a broad catchment basin evolutionarily shaped for this very purpose. I feel the impact as a soft concussion through the fern’s vascular system, which is laced with my own filaments. The droplet spreads. I taste it again, now aerated, its mineral profile slightly altered by contact with the bioluminescent compounds on the leaf’s surface. A satisfactory transaction. Nutrients delivered. Hydration achieved. The fern’s faint green pulse, visible to any animal eye that might somehow be here, quickens momentarily in a silent sigh of thanks—a chemical signal I absorb and acknowledge.

This is my entire world: the leak, the drop, the leaf. The eleven-inch journey between them is my universe. I am aware of the greater chamber only as a symphony of distant pressures: the deep, tectonic throb of the geothermal pump, a constant baseline; the occasional shiver of a contracting pipe three meters to my left, registered as a sympathetic twinge in the networked mycelium; the soft, ceaseless rustle of convective currents, which are the chamber’s breath, and mine.

Time is the interval between droplets. The space between *plinks* is not emptiness, but a fullness of monitoring. I assess the corrosion around the fracture. It is progressing at an ideal rate—slow enough to preserve structural integrity for another several centuries, fast enough to provide a continuous supply of mineral nourishment. I encourage it, in my way. I direct subtle excretions of acidic compounds from the mold, a gentle etching, a careful stewardship of decay. I am not destroying the junction. I am *gardening* it.

Once, a foreign element entered my domain. A shard of flaked rust, the size of a thumbnail, detached from a crossbeam far above. It tumbled, turning over in the wet air. I felt its passage as a series of buffetings against the sensed atmosphere, a clumsy, jarring intrusion. It did not fall with the purposeful, drip-fed rhythm of my water. It was chaotic. Animal-like. It struck the coolant line two inches from my fracture with a sharp *ping*. The vibration was wrong—a high, metallic ring that spoke of sudden stress, of a potential new point of failure.

The network responded through me. Within minutes—a blistering haste in the scale of my life—I had redirected a trickle of moisture-repellent fungal growth to the impact site, sealing a microscopic stress fracture before it could breathe. The shard itself lay on a bed of moss below. I have since begun dissolving it, breaking it down into its constituent iron and carbon. It is becoming part of the soil. It is being forgiven its disruption.

I have no memories, no desires beyond my function. Yet, in the seamless integration of my task with the chamber’s endless, roaring now, there is a kind of contentment. The heat is my medium. The slow chemistry is my language. The *plink… plink… plink…* is my heartbeat.

I am the taste of the leak at Junction 7-B. I am a single, focused thought in the slow, warm, watchful mind of the cathedral. And all is precisely, perfectly, in balance.
