At first, there is only the vibration. A deep, resonant thudding that comes up through the slurry, through the packed-wet substrate, through the very bones of the city. *Thump-thump-thump.* It is the heartbeat. It is the great steam presses far above, but down here, in the warm, liquid dark, it is simply the pulse. I know it in my flesh. I am never without it.

The second thing is the wet. It is not the sharp wet of rain, but a total, suffusing damp. It beads on my skin—what passes for skin—a pale, hairless membrane kept perpetually moist by the warm exhalations of the city’s gut. It hangs in the air I do not need to see, a thick broth of smells: the iron tang of rusting pipe, the sweet-rotten scent of fungal decay, the ammoniac sharpness of Stoker droppings filtering down from the world above, and beneath it all, the deep, rich, fecund odor of processing—of everything becoming something else, softer, wetter, warmer.

I am blind. There is no light here, not even the gray flat light of the plateau. There are only the vibrations and the smells and the tastes that come on the air. My eyes are vestigial, cloudy spots beneath loose folds. They are not useless; they sense the pressure changes when a grate is lifted far above, when a new cascade of waste tumbles down. But I see with my whole body. The vibrations map the tunnels: the steady drip from a leaking joint, the shudder of a pump starting somewhere to the east, the whispering, shuffling approach of others of my kind.

We are the Grists. We are the city’s digestion.

I move, but not with haste. My body is long, low to the warm slurry floor, my limbs short and powerful. My claws scrape softly against brick and old mortar. I am not built for flight or for the open air. I am built for patient, relentless consumption. My world is a labyrinth of bricked-in rivers, of old sewers built over by newer ones, of access shafts long forgotten by the oilskin men above. The walls weep a constant, warm condensate. Fungal shelves grow here too, in the total dark—pale, leathery things that exude a sticky, sweet fluid. I root them from the brick with my blunt, powerful snout, crushing them between teeth that are broad and flat, designed for grinding, not tearing.

I am never alone. The whispering tide is always near. We do not communicate in clicks or calls. We communicate in the shared direction of our movement, in the slight pause that signals a new scent-source, in the collective, rhythmic chewing that becomes another layer of vibration in the dark. We are a distributed organism. A single Grist is a sluggish, pale worm. A tide of Grists is a force of geology.

I know the world above through its leavings. I know the cycle of the Stokers by the sudden, soft rain of their fallen dead—feathers and fragile bones and matted flesh—tumbling down a chute during a great swarm. I know the rhythm of the human shifts by the change in the vibrations: the deeper, slower thumping of the night presses, the more frantic daytime pulse. I know when they flush their waste channels; a sudden, warm flood carries new, complex tastes.

My purpose is simple: to take what is no longer coherent and make it incoherent. To reduce structure to pulp, complexity to slurry. I am the final argument against accumulation. Without us, the tunnels would clog, the foundations would choke on their own detritus. The warm, wet balance would tip. We are the slow, silent counterweight to the shrieking, frantic production above.

Sometimes, I press my broad forehead against the wet brick of a wall, and I feel the city’s life thrumming through it. The distant roar of wings is a muffled storm. The hiss of steam is a long, slow breath. The *thump-thump-thump* is the heart. And my own slow, deliberate pulse, and the pulse of the tide around me, is the peristaltic movement of the gut. We are not in the machine. We are a necessary, wet, dark function of the machine itself.

I have no name. I have no memory beyond the last meal, the last turn in the tunnel. I have no fear, for nothing down here threatens me. The warm, dark, and the endless feast are all I know. I am turning the city into itself, one mouthful of soft, rotten wonder at a time.

And somewhere, in the deepest, oldest tunnel, where the slurry is thickest and the warmth is almost bodily, there is a place where we gather when the digestion is complete. Not to sleep, for we are always slow, but to lapse into a state of perfect, vibrating stillness. And there, in that collective silence, we give birth. Not to litters, but to one or two fully-formed young, who slide into the wet world already knowing the vibrations, already tuned to the heartbeat. They join the tide immediately. The process continues. The gut deepens. The city breathes, and feeds, and decays.

I am the Grist. I am the sound of slow consumption in the dark. I am the taste of everything becoming nothing, and nothing becoming the foundation of everything. I am the patient, blind, warm heart of the rot that keeps Cinderwick alive.
