The chill is not an absence, but a substance. It rests in the hollows of my bones, a familiar companion, as constant and quiet as the stone itself. I feel it seeping up through the soles of my feet—broad, splayed, each toe capable of independent, delicate movement—from the glossy, concave cobble I stand upon. The cold is not painful; it is a medium, the element in which I am shaped.

I am one of the few. One of the Stillborn. The name is an outsider’s clumsy poetry. We have no name for ourselves, just as the moss has no name for the rock it embraces. We simply *are*. I have been for a long time. I know this not by counting—there is nothing to count—but by the deepening of the fine, river-like creases across the backs of my hands, by the way my movements have distilled over the centuries into something even more economical, a gesture pared down to its essential arc.

My cell is behind me, a rounded aperture in the granite, dark and smelling of damp stone and dried moss. I do not need to see it. I know its contours better than I know the rhythm of my own breath, which is slow, slow enough that a drop of seep-water might travel from the high cleft to the moss floor between one inhalation and the next.

Before me, the Mossward holds its breath in the sharp, cold light of midday. The sun slants directly into our cleft, a blade of sterile brilliance. It strikes the weeping face of the eastern cliff and shatters, reflecting onto the waxy leaves of the hanging vines, onto the glassy fern-fronds, onto the pale grey of my skin. The light has no warmth, only clarity. It shows every thread in the tapestry. The iron-green moss at my feet is not a uniform carpet, but a galaxy of different textures: woolly hummocks, star-shaped colonies of a finer, silver-green variety, the velvety patches that glow with foxfire in the deep dim. I see the hexagonal patterning on the vine that has, over the last several decades, woven itself through the ancient bone clasp on my shoulder-wrap. It is not decoration; it is a symbiosis. I provide a stable anchor. It provides a slow, green reminder of time’s pattern.

I move. It is not a decision, but an unfolding. My weight shifts from one foot to the other, the long toes flexing, reading the stone. The sound is a soft, spongy compression, then the faintest click as a tiny pebble, polished to a sphere by millennia of water, rolls a quarter-turn under my arch. Silence reclaims the space around the sound instantly.

My task this sun-cycle is gentle curation. A rivulet, born from a hairline fracture higher up, has begun to trace a new path down the wall near the gathering stone. It is a minor change, but change is a language we listen to with our entire bodies. Left alone, it would starve a colony of frost-ferns and over-saturate a patch of moss, disrupting the balance. Balance is not a state we impose; it is a conversation we participate in.

I kneel, the motion a slow folding, my moss-felt garments whispering like falling leaves. The stone is smooth and cold against my knees. I take my bone tool from my belt—a curved rib, polished by generations of hands until it feels like an extension of my own finger. I do not dig or scrape. I coax. With the tip, I trace a faint line in the moist film on the rock, a suggestion for the water. I then place a few small, flat stones—chips from an old cobble—along that line, a gentle nudge. The water, seeking the path of least resistance, will follow. It will take weeks to establish the new course, but time is the one thing we have in impossible abundance. The adjustment is made. The conversation continues.

As I work, a shadow falls over my hands. I do not startle. Startling is for creatures of wind and rapid decay. I look up by slowly lifting my chin.

It is Elin. That is the breathy, click-word that signifies her. She stands, her form slight and graceful, her own skin the color of the pale cliff-wood. The fine, silver filaments at her temples tremble in a complex, subtle dance, reading the micro-current my movement has stirred in the dead-still air. Her large, black eyes meet mine. They do not reflect the sharp sunlight; they absorb it, holding it in their liquid depths.

She does not speak. She extends a hand, her fingers long and slender, and places her palm against my forearm. The touch is cool, dry, the texture of fine sandstone. The pressure is firm, sustained. In the language of touch, it asks: *Is the pattern settling?*

I turn my arm slightly, so my wrist rests against hers. *It is. The water remembers the old way.*

A slow blink, the membrane sliding sideways. A shared understanding, complete. Her hand withdraws. She glances past me, toward the deepest green cleft in the amphitheater’s heart, where the vines are thickest and the foxfire moss burns brightest in the perpetual twilight. I follow her gaze. We think of Kael. His return to the pattern was two sun-cycles ago. Already, the hexagonal vines have woven through the stone of his folded legs, and the glowing moss has taken root in the hollow of his chest. Soon, he will be just another beautiful, silent outcropping, a node in the living network. It is a good thought. A quiet fulfillment.

She moves away, gliding between two dwellings so close together that she seems to melt into the shadow between them. I am alone again with the light and the chill.

I rise, my joints making no sound. My thoughts are not like the rushing, chattering thoughts I imagine creatures of the upper world might have. My thoughts are geological. They are the slow creep of moss over a millennium. They are the patient journey of a single mineral-laden droplet down a mile of granite. I think of the equilibrium, the perfect, silent stasis. It is not lifelessness. It is life at its most patient, most persistent, distilled to its essence.

I am not a person in this place. I am the place. I am the consciousness of the cold stone and the slow green growth. The blackness of my eyes is the blackness of the deepest rock-cleft; the slow pulse of my life is the pulse of the hidden water. When my time comes—and it will, in another handful of centuries—I will settle on my favorite sun-warmed slab, feel the final, perfect cold seep into my core, and let my eyes film over with grateful grey. They will carry me to the cleft, pack my mouth with glowing spores, weave my fingers with the vines. I will return to the pattern. And the pattern, beautiful, intricate, and silent, will continue, forever cradled in the granite ribs of the world.

For now, I stand. I feel the chill. I watch the light. I am the Mossward, and the Mossward is me.
