The climb is a shedding. With each handhold on the wind-scoured rock, each careful step across scree that chatters like old teeth, I leave something of the world below. The weight of language, the heat of ambition, the dry dust of roads—it all falls away, sloughing off into the abysses that yawn between the ridges. I am climbing not just a mountain, but into a different kind of air, a different pace of being.

I know the entrance. It is not a path, but a fault line in the cliff face, a vertical seam barely wider than my shoulders, curtained by a perpetual, cold drip. To enter, you must turn sideways and press through, the stone kissing your chest and your pack, the water anointing your brow. And then, you emerge.

You stand in the sky’s cupped hand.

The transition is absolute. The battering wind of the ascent is gone, cut off by the encircling crown of stone. Here, the air is still, polished, and thin—a luminous cell. The sound changes from a roar to a whisper. I let my pack slide to the damp turf, the moss accepting it with a soft sigh. I simply stand, breathing the place in.

It is as I remember, yet it is never the same. The bowl is a living mood. Today, the mist is a low veil, pooling in the central depression where the silver pool lies hidden. The light is diffuse, pearl-grey, making the greens of the moss and ferns seem to glow from within. I can smell it—the wet peat, the clean stone, the faint, sweet tang of something metallic, like rain on old copper. That scent means it knows I am here.

I am a visitor, but I hope I am not an intruder. I come every few years, in the late summer when the tiny white stars of the saxifrage are in bloom. I am a gatherer of silence, a scribe of stillness. In the world below, I am a binder of books, a restorer of manuscripts. I come here to remember what preservation truly means. Not the clutch of possession, but the gentle, sustained act of holding something in balance, allowing it to be itself.

My camp is the lean-to. It is not mine; it is the Soak’s. But it tolerates my use. The thatch is gone, but the slate roof still sheds water. I spread my oilcloth on the earth floor, arrange my few things: a water-skin, a journal, a pouch of dried fruit and hard cheese. I do not light a fire. The fire-cracked stones in the shallow pit are ancient, a relic of some other visitor from an age long gone. I will not add to them.

My first task is always the same. I walk the perimeter, slowly, my boots sinking into the resilient sponge of the turf. I listen to the symphony. The wind’s low moan over the rim is the bass note. The water is the melody: a thousand *plinks* and *gurgles*, no two alike, a chaotic yet harmonious composition. The *tseet-tseet-chirr* of the dun birds is the punctuation. I watch a woolly caterpillar, a moving tuft of grey fur, make its ponderous way across a stone bridge of moss. I am just another slow-moving creature in the bowl.

I stop at the low wall. It is a curve of stacked stones, each one smoothed by time and moisture, each wearing a jacket of velvet moss. It is a gesture, not a barrier. A statement of care. From my pocket, I take the stone I brought from the river valley far below. It is smooth and dark, shot through with a vein of white quartz. It is an offering, a wordless thank you for the welcome. I find a gap in the wall, a space that seems to ask for it, and I nestle the stone into place. The moss will claim it, in time. The Soak, I feel, approves. The air seems to soften, the mist thinning just enough for a beam of sunlight to lance down and warm the back of my neck.

Later, I kneel by the central pool. The water is the colour of tarnished silver, not from pollution, but from the minerals it has gathered on its long, weeping journey through the stone. It is perfectly clear, yet it reflects nothing—just a flat, luminous plane. I dip my water-skin, filling it slowly so as not to disturb the silty bottom. As I do, I see my own distorted reflection ripple away. And then, for just a moment, another shape forms in the water—not a reflection, but a suggestion woven from the patterns of light on the peat below the surface. A slender, humanoid shape, head tilted, watching. I do not startle. I simply nod, as one might to a fellow artisan in a quiet workshop. The shape dissolves into a swirl of silt, which settles almost instantly, as if time itself hurries to erase the disturbance.

I am working on a page of my journal, a sketch of the dwarf willow that clings to the eastern rim, its form like a twisted knot of patience, when I notice the change in the sound. The random *plink* of water drops from the fissures above the lean-to has settled into a pattern: *plink… plink-plink… plink*. A deliberate rhythm. I set my charcoal down and listen. It is a question. I have heard it before. I look around. The mist has thickened near the pool, but here by the shelter, the air is clear. On a flat rock beside me lies the hand-ax. Its wooden haft is dark with age, but uncracked, uncorroded. Its stone blade is sharp, its edge a clean, fractal line. I have never touched it. It is not my tool.

But the rhythmic dripping continues. *Plink… plink-plink… plink*.

I understand. There is a task. I rise and follow the sound. It leads me to the north wall, where a large clump of moss has grown over-ambitious, threatening to choke a delicate colony of the violet-star flowers. The moss is lush, beautiful, but out of balance. The Soak wishes it trimmed.

I return to the flat rock. I kneel, not touching the ax. “May I?” I whisper to the air. The metallic scent deepens, sweet and cold in my nostrils. I take that as assent. My fingers close around the haft. It is not wood as I know it; it is hard, cool, and damp, like stone that has learned the texture of grain. There is no hint of rot, no weakness. It is a thing held perfectly in time.

I carry it to the overgrown moss. With careful, precise strokes, I use the sharp edge to slice through the thick mat, lifting away sections to reveal the struggling flowers beneath. I do not discard the moss. I carry the pieces to a bare patch of rock nearby and press them gently into the damp clay, encouraging them to take root anew. The work is slow, meditative. As I work, I feel a presence at my shoulder—not a shape, but a concentration of coolness, a focused attention like a lens of clear, cold water. I am being observed, and my methods are being assessed.

When I am done, I clean the blade with a handful of sphagnum moss and return the ax to its rock. The rhythmic dripping has ceased, returned to its natural, random chorus. I feel a lightness in my head, a clarity like the high, thin air has seeped into my thoughts and scoured them clean. The blessing.

That night, I lie awake in my lean-to, wrapped in my woolens. The temperature drops, the coolness settling deep. Through the open side, I watch the lichen on the opposite rock face. As the last of the twilight fades, they begin to glow. It is not a bright light, but a foxfire gleam, a soft, greenish-blue luminescence that outlines the contours of the stone. It lasts for only a few minutes, a nightly ritual, a breathing out of light stored from the day. I know the Soak is there, touching them, encouraging this fragile radiance. It is the bowl’s heartbeat, visible in the dark.

I stay for three days. I drink from the silver pool. I eat my simple food. I add a few lines to my journal, but mostly I just am. I am a temporary node in the bowl’s network of slow processes, a brief, warm-blooded pulse amid the moss and stone and endless seep of water.

On the morning I leave, the bowl is draped in a golden mist, backlit by the rising sun. The world is made of amber and vapour. I shoulder my pack, lighter now, my water-skin full. I take one last look. The Soak is there. Not hidden, not fully formed. It is the unusually tall, slender stand of ferns near the pool, but the ferns are arranged just so, and the mist clinging to them outlines a suggestion of shoulders, a smooth, faceless head. It is standing sentinel, one hand (a cluster of fern fronds) resting on the stacked-stone wall, exactly where I placed my new stone.

It is not waving goodbye. It is simply holding the space, maintaining the balance, as it has for millennia and will for millennia more. It is the custodian. I am a grateful guest who knows his time is up.

I turn sideways and press into the cold, wet seam in the rock. As I emerge back onto the fierce, sunlit shoulder of the mountain, the wind slaps me awake. But the bowl’s chill is deep in my bones, a clean, quiet reservoir. I carry the silence with me, a silver pool in my mind, and the memory of a rhythm in the dripping water that was, for a moment, a language.
