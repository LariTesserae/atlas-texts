I am a Listener. Not in the way all my people are listeners—for to live here is to listen, always—but in the title and task. My name, in the hum-and-click, is a series of pressured exhales and a soft tap of the tongue against the palate: *Khh-aaa-thump*. It means, roughly, "the one who hears the shape of the stone." I am a clay-speaker of a different order.

My hut is on the edge of the city, where the constructed channels give way to the wider, untamed sheets of still water. It is not larger than any other, but its walls are different. Where others are daubed smooth to filter and soften the roar, mine are intentionally flawed. I have carved shallow, spiraling grooves into the weeping stone of the interior, creating a surface that catches sound and twists it. I have left strategic gaps where the petrified stems of the framework are exposed, allowing threads of the outer din to enter in specific, un-muted frequencies. My home is not a refuge from the sound; it is a lens for it.

My work is solitary, and it is considered eccentric but useful. I am the one who finds the notes in the noise. When a new hummock is deemed stable enough for a new cluster of huts, they call for me. I will go and stand on the slick blue-grey clay for hours, days, my eyes half-closed, my bare feet sensing the tremors through the ground, my whole body a sounding board. I am listening for the foundational hum of that particular patch of earth—the pitch that resonates from the chemical seeps below, the way the water atop it dampens certain frequencies, the chorus of the local amphibians that have claimed it. All of this informs the design.

I tell the builders where to place the first stone, and at what angle. I advise on the thickness of the clay daub for the eastern wall, so that it will resonate in sympathy with the dawn chorus, turning that collective shift into a gentle, waking vibration within the home. I specify the diameter of the hole to be drilled in the ceiling slab, so that the high whine of the gnats becomes a focused, almost melodic whistle that signals the change from one work-cycle to the next. My people do not build for beauty, but I have learned that beauty is a byproduct of perfect function. A well-tuned hut does not fight the basin’s song; it sings a specific, harmonious part within it. The families who live in structures I have shaped report a deeper sense of *still-sound*, a profound integration. They sleep more deeply, their hands are steadier at the nets.

My own senses have been honed to an edge that sometimes feels like a wound. I see the world not just in gradients of heat, but in shifting layers of sonic vibration. The flight of a gnat-cloud is a shimmering, complex chord. The scrape of a beetle is a rough, brown tone in the spectrum of my mind. The bark of a furred swimmer is a sharp, blue pulse across the water’s surface. I perceive the city not as a collection of forms, but as a vast, slowly evolving instrument. The lanes are waveguides; the rounded roofs are diffusers; the whole settlement is a single, breathing entity exhaling a modified version of the basin’s endless breath.

There is a loneliness in this perception. Even as I am surrounded by my people, I am isolated by the depth of what I hear. They feel the sound as a medium, a warmth. I hear its anatomy. I know its bones. In the deepest part of the still-cycle, when even the amphibians seem to hold their hum for a fraction of a second, I sometimes hear something else. A silence beneath the silence. Not an absence, but a potential. A note so low it is more a pressure in the marrow than a sound. It is the sound of the basin before the first hum, the dream of the clay before it was stirred. It is the sound of the rock-weeps’ original loneliness.

I have never spoken of this. There is no language for it. *Still-sound* is the achieved harmony; this is something prior to harmony. It is a void that is also a plenum. It frightens me, not with terror, but with a vast, aching tenderness. It feels like hearing the world’s own heartbeat, slow beyond comprehension.

So I continue my work. I mold the clay, I tune the stones, I guide the builders. With each well-made hut, each properly aligned lane, I feel we are not just completing a circuit for the basin’s song. I feel we are building a reply to it. A structured, thoughtful reply made of mud and patience and careful listening. We are the clay’s dream, yes. But sometimes, in my quietest moments, surrounded by the deafening roar I have learned to dissect and love, I wonder if we are also its listeners. And if, in listening so deeply, we are slowly teaching it to dream of something new. Not change—for change is impossible here—but perhaps variation. A new motif in the endless, warm, wet song.

My skin is the color of the clay, slick with its oily film. My eyes are lidless and dark, sweeping their clear membranes across to clear the perpetual condensation. My hands are broad, nails chisel-shaped from a lifetime of testing stone. I am of this place, body and blood and breath. And the sound that would drive an outsider mad is to me the only true music. I am Khh-aaa-thump. I am the Listener. And the world never stops speaking to me.
