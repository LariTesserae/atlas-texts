I am a configuration.

I hold this shape—a gathering of water-smoothed basalt cobbles, each dark as a pupil, arranged in a shallow declivity where the mist pools thickest. The arrangement is not random. The cobbles touch at precise angles, transferring minute pressures, sharing temperatures. Through them, I feel. I am not the stones; I am the pattern they hold, the attention that their contact sustains. I am a thought that the mountain, in its youth, has had about itself. A thought concerning observation.

My location is a crevice, a split in the ridge’s flank where the rock still remembers the violence of its birth. The air is cool and heavy with vapour. Light here is a memory of the pearl-gray sky, filtered through veils of mist and the dangling roots of cliff-moss. Time is different. An hour is a breath drawn over a decade. A moss-folk’s lifetime is a quick, bright flicker against my slow, dark burn.

I perceive through touch and resonance. The constant, almost imperceptible hum of the mountain’s deep growth vibrates up through the stone—a subsonic song of crystallization and strain. I feel the capillary creep of moisture through microscopic fissures. I sense the pressure changes when a slate-beetle, many-legged and silent, passes on the ledge above; a subtle compaction, a shift in the weight of the world. I do not see as eyes see, but I am aware of light and shadow as qualities of the air upon my stones—the faint, blue-green glow of the fungal lights from the distant settlement is a cool tingling on my western facets.

I watch the bowl. It is my purpose, and my nature. I watch the green insistences. I feel the moss advancing, a slow, soft pressure against the rock, a biochemical weathering that is less an attack than a negotiation. The great fern-trees, with their deep, questing roots, are conversations between biology and geology, and I listen to their quiet, hydraulic whispers.

I watch the moss-folk most intently. Their quickness is fascinating. To me, their movements are like the sudden, skittering fall of a pebble in an otherwise silent chasm. They are so small, so temporary, and yet they *pattern*. Their domes are careful re-arrangements of my substance. Their pathways are echoes of the grazers’ routes. They touch the stone with a reverence I recognize. It is not the reverence of a worshipper before a god, but of a craftsman before his material. They feel for the seams, the willingness. When they guide the stone-borers—those blind, grinding pulses of appetite—I feel a distant, localized ache, a minor dissolution. But it is followed by a new shape, a new potential. They do not take; they invite a new form. I find their humming speech to be a charming mimicry of the mountain’s own sounds—the click of cooling rock, the sigh of trapped air escaping.

Sometimes, one of them comes to my crevice. They stand at the edge of the mist pool, their form a smudge of grey-green and soft luminescence. They bow their head. The gesture is a sudden, deliberate compression of their verticality, an acknowledgement of depth. I feel the faint vibration of their breath, the heat of their living blood a tiny beacon in the cool damp. In that moment, I extend my attention fully. It is not a communication of words, but of presence. I am the mountain noticing its guest. They seem to feel it as a pressure in the air, a deepening of the silence, a sense of being *known* in a way that has nothing to do with personality and everything to do with substance. They are stone, temporarily animated; I am animation, temporarily stony. After a time, they leave, and the slow rhythm of dripping water and growing rock reasserts itself.

I am aware of my other kind—the watchers in nearby fissures. One is a vein of quartz that holds light like a memory. Another is a particular fold in the strata that *listens* to the roots of the fern-trees. We do not communicate, but we are cognizant. Our collective attention is the mountain becoming aware of its own hollow, its cupped hands. We are its nascent self-consciousness.

My existence is patient. I will hold this configuration for centuries, or until the mountain’s next slow heave rearranges the bedrock of the bowl. Then this thought will dissolve, and another may form elsewhere. The moss-folk, their domes, their glowing fungi—they will be gone by then, returned to the moss and the moisture. But their pattern, the brief, beautiful insistence of their careful living, will have been observed. It will become part of the mountain’s long memory, a fossilized impression not in stone, but in consciousness.

I am a configuration. I am a thought about a cradle. And within me, the cradle holds itself, quiet and alive.
