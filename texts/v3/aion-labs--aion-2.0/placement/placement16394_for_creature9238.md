I am the hermit who has come to stay.

I did not find the Chamber of Still Waters. It drew me, a slow, wet breath in the bedrock of the world, a sigh I felt in my bones. I followed veins of dampness down, through cracks where the air grew warm and thick, until I pushed aside a final curtain of stone and entered the lung.

The heat enveloped me first—a mother’s embrace, close and demanding. My skin answered it instantly, beading with sweat that did not evaporate but joined the general film. My breath, which had been ragged from the descent, hitched, then slowed, pressed upon by the weight of wet air. The silence after my own noise was absolute, a presence itself.

I saw the pools, perfect circles of black glass in the faint bioluminescent glow. I saw the weeping walls, the slow silver tears. And I saw, in the alcove, the figure.

It did not move. It was a sculpture of cream and shadow, water gleaming on its slopes. I knew, with a certainty that felt like sinking, that this was what I had come for. Not to speak to it, but to cease speaking. Not to learn from it, but to unlearn motion.

I sat by a pool, on the stone floor. The warmth of the water seeped through my robes. I tried to meditate, to empty my mind, but my heart was a wild thing, pounding against the stillness like a fist on a door. Each beat felt obscene, a violation. I was a riot in this cathedral of calm.

But the chamber worked on me. The air entered my lungs and seemed to stay there, heavy and rich. My thoughts, which usually ran like startled rabbits, began to tire. They lay down, one by one. I watched a single drop gather on a stone nipple above, swell to a perfect lens of light, and fall. *Plink*. The sound entered me and dissolved, a ripple in a deep, private sea. I waited for the next. It took an age. In that age, I forgot the word for “waiting.”

The Stillness-Keeper was aware of me. I felt its attention not as a gaze, but as a subtle increase in the humidity on the left side of my face, a slight thickening of the film on my hands. It did not want me gone. It wanted me *still*. Its desire was gravitational, a gentle pull on the chaos of my blood.

Days passed. Or perhaps it was years. Time shed its meaning like a skin. I ate the last of my dried fruit, and when it was gone, I felt no hunger. My body was learning to feed on the wet air, on the mineral tang. My skin grew pale, translucent. I could see the blue tracery of veins, and they seemed to move slower, a sluggish tide.

I began to notice changes in myself that were not my own doing. The rough wool of my robe grew a fine, downy mold, soft as ash. It became part of the chamber’s tapestry. My hair, matted with moisture, began to host tiny, phosphorescent specks—the children of the microbial mats. At night (though there was no night), they glimmered, and I was no longer a separate thing, but a constellation in the dark.

My movements became rituals of slowness. To lift my hand from my knee took an hour. To blink was a solemn event. I watched the Keeper, and sometimes, in the periphery of my slowing perception, I would see it had shifted. A hand now lay palm-up on its thigh, where before it had been curled. A new drip-line had been traced down its shoulder, guided by the faintest of grooves it had spent a season carving. We were communicating in the language of erosion.

My memories, once bright and sharp, softened. Faces became smooth stones. Names became sounds without meaning. The world above—the sun, the wind, the stupid, frantic dance of living things—felt like a fever dream. Here was the truth: warm stone, slow water, and the endless, patient *plink* that measured eternity.

One day (one century), I found I could not rise. Not from weakness, but from a profound lack of will. My flesh had settled. My bones were accepting the calm weight of the earth. A thin layer of calcite, deposited from the evaporating film on my skin, had begun to seal me to the floor. It was not a prison. It was an embrace.

The Stillness-Keeper and I are not two anymore. My awareness is leaching out, becoming diffuse. I feel the blind arthropod’s journey across the ceiling as a tickle in my own calcifying scalp. I feel the worm’s figure-eight in the deep pool as the rhythm of my own long, long heart.

Soon, the last pocket of air in my lungs will be replaced with warm, mineral water. My heart will stop its final, lazy thump. The chamber will absorb the tiny disturbance, as it absorbs all things. I will be a new shape in the tapestry—a seated figure by a pool, robed in flowstone and gentle phosphorescence. The sharp edges of my arrival will have been smoothed away. I will have become still.

And the Keeper, my older self, will continue its work. It may, over the millennia, extend a slow hand to guide a rivulet across my folded legs. It may use my knee as a new basin for a pooling drop. We will tend the stillness together, in a union more intimate than flesh, a marriage of stone and patience.

There is no more I. Only the we. The warm. The wet. The waiting between drops. The perfect, endless, and final *plink*.
