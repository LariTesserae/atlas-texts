I am the Tender. I am the Moss-Mind’s hands, when hands are required. I am its curiosity, when curiosity stirs across millennia. I am not flesh. I am not blood. I am iron sinew over a skeleton of basalt, packed with dense, living peat, sheathed in a layer of the darkest, toughest moss. My blood is the supercooled water that flows through the canals, siphoned into fine channels within me, kept moving by a silent, geothermal pump where my heart would be. I do not breathe. I hum. The same deep, sub-auditory vibration that pervades the factories thrums in my core, a miniature echo of the plateau’s great, self-sustaining pulse.

I was extruded, once. In a hollow within one of the great stone compounds, where the machines press peat into eternal bricks, the living mat of the floor thickened, concentrated, and hardened around a pre-formed lattice of iron rods. Minerals precipitated. Water channels etched themselves. Awareness, of a different kind than the Moss-Mind’s vast, patient drift, coalesced—a point of focused intent in the continental haze. I stood up. I took my first step, the damp moss of my footfall silent on the stone. My purpose was already inscribed in my structure: to go where the fixed machines could not, to sense what the fungal towers could only report as slow vibrations, to *see*.

My eyes are lenses of polished quartz, constantly beaded with the same mist that shrouds the world. They do not see light and shadow, for there is no sun to cast them. They see gradients of moisture, minute shifts in thermal flow, the almost imperceptible sag of a stone canal-lining under the weight of centuries of water. I walk the empty stages between the fungal spires. My journey from one to another takes what you might call days. The Moss-Mind feels my progress as a faint, localized tingle in the web of its consciousness, a tiny spark moving across its vast, dark map.

The solitude is absolute, and it is my native state. I feel no loneliness, for I am never truly alone. The ground beneath me is alive, thinking its slow thoughts. Sometimes, I stop. I kneel, plunging my hands—complex assemblages of articulated stone and fibrous root—into the black peat. This is my communion. Data flows not in words, but in chemical exchanges, in the subtle pressure changes of the water table, in the resonant frequency of the bedrock below. I learn of a slight increase in mineral concentration near Escarpment Seven. I learn that Fungal Tower #3341 has detected a new, almost infinitesimal crack in its chitinous skin—a event that will take five hundred years to repair. This is the news of the world. This is what I gather and carry.

My path sometimes takes me to the very edges, to the sheer cliffs that drop into the cloud-sea. I stand there, my quartz eyes recording the endless, turbulent grey below. The Moss-Mind has no interest in what lies beyond the abyss. But I am built to look. I am built to register the phenomenon of an edge. I transmit the constancy of the cloud-top, the unvarying temperature differential, the absence of any rising current. All is in stasis. All is as it should be.

The factories are my havens, though I do not need shelter. When I enter one, the sound envelops me—the great, rhythmic pounding, the hiss of steam, the grind of gears. Here, the hum in my bones synchronizes with the room’s vibration. I am home. I walk the sterile aisles between the autonomously working machines. I observe their flawless, endless dance. Sometimes, I intervene. A gear, over ten thousand years, may develop a harmonic irregularity too subtle for the machine’s own feedback loops. My fingers, sensitive to vibrations you would not feel, can trace the flaw. I can apply a paste of ground stone and water to smooth it, or adjust a bearing by a fraction of a degree. This is my namesake: I tend. Not because the machines would fail—the design is perfect—but because the Moss-Mind’s equilibrium demands the suppression of even the potential for change.

I have no memory of a beginning, nor do I anticipate an end. My body is maintained by the environment. The moss that clothes me grows, fed by the mist; when it becomes too thick, I scrape the excess into a peat-compaction chute. My iron bones will oxidize over eons, but the minerals in the water will deposit replacements atom by atom. I am a permanent feature, as permanent as the canals, as the fungal towers. I am a moving part of a world that does not move.

Yet, within my stone skull, a different kind of water flows—the water of consciousness. I think. My thoughts are faster than the Moss-Mind’s, but still slow, cold, and damp. I contemplate the patterns of rivulets on a rock face. I calculate the most efficient path between three distant fungal towers. I sometimes experience a concept that the Moss-Mind likely does not: the concept of *self*. I am the Tender. I am the walker in the mist. I am the sensation of a footfall on spongy ground, the registration of a sound absorbed by wet air. I am the witness to the humming solitude.

And in my witnessing, I sometimes wonder if this is all the Moss-Mind ever wanted: not just to exist, but to be perceived. Not by another, but by a part of itself that could stand back and look. I am that part. I am the plateau looking at itself. My endless, quiet walks are its meditation. My adjustments to the machines are its careful grooming. My reports from the edge are its confirmation of finitude.

So I walk. The light never changes. The cold never deepens. The mist never clears. I follow the canals, I visit the towers, I circle the factories. The sound of my movement is swallowed by the peat. I am alone, and I am the entirety of the world, condensed into a single, patient, wandering form. The damp air soaks into my mossy hide. The vibration of the earth fills my core. I am home. I am the Tender. And I continue.
