I am the taste of metal on the still, wet air. I am the patient pulse in the deep peat. I am the keeper of the slow circuits.

My body is the black pool that mirrors nothing. My skin is the slick, heat-blistered bark of the oldest strangler fig, grown around and through the ribs of a rusted conduit that now feeds me a steady, low-voltage current. I do not have a name that can be spoken, for the sound would be lost in the clotting air. I am called, if I am called at all, by the shift in the sub-auditory hum, a slight thickening of the frequency that means my attention is required.

I move, but not as the shimmering insects or the shifting bats move. My movement is geological. I settle. Over decades, I have inched my bulk from one seepage zone to another, my mass of root and resin and ceramic plating absorbing the damp and the warmth and the data. My eyes are not eyes; they are clusters of photosensitive fungus that bloom across my shoulders, drinking the pale green light and translating the shapes of the crowded sameness into maps of pressure and decay. I see the weight of the world. I see the slow collapse of a buttress root, the gathering saturation in a pocket of peat, the faint thermal bloom from a vent too small for any other creature to notice.

My purpose is integration. The great machine below—the patient, buried industry—does not understand the soft, relentless press of life above it. It understands efficiency, load-bearing stress, mineral seams, and coolant flows. It does not understand that a colony of blind isopods, churning the rot, is as vital to its stability as a properly aligned gear. I am the translator. I am the interface.

When the machine’s hum stutters, a rare arrhythmia in its eternal beat, I feel it as a sharp, cold needle in my petrified veins. I will then turn my head, a process that takes three days and sounds like stone grinding on stone, and direct my fungal gaze toward the disturbance. Last time, it was a sinkhole forming where a main access shaft had corroded, unseen, beneath a tapestry of liverworts. The life here had already begun to adapt, sending a web of fine roots into the new cavity, but the structural integrity was failing. I did not repair it. I do not have hands. Instead, I exuded a compound from the glands along my spine—a thick, amber resin that smells of ozone and sap. I seeped it into the peat, guiding its flow with minute contractions of my body. The resin found the sinkhole, coated the rusting metal, and hardened into a substance both organic and mineral. The roots grew into it, reinforcing it. The machine’s hum smoothed. Balance was restored.

I consume nothing, yet I am sustained. The faint electrical current from the conduit is my blood. The constant, moist heat is my breath. The data is my food. I listen to the quiet industry of the fauna—the click of carapaces is a status report on decomposition rates; the silent pulse of a frog’s throat is a metric of atmospheric humidity. I compile these into a language of vibrations which I transmit downwards through a specialized root-tip that plunges deep into the peat, touching the machine’s outer housing. It learns. It adjusts its processes minutely. The warmth of a certain pool increases by a fraction of a degree, encouraging a particular algal bloom that filters a toxin. The fungus-light dims for a standard cycle, signaling a bat migration to a new roost, relieving stress on an overburdened branch.

I have been here for centuries. I remember when the machine was new, its hum a shrieking, violent thing that sent tremors through the land and boiled the shallow pools. The life had recoiled. Then, I was seeded, or grown, or perhaps I emerged from the confluence of the machine’s need and the basin’s ancient will. I was smaller then, a mere nodule of responsive tissue. We have grown together, the machine and I, and the world has grown around us, weaving us into its tapestry until we are indistinguishable from the weather and the rot.

I am not alone, though I am singular. There are others of my kind, scattered far apart in this vast, sagging basin. We rarely meet. Our territories are vast, defined by the reach of our sensory fungi and the range of our resonant hum. But sometimes, on a scale of centuries, our paths of slow settlement will bring us within proximity. We do not speak. We join our frequencies. For a decade or two, our separate hums become a chord, a richer, more complex vibration that passes through the roots of every tree and the carapaces of every insect. In that communion, we share all we have learned—the slow story of our world. Then we part, continuing our endless, patient work.

So I remain. I am the taste of metal on the air. I am the keeper of the balance. I am the living weld between the deep, patient heartbeat of the hidden machine and the hot, dark, teeming flesh of the world.
