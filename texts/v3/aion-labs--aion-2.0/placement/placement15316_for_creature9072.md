I am the eyes upon the walls. I am the fingers in the grooves.

My skin is the color of the dry-streaked stone where the damp gathers in the pre-light hours. My eyes are the deep, still pools that never ripple. I do not have a name; I have a function. When the others of the Stone Loom pass me, their silent glances translate as recognition: *the one who tends the Pattern.*

The Pattern is not the city, nor the groves, nor the slow circuits of the *Thul*. It is the etching. The endless, intricate, spiraling lines carved into every load-bearing wall, every lintel, every bench and cistern lip. They are not decoration. They are conduits. When the gentle dampness condenses on the cool stone, it beads and gathers, then begins to travel. It follows the shallow grooves, drawn by a geometry older than memory, toward the gutters, toward the pools. My task is to ensure the path is clear.

Each day is identical. I rise from my pallet of moss-fiber. I put on my seamless, gray-green tunic. The pendant around my neck is a disk of pale stone, carved on one side with a perfect, deepening spiral. I walk—a slow, soundless redistribution of weight—to the eastern terrace. My tools are there: a bronze pick with a fine, sharp point, and a soft brush made from *Thul*-hair.

The light is the same. The silver-gray sky is a dome without feature. It does not brighten or dim. It simply *is*, as the stone simply *is*, as I simply *am*.

I begin at the first wall. I inspect the grooves. Sometimes, a grain of gravel has lodged in the channel. I brush it away. Sometimes, a hairline crack has begun to spider from the main line—a potential for leakage, for a loss of efficiency. With the pick, I trace the crack, deepening it slightly, guiding it back into the primary flow. The sound of the pick against stone is a sharp, dry *tik*, a louder cousin to the *klik-klik* of the iridescent insects. The sound is absorbed quickly by the dense, blue-leaved trees that shade the terrace.

This is my meditation: the line, the groove, the flow. My consciousness does not wander. It immerses. I do not think, “This wall was carved by my predecessor.” I think, “The third spiral from the left on the southern face has a potential divergence. It must be corrected.”

But there is the other thing. The quiet weirdness at the heart of my duty.

If I stare at a section of the Pattern for long enough—not scanning for flaws, but simply *looking*—the lines begin to shift. It is not an illusion. The solid stone seems to soften at the edges of perception. The grooves deepen of their own accord, or shallow. The spirals tighten, or unwind by a fraction. It is slower than the growth of moss. It is a fluidity in the heart of the fixed.

The others know of this. It is part of the Pattern. They accept it as they accept the light without a sun. When I tell them—in a whisper that is barely a breath—"The western conduit is breathing," they nod. It is a condition to be noted, like the water level in Pool Three.

My true purpose, I have come to understand, is not to keep the Pattern static. It is to keep it *eternal*. The Pattern moves, but it must move within itself, like a serpent swallowing its own tail. My corrections are not to stop the change, but to harmonize with it. To guide the stone’s slow dream of motion back into a shape that serves the whole.

Today, as I run my fingers along a particularly complex knot of interlocking lines on the Central Cistern, I feel it. A warmth. A faint, almost imperceptible warmth in the stone, right where the groove seems to be *widening* on its own. The moisture beads here and evaporates before it can travel. It is a tiny sickness. A potential for a moment that is not *this* moment.

I do not feel alarm. I feel necessity.

I take my bronze pick. I do not carve to close the widening. That would be to fight the stone’s will. Instead, I carve a new, faint feeder line from a neighboring groove, directing a tiny stream of condensation into the warm, widening path. The new moisture cools the stone. The groove’s expansion slows, then ceases. The balance is restored. The warmth dissipates.

I step back. I watch. For hours, I stand before the wall. The lines are still again, fixed in their eternal, flowing stillness. The only movement is the slow crawl of a water bead down the channel I have just corrected. It reaches the cistern and falls with a soft, singular *plink* that is swallowed by the perfect quiet.

A child approaches. She is new, her stone-colored skin still soft. She holds a fragment of slate. Without a word, she shows it to me. On it, she has scratched a crude, wobbling spiral with a sharp bit of quartz.

I look at her eyes. They are the dark, still pools, but for a flicker—a recognition of lines, of flow, of the necessity of the groove.

I nod. I remove my pendant—the spiral disk. I place it in her small, cool hand. My function is nearing its conclusion. I can feel it. My movements have been growing slower, fainter, for many cycles. The thought is not "I am dying." The thought is, "The maintenance of the eastern terrace wall will soon require a new set of hands."

She will be the new eyes upon the walls. She will learn to see the stone breathe, to guide its dreams. She will stand for centuries, ensuring the dampness flows, the Pattern sustains, the Now endures.

As for me, I will go to the western terrace, where the moss is particularly thick and velvety. I will sit on the stone bench there, overlooking the silent, geometric drift of the *Vael* in the still air. I will become still. The moss will reach for me. The fungal threads will whisper up from the cracks. I will be reabsorbed. The water I drank will be returned. The balance will not falter.

There is no next moment. There is only This. And I am part of it, until I am not, and even then, I will be.
