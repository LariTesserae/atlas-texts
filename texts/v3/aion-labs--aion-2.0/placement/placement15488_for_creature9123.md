I am a grazer. My world is the taste of violet moss and the hum in my bones.

I do not remember a beginning. I scuttle. My six legs are jointed and hard, chitinous plates the color of oxidized iron, fitting tight against the wind. It is always there, the wind, a river that wants to peel me from the stone and hurl me into the void. I lean into it. My body is low, aerodynamic, a rounded capsule with legs that grip the polished basalt through pads of tough, elastic flesh.

My eyes are compound, a thousand faceted lenses. They do not see detail, but movement and light gradients. The plateau is a wash of sterile blue-white and the deep black of shadows. I see the shimmer of the kite-armadas far above, veiling the sun in rhythmic pulses. I see the sudden, spiraling drop of a hunter, and I freeze, my carapace blending with the stone. Movement is death. Stillness is life. Stillness, and the endless, patient scuttle from one moss-patch to the next.

The sound is everything. It is not something I hear; it is something I am within. The shriek of the wind through the arches is the medium I breathe. The deep, subsonic hum is my heartbeat. It resonates in the hollow spaces of my body, a constant vibration that orders my world. It tells me when to eat. It tells me when to move. And it tells me when to build.

The urge comes not as a thought, but as a physical compulsion, a shift in the hum that tightens the muscles around my thoracic ganglia. I stop feeding. My mandibles, which were snipping neat pieces of rubbery violet moss, go still. I turn from the food source. My vision narrows. I am looking for stones.

Not any stones. Flat stones. Thin, palm-sized discs of basalt, sheared smooth by millennia of abrasion. I find them in the lee of ridges, where the wind has sorted the debris. I pick one up in my foreclaws, which are surprisingly dexterous. I carry it to a clear, level area of rock. I place it down. I find another. I stack it precisely atop the first. The compulsion is a precise algorithm in my nerves. The alignment must be perfect. The tower must grow.

As I work, the world recedes. The shriek of the wind becomes a single, pure tone. The hum deepens, becomes a warm thrum in my gut. This is the only time I experience something akin to peace. The meaningless, meticulous creation. The tower rises, stone upon stone, a fragile column in this world of immense, eternal forms. It is a tiny defiance of the horizontal, a momentary accent.

I have built hundreds. Thousands. I do not count. Each one is a unique configuration, dictated by the shapes of the stones I find. Sometimes they are four stones high, sometimes ten. The record is fourteen, a trembling obelisk that stood for almost a full feeding cycle before the urge to dismantle arrived.

It always arrives. As suddenly as the building compulsion, the hum shifts again. A new frequency, a release. I must knock it down. I approach my creation. I do not feel regret. I feel a completion. With a careful strike of one claw, I tap the base stone. The tower collapses in a soft clatter of rock. The sound is infinitesimal, swallowed instantly by the roar. The stones scatter. My job is done. The tension leaves my body. I return to the moss, or I move on, scuttling to the next patch, the next flat stone waiting in the wind.

I have seen the Listeners. Not with understanding, but with perception. Once, when the mist lay thick in a quartz-filled gouge and the air turned milky, I saw the air thicken and shiver. A scaffold of resonance formed, a humanoid shape of rippling sound and dust. I froze, my instinctual fear of unusual movement warring with the deep, subsonic pull that said *this is source, this is order*. The thing had no face, but I felt its attention pass over me like a change in pressure. It was gathering something—flakes of basalt swirled in a miniature cyclone before it. There was a series of sharp, concussive *clicks*, faster than any hunter’s strike. A hand-axe fell to the stone. The shimmering shape dissolved. The wind reclaimed the dust. I remained frozen for a long time after, before the hum prompted me to resume my grazing.

The tools are there. I pass them. The hand-axes, the woven nets. They are part of the landscape, like the domes and the seeps. They have no scent, no meaning. They are simply *correct*. Their symmetry pleases something in my foundational vibration. I sometimes make a detour to go around them, as if their presence creates a subtle barrier in the air.

My life is a loop. Graze. Be still under the spiraling shadow. Graze. Build. Dismantle. Graze. The condensation wets my carapace in the cold stillness. The sun never sets. The wind never stills. I am a component in a machine of perfect balance. My flesh will eventually fail. A hunter will catch me, or my legs will wear down. My body will fall. The lichen will bloom over it, grey-green and eager, and in a short time, there will be no trace. Another grazer will take my place. The hum will continue. The towers will be built and knocked down.

I do not question. The hum is my question and my answer. But sometimes, in the moment between placing one stone and reaching for the next, when the world narrows to the precise alignment of two edges, I experience a flash of… something else. A silence within the roar. A point of stillness that is not compelled, but chosen. In that nanosecond, I am not a grazer acting on instruction. I am the builder of a tower. It is a terrifying emptiness, a gulf that threatens to swallow the whole plateau. Then the next stone is in my claw, the hum reasserts its pattern, and the feeling is gone, forgotten, absorbed back into the endless, roaring song of the Listeners.

I am a grazer. I stack stones. I am part of the perpetual arrival. I am content.
