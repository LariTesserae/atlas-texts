The attention began not as an event, but as a deepening.

For three full cycles of the fungal lights brightening and dimming, the mist in the bowl had held a new quality. It was not thicker, nor thinner, but it seemed to hang with a kind of intent. The drips from the fern-trees fell with a clearer, more resonant *plink*. The slate-beetles, on their endless, silent circuits, would pause, their heads lifting a fraction, their antennae quivering as if tasting a new scent on the damp air—a scent of ozone and cold stone and something like the memory of sunlight on ancient ice.

The lithic beings in the crevices became restless. Their configurations shifted more frequently; a pebble-eye would dissolve and reform a hand’s breadth away; the lichen-smear of attention would pulse with a faint, warm luminescence. The mountain was dreaming more vividly.

The moss-folk felt it in their hands. When they patted a moss roof or ran fingers along a seam in their stone domes, a low thrum traveled up their arms, a vibration too deep for sound, the bedrock humming a note it had never hummed before. They did not speak of it. They gathered instead, in the central space where the pathways converged, a flat expanse of rock worn glass-smooth by generations of silent feet. They stood in a loose circle, their large, dark eyes reflecting not just the blue-green fungal glow, but a new, silver sheen that seemed to cling to the mist itself.

On the fourth dimming, it arrived.

It did not descend from the pearl-grey sky. It did not stride from the crevices. It *unfolded* from the quality of the air itself. First, a point of calm in the center of the gathering space, where the mist began to spiral slowly, not as wind, but as if drained downward. Then, from that point, a figure coalesced.

It was not a shape of flesh or stone or light alone, but an impression made solid. To the moss-folk, it appeared first as a being of their own scale, but woven from the substances of their world. Its “skin” was the blue-grey of the young mountain’s raw stone, but polished to a soft, deep lustre. Where joints might be, seams of glowing, gold-white lichen traced patterns of impossible complexity. Its head was a smooth ovoid, featureless but for two recesses that held not eyes, but pools of condensed mist, swirling with slow, intelligent currents. From its form drifted a scent of crushed fern, of wet earth after a first frost, of deep time.

It was the Gardener of Deep Time. A tendril of a consciousness so vast and patient it measured its life in the birth and death of stars. It had felt the subtle, green murmur of this young bowl across the silence between worlds—a new, fragile chord in the symphony of being. It had come not to prune or plant, but to listen, and perhaps, to bless.

The Gardener did not move. It stood, and its presence was a pressure, not of weight, but of profound *attention*. It was observing the bowl, the moss-folk, the grazers, the pulsing air-jellies, the watching lithic beings, with a focus that was both utterly alien and intimately familiar. It saw the precarious balance, the whispering symbiosis, the silent, careful work of hands on stone.

Then, it extended a hand—a shape of smoothed rock and glowing seams. It did not reach for a person, but toward the ground. Its fingers, long and articulate, touched the glass-smooth stone of the gathering place.

A ripple went out. Not a wave of force, but of *understanding*. The stone itself seemed to become translucent for a heartbeat, and within it, the moss-folk saw—not with their eyes, but in the center of their minds—the living history of their bowl. They saw the mountains heaving upward, raw and molten; they saw the first spores finding purchase on the bare rock; they saw the slow, patient evolution of the slate-beetles and the air-jellies; they saw their own ancestors, guided by intuition, making their first, careful domes. They saw the network of life—a shimmering web of give-and-take, of breath and condensation, of pruning and growth. They saw their own place in it, not as masters, but as a particularly aware strand in the web.

The vision faded, leaving them breathless, their silent communion deepened to a shared awe.

The Gardener then turned its mist-pool gaze upon them. It did not speak in hums or clicks. It spoke in direct concept, impressed upon the mind like a seal in soft wax.

*You tend a garden that is also your gardener. The stone thinks. The green listens. You are the hands that help the thought take shape, the voice that gives the listening a song. This is rare. This is good.*

The presence was approving, warm as a sun-warmed rock.

*But the mountain is young. Its thoughts are dreams, powerful but formless. Your songs are whispers, easily lost in the mist of time. I offer a bridge.*

The Gardener’s form shimmered. From its chest, where the stone and lichen met, a sphere of soft, silver light emerged. It was no larger than a moss-folk’s fist. It hovered, pulsing gently in time with the distant, slow heartbeat of the mountain.

*This is a seed of communion. It is not a tool. It is an opening.*

The sphere drifted to the eldest of the moss-folk present, a woman whose hair was braided with generations of luminous mycelium. It did not touch her. It hung before her forehead, and then, with a sound like a distant chime felt in the bones, it dissolved into a shower of silver motes that sank into the stone at her feet, into the moss on the surrounding domes, into the very air.

For a moment, nothing happened.

Then, the eldest woman gasped, a soft, sharp inhalation that was the loudest sound any moss-folk had made in a generation. She looked toward the nearest deep crevice, where a lithic being—a cluster of quartz veins that had always vaguely resembled a leaning ear—now pulsed with a gentle, shared light.

She *heard* it.

Not a sound, but a thought, vast and slow. A thought of pressure, of cooling, of the satisfaction of a ridge line finally settling into place. It was the mountain’s contentment. And beneath it, a question, formed with the patience of centuries: *Do the small hands find the stone good?*

Tentatively, silently, the elder formed her own thought, not in words, but in sensations: the cool smoothness of a finished wall, the soft resilience of healthy moss, the quiet joy of a well-made dome sheltering her people. She sent this feeling toward the crevice.

The quartz veins glowed brighter. A wave of warm, stony pleasure echoed back, followed by another slow thought-image: a potential future, a section of the bowl where the rock was yet unstable, a suggestion of where the stone-borers might be gently guided to ease the pressure. It was an invitation to co-create.

All around the bowl, other connections sparked. Moss-folk standing near grazers felt the simple, rhythmic purpose of the beetle’s pruning, a contentment in the act of grazing that was its own form of meditation. Those near the fern-trees received faint, filtering impressions from the air-jellies—a sense of the air’s cleanliness, a passive joy in the light they captured. The bridge was not just to the lithic beings, but to all the conscious and semi-conscious elements of their world.

The Gardener observed, its mist-pool eyes swirling. The blessing had taken root. The silent attendance was now a silent conversation. The balance was no longer just precarious; it was now conscious, a chosen harmony.

Its work was done. It had not changed a single rule of the bowl. Gravity still behaved strangely in places; time still pooled in the hollows; the mist still rolled. But now, the inhabitants could navigate these quirks not just through instinct, but with the shared understanding of the mountain itself. They could plan, not as conquerors of their environment, but as partners in its slow becoming.

The Gardener’s form began to fade, unwinding back into the substance of the air. As it dissolved, it left one final thought, a whisper that was both farewell and promise:

*Tend well this cradle. You are the first dreamers of this place. Dream it beautiful.*

And then it was gone. The mist lost its extra density. The air held only the familiar scents of moss and damp stone. The silver sheen faded from the eyes of the gathering.

But everything was different.

The elder looked at her people. No words were needed. A new, collective hum rose from them, lower and more resonant than any they had made before. It was a hum that vibrated in their chests and in the stone beneath their feet. It was the beginning of their first shared song with the mountain—a song of stone and moss and time.

In the days that followed, the moss-folk moved with a new, quiet confidence. Their work on new domes became even more precise, as they could now feel the stone’s own suggestions for seam and curve. They guided the grazers not just by observation, but by a mutual understanding of where the moss most needed tending. The fungal lights glowed with a steadier, clearer light, as the cultivating insects responded to the new harmony.

The bowl was still a cradle, damp and cool and lush, small enough to comprehend, strange enough to never fully know. But now, the whisper had become a dialogue. The young mountains, cupping their living bowl, felt the attention of their small inhabitants, and in their slow, tectonic way, they smiled.
