They are there the whole time you are. That is the hardest part to accept afterward.

You do not meet them coming down the wall, or on the root-mat, or in the clearings where their houses wait. You come to understand, slowly, that the absence you have been reading as animal life — no trails, no dung, no gnawed bark — is not absence at all. It is discretion carried to the point of physiology. Everything that lives largely in the basin has learned not to move in a way that leaves a difference behind it. The people have learned it best.

One to a clearing. Sometimes, as the description of their houses suggests, within sight of another's roof, which is as close as they ordinarily come. They are not a village and they are not exactly hermits, because hermitage implies a world elsewhere to withdraw from. There is no elsewhere down there, once you have stayed. The pit is eight kilometers wide, and for them it is not small. A life can be spent in the territory between three great parasol trees without exhausting it, because the territory is vertical as much as it is horizontal — mat, under-mat, trunk, crown, the cups and hollows and shaded forks of a single tree being separate countries with separate weathers — and because they traverse it at a speed that makes distance mean something else.

They look human, first. That needs to be said, because after a few hours in that green pressure your eyes start granting personhood to trunks. But they *are* human, or were the same stock as the people of the plain above: tall in the frame, narrow, long-handed. And then the basin has worked on them in the only way it works on anything, not by changing them into something new but by arresting them halfway through changes that never finish.

Their skin is filmed, as yours becomes within an hour, except that for them the film does not wash or wear off. It is a permanent, living glaze — sweat, plant oil, a fine green algae that takes root in the outer layer wherever the light is strongest, across the shoulders, the backs of the arms, the brow, the shins. In the glare-meadow by the north wall they are almost emerald on their lit side and brown on the other. In the center groves they go darker, dappled, their hair — black, uncut, worn loose because anything tied would eventually be grown into — matted with moss at the nape where they rest their heads. They wear nothing that was not once alive and is not still, in some sense, trying to be: skirts and wraps of broad leaf replaced before they can rot, which is to say rarely, a girdle of plaited vine that has rooted into itself and must be unwound rather than untied. Some of the older ones have vine tendrils that have found the warmth of their waist or ankle and clung there for long enough that skin and bark have negotiated a border. They do not tear them off. Nothing down there is torn if it can be persuaded.

They move the way you find yourself moving after a few hours, only perfected, and without your underlying wish to get somewhere. A hand rises to part a leaf and takes so long about it that the leaf seems to have decided on its own to bend. They walk on the root-mat barefoot, the soles of their feet thickened and splayed from a lifetime on a floor that gives, toes gripping root and bark unconsciously even at rest. They do not step over things. They step *between* things, placing the whole foot down as if testing whether the world will consent to bear them this time, too. A crossing of a clearing that would take you forty paces takes them the better part of what you would call a morning, not because they are feeble — their arms, when they climb, are corded and absolute — but because there is no reason contained in their bodies for it to take less time than that.

And mostly they do not cross clearings. Mostly they are still.

That is why you do not see them. A person of the basin spends the bulk of existence in one of a handful of postures maintained for durations your mind keeps trying to shorten in retrospect. Sitting in the circle of eight stones in their clearing, where the mat is kept bare not by weeding but by the steady, mild chemistry of a body returning to the same place forever — skin oil, urine absorbed without trace, the simple refusal of their presence to let seedlings establish. Sitting up in the crown of their tree, in a fork lined with humus and orchid root, knees drawn up, back against a limb, becoming one more vertical element in a place that reads all vertical elements as trunk. Lying on the sleeping platform between three trunks, on their side, breathing. Sleeping, there, is not sharply distinct from waking, and neither is sharply distinct from the long, alert rest that occupies most of their lives. Their eyes will be open. They will be looking at a leaf perhaps a meter away, at condensation gathering to its invariable bead and evaporating at its invariable rate, and that leaf will be sufficient. If you passed below, in your crashing hurry, lifting your boots and putting them down, they would watch you the way you watched the emerald lizard on the trunk: with a complete, unhurried attention, and without any impulse to announce themselves to a creature whose business is so obviously temporary.

Their temperature is the temperature of the air. Ninety-eight, a hundred degrees. They do not experience the basin as hot, any more than you experience the inside of your own mouth as hot, and this is the deepest of their adaptations, because it removes the body's clock. No cold to wake to, no evening cool to sleep by, no hunger fired by the need to keep warm. Their hearts beat slow. In rest — which is to say, usually — you could press your ear to one of their chests (they would permit it, if you moved slowly enough arriving) and wait a long, embarrassed interval between beats, long enough to wonder if you had imagined the last one, and their breath would come perhaps four times in a minute, each one shallow, through the nose, tasting the green. They eat in the same register. A fruit that has hung ripe until its seeds sprouted through its skin — they will take such a fruit, eventually, and eat it seeds and all in small bites over hours, or squeeze a fleshy stem between their teeth for its peppery water, or eat the soft heart of a fallen palm shoot that the mat has already half-accepted. It is enough. Almost everything they might need the light does for the algae in their skin, in a small way, and the rest they need very slowly.

They do not cook. There has never been fire in the pit, and not only because nothing ever dries enough to burn well. Fire is an event, and the basin does not do events. Their teeth are worn flat and stained green-brown from stripping fiber; their hands are their only tools, and the teeth of others. When a dome is built — a thing that happens perhaps once in a generation, when a child finally grows, or an old house is finally surrendered to the plants' slow decision — it is built by a single pair of hands bending saplings over the course of months, weighting them with stones carried one at a time from the wall, lashing them with vine that is then allowed to root and make the lashing permanent. The eight stones of the circles are the pit's only furniture and its only monument. No one knows, including them, who first stood them up. They have been maintained so long that maintenance and origin have merged.

They speak the way the frogs in the bromeliad cups speak. On some days — there are days, down there, despite everything, marked by a barely perceptible thickening of the light — a voice will lift out of one crown or one clearing, a clear, unadorned note that resolves, if you are close enough and have stayed long enough for your ears to be rinsed of hurry, into a word or two. A name. A statement of placement: *here still.* And much later, from another part of the basin, an answer. Their language has shed everything that language up on the plain uses to handle change. It has almost no verbs that distinguish past from future. It has very many words for kinds of green brightness, for the feel of different mats underfoot, for the states of condensation, for the particular quality of stillness in a particular grove. It has no word for *alone*, though each of them is alone for years at a stretch, because aloneness requires the idea of company as a norm. They visit one another the way weather visits, which is to say almost never and without announcement: after a decade of exchanged notes across the canopy, one will appear at the edge of another's clearing, and sit at the edge of the stones, and after a while be joined, and after a while longer share food or sex or the raising of hands to each other's mossed hair in a grooming so slow it looks like a plant adjusting to light. Children are conceived on that timescale and born into it. A child of the basin stands knee-high beside knee-high saplings for what would be, up above, an entire childhood, tended by whichever adult's tree it happens to be under, fed mouth to mouth with chewed shoot-pith, learning stillness before it learns words. Some of them grow. The pit is full, in its perfect equilibrium, so most do not need to.

They do not die in any way that leaves evidence you would recognize. Age is not forbidden down there — the oldest of them have skin where the algae has died back to grey in patches, hair gone white and then gone green again with a finer moss, spines curved to the exact curve of the fork they sit in — but it is not a direction, either. It is another kind of staying. When one of them finally stops — and you gather, from the one or two stories that can be coaxed out over days of sitting nearby, that this is felt coming as a kind of internal permission, a loosening — they go, if they can, toward the dark center where the mat is spongiest, and lie down in a hollow between roots, and the basin does to them what it does to a fallen log. They are not buried. They soften, to exactly the texture of the mat, and no further. Skins of living growth close over. There are no bones in the pit because there is no process in the pit capable of leaving a bone bare.

It may be that they came originally from the plain — that the first dome-builders slid down the moss wall on their bellies fleeing a war, a drought, a punishment, some event too large for the world up there. Their stories do not confirm this. Their stories, such as they are, begin with the pit already green and themselves already in it, and the lack of a beginning does not trouble them. Occasionally one of them climbs out. You find the evidence sometimes on the rim: a set of root-gripping handholds worn slightly deeper than the plants explain, a place where the moss has been pressed by a body coming up. Why they go, and whether they come back older for the dry wind and the moving time up there, they cannot say in words you would keep. The houses wait in the meantime, beaded with condensation, roofs alive at the edges, stone circles kept clear by an absence shaped exactly like a person.

If you stay long enough you will be offered the only hospitality they practice: not food, not conversation, but a place. An adult will vacate, by slow degrees, a fork in a tree, or a corner of a platform, and that space will remain vacated beside you for as long as you can manage to occupy it without fidgeting it into a mere campsite. To accept is to begin. Your sweat will stop drying between one hour and the next. Your movements will begin, despite yourself, to make editorial decisions about what is worth breaking. A green film will establish itself along your forearms and be in no rush to spread.

And from the crown, some time later, a white, long-necked bird will beat up from a neighboring tree and be gone, and a voice from very far away will produce one clear note, and you will understand that it is not calling to you, exactly — it is registering, for whoever keeps the basin's long account, that another clearing is now, occasionally, occupied.
