Nothing up there lives spread out. Everything lives in pockets, and in lines between pockets.

You learn that first by looking for the smallest of them. In the brine an arm's depth under the crust, and in the pumped ponds left turquoise and cobalt and arterial pink to concentrate under the sun, there is a bloom that needs almost no fresh water at all. Algae that thrive in poison salt, that color the water and then color everything that eats them. Brine shrimp cloud the warmest, pinkest ponds so thick the surface moves the wrong way against the wind. Brine flies own the edges — a black smudge that rises a million at a footstep and settles again without a sound. They are not decoration. They are the food base, in a place with almost no plants to start a chain with. Everything else eats them, or eats what eats them, in an exchange so tight and rapid you can watch it turn over in an hour.

The flamingos are ankle-deep in it all day. Three kinds of them, in lines of hundreds, thousands stretched from pond to pond across the whole floor, heads down, heads sweeping. They do not stop at dark. At midnight you can still hear — not hear, feel in your chest — the soft collective rush when a flock lifts, and then the settling again, and the feeding resumed in the black. Their pink is not theirs. It is borrowed straight from the algae, taken up through the shrimp, worn on the feather. Where the pond is paler they are paler. Where it blooms, they flare. They are economical birds for a place where sound will not carry and water will not last: no wasted calling, no wasted flight, just that metronome sweep through brine that would burn your skin in a cut.

On every warm pipe joint is a lizard the exact color of rust on salt. They do not hunt so much as wait in the heat other animals flee, tongues tasting flies. Swifts have taken the eroded bases of the cliffs where slope meets flat, and at the peak of the day, when the workers are asleep and the machines are rested, the swifts pour out anyway into the thermals until the rising air looks feathered. Nothing here has only one hour for living; some take the noon that kills the others.

On the margin, where the white gives way to stone and tussock grass grows in single yellow tufts that never touch each other, and a red halophyte creeps in mats no higher than a finger, the larger animals keep the same rule of pockets. Vicuña pick with impossible delicacy between salt and grass, their blood built for this thin, bruised-blue air that leaves a newcomer panting at a walk. Hawks nest, improbably, on pump-house roofs, the only high thing for kilometers. And a small pale fox has given up hunting entirely on any schedule of her own. She has learned the workers' shift changes by heart. She knows the condensers on the rim, too.

Those condensers are the clearest portrait of whoever lives here. At evening, when the ground is still giving back the day and the ponds stay warm to a hand, moisture beads on cold metal fins meant to pull liters from air that seems to have none. And under them, untaught, a queue forms. Insects first, at the first bead. Then birds, lapping from pipe and leaf and salt crust in the cold hour before dawn. Then the fox. Last, if at all, anything larger. Everyone licks condensation. Everyone knows shade is not a comfort here, it is a material, rationed like water.

The people belong in that queue, whether they like it or not. They do not live in a town on the flat; there is none. They live scattered, like the pump-houses — low salt-brick sheds every kilometer, half-buried against wind, thick-walled so that inside it is merely hot instead of dangerous. They wear UV hoods and reflective suits and move only along the causeways, because they know the beautiful plates between ponds may be a centimeter of crystal over black caustic mud that will take a boot. They drink before they are thirsty. They sleep at noon while the crystals grow, white and feather-fine, a centimeter in a hot week along the pond's edge. Their trucks crawl raising dust that hangs and dissolves in silence, but mostly they walk slowly, to spare their breath, and speak little, because the flat drinks voices.

And the salt gets into them the way it gets into everything else. Not metaphorically. Their faces are powdered permanently white. It cakes in the creases of the suit, at the lip, in the hair. It fills abandoned windows and repairs tire tracks overnight by wicking brine up and freezing it over in new pattern. Abandoned things here do not rot and do not wash away — they encrust, roof intact, gradually salted into the floor. The workers are on the same trajectory while they are still alive. A season and they move like natives: never trusting distance, because a shed that looks twenty minutes away is three hours in that clear thin light; never trusting a blue lake at dusk, because it is only hot air bending sky down to salt; listening at night not for noise but for the faint ticking as the crust contracts.

Because that is the final shared trait: quiet, and fullness without waste. At this height the pumps hum below what thin air can carry. Evaporation makes no sound. Crystals grow visibly and silently. Overhead the stars are not sparse and romantic, they are crowded and hard and close, packed as densely as the hexagons below, and below the ponds and flies and feathers and suits are packed into every seam where a drop, a shadow, a warm joint exists. There is no empty place for an inhabitant to be alone in.

Before dawn the salt ticks, the flies stir first on the warm brine, the flamingos are already feeding, the fox slips back from the condensers, the pumps resume their inaudible work. And the people come out of the sheds powdered white as the ground, hooded against a sun that has not yet risen, and take their place again in the line — not above the shrimp and the swift and the vicuña, just larger, slower-breathing, and learning, like everything else that manages to live on the roof of the world in a shallow oven of salt: to take color from what you eat, to eat in the dark, to rest at noon, to lick dew from metal, and to let the place slowly, beautifully, encrust you rather than try to wash it off.
