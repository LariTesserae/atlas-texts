You don't see them at first.

That is not because they are hiding. Nothing in the room has room to hide — six feet across, and every inch of it already occupied twice. It is because, at first, you take them for another part of the room that has not finished being made.

There is a shape by the basin. Curled. Low. The color of the walls where the fresh mud has been smeared that morning, and sprouting, like the walls. A knee, perhaps, under a taro leaf. A shoulder under moss. A back breathing very slowly under a woven mat that has gone green and soft with mildew and begun, in places, to root into what is underneath it.

You brush it moving sideways, because you brush everything moving sideways, and it is warm.

Warmer than you. Warmer than the air, which is already hot enough to rain on your skin. That is how you know. You are the coldest thing that has entered in a long time, so the room condenses on you. It does not condense on them. Around their skin the steam thins and lifts — falls upward, in the local way — and for a second you can see a face.

They have been looking at you the whole time.

They are not tall. Nobody could be tall in here for long. The altitude packed into the room would punish it — ears, nose, the soft places behind the eyes. They have learned to live folded. Sitting, crouching, kneeling in the manufactured soil, lying along the curve of the wall in the sleeping blankets, they occupy a series of low postures like a plant occupying shade, changing shape by the hour to fit the light. When they do stand — and they must stand, to lash the cane of the ceiling one notch higher, to reach the ceiling garden — they rise in stages, pausing, swallowing, letting their ears pop and clear and pop again, blood brightening at one nostril, until at full height, with a hand braced on the dome, they are breathing frost off the stool-top air while their bare feet are too hot on the floor. Nine inches, and a whole climate, run the length of them.

So their body has sorted itself by altitude too.

Their feet are broad and scarred and almost horned from the compost heat under the floor tiles, perpetually muddy to the ankle in soil they made themselves — charcoal dust, fermented leaf, ground bone, rotted thatch, ash, their own hair and nail parings and shed skin worked in without distinction. Nothing is wasted because there is no natural substrate to waste it on. The garden feeds them, and they feed the garden, and the floor gets deeper, and the mountain gets higher, in the same gesture.

Their hands are the clearest part of them. The rest may be clay-smeared, leaf-shadowed, draped in a shirt that has been patched so often with woven grass that the grass has taken and grown, but the hands are kept clean by constant work in water and wet earth. Broad knuckles. Nails worn to nothing. Palms permanently stained — green from bruised stems, black from charcoal, ochre under the thumbnails from painting flies on the wall. The fingers never stop. Even while they look at you, even while they speak — and they must raise their voice over the knocking bamboo, the sighing pot, the kettle buried in the floor switching notes in its split reed — the fingers go on teasing a root straight, pressing soil around a seedling, dabbling a hair brush from blossom to blossom.

That brush is their hair. You understand that after a while. There is almost no fauna here, so the inhabitant has had to become it, species by species, as needed. They are the bee for the orchids and the fly for the taro flowers and the beetle in the black compost, turning it, and the worm in it after rain, and the bird, in a limited way, whistling through a reed to answer the kettle so it doesn't scream. They hum while they pollinate, low in the throat, not a song exactly but a vibration the stamens have learned to expect. Painted flies may encourage the idea of flies. The hands do the actual work.

Their hair is cut short in uneven handfuls where brushes have been harvested from it, and what remains is plastered close with clay against the wet. Clay is also in the cracks of their elbows, in the creases of the neck, packed along a long scar on one forearm — not decoration, though patterns have formed where seedlings have rooted in it and been gently removed, leaving pale, leaf-shaped prints. They maintain themselves the way they maintain the walls: daily, by addition. A little fresh mud before it dries. A little soil pressed in. If the making stopped, they seem to understand in their bones, the pocket would equalize. So would they.

Their age is difficult in the way everything here is difficult. The hands are old. The eyes, when the green-brown dusk finds them, are not. The teeth are worn flat from grit in the food, but new ones — you could swear this, and you would not swear to direction or weather or duration in here, so perhaps it means something that you would swear to this — new ones are coming in behind, small and white as the frog that sometimes sits in the frost. Generations may be packed into them the same way altitude is packed into the room, occupant after occupant climbing the pipe from below, staying a season, a year, a life, adding their bone eventually to the manufactured soil, until the one who tends the summit now is less a person than a position that has grown a person around itself to get the work done. The builders below, carrying earth basket by basket up the scaffolding, have a name for them, but it translates only as *the top one*. Or *the one still deciding*. Or *the potter* — because from their perch in the clay bulge, coiling fresh wall over the basket frame, lashing cane higher, smearing, planting, they are not so much living on the mountain as throwing it, ring by ring, downward and outward beneath them, a pot inverted into a world.

They do not leave. Leaving is a technical possibility, like the door you would expect to find and don't. The pipe goes down. Someone could crawl it. But the summit rises every day by exactly the amount they add, and the inside rises faster, so the longer they stay the further down it is, measured in ears clearing and hot water in the face and a body-length that can contain several thousand feet. Their lungs have adjusted to an air that isn't air anymore, up where it is a bright ache in the teeth, nested inside air that is too thick to breathe quickly. Their blood knows the sudden drops when the frog appears and every sound stops. They live braced for those drops the way sailors live braced for roll — knees soft, one hand always on something living.

And they live in the wrong direction, cheerfully, without correcting for visitors. They reach *down* for the ceiling seedlings when condensation fails upward. They sweep smoke off the floor each morning where it has pooled warm around the sleeping blankets, and breathe the clean, steamy layer above it. They hang tools by their shadows because hooks slide. When a dropped seed hovers, turning, undecided between root and shoot, they leave it alone for days, sometimes addressing it by a temporary name, feeding it mist from their own mouth until it chooses a wall. They can read the slow rain in the corner the way you would read a calendar. A drop you ducked under at dawn will be greeted again at dusk, still trembling on its way down, and they will tell you — this is the only warning you are given that sounds like a warning — not to touch. It is still deciding to be weather. So are many things here, including, perhaps, them.

They eat sparingly and continuously, the way the garden produces: a mushroom still warm from under the taro shade, a shoot snapped and dipped in ash, tea from the compost kettle that tastes of iron and leaves and animal fat from the lamp. There is a stack of unfired bowls but they mostly eat from a leaf, from a coconut half, from their cupped clay hands. What they are cultivating, in tray and gourd and crack and basin, is not exactly food anyway. It is density. More kinds of life per inch than the six feet should be able to argue for. Ferns ticking open. Orchids rooting in air. Moss taking the hammer. A beetle-garden under a mushroom under a leaf. Each new complexity makes the pocket harder to collapse.

By the time they press your visitor's lump of wet clay into your palm, and the handful of dark soil that smells sweetly, impossibly, of other people, you have stopped being able to tell where the inhabitant ends and the maintenance begins. They show you — not with words, with the placement of your own hands — where a pipe joint weeps and needs mending, where a seedling is loose. Their fingers over yours are hot and sure. If you do it wrong the clay still takes your fingerprint. Everything here does.

Later, when the pipe has lengthened against your face and you are out again in the thin, freezing wind, soaked, muddy, smelling of orchid and compost, you will try to describe them and find you are describing a posture more than a face. Low, warm, endlessly adding. A gardener who has become the missing fauna. A potter coiling a mountain up around a garden. A body with frost at one end and burns at the other, holding still in the middle so that up can keep disagreeing with itself a little longer.

Inside the bulge, already, they will have forgotten the particular shape of you. Not unkindly. The way weather forgets. They will be lashing the cane one notch higher for someone whose ears have not yet popped, painting another fly beside the older flies to keep the idea going, listening for the season's one silence —

and pressing, with a thumb, a new green shoot gently into the soft wall of the world, so it does not finish.
