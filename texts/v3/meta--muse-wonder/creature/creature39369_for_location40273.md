The inhabitants begin, long before you meet a person, as hands.

Hands over a market counter, flickering so fast the wet fish in the basket seem slow. Hands on a rope at the bucket-chain, three men to a crank, counting not aloud but by the squeeze of a palm on the next man's shoulder: pull, pull, pause. A woman on a roof, re-setting a stone tile in the perpetual drip, signaling down to her daughter — *more lime, thinner, watch the edge* — with her chin tucked and both forearms busy. A child no more than four, standing in a canal-alley in water to her ankles, holding a serious conversation entirely in sign with a grandfather who answers, at the same speed, while mending a net full of snails.

If you stay long enough to stop trying to shout outdoors, you learn that deafness here is not a condition. It is the weather.

### The People of the Basin

They are short, broad-handed people for the most part, built low to the work. Hauling stone, hauling water, hauling hide slings of lime and ash up from the pits — it makes shoulders thick early. Their forearms are ropey, their fingers blunt and astonishingly articulate. A mason can hold a whole argument about the quality of a block while still holding the block. A fish-seller's hands never quite stop moving even in sleep; old women in the bathhouses say you can read their dreams by their fingers twitching against the steam.

Their skin is always damp. Not sweating, exactly — there's nowhere for sweat to go in this air — but filmed, warm, softened at the knuckles and the soles from never being truly dry. Cuts heal slow and pale here. Everyone smells faintly of sulfur, wet plaster, fish oil, and tanned hide, and after a week so do you, on the skin and in the hair and in the back of the throat where the water has rinsed you from inside.

They dress for wet stone, not for seasons, because there are none. Tunics and wraps of hide and woven hair, dark with use, that can be wrung out and put back on. Sleeves bound tight at the wrist so they don't catch when signing. Thin stone-soled sandals, replaced often, because the ground eats leather and in the marked streets it is hot enough through a thick sole to make you shift your weight from foot to foot while you talk — though you wouldn't be talking, not out there. Hair is worn cropped or braided flat against the head and threaded with bone beads that click when they move indoors; those clicks are a private language too. Outdoors, nearly everyone over the age of six goes plugged: waxed wool, wadded and re-waxed, or little carved bone stoppers on a cord around the neck, worn as plainly as jewelry elsewhere. To go bare-eared in the streets is a mark of a visitor, a fool, or a rim-porter who will only be down for an hour.

Their faces have the look the road down first taught you to notice. Calm. Turned slightly inward. Eyes a little narrowed against the dim, even at noon — for the light under the steam cap is a permanent, heavy twilight, bright enough to work by, never bright enough to throw a sharp shadow. People raised here find direct sun painful for days after leaving the rim. They also find silence painful, in a different way. Take a basin-born porter up to the cold country for a delivery and he will sleep badly in the quiet, wake reaching for his earplugs that aren't there, unsettled by being able to hear his own breathing so clearly. At home he sleeps with the frogs coming up through the floor and the crickets singing inside the walls, and that pressure is what quiet means to him.

Because the world outside the house never stops roaring — the sinkhole spring at the far end of the floor, falling and boiling under everything like a river underground; the canals loud with frogs, tens of thousands of them, shoulder to shoulder on the lips and in the mortar joints; the insects grinding from every warm crack — speech has retreated indoors and become an intimate art, and sign has become the public one. And sign here is not a substitute. It is richer, in some domains, than their spoken tongue. There are signs for twelve grades of fish freshness that have no single spoken word. There are signs that incorporate the direction you're facing relative to the spring, so directions are never abstract. Insult is a high art form, performed across market squares to general silent hilarity — shoulders shaking, hands slapping thighs, no sound anyone can hear. Jokes told outdoors are visual, physical, often filthy, and children learn the rude ones first and are not much scolded for it.

Indoors the balance reverses. You duck through one leather curtain, then a second, into the dead-air space between the double walls, and the roar drops to a pressure, a vibration in the sternum and the soles of the feet. And then people *talk*. They talk constantly, companionably, at a volume that would be a shout anywhere else and here is just conversation, knee to knee around a stone table. They are hoarse-voiced as a population by middle age — not damaged, just thickened, like the vocal cords of singers who have performed in one smoky room all their lives. Old people lose the upper registers first and don't much mind; they drop back into sign at home too, and the young lean in.

Storytelling is an indoor, winterless, seasonless evening art that happens at any hour the water-clocks decree is rest-time. So is singing, which is done close and low, sometimes forehead nearly to forehead, sometimes with a hand laid flat on the singer's chest to feel the tune as much as hear it. Lovers do this. So do mothers with infants, who learn rhythm through the ribs before they learn it through the ears. A basin lullaby is felt as much as heard. Children acquire sign months before intelligible speech — their first words are usually *frog, water, hot, more* — and acquire speech later, indoors, as a kind of second, private fluency. To be basin-born and never learn to sign well is a deeper illiteracy than never learning to speak.

Their time is not sky-time. Appetite and sleep and the shift-bells — felt, not heard, great wooden clappers struck against stone in the gang houses so the blow travels through the ground — divide the unvarying dimness. The work-gangs who maintain the sluices and the canal levels are the closest thing to a priesthood and a government combined, not because the water is sacred but because it absolutely cannot be allowed to change. If the spring ever faltered, if the canals ever cooled, the frogs would stop breeding continuously, the midges would die back or explode, the fish would turn in the shallows — and the whole packed, interlocking density of the town would shudder. The gangs keep tallies of years on white stone. They do not keep tallies of rain. Rain falls straight out of the steam cap almost daily, warm as bathwater, and runs off the uncut lava into the canals and changes nothing.

They eat what the warm water gives them, which is a narrow diet made inventive by necessity. Whiskered carp from the canals, snails by the basketful, frogs' legs never — that is the one absolute taboo, carved in white stone at every bridge, less a law than a condition of survival. Pale, fat mushrooms from the cellars under the bathhouses, grown on imported dung and shredded hide. Duck, from the fearless ducks that work the market canals. And from outside, at great expense, everything the sterilized ground refuses: grain in grey-brown sacks, dried meat, wood. A roof beam is an heirloom here. Doors are patched across three generations and everyone knows which family's great-grandfather hauled that oak down the rim road. To waste wood is the local form of obscenity.

Death, in a place with no soil, is a stone problem. The dead are carried to the cold side of the rim — the one procession that always leaves the basin — or laid in sealed basalt chambers under the oldest houses, where the heat dries rather than rots. There are no graveyards to soften into green. An abandoned house does not fall picturesquely; it just stands, dripping under the steam, swifts pouring silently in and out of the holes left for them under the eaves, until someone needs the block and knocks it down. There are, in the local sense, no ruins and no ancestors visible on the landscape. Ancestors live indoors instead: in the reused beam, in the story told knee to knee, in the particular flourish an old woman's family puts on the sign for *home*.

### The Other Citizens

But to describe only the humans is to misunderstand the census entirely, and no basin-dweller would make that mistake.

The frogs are residents. Legally, practically, almost civically. Small brown canal frogs packed mortar-joint to mortar-joint; bullfrogs the size of two fists, mottled and unhurried, who sit on the sluice gates like toll-keepers; bright-throated toads that call without pause because the water never cools and there is no breeding season, no silence written into their year at all. Tadpoles of every stage cloud the shallows simultaneously, a living calendar that refuses to turn. Kill frogs for a week — the old warning goes — and the biting midges that hang in columns over every pond would make the town unlivable. So the frogs' continuous, deafening, maddening chorus is protected with the seriousness other towns reserve for a city wall. Children are set as frog-wardens on the settling ponds. Boys with nets at the canal ends shout silently at one another in sign, clearing snails, leaving the tadpoles.

Above them, the swifts. Hundreds nest in holes intentionally built under every eave; they leave and return in dark, silent streams — silent only because their calls are drowned — and eat midges on the wing from dawn-twilight to dusk-twilight. Bats take the night shift that is not really night from the bathhouse rafters. Lizards occupy the ground-warmed walls at every hour, flicking after crickets, and the crickets themselves live *inside* the houses, in the warm cracks, feeding on refuse and fungus, so that a quiet room at close range still hums at the edge of feeling. Herons stalk the aqueducts statue-still, patient beyond human scale, and nest out by the fissure where nothing at all grows and the water pours over glassy rock into heat. Ducks commute through the crowds like porters with somewhere to be.

No one here experiences this as nature surrounding a town. It is one population, layered into a single warm, wet, roaring volume of air a few miles wide: humans in the doubled-walled cores, frogs in the canals, insects in the walls, birds in the eaves, fish under the tadpoles, all of it eating all of it in a closed, balanced, entirely artificial and entirely inevitable arrangement. Separate the layers and every layer dies badly.

### What They Are Like, Finally

They are not a grim people, which surprises visitors most. The inward calm outdoors is not sorrow; it is simply what a face does when it isn't performing for sound. Indoors they are loud, tactile, funny, and constantly touching — a hand on a wrist to get attention, a palm on a back to say *I'm passing behind you*, children climbing over adults like furniture, arguments conducted with foreheads an inch apart and ending in embraces that are partly, literally, a way of feeling the other person's voice settle down. They have a proverb, usually signed, that translates roughly as: *Nobody here says anything important where the frogs can hear it.*

They live on ground that was molten within ancestral memory, in a city that looks finished and embryonic at once, in a climate with one season that has no name, under a sky that never clears. They have adapted not by conquering the deafening, steaming, frog-crowded basin but by moving into it the way the frogs moved into the canals — completely, protectively, and without any expectation that it will ever become quieter, cooler, greener, or easier.

Climb back up the rim road with a basin family seeing off a son to the outside trade and you will see the exact moment the place releases them. Halfway up, the roar thins. Birds become audible, somewhere cold and far above. The boy pulls his earplugs out, winces, laughs in open air at the smallness of his own voice — and talks, and talks, in the sudden space, while his mother, plugs still firmly in, smiles and answers him entirely with her hands.
