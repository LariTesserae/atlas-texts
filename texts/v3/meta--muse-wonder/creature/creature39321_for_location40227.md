At first you think no one does.

That is part of how it hides. There are the crickets woven seam-to-seam, the moths making a second, vibrating ceiling, the thumbnail frogs packed cheek to cheek in the damp corner, the beetles ticking wetly in the floor. There are the white salamanders, folded so translucent among the frogs that you can only find them by the dark thread of their gut. The whole room is so crowded, so equally lit, that a body can be in plain sight and still take minutes to separate from the rest.

It is lying the way you are lying. It has to be.

On its side along the far seam, cheek pressed into the cool floor, knees drawn up not quite to the chest, one arm folded under its head and the other lying loose before it, palm up. At first it registers as another salamander, only too large. Then as a root come through wrong, white and swollen. Then as a child drowned in limestone light, which it is not — or not exactly, not anymore.

It is about the length of a forearm and a hand. No, longer when you look again. As long as a small person curled hard, though which parts are limbs and which are only the soft, dentable shape the pressure of this place has made of it is difficult to say. It has hands. That is definite. The fingers are long, numb-looking, slightly webbed with pale skin, and the antennae of the nearest crickets are woven between them, not trapped, just included. Near the fingertips, by the still pool, are three pea-sized pebbles, one on one on one.

Its skin is the same cream-grey as the wall, and gives the same way when a frog shifts against it. It gives — and then, impossibly, has not given. There is no mark. Its surface carries a thin biofilm, almost a bloom, and springtails move in its creases in a granular, restless way, so that its hip and the cracked floor beside its hip seem to be made of the same stirring matter. Woodlice rest along the line of its spine like a row of grey beads. An earwig has made a home of the shell of its ear and does not leave it, because there is nowhere more private to go.

Its hair is the sparest thing here: a few pallid threads, root-thin, plastered along the scalp and drifting a fraction in the air without ever quite settling, lit through like the moss leaves in the upper wall. Where hair should shadow the neck, there is no shadow. Light comes off its shoulder as evenly as off its throat. You see its eyes because they are open.

They have been open, you understand, for the entire noon of this place.

They are not blind white, or not only that. The irises are so washed that they are a kind of visible seeing — two clear, wet surfaces that reflect nothing because there is nothing here that is not already in front of them. It does not blink toward you. Blinking would be a small night, and night has no room. Its lids, when you arrived, were already raised; the crickets displaced by your shoulder were already back; its gaze did not turn, because turning would imply a before in which it had been looking elsewhere.

And yet it knows you are there in the precise way everything here knows everything else: completely, and without event. A frog is pressed against its cheek. Its cheek is pressed against the floor. Your breath reaches it diluted through packed air and the moths above its mouth shiver once and resume the exact rate of their trembling. It is not startled. Startlement would require a gap in the chord.

Because it is in the chord. That is the next thing you hear, once your own breathing has been lost inside it. The crickets make the bright, tearing upper note. The moths make the powdery hum. The beetles make the wet clockwork underneath. The frogs peep, thin as circuitry. But below all of it, so low it is more pressure in the molars than sound, is a single sustained tone being made through barely parted teeth.

Its teeth are small and limestone-pale, worn to no edge. Its mouth does not shape words. It has been holding one vowel — not a cry, not a song, something nearer the sound a body makes halfway through an inhale when it has forgotten whether it is taking air in or letting it out. That is its breathing, too: a breath arrested at the center, neither full nor empty. The ribs do not rise and fall. Or they rise, and fall, and rise inside the same fraction so densely that motion becomes texture, like the moth wings. Everything about it is a cycle squeezed until the cycle stops being time and becomes a surface.

It does not eat in any way you can witness. The biofilm thins where its lips rest and is already restored. A cricket dropping falls by its wrist, is taken by a springtail, is ground by a beetle, is film again against its skin. It may absorb through its belly, which is pressed to the damp and faintly lit from within, not glowing — there is no source here — only translucent, so that you can see, as in the salamanders, a dark coiled thread, but larger, slower, arranged in loops that suggest both gut and something unborn. Its whole middle has that embryonic candor. It is a creature that may once have had an outside life, an above life, under a sky with evenings in it, but the pocket has simplified it back to an earlier draft: soft, sexless or not yet sexed, warm-blooded cooled to the exact temperature of stone, its heart not beating in beats so much as insisting at a constant pressure, like water behind a wall.

Do not mistake its stillness for sleep, or its quiet face for peace. Up close, equilibrium is violent. It is holding itself together against the impossibility of change by making its entire body into a note that never resolves. The muscles in that loose, upturned hand are not relaxed. Each finger maintains its curl against the air. The jaw maintains the vowel. The eyelids maintain their openness in blinding, sourceless noon. It is effort without progress, the way the droplet on the ceiling maintains its sphere by never falling. If it let go — really let go — something might finally happen here: a shadow, an evening, a death, a pebble toppling. It does not let go.

The other animals do not worship it, or avoid it, or feed it. They use it as they use the seams in the rock. Its warmth, such as it is, is only another cool surface. Its armpit shelters the smallest frog. A salamander lies across its ankle and wears its translucency like camouflage. The moths settle on its brow as densely as on the ceiling, so when you look up too quickly after looking at it, you lose the boundary between its head and the roof of the world. That, perhaps, is the closest thing to a crown it has, and it bears it without meaning to.

It was the one who balanced the cairn. You know this not because the act is imaginable — though it is, terribly: lying just here, in the deafening light, working by touch because sight is useless, tongue between its teeth on that held vowel, stacking pea on pea on pea with fingers already half cricket-woven — but because its loose hand is still arranged around the having-done-it. Not touching the stones. Nearness without contact is the most relation anything can afford here. To touch them again would be a second event. One event has been permitted, though even that is no longer certain; the cairn has the stunned permanence of a thing that may always have been stacked, and the hand may only be echoing a shape older than itself.

If you speak, it will not answer, because it is already answering. If you reach toward it, the crickets will re-weave, and its fingers will somehow be exactly where they were, and your reaching will be less a memory than an ache in your shoulder from the narrow entrance. It has no message and no wound to show. It is not a guardian, not a prisoner, not a god hatched wrong under a pasture. Those are shapes a life takes when time can get at it.

It is the dweller because the hollow needed a center that could want nothing. It is the knot in the fixed chord, the one living thing in which this single, crowded, unweathering moment has learned to keep its eyes open.
