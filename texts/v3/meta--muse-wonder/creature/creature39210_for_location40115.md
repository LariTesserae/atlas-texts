You would not see him the first time.

You would see the evidence of him, which is a different thing, and slower. A place in the middle catwalk where the lagging is not just polished but warm in a body-shaped hollow, still holding heat when the pipes around it have ticked cool. A tin cup, black outside and bright inside, set exactly under the one drip in the condensation rain that does not splash. A neat pile on a junction box: shirt buttons, boot eyelets, a spoon worn thin as paper, all arranged by size. The central valve bright from turning — but not in the past tense. Turned recently. Turned while the room was at full swell, by a hand that knew how much it would resist.

Then you would hear him, which is also not quite hearing. A palm coming down, unhurried, on iron. A pause where breath should be. The faintest displacement of close air against your cheek in a passage you thought was empty, and a smell arriving after he has already passed: hot skin, wet wool long rotted off him and kept anyway in the pores, lime dust, oil worked down to something almost sweet.

He keeps one hand on the warm line, the one with the yellow bands worn almost to the iron. So does he. It is not a rule he follows; it is how his walking is made. His right arm hangs a fraction lower than his left from sixty years of reaching for that pipe, and his right palm is a single callus, smooth as the valve wheel, the lifeline and heartline long since planed away. He moves crabwise even in the wider intervals, shoulder first, chin tucked, one hip leading — the passages got into his skeleton and rearranged it a knuckle's width at a time. In the low places he goes down without thinking onto knuckles and the balls of his feet, and in that posture, in that light, white-bearded and naked to the waist and quick, you understand the rat. Singular. White around the muzzle with age. Never seen twice in the same passage. The old-timers above were describing two residents and filing them under one name.

He is not tall anymore. Heat and stooping have taken him down and out: chest broadened like a barrel stave, spine curved to the vault's own curve, legs bowed from the grating. His feet have gone splayed and prehensile, toes gripping the mesh as he crosses, soles so thickened he can stand a moment — only a moment, he is respectful, not immune — where the paint has worn to a cross. His skin is the room's mahogany patina on a softer material. Rust-colored in the pilot light. Crosshatched with pale scars in the exact spacing of catwalk grating where he has slept against it, and at his wrists and throat, faint permanent bands, yellow-grey, where cuffs and collar and the warm line have marked him over and over until marking became coloring.

His head is shaved by damp and friction to a few white fringes. What hair remains he keeps cropped with a knife ground from boiler plate, not from vanity — loose hair catches, and catches smell of burning. His eyes have learned the new economy so thoroughly they have stopped spending on most of it. The whites are gone tea-brown. The lids sit at half-mast. He does not look at things; he lets differences in blackness accumulate until a shape is worth having, and mostly he navigates the way the room taught him, by warmth on the left cheek, by the nearness of air, by listening with the skin of his forearms. But when a pilot flame gutters he is there before it fails, cupping it, breathing across it in a slow, wet breath of his own, coaxing it back to blue — because if the pilots go, he understands, the dark stops being a place and becomes only an absence. He is not afraid of it. He is its tenant, and tenants maintain.

He has a cycle because the room has one.

Cool hours: the low routes, the sump end, the wet corner. He squats by the black mirror and feeds the fish. It comes to his finger's shadow — not to his finger, he has learned not to touch it — and turns its silver over once, eyeless, patient as everything down here, and takes what he gives it: a cockroach drowned in the drip tray, silverfish shaken from the insulation, crumbs of the pale leathery mat that grows there. He does not eat the fish. He tends it the way he tends the ferns, trimming back the black fronds where they creep toward a flange and would cook, tonguing condensation off a leaf to taste whether the inlet has changed. He eats that mat himself, sometimes, boiled against a hot pipe in the tin cup. He eats the enormous lacquered cockroaches when they are slow in a cool hour. Mostly he lives, as the ferns do, on a leak that has lasted longer than the building above: mineral water, dripping city grit filtered through a hundred joints, whatever washes down the hatch stair in a storm and arrives days later through a seam, tasting of pavement and distance. His teeth are worn to the angle of that tin cup's rim. His gums have lime in them. He is becoming, slowly, an inch a decade, the deposits he scrapes from the flanges and stacks in a corner in white and orange cairns, because throwing anything away down here just means meeting it again in the sump.

Swelling hours: he goes high. Up the rail-less ladders backwards, without looking, to the third catwalk where the air under the vault is hottest and thinnest and no one who came down for an afternoon could breathe. He can. His breathing has slowed to match the distributed activity of the place — long sips, longer pauses, a ribcage that rises the way the sump rises, almost never. Up there he waits out the narrowing, mending nothing, because nothing here is new and nothing is quite failing and his whole work is the keeping of that balance: a quarter-turn against the swelling, a rag of sodden insulation tucked into a seam that is not quite a leak, a palm held flat to a sweating standpipe to feel, through the green enamel, whether the pressure inside is speaking at its normal pitch. Sometimes, in the high heat, he sleeps, wedged between two tanks shouldered together, in the hollow that is exactly him. The beltless flywheel above him completes a fraction of a turn. It is not purposeless. He reaches up in his sleep, sometimes, and gives it the weight of one finger.

He does not talk. Talking is a misuse of the air and his voice, on the rare occasions the room draws it out of him, comes as something the pipes might have said — a click against the teeth, a word shaped mostly by lips, labels read aloud to gauges whose faces are cracked: numbers that no longer correspond to anything above, kept current by being spoken. He has a name cut into the boiler plate by the wired-shut firedoor, but the rust has bloomed and flaked and bloomed in it so many times that only he could read it, by touch, and he has not bothered in years. Names belong to the hatch side of the world. Down here he is a function that acquired a body: the tender, the turner, the warm hollow in the lagging.

And when the pump finally commits — once or twice a day it clears its throat and thinks better of it, but sometimes it commits — he is already where he needs to be, high, clear of the low catwalk, fingers laced through grating, face turned into the standpipe's sweat. The room exhales through its passages in sequence, white hotter than the black was, erasing the phosphorescence, flattening the ferns, and he breathes it in the way you would breathe weather. Not enduring it. Using it. Letting it pass through the slowed bellows of him and out again. For nine minutes the whole flexing, ticking system and the man inside it are one pressure, one temperature, one held note — and then it settles, gauges crept, passages rearranged, and he comes down hand over polished hand in the renewed dark to set his cup back under its drip, give the fish its silver moment, lay his palm once more on the warm line, and begin the slow route through the intervals, turning what needs turning, listening with his skin, keeping the room in the long, patient middle of its cycle, and kept by it.
