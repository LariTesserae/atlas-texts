You would not know her from the doorway she is holding shut.

In the lower city, where the sponge towers give out and the houses are low rings of stone and shell, there is a round entrance no wider than a spread hand. Across it lies an arm. It is the same tea-brown as the silted limestone, pocked and frilled along the edge, each sucker a pale, perfect cup pressed flat to the stone. If the current shifts, the arm shifts with it, tasting. If a crab ticks too close over the shells, the suckers nearest it lift, one by one, and taste that too.

The rest of her is inside, folded into a room she did not make and has made entirely hers.

She is an octopus, though not the bright, theatrical kind from the stories of the upper water. She is perhaps the weight of two fists, mantle soft and high and scarred, skin gone loose with age in places and gathered into horns and flaps that mimic, without anyone left to see it, the exact roughness of oyster shell and dead sponge. Her color still changes — it must, it is how her skin thinks — but here it is not display. It is adjustment by touch. Russet over warm stone. Grey-mottled where the silt lies thick. A sudden, bloodless pallor when a barracuda's pressure-wave rolls down the avenue and is gone.

Her eyes are still there, in their dark, horny sockets on either side of her head, and they are almost useless. She can tell, if she turns toward the rooftops at just the right hour, whether the thinning above her is a little less thin. That is all. A rumor. She does not hunt by it. She hunts the way the whole city lives: by pressure along her skin, by the faint electrical ache of living things in water, by smell dissolved into taste, by vibration carried up through the floor of her house into the webbing between her arms.

Everything she knows arrives as contact.

She knows the tide before it turns because the water on her tongue changes. Inflow: a cool-sweet greenness from the eelgrass flats, plankton gritting faintly against her mantle, the shrimp-crowds loosening in the alleys above. Outflow: her own city coming back to her, warmer, familiar, carrying sponge-spit and crab-waste and the chemical signatures of a thousand neighbors she will never meet. She knows her street by its particular sequence of tastes — clam beds under the foundation to the left, a white mat of bacteria blooming somewhere in a side crevice, the iron tang of an old, rusting piece of rubble the crabs could not shift. She knows the hour by the direction her own doorway leans her.

Her house is a single chamber with a gathered floor, and she keeps it the way water keeps the avenues: by constant, unthinking correction. Silt comes in on every tide. She blows it out with a slow pulse of her siphons while she sleeps, one arm always loosely curled around the door-stones. Fallen shell is tested, turned, placed. A good stone is not the prettiest but the one that fits the thumb of the entrance and can be drawn close from within, fast, without sound. Inside, the floor has been arranged by preference she could not explain. Crab carapaces to the eating side, their legs pre-broken. Clean pebbles along the back wall, sorted by a smoothness only her suckers can appreciate. And in the farthest niche, where the current almost fails, a single perfect spiraled shell, too small to live in, too fragile to eat, kept for years because its ridges please her arms to trace.

She does not leave often, and never far. A hunting trip is an unspooling, one arm and then another feeling ahead into the dark, the body following like a thought reconsidered. She can pour herself through a gap between two stones that would not admit your wrist, bone being a rumor in her as light is. She moves mostly at the turning of the tide, when the streets are confused and the small crabs are busy clearing their own doorways and not looking up — though up means nothing, here; they are not looking *out*. An arm extends from her doorway while the rest of her stays home. It travels, apparently idle, over the shells. It finds. It closes, with terrible gentleness, and draws the kicking thing back.

She eats slowly. Nothing is wasted because waste is a kind of theft from the house itself. Meat first, then the soft joints, then the shell set aside for the floor, to be ground down in time by her own weight and the urchins who come rasping in the long non-night. Even her own shed beak-tips and the membranes she cleans from her suckers are pushed out on the exhale for the cucumbers working the avenue.

There are neighbors, in the way there can be in a place built of living walls.

Above her, in the sponge tower at the corner, a hundred tenants shift and filter and occasionally expel a tenant who has died, letting it drift down past her door. To the left lives another of her kind, larger, younger, whose exploratory arm she has bitten cleanly twice at the second joint; it has grown back shorter, and now stops, respectful, at the invisible border where her smell laid down in the silt begins. Below, under her foundation stones, a family of gobies darts in and out of a shell-curved hollow, and she tolerates the ticking of them against her ceiling because they eat the parasites that gather in her doorway. She is not kind. Tolerance, here, is just a use that hasn't ended yet.

She avoids the plaza at the center of the bowl. Not from knowledge. Her mother died, as all her kind do, gaunt and guarding eggs in a house that became a tomb, long before she was more than a speck in the plankton cloud. She never learned the detour by being taught. She learned it as a juvenile small enough to live in a tube-worm pipe, following the fish-streams that bent, always, around that open grooved limestone, feeling in her own young body the faint tone the current makes there, below hearing, in the hollow of her mantle. It troubled the water. That was enough. She still goes around. On spawning tides, when the bowl goes milky and every mouth opens at once, she feels the vibration through the stone of her house and pulls her door-stone a fraction closer.

She is old now in the only way the city measures age: the stones of her ring have sunk half their depth into silt and been re-laid twice; the groove her arms have worn across the threshold is smooth as the main avenue. Her skin has taken on some of the patterning of her own floor and will not entirely let it go. Her left rear arm is shorter than the others, from a fight with an eel in the drains that she won by losing it and won again, months later, when it had grown back blunt and extra-sensitive at the tip.

Soon — not this tide, but not many — she will do the last thing her kind does here. If she mates, she will stop hunting, stop rearranging, stop tasting the street, and turn all that attention inward on a curtain of eggs strung in the darkest part of her chamber, blowing clean water over them while her own body thins to membrane around the work. Or age will simply slow the pulses in her arms until one inflow she does not bother to exclude the silt. Either way, the house will know first. The floor will go ungathered. A sucker will leave a stone untested.

The next tide will bring the challengers, feeling their way down the avenue, arms first, reading the vacancy by its changed smell. The fight in the doorway will be silent and thorough. The loser will retreat over the roofs into the shrimp-curtains to look for a collapsed wall to die or recover in. The winner will spend a full cycle turning everything in the room — throwing out the crab shells she saved, keeping, for no reason it can account for, the small spiraled shell in the back niche, because its ridges please an arm to trace.

And the body she leaves will not leave. It will be taken apart by crabs and cucumbers and the blind silver fish worrying in from above, soft parts first, then the tough beak ground down, then the last stain of it loosened and carried out on the exhale to feed the towers. Generations cemented to generations.

That is what lives here: not a visitor in the city, not even, finally, a resident of it. A soft, tasting, eight-armed habit of a place, keeping a door, keeping a floor, keeping around an emptiness it never understood — until the water moves through where she was, and the doorway, briefly, is just a ring of stones again, already being learned by someone else.
