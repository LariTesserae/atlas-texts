At first you do not see them as separate from the town.

A shape on a street-rail, below you in the green dimness, hauling itself along hand over hand, is only another variation in encrustation. Algae has taken it. Something pale trails from it like vine. It moves no faster than the crane crossing the square, no faster than a shadow when a cloud crosses far overhead, and your eye, still obedient to the upper world, keeps losing it among lampposts and kelp.

Then it turns its face up toward your descent, and holds still to let you pass, because down here stillness is courtesy. Passing displaces. Everything has to make room without making a current.

They are people. That is the second, slower thought.

People in the way the town is a town: eroded into equilibrium, nothing about them new, nothing ruinous. They were not built for the water in one generation. They were accreted into it.

Their ancestors came down the stair to tend the presses and stayed for a shift that lengthened. Their children learned the rails before they learned any dry floor. Their grandchildren never learned a dry floor at all. The air bells are not for them, except as workshops. They do not go up into those inverted domes to breathe. They go up to keep paper from dissolving, to cure leather, to mend clocks, to light the small blue fires for soldering and for the chapel feast days, and they do it holding their breaths absently, like a man in the upper world might hold his breath passing through smoke.

They breathe the town.

You can see it if you are close and patient. Behind the jaw, where the skin is thinnest and almost translucent, there are frilled slits that open and close in a rhythm unrelated to speech, unrelated even, mostly, to exertion. A slow fanning. When they work the forge hatches or haul on a cable, it quickens; when they sleep, tethered by a wrist-loop in their boiler houses so the six-hour current does not take them into another district, it slows until minutes seem to pass between motions. Their chests hardly rise. Their blood has learned a different economy. Warm water goes in full of the exhalations of plants and factories and comes out a fraction poorer. Children are born with the slits sealed, and for their first weeks they are kept in the root-mats at rooftop level where the lily cords bring down water so rich it is almost milky, and the older women worry the membranes open with fingertips, petal by petal. Some still keep a vestige of lungs. In an air bell they can make a true voice, hoarse and surprised at itself, and they use it mostly for singing and for naming the dead. Underwater they speak otherwise.

Speech down here is displacement made intentional. A hand opened toward you, closed very slowly, fingers rippling in sequence: that can be a greeting, a question, an agreement to a price in the market. A tap on a rail travels farther than a shout and means attend. Two taps, a pause, one: the press will close. They press palm to sternum to mean I understood in my body, I felt it. They touch each other's forearms, shoulders, the backs of hands, constantly and without erotic urgency, to locate one another in the murk and to finish sentences. A conversation at market may take the better part of an afternoon drift, hands exchanging crates, eyes half closed, a thought arriving by degrees like a freight sled. Up above it would look ceremonial. Down here it is just not wasting water.

Their eyes have gone large and dark and a little remote, adapted to a perpetual evening. The whites are nearly gone. In lamplight there is a brief green reflection, then nothing. They do not blink the way you do; a clear lid slides, slow as a shutter on a deep-sea camera, and clears silt. Many of the oldest are functionally blind and entirely unconcerned by it. They navigate by rail, by current-temperature on their skin, by the hum of cables through their teeth, by the known sequence of rough and smooth encrustations on a street they have pulled themselves along for seventy years. A blind root-tender can find one particular white cord among ten thousand, by the way the water cools around a spring seep three streets away.

Their skin is their most townlike feature.

It is not scaled, exactly. It is lived on. From the shoulders down the back, along the outer arms and thighs where the current passes, a fine pelt establishes itself in childhood — green algae first, soft as velvet, then in adolescence a feathering of moss, then in the warm districts near the works a tracery of almost transparent vine that takes root in the shallowest layer of them and gives back something, sugar or salts or a steadiness of temperature, in return. They cultivate it the way you would a good coat. They prune it. They transplant prized cuttings from a grandparent to a grandchild. They scrape each other clean — face, palms, the breathing slits, the soles of the feet — with flat shells in a nightly ritual that is hygiene and gossip and medicine all at once, while small translucent shrimp come out of the root-mats to work the edges for what has been loosened. Where the skin stays bare it is pale to the point of luminosity, not white but cream, like the blind flowers at rooftop level, and it bruises in slow blooms that take days to arrive and weeks to leave.

Hair, where they keep it, is never cut short: it would float into machinery. They braid it with copper wire, with weighted beads of slag, with living ribbons of eelgrass that must be renewed. Some of the factory tenders shave their heads entirely and let mineral scale form a cap, thicker every decade, so the hourly concussion of the presses meets something already stone. Their teeth, from the cables, from grit, from a diet that must be chewed against drag, wear down to broad, even surfaces. Their nails grow thick and translucent and are allowed to lengthen, useful as scrapers, as picks, as styluses on wax tablets. Their hands are enormous in effect if not in size, fingers elongated by a lifetime of hauling on rails, the webbing between them not a membrane so much as a thickening, a reluctance of flesh to separate. Their feet find purchase and remember. An old salvager can hang upside down from a bridge by one flexed foot, raking the silt below with a pole twice his height, entirely at rest.

They dress for drag and for weight. Weighted cloth, woven with lead thread and vine fiber, that sways around them without billowing. Oiled canvas kilts and vests, brass toggles that can be worked by numb fingers. Nothing loose near the turbines. Nothing bright. The colors up above would be called drab; down here amber, verdigris, rust, bottle-green are signals, district colors, family encrustations rendered portable. A child of the southern lock wears a collar of pale shells that click, very faintly, when the outward current takes her, so her mother can hear her turning for home. A forge worker goes almost naked under a breastplate of riveted iron he has worn so long the straps have been absorbed into his pelt, and the plate itself has grown a reef. People acquire silhouettes by trade. You can tell a cable-minder by the limestone built up in white casings along her forearms, left there deliberately so her arms will rest naturally on the humming lines without burning. You can tell a historian — one of the dry workers — because his hands are unwebbed and uncallused and papery, and he has a smell, even underwater, of the air bells: hot dust, blue fire, ink.

They eat what persists. Preserved fish from above, traded down through the freight lock in sealed tins that are treasured afterward as roofing. Preserved everything, and then the town's own leavings: the inflated pale leaves, boiled in forge-heated seawater until they go tender; shrimp by the netful from the root-mats, eaten whole and crunching faintly; a paste made from kelp and mineral meal; and on the first Sunday of the slow month, catfish — not the great one in the settling tanks, whom no one would eat any more than they would eat the scarred eel, but its smaller, dumber descendants, farmed in a flooded chapel crypt and tasting, by all accounts, of warm mud and iron. Meals are taken communally where possible, tethered around a table in a vat-house, food held down with little cages lest it drift and be incorporated. To share food is to share the labor of keeping it in place. It is intimate. They feed each other pieces directly, watching the current between mouths.

Their work is the town describing itself again: salvagers raking what has sunk ten feet and entered the silt; root-tenders moving through rooftop groves on their knees, clearing copper runoff from the mangrove filters with their long nails; press-priests — for the forge crews have become something like that, in their plate and scale and hourly liturgy — who listen with their whole spines for a wrongness in the closing of the great hatches, and who enter the sealed hearts in suits fed, briefly, from the air bells, emerging with their pelts singed back to bare cream skin that must be re-grown in the mats for a season. Children run messages because children alone still think an hour is a long time. They pull themselves, shining and unencrusted, from house to house in bursts that stir the eelgrass and make their elders turn in mild reproof — not because speed is dangerous, exactly, but because speed is wasteful, and because a wake is a kind of rudeness that goes on traveling after you have stopped.

They are tender people in the literal sense: they feel more through surfaces than through distance. Courtship is a long co-working. Two people take adjacent rails on the same long haul, match pace until the matching is no longer effort. They exchange cuttings. One will scrape the other's back in a place the other cannot reach, which is trust. If it deepens they braid their hair together with a single strand of copper and go to be recorded by a historian in an air bell, where they must, for the certificate's sake, lift their heads into dryness and say their names aloud with their vestigial voices, laughing at the sound while their breathing slits flutter in panic and pleasure at the thinness up there. Babies are born in the warm outflow in a crowd of held hands keeping mother and child from drifting while the basin's current takes the birth-water away around the town, once, completely, in six hours, which is considered the correct time for anything to be distributed.

They do not bury their dead in any ground there is. When an elder's slits at last cease their fanning — and they live long, these people, or briefly; drowning diseases adapted to them are patient too, and a burst cable can unmake a street — the body is kept. Not mourned over quickly. Weighted, dressed in its best encrustation, it is carried by the whole slow neighborhood on the current to the settling tanks, and given to the silt, and to the great catfish, and to the roots that will find it in the dark and translate it, over a season, into leaf and shrimp and a slight thickening of the water that the grandchildren will then breathe. The historians write the name on paper and also scratch it, in a code of depth and length, onto the inside of an air bell, where the dry air will preserve the scratches long after the paper goes.

They have almost no animals because, in a sense that is not metaphorical if you watch long enough, they have taken the animals' place. They are the large warm moving things in this ecosystem. They are what the eel tolerates, what the shrimp clean, what the plants require to prune and carry and replant them. The town was kept drowned to make certain things under pressure that can only be made so. It is not recorded anymore — the paper in the bells only goes back so far before it too is pulp — whether one of the things the pressure was meant to make was them.

They do not seem to mind the possibility. To stay is to accept that nothing will conclude today, and they have stayed past the point where staying is a decision. At nightfall, when the green column thins to brown and the chemical lanterns in the kelp take up their slow pulse in relay, lamp by lamp, district by district, the people tether and go still in clusters, pelts drifting around them, slits fanning, hair rigged out among the lily cords, feeling through the paving the presses close and close again. A child half asleep will reach, without waking, and close her hand on the nearest rail, because that is what hands are for.

And if you drift with them long enough, your own gestures slow into ceremony, your own skin begins its first green velvet at the shoulder where the current passes, and you understand that they are not waiting for the water to be drained someday, nor for rescue, nor for the work above to end. They are in the middle — the permanent, inhabited middle — of a stroke that began before they were born and will not finish in any one lifetime. They pull. They tend. They hold still to let each other pass.

The water takes the rest.
