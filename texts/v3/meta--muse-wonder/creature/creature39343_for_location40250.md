At first you will mistake them for stones.

That is not camouflage, exactly. Nothing in the bowl is trying very hard to hide. The ibis are white against black peat. The frogs are emerald and rust and candied yellow, pulsing on the reed stems in plain sight. Even the snakes leave their bright bellies showing when they turn.

But the stones on the central disc — the low, grey, oval stones, thirty or forty of them scattered in the clearest water — have a way of not quite being where you left them.

You come back after the twenty-minute rain and the largest one has moved a hand's breadth toward the sun. Another has turned. A third, which you would have sworn was flush with the disc, has lifted itself just enough for water to run bright underneath it, and a school of finger-long fish is sheltering in the green shadow of its belly, flashing silver, copper, silver, all in unison.

The keeper calls them the Finished Ones.

She does not mean dead. She means finished the way the bowl is finished: come to their final shape so long ago that all the time since has been refinement, not change.

They are turtles, if a turtle is still the word after ten thousand years in ankle-deep water under a doubled sun. Or softshells, once. Something riverine that walked in when the depression still drained somewhere, and then stayed while the lattice closed the exits, held the water at marsh-height, and gently, politely, unmade every urgency in the place.

What is left is low and patient and nearly flat.

A grown one is the width of a man's outstretched arms, though you would not guess it until it rises. Most of its bulk is kept pressed into the mud, spread, the shell no more than a smooth grey dome an inch or two above the surface film. The shell is not patterned the way other turtles' shells are patterned. It is veined. Fine ceramic-grey lines run up through the keratin in branching maps, following the scutes and then ignoring them, and under the water-skin they catch the light a fraction of a second late, so the animal seems to be outlined twice: once where it is, and once where it was just about to be. Stand over one at noon and its displaced shadow pools *inside* its real shadow, darker at the center, like an eye.

Their skin, where it shows at the neck and the great splayed legs, is the color of wet bone darkening to peat at the joints. It is never dry. A film of water holds to them even when they haul up onto the marl at evening, traveling with them, beading and rejoining across the shell in a living sheet. Condensation loves them. By mid-morning each one wears a second, brighter outline of dew, and the dragonflies land on them preferentially, dozens at a time, turning their abdomens to the sun on that warm grey back as if it were a raft built for the purpose.

The head is broad and low and blunt, made for pushing through mud that resists anything hurried. No visible ears. No reason for them. The eyes are set high and far apart, gold-irised, with a double lid — one clear, one smoked, like a piece of blown glass — that lets them look straight up through water, glare, and reflected glare without narrowing. They are very good at watching two things at once: the bird, and the bird's shadow arriving a breath out of place below it. When a swallow stoops through the dragonfly layer, every Finished One in the pool tracks it calmly with one eye each, unstartled, the way old people follow weather.

Under the chin hang two pale barbels, soft as frog skin. In the depths those barbels are always moving, tasting the mud ahead a fraction before the foot comes down. Which matters, because they do not really walk so much as *negotiate*. Their feet are enormous for the body, webbed to the last joint, toes tipped not with claws but with smooth ceramic nubs, unbreakable, faintly opalescent — the same stuff the snails build into their shells and the plinths are made of, taken up grain by grain out of the mud over a lifetime. A dropped knife comes up in a week as bright dust. Over a century, some of that dust comes up as a turtle.

Each footfall is placed, spread, and held a moment before the weight follows. Through those wide soles they read the bowl. The buried lattice is loud down there, a vast faint ticking and stirring that travels through bone and shell and never quite reaches air, and the Finished Ones are built as receiving instruments for it. Put your own bare foot beside one in the shallows and you will feel it change as they do: a slow adjustment, a settling, almost a sigh through the mud, when the partitions are about to lower. They feel herons land. They feel the rain-curtain touch the west rim twenty minutes before it arrives. They feel you, specifically you, by the pattern of your weight and the small clumsiness of your hurry, and they will know you again tomorrow.

They cannot hurry. The bowl would not permit it, but they would not anyway. A lunging foot thickens the water; a patient one is let through. The Finished Ones have made a whole locomotion out of being let through. They move at the speed of mud accepting weight. It looks like stillness until you measure it against a reed stem. Then it is a tide.

And they can pass the partitions.

Dragonflies turn at the invisible walls, always, precisely. Fish go through with a shiver and a change of color. The Finished Ones go through the way a thought goes through a mood. They press the broad front of the shell against that clear vertical sheet, an inch, two inches — and the color comes up in them as it does in the fish, not on the skin but under it, a slow bloom of copper along the throat, blue at the edges of the shell, sudden coin-silver down the veining, held — and then they are on the other side, in water a finger higher or lower, and the bloom drains away downstream through the pattern of the shell until only the grey map is left. The keeper says the walls know them. More likely they have simply learned, over longer than anyone can count, the exact slowness the walls require.

They are almost entirely silent, even by the standards of the bowl. No call. No hiss worth the name when a heron lands too close; just the head drawn in a precise half-inch, and a widening ripple, and every frog in the pool flashing once, in a ring, outward — a sentence passed stem to stem. Whether the turtle sent it or merely started it is a distinction the bowl does not seem to bother with.

Their language, insofar as it is theirs, is worn on the underside.

Turn one — you will not; they weigh more than a grown man when the mud has them, and they dislike it with a slow, irresistible leaning that ends any experiment — but wade beside one when it lifts itself to cross a sand patch and you will see the belly in the clear water: not shell there, but soft, translucent plate, and beneath it color moving in bands. Rose. Amber. A green so pale it is almost light. The colors do not flash the way the frogs' throat-sacs flash. They *travel*. A wave of rose will start at the throat of the largest female in the south pool at dawn and arrive, turtle to turtle, pool to pool, at the north rim an hour later, losing nothing, answering nothing you can name — unless it is an answer to the mist lifting. At noon they color all together, undersides lit from below by the doubled light coming up off the disc, and for a few minutes the shallows are paved with slow lanterns.

The fish attend to it. The crabs raise their white claws toward it. Whether it is courtship, prayer, instruction to the lattice, or simply what their blood does at that hour, the effect is the same: the whole bowl organizes itself around the signal without a sound.

They eat what the stillness provides. Algae cropped off the invisible walls with a horny, toothless beak, in long unhurried sweeps. Fallen lotus. Dead fish, found by the barbels before the otters find them. Snails, ceramic shell and all, crushed with a sound so thoroughly eaten by the air that you perceive it only as a tiny jolt through the water against your ankles. In the late afternoon, when the partitions lower and the pools equalize, they take their stations in the drift-lines with the herons and kingfishers, heads raised, and take the occasional passing fish with a strike so economical it seems less like predation than like being permitted.

Nothing hunts them. Partly the field, which dulls teeth and tension and charge. Partly size, and the unbreakable shell. Partly, the keeper insists, custom — ten thousand years of custom, which in the bowl may be a stronger force than hunger. The herons walk across their backs. Cleaner birds pick at the veining. Otter cubs, rolling in the deepest channel, will roll straight over a submerged Finished One and use it, briefly, as geography.

Once in a wet season — not every season, and never in a hurry — the oldest females climb at evening onto the white marl of the rim, water still sheeting off them, and dig with those ceramic-tipped feet a single shallow nest apiece in the polished stone-soft ground. One egg. Sometimes none. The egg is the size of a goose's, dull grey, warm for far longer than an egg should stay warm, because the lattice has grown a thread of itself up into the shell before it is even laid. The keeper marks the nests with reed hoops and otherwise leaves them alone.

What hatches, months later, is a coin of bright color with legs.

Hatchling Finished Ones are all signal and no shell: translucent amber, veined with light, throat and belly flashing in fast, uncoordinated pulses as they make their way down the marl to the water, visible for a hundred paces in the luminous shade. Most things in the bowl that would eat them are watching, because everything watches everything; it is a place run on attention. Some are eaten. That, too, is custom. The ones that reach the shallows go suddenly, mercifully grey over their first year, take up their first bright grains out of the mud, and begin the long process of being finished.

It takes a century to be unremarkable. Two, perhaps, to be large. The keeper's grandmother knew individuals she could name by the map of their veining, and her grandmother knew the same ones, already old, already in no danger of becoming anything else. A few carry maps so dense the grey has nearly closed over the shell altogether; those are the ones that gather at the center.

Every day at noon they are there, or arriving, unhurried as weather. Thirty paces of seamless disc under an inch of green water. The frogs ring the edge in their hundreds, pulsing. The dragonflies hold their stratum at knee height. And the oldest Finished Ones settle onto the disc in a loose, exact arrangement that is never quite the same and never quite different — a constellation revising itself — press their wide feet flat, and go still.

Then, through your soles, the lattice answers something in the sky.

One adjustment. Patient, enormous, felt in the teeth. Every partition in the bowl ripples once, in sequence, pool to pool, the vertical sheets bowing and recovering like things exhaling. The displaced shadows of birds cross above and below at once. The color goes through the assembled bellies in a single traveling wave, throat to tail, copper to blue to a silver so sudden it whites out their veining for a heartbeat —

— and it is done, and the afternoon pools begin, fractionally, to lower, and the fish begin their drift, and every heron takes its customary station without negotiation.

Whether the turtles are commanding that, or translating it, or simply standing in the place where the bowl and the sky conduct their business and lending it their bodies as the needle lends itself to the compass, the keeper will not say. She brings them lotus stems in the dry weeks. She steps around them in the dark by memory of their maps. She is, she will tell you, not the keeper of the bowl. She keeps the hut. They keep the rest.

They live, endlessly, at the exact moment before stillness — too slow to be called behavior, too responsive to be called stone. A place that finished becoming itself could only be inhabited by something that had finished too, and then, inside that final shape, found room to go on living forever: eating light off the walls, wearing lost knives as veins, speaking in color through the water, keeping time with their feet.
