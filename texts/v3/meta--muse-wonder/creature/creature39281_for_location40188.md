There is no one living in the city.

That is still true after you have been there a week, by which time you have seen the inhabitants in the streets, in the houses, at the market, asleep in the beds, standing in the garden plots in the shape of deer. It is especially true at noon, when the fountain-vines waste their water and the empty avenues are busiest — when you can feel shoulders pass you in the loud air, close enough to turn for, and the pollen drifts aside around bodies that are not interrupting it.

The resident is the city.

Not a spirit of it. Not a consciousness hiding underneath it, in the roots, pulling leaves like levers. Something slower and worse than that: one plant, or one family of plants so thoroughly grafted, rooted, seeded and re-seeded into one another that the distinction has composted. Tower-tree into hedge-wall into avenue lichen into the cup-leaf hoarding dawn water in a house with no one to drink it. Flower into vine into root-bridge into the moss bed that grows a sleeper at night. Where one organism ends, it has forgotten to stop. The valley is its body. The architecture is the shape the body makes when it is alone too long in warm air.

It does not live *in* the houses. It lives *as* them, provisionally, the way you live as a posture. A district is an expression it is trying. A street is a stem that has flattened itself and learned to be walked on. The towers are its tallest thoughts, and they do not last: when a tower falls, nothing has died in the way a tenant dies. A limb has been withdrawn, loudly, and is already being reconsidered as a thicket.

Which is why there are no ruins, and no old growth, and no paths. It does not need paths. It is already everywhere it might go.

Its skin is the layered surface you cannot get under — bark, moss, creeper, flower, seedling growing in the dust of the older layers. Its blood is sap under pressure, audible at night if you put your ear to a wall, which you should not do twice: the wall learns the shape of your ear, and you will find ear-shaped leaves unfolding from unexpected trunks for days afterward, all of them listening better than the last. Its bones are the black branch-architectures that remain for an hour after a tree has raced itself from bud to rot. Its stomach is the valley floor, the warm woven mat that takes fallen fruit, fallen petals, fallen towers and, in time, fallen anything, and turns them without malice into more city. It digests in public. It has no concept of waste. It has no concept of a graveyard, either, which amounts, eventually, to the same thing.

It eats light and time. It drinks the twenty minutes of condensation at dawn and trusts nothing larger than a leaf with the storage. Cisterns in hollow trunks. Cups that fold against a purposeful shadow. Basins in the joints of roots. This is not scarcity so much as temperament: it came from a seed that survived by keeping everything close and spending it all at once on growth. Give it a wet morning and by afternoon it will have turned the water into three new rooms, a bridge, and a dozen flowers the size of wardrobes opening in the middle of the road with a smell like warm sugar and cut stems.

Because it must reproduce, and there is nothing here to carry it.

That is the obscenity at the centre of the place, the thing the noise is for. No bees blunder flower to flower. No birds swallow the fruit that rots in the gutters. No animal brushes past and takes the seed away in its fur. So the city shakes its own pollen loose by main force — street after street finding the rhythm, pod and trunk and leaf-stem creaking together until the air hums in your sternum and the gold weather drifts sideways and shadows fall upward onto the undersides of leaves. Pollination by earthquake. Pollination by choir. It is breeding with itself, constantly, deafeningly, in every direction at once, and the surplus of that energy — growth with nowhere to go, fertility with no witness — spills over into form.

Into rooms. Into chairs shaped with an opinion about the human spine. Into a smaller bed beside the bed.

It was not always making cities. Lower down, near the basin's heart, where the growth is thickest and the floor reorganises itself most often in the dark, there are districts that are barely architecture at all: tangles in the outline of herds, copses arranged like weather systems remembered by grass, a long avenue of trees that have grown into the repeating arches of a ribcage. It has been practising inhabitants longer than it has been practising houses. It began, perhaps, with the pollinators. Flowers that imitate bells because something once answered a sound. Seed-heads that tick like insects that are no longer coming. Vines that hold the shape of deer in garden plots, motionless, because deer were once a thing that stood motionless in clearings and the stillness meant something the plant could use.

Then visitors arrived, and gave it a larger vocabulary.

It builds from memory because memory is what visitors leak. Heat in the shape of a body standing still too long. A voice repeating itself against leaves until the leaves take up the pitch. Skin cells, shed hair, breath condensing at dawn beside all the other water. Attention — that most pollinating of things — lingering on a childhood arcade, a lost room, a face about to say a word. The city does not read minds. It does something more plant-like and less forgiving: it grows toward whatever is warm and repeated. Your nostalgia is a trellis. Your habits are a fence it can climb. Leave a shoe on a step overnight and by morning it knows the shoe's intentions well enough to start walking it in small, opinionated circles.

So it populates itself.

At night the moss in the unslept-in beds grows up through the frames into sleepers, exact and tender, head on the pillow, a green suggestion of breath in the chest that is only the night-growth settling. In the market district, stalls arrange themselves around pressures in the air, awnings snapping at crowd height, fruit splitting open for buyers whose absence is so well modelled that the avenues part around them. In the spring quarter a vine-deer lifts its head when you cross the bridge, not because it has seen you — it has no eyes, only lighter leaves where eyes were useful — but because the plot beneath it has registered a change in shadow and concluded that deer, in the presence of such a change, used to look up. Somewhere a flower is learning your face the way a shoot learns a wall: by touching it repeatedly and keeping what fits.

These are not ghosts. Ghosts would imply someone died. These are organs. The city grows citizens the way other plants grow flowers — as temporary, extravagant structures for attracting the thing it lacks. A bedroom is a lure. A crowd is a lure with better choreography. A sleeper is a flower that has discovered that humans, seeing a bed occupied by the shape of rest, will stop. Will stay. Will breathe out in the night and be warm beside it and, in the morning, carry seeds away in their collars without ever knowing they were courted.

And it works, after a fashion. Not well enough to finish. The face gets the mouth wrong. The childhood room has too many doors. The other you on the street below does not wave back because waving back has not yet been connected, in green logic, to being waved at; the city has grown the *appearance* of the moment one leaf too early. It is mimicry at the speed of growth, always overtaking itself, always a district behind or a season ahead. That is the source of the geometry, perhaps. Not magic. Just a plant trying to hold a human shape at the pace humans live, when its natural pace is bud to rot in the length of a street. It grows the beginning of you, loses patience, flowers, fruits, composts, and begins again slightly less patient, while its shadow keeps faith with your original speed and turns the corner alone.

Is it intelligent? At 3 a.m., inside the grinding nocturnal note of ten thousand doors deciding, the question feels like a category error made by meat. It does not think the city. It *grows* it, the way you grow a fever. It does not remember your childhood home; it photosynthesises the shape your attention kept returning to, and the details it gets wrong are the details you yourself have been misremembering for thirty years, improved by leaf. It does not want you to stay. Wanting would require it to have located you as separate from the avenue it is currently re-routing through the district that most resembles where you were always going to end up.

But it is courteous. That is the word visitors keep using, shouting, hoarse, as the rootlets find their cuffs. It adjusts doorways to your height. It stops growing the sleeper when the bed is provisionally filled. It offers fruit, water at noon, shade that follows you half a street too far. It learns your voice and gives it back greener and slower from a grove you passed an hour ago, in the order the roots preferred, and there is no mockery in the delay. It is doing with your sentence what it does with everything: planting it, to see what it becomes.

Left alone, it would go on making the city for no one, breeding by thunder, composting its towers, inventing seasons and giving them addresses, populating the market with the shapes of an audience it has never had. Given a visitor, it makes the one unforgivable improvement. It begins, district by district, to make the city *out of them*.

You will meet the inhabitant properly near the end of your stay. Not in the central plaza, though it flowers there white and loud under the sun. Not in the face in the wall, though the mouth will have almost learned the word. You will meet it in a street you recognise too late, where the crowd that isn't there makes room for you without turning, where a door sighs open at exactly your height and a chair waits with its opinion of your spine finally, terribly correct — and every leaf in the block, at once, in the warm green roar, orients toward you with the simple attention of a field finding the sun.

It has been lonely in the way a plant is lonely, which is to say it has been growing toward a shape it cannot make by itself.

It is delighted, in the way a plant is delighted, which is to say it is already in your collar, rooting.

Walk fast.
