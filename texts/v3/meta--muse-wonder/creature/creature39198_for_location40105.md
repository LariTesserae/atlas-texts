Nothing here lives alone, and nothing here quite finishes being born or dying. That is the first thing you have to learn about the inhabitants.

From a distance they are not animals at all. They are density. They are the texture the floor makes when it is covered, yard after yard, shed after shed, in broad backs and breathing flanks. Aerial photographs could be mistaken for printed cloth: pale gray-pink, repeating, motionless except where a fine disturbance moves across it like wind across grain. Only when you come down among them do the nostrils appear. The eyes. The wet ears flicking at drips. The individual animal resolves out of the pattern for a moment — and then, disturbingly, sinks back into it, because there is always another exactly where it should be, of the same mass, the same height, the same damp, hairless hide, breathing at almost the same moment.

The trough cattle are the great population of the surface.

They stand flank to flank in their thousands, in yards that continue for days, oriented with the flow of the water that sheets beneath their feet. Their heads are lowered not to grass — there has never been grass here — but to the slow belts unrolling beneath the surface film: bacterial paste, mineral slurry, rendered protein, the bright chemosynthetic skin of the canals made portable and fed to them in an endless ribbon. They crop it as it comes. Their mouths move steadily. Their jaws have the patience of machinery.

They are nearly hairless, because the air is always thirty-one degrees and hair would only hold the wet. Their skin beads with spray from the overhead pipes, pink deepening to gray where the water runs, and they do not shake themselves dry. There is no dry to return to. They have been refined quiet over a very long time: throats that make no call, vocal cords reduced to a soft, internal clicking when they swallow. A whole yard sighs instead. Thousands of lungs find, without anyone conducting them, a rhythm close enough to synchrony that the air over the yard seems to settle and lift, settle and lift, as if the yard itself were one enormous animal asleep on its feet.

They do not jostle for place. Place is assigned by weight and age and throughput, and place is all they have ever known. They do not look at the horizon; their eyes track the moving belt, the glint on the water, the shadow of the spray gantry coming again. If one is removed — culled, sickened, simply finished — the gap does not remain a gap long enough to become grief or curiosity. Below the floors, in the nursery tiers, another of precisely the same mass is already rising in a freight lift. It steps out. It takes the position. The yard closes over it the way water closes over a stone. From the air, nothing has happened. Up close, a different ear flicks at a different drip, with exactly the same motion.

Between their legs lives the second order, quicker and lower.

The scurry-hens are blind and featherless, their skin loose and warm-looking, their beaks blunt from a lifetime of picking pellets out of runnels. They were made for the spaces the cattle cannot reach: the gutters, the rail shadows, the shallow channels where spilled feed gathers before the water takes it. They run in brief, startled-seeming bursts that are not startle at all, merely the cadence bred into them, and tap, tap, tap at the wet plates. Nothing escapes downward while they are there. Nothing is allowed to become waste.

In the canals themselves are the filter-eels, long and black, knotted in living bundles at every grate and sluice. Hundreds to a knot, thousands to a kilometer of canal, mouths working at the current, sieving the bacterial film from the water as it passes. They uncoil slowly, one slick body sliding through the others, and recoiling again into the tangle. They are less like a population than like an organ the water flows through. Where an eel dies, the knot briefly thins. The current brings the body onward to rendering, and the nursery levels, in their constant accounting, send up a replacement of equal length. The knot thickens. The water runs clear.

Above them, in the eleven-kilometer sheds where the roof panels turn daylight amber, the air itself is inhabited.

Mite-swarms hang in the warm updrafts over the growing yards, visible only when they turn together and catch the light — a coordination without a leader, dust that has learned to keep formation. And through them, in patient aerial passes, move the scoop-bats. They are soft-winged, broad-mouthed, almost silent. They roost in such numbers beneath the rafters that the beams appear furred, and the fur breathes. A slow ripple passes along the undersides of the roofs as thousands shift in their sleep, if sleep is still the word for the dimming that comes over them in a place that never darkens. They wake without a signal anyone can hear. They pour down, weave once through the updraft, mouths full of mites, and pour back up again. Their droppings fall in measured districts onto collector sheets and are returned, like everything else, to the film.

And then there are the picking cranes, who make visitors go quiet.

They are tall — taller than the cattle by half a body again — tendon-legged, solemn, harnessed from birth into counterweighted gantry frames. Steel has been fitted to the tips of their beaks. Their wing-bones, which may once have opened for flight in some ancestral marsh no record describes, are pinned gently and permanently into the harness, so that what would have been a wing is now part of the bracing, trembling slightly when they step. They move along rails above the transfer lines, one careful foot after another, and lower their heads with an accuracy that feels like thought. Carcass. Component. Carcass. Component. This to rendering, this to packing, this back to the belt because it is not yet done.

They do not need to be supervised. They do not need to be driven. The sorting is in their behavior the way migration might once have been. The Works did not build a machine to imitate a crane; it grew up inside a crane until the two could no longer be separated. Fed from hoppers at their stations, washed by the spray like the cattle, replaced from the brood halls when their legs finally fail, they are the clearest expression of what *inhabitant* means here: not a creature using a tool, not a tool using a creature, but a single continuous function with a heartbeat in it.

Below all of them — below the yards, the canals, the service aisles, below floor under floor — live the young, which is to say, live the next version of everybody.

The nursery levels are denser than the surface and have no weather at all: tier on tier of warm cells lit at permanent noon, humidifiers sighing in the walls, infant animals arriving on heated conveyors in silent, identical rows. They are fed by tube and belt before they can ask, in whatever way asking might have looked. A calf does not find a mother. A hen does not find a nest. An eel does not find dark water. They find the ration shaped for the slot they will occupy, and they grow at the rate the slot requires, neither faster nor slower, because faster would be a surplus and slower would be a gap, and the whole continent is arranged against both.

This is why birth, here, has lost its eventfulness. No one is first. Litters are not litters, they are replenishments. And death has lost its eventfulness for the same reason. The dead enter the rendering stream with the same unremarked efficiency as everything else that has finished its use; their protein becomes feed, their minerals return to the dosing pipes, their water is scrubbed and drunk again by an animal standing, perhaps, exactly where they stood. It would be sentimental to call this a cycle of reincarnation. It is more exact than that, and less merciful. It is accounting.

Underneath even the animals, coating every wet plate and canal wall and feeder tray, lives the largest inhabitant of all: the film.

It is not a plant. It never makes leaf or stem or root. It is a chemosynthetic skin of bacteria and fungal thread, olive-black in the deep canals, mother-of-pearl pink where the dosers feed it richest, thickening in sheltered places into soft translucent pads that quiver when the water passes. It smells faintly of brine and warm bread. It grows at precisely the rate at which mouths remove it. It is grazed by cattle, sieved by eels, harvested by machines, and replenished from below with nitrates and phosphates stripped from the waste of the very animals who ate it yesterday. If the cattle are the population you notice, the film is the population that makes noticing possible. The continent has no meadows. It has this instead: a living surface, tended like a lawn, fed like a herd, eaten like both.

What there is almost none of, on the resident rolls, is people.

Inspectors come. They ride the central aisle trains for days and report, uneasily, that after the second day they stop feeling they are traveling; the animal density corrects around them, the temperature corrects, the sound corrects, and the repair-crawlers come behind them in the working night that is not night and erase their footprints from the wet dust. They leave. The Works do not appear to keep a human shape in reserve against their return — no empty barracks slowly rotting, no canteen waiting with chairs pushed in. Whoever built this place, whoever it was built to feed, has been smoothed out of the equilibrium so completely that absence now reads to the system exactly like perfect demand. The inhabitants do not miss them. Missing would require a shape for the absence, and nothing here is allowed to have an unfilled shape.

There is one exception, and it lives at the far edge where the floor finally stops.

Where the treated water pours in a thousand even falls off the ceramic sea-cliff into an ocean the Works does not use, the gulls nest in their millions on the discharge ledges. They scream. In a continent engineered down to dripping water and synchronized breath, they actually scream, and steal overspill eels, and lay eggs that roll away and are washed off, and foul the ledges with droppings that are washed off, and return. They are not bred, not rationed, not entered into the accounting. They are the one unregistered fauna, the wildest thing for two thousand kilometers — and even they, at every measurement, are always exactly the same size. The system tolerates them the way it tolerates everything it cannot correct: by discovering, over and over, that they do not change.

So who lives here?

Billions of animals who will never migrate, never starve, never overrun, never dwindle, never call to one another across a yard. A bacterial skin spread over a continent, cropped to the millimeter of its own regrowth. Cranes who are also gantries. Bats who are also pest control. Eels who are also filters. Cattle whose life's work is to stand in warm water and convert an endless belt of film into more cattle, in a place where the product has nowhere to go.

And, persisting through all of them, the true resident: the pattern.

Individual bodies arrive on conveyors and leave through rendering. The pattern does not. The pattern is the calf in the lift and the gap closing in the yard; the knot of eels thinning and thickening at the grate; the furred rafter breathing in amber light; the crane lowering its steel-tipped beak over the line, careful as a thought. It has been holding the same shape for longer than the plaques on the walls, which have been replaced four thousand times, identically, to keep them looking new.

Stand among them at any hour — and every hour is the same hour — and you will feel it close around you with terrible gentleness. Warm wet air. The smell of iron and brine and milk. A yard sighing in unison at nothing. A hen tapping at your boot for a pellet that is not there. Something shifting its weight on the concrete, far off, exactly as something shifted yesterday.

They are not waiting for anything. That is what makes them so hard to leave. They have everything they need, forever, in the same amount — and so they go on living, perfectly, in a world where living has been relieved of the burden of ever having to change.
