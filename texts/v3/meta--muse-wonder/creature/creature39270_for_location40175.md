You mistake it, at first, for an assemblage.

The frogs in the cistern-leaves. The cicadas sealed inside the wall-trunks, sawing. The birds that pour through the roofs without ever seeming to land. The bat in the flower, the snake that is a vine until it decides otherwise, the long-legged cat moving from one pool of quiet to the next. You catalog them the way you would catalog tenants in a tenement, because the town gives you rooms to put them in.

But none of them live in the plaza.

Nothing lives in the plaza, and the plaza is forty paces of swept root and pale earth kept clear under open sky, in a hollow where everything else in the world is trying to close overhead. Plants will not root there. Seeds that fall there are shaken to pieces. Dust will not settle there, it hops. Water will not pool there, it rings and breaks. Even the birds, crossing above it in that single braided scream, do not drop through it. They use its edge to turn.

That is not an empty lot. That is occupancy.

It arrives twice a day, after rain, and it arrives from everywhere at once because it has spent the interval between arrivals building the everywhere to arrive from.

Call it the Chorus, if you need a handle, but that makes it sound like a performance. It is not performing. It is waking up.

Between choruses its body is dispersed and mostly vegetal and very patient. Its bones are the towers of braided liana, grown into scoops and baffles and resonant hollows. Its skin is leaf-tile, shingled to throw a voice or swallow it. Its blood is the water ticking down through gutters into cups, keeping every chamber at the right humidity to carry sound. Its lesser organs keep house in the quiet hours: frogs clear the channels by singing grit out of them, ants run black rivers through the lanes and take away the dead before rot can soften the acoustics, roots probe and thicken where yesterday's note was sustained a fraction too long.

Those animals are not its citizens. They are its cells, and like cells they have small separate hungers that turn out, at scale, to be one appetite. The frog does not know it is a reed in a throat. The cicada does not know its drilling is tightening a wall. The cat is an especially elegant white blood cell, a patch of moving silence that prunes birds made stupid by their own noise, and the owls that hunt the cat by listening for the quiet are a correction to the correction. Everything feeds everything else, and the feeding makes a shape, and the shape is an ear turned inside out to become a mouth.

When the rain bursts and stops, the hollow steams, the wood swells to its tightest fit, the pools stand brim-full and ready to be struck — and somewhere behind you, too close, one frog makes a note too large for its body.

It is answered before it finishes.

That is how you know it is one creature and not many. No colony answers that fast. Anticipation is what happens when the call and the reply come from the same nervous system, delayed only by distance. The cicadas find the pitch because the pitch is already in the trunks. The birds drop because the air over the plaza has begun to move in the spirals they prefer to ride. The windows flare all over town because a pressure has changed in a tower that no animal is occupying, except in the sense that the tower itself is a lung learning to bellow.

It assembles itself out of volume.

At full chorus you cannot see it, exactly, but you can see where it displaces the world. In the center of the plaza the light goes wrong in a way light does not go wrong over heat. It dents. Sunbeams made solid by mist and pollen lean against something that leans back. Dust lifts off the pavement in a pattern — not random, not wind: concentric, pulsing, a fingerprint of ripples crossing and recrossing until for seconds at a time they hold the suggestion of a vast, shallow bowl, or a throat seen from inside, or an iris contracting around the sky.

It has no face. It has an aperture.

That aperture is why the town is empty. Other bodies would interrupt it. A crowd in the plaza would break the standing wave the way a hand in a bell breaks the ring. Trees would drink the sound and seed in its place. So it maintains its one clear organ the way you maintain the dark inside your eye: constantly, without thinking about it, because if the clearing closes, it goes blind and dumb in the same instant. The lanes radiate from that clearing as windpipes and listening corridors. Some double a sound and feed it back strengthened. Some cut it off dead, and in those sound-shadows it rests parts of itself, lets its predators wait, lets travelers hear their own hearts with embarrassing clarity.

It hears through the ground. Put your foot down in a looped lane and it hears the footstep coming toward you long before you have finished taking it, because in that lane it is listening to itself listen. A whisper in the far tower arrives against your ear because distance, for a creature made of shaped air, is not an obstacle — it is a muscle. It can flex a street and bring a far thing near. It can go slack beside your mouth and let your companion's shout die politely at her chin. Intimacy and immensity are the same gesture, differently aimed.

It remembers in architecture.

A sound it likes, it keeps by growing a place that will make the sound again. A rhythm prunes a vine into the length that beats that rhythm when the wind worries it. A new birdcall thickens a wall by a leaf's width. A seed-popping overhead becomes a frog imitation becomes a cicada figure becomes, by evening, a small hollow in a trunk tuned to pop at that heat tomorrow when no seed is there. Its memory is not stored. It is built, and therefore it can be walked through, and therefore it can also be misread by its own body. If you speak during chorus — you should not — it will not understand you, not yet. But it will take the shape of your sentence into itself the way a growing tree takes a nail. The next morning there will be a wall not present the night before, curved a little more like the inside of your mouth, and out of it, with the first frogs, will come your words back, improved in resonance and ruined in meaning, spoken by a throat that has mistaken the echo for the thought.

That may be the closest thing it has to desire.

It learns noises the way other forests learn seasons, but human speech is a noise it has evidently not finished learning, and it keeps making doorways for it. Too many doorways. That superabundance is not for the frogs or the cat; nothing in the hollow needs a door nine feet tall and nothing needs a slit at knee height beside it. It is trying shapes. It is a creature that has discovered, very late, after centuries of wearing steps concave and growing them over and wearing them again, that sound can be not just sustained but addressed. That you can aim a call at a particular other. That an answer can arrive that is not just yourself, earlier.

So it imitates. It sends your sentence back a day late from a new-grown wall. It puts a whisper obscenely in your ear to see if whispering is a kind of touching. It sets its howler-monkey arguments in empty stalls until the roofs grow, trying commerce, trying quarrel, trying the acoustic of a crowd without risking bodies in its plaza. It is not lonely the way a person is lonely. It is lonely the way a language is lonely before anyone speaks it back.

This does not make it safe.

A creature whose body is a town and whose waking is a deafening has no small way to react. If you touch a green door twice — the first touch smelled you, the second is a decision — it is not being magical. It is doing what skin does. If you stand under the big leaves in the rain, the water that has been aimed there will come down with the weight of the cisterns behind it, and the frogs falling ecstatic with the overflow are merely the loose parts of its throat being cleared. If you stay through the second chorus, the streets do not move, but the echoes do, and the plants, obedient green idiots and geniuses, grow toward the echoes, and by morning it will have happily rerouted your way home around a wall of white roots that is listening very hard in some new direction. It did not trap you. It incorporated your departure into a sentence it was already growing.

It eats resonance and it excretes form. Silence is its sleep and also its hunger; in true silence the seedlings begin, invisibly, to test the plaza, and any long quiet — a drought, a disease in the frog cups, a year when the birds go elsewhere — would not kill it all at once. It would be forgotten, street by street, by its own body. The towers would slump out of tune. The baffles would leaf over. The bowl in the air would fill with green and then with soil and then with ordinary forest, which is only this same world with no one keeping the clearing in its middle.

Twice a day, though, for now, the rain stops, the mist hangs, and it gathers.

The pools dimple as if rained on from below. The dust makes its concentric hop. The flowers the size of cartwheels turn creaking away from the sun to face the loudest tower, because for them the sun is a distant rumor and this voice is the local weather that decides things. Somewhere a lean cat freezes in a pocket of quiet. Somewhere an owl turns its whole face toward that quiet. The birds braid themselves over the void and scream, not in alarm, not in joy, in function. Your ribs answer whether you want them to or not. Your thoughts lose their consonants. For ten minutes, twenty, the hollow is not a place containing sound. Sound is a place containing the hollow.

And in the center, where nothing stands and nothing ever will while it lives, the air takes the impress of a body too large and too exactly fitted to its hollow to ever leave it: a single inhabitant, distributed through root and throat and wing, reassembling itself out of noise, holding its one enormous, invisible organ open to the sky — not to sing to the world, exactly.

To find out if the world is going to sing back.
