At first you will think nothing lives there.

You can walk a whole dim day between one living thing and the next and still believe it. A smear of lichen no thicker than paint. A hand-sized cushion of moss, black-green, frozen hard in the middle of summer, growing perhaps a millimetre a year around the edge of an old footprint. Something glassy that moves once in a melt-pan and does not move again.

Then the scale corrects itself.

They live so far apart, and move so slowly, and keep themselves so quiet, that your senses keep classifying them as geography. A boulder that was not there in the morning. A low ridge that has shifted a hundred metres by dusk. A whole basin rim that, over three days, resolves into backs.

The largest of them are called, by the few who have seen them from the ground and lived to misdescribe them, Furnace Beasts.

Nothing that large should be warm-blooded in that cold. Nothing that large should be able to feed there at all. They are up to thirty metres long, six-legged, low and broad, with heads kept almost permanently down. Their backs are not backs so much as overlapping fields of plate — bone grown outward through hide, dark red-black with iron, scoured bright at the edges where plate grinds against plate as they walk. Underneath, the legs end not in hooves but in vast felted pads, spreading their weight over frost and gravel so they leave only shallow, steaming ovals. They make almost no sound arriving. You feel them first in the soles of your feet. Then in your teeth. Then your hair begins to lift.

They eat the plateau itself.

The front of a moving herd is made of Breakers, the smallest adults — only ten, fifteen metres — heavier in the forelegs, with tusk-like wedges of grown metal at the jaw. They loosen what the others will take: prying at frost polygons, splitting the mineral crust around melt-pans, turning over stone that has not been turned in a thousand years so the lichen on its underside sees light. Behind them come the Furnaces proper, heads swinging in a slow lateral scrape, taking in lichen, rock flour, frozen soil, salt, black mud, with mouths whose teeth are not enamel but dense, alloyed ridges that grow continuously and wear into shining facets. A Furnace can spend a week on a single whaleback of granite and leave it polished and striped clean.

What happens inside them is the industry of the place, and it is entirely inherited.

Chamber after chamber: first grinding, with swallowed grit; then a long, dark fermentation where bacterial cultures passed from mother to calf at birth break lichen acids and free what little protein the plateau offers; then deeper chambers that run hot — genuinely, industrially hot — where microbes, electrical discharge from specialised organs along the gut wall, and catalysing metals strip ore down to its elements. Usable iron, copper, manganese go into blood and bone and plate and those impossible teeth. The rest passes out behind them in slow falls of steaming, sterile slag, so rich after a decade of frost and recolonisation that moss takes there before it takes anywhere else. A herd's wake is visible for sixty years: a meandering, greener darkness across pale stone, following footprints no single animal remembers making.

They do not waste heat calling. A mouth opened is a chimney opened. They speak through pressure, stamping patterns into the ground that travel kilometres through permafrost and are read by the pads of their feet, and through the whiskered fans along their jaws, which exchange bursts of static in the dry air — brief crackles, blue-white dots brightening and dimming in rows along their throats at dusk. Near a herd, compasses abandon north and point, courteously, at the nearest animal. Salt water under ice lights from below, induced currents swirling as they pass. Their sleep is a loose circle on open ground, heads inward, plates frosting over until the herd becomes, again, terrain. Calves are born standing in the wind-shadow of their mothers, already furred, already seeded with their mother's gut-fire, walking within the hour. There is nowhere to hide them. There is almost nothing fast enough to need hiding from.

At the rear come the Seeders, leaner, longer-necked, almost delicate beside the Furnaces. They work the warm waste with sieved mouths, sorting, spreading, ingesting and re-excreting it finer, while glands along their flanks dust it with lichen spores and the eggs of insects carried dormant through their bodies. They are gardeners who have never seen a garden finished. The circuit of one herd — basin to basin in a slow spiral across the interior — takes longer than a human life. The Seeders plant for Furnaces not yet born.

And the Furnaces are only the weather system the rest of life orbits.

Small herd animals travel permanently in their lee, dense-furred and low, their faces hidden under manes of sensory bristle that read heat and static and air pressure. They live in the few degrees of warmth leaking from the great plates, and pay for it by climbing — astonishingly, patiently — into the folds between those plates, cleaning away parasites and dead skin with tongues rasped like files. The Furnaces stand for it for hours, one eye, enormous and black and lidless, turned toward the horizon. Beetles with copper shells harvest a carcass for a century. When a Furnace dies, which may be after two or three hundred years of walking, it simply stops and then subsides, and for decades it looks like a wrecked installation: ribs curving like hangar frames, collapsed lungs pleated like turbines, iron bone oxidising out into the gravel until burrowers and lichen make the skeleton indistinguishable from a natural ore deposit. Nothing here buries anything. Time does the covering.

Under your feet, inside the polygons, blind white burrowers follow the faint electrical roots of lichen colonies through the top layer of soil. They have no eyes to speak of, only heat pits and pressure organs, and they move in winter when the surface is locked, eating, excreting, aerating a world no thicker than a hand. Above you, rarely, gliders cross — wings like taut grey sails, bodies no bigger than your torso, riding the almost imperceptible rise of air over sun-warmed stone. They can travel for an hour without a wingbeat. Their shadows take minutes to cross a melt-pan. They do not cry out. Nothing here wastes breath on advertisement.

The pans themselves hold a compressed summer. For a few weeks the rust-red algae blooms, a glassy hatch rises to feed on it, and copper-shelled segmented swimmers filter the iron water in slow spirals. At the edges stand the fishers: knee-high stalkers, long-legged, stone-coloured, who freeze one foot deliberately into the surface ice and wait, metabolism guttering, until a vibration tells them to strike without a splash. They can wait half a day between movements. So can their predators, and the predators of the Furnaces: great flat ambushers the colour and texture of basin gravel, who lie for days with their nervous systems threaded into the frost, listening with their whole bodies for foot-pressure, rising only if a calf strays beyond the thinning edge of the herd's electrical field. A kill there is conducted mostly by weight and suffocation. There is no roar to warn the others. The herd registers a change in the ground-pattern, closes slightly, and moves on.

There are no nests, no burrow mouths you could recognise, no dams or tools or made things. Young are not sheltered, songs are not sung, territory is not marked except by the slow chemical transformation of stone into soil in a herd's wake. Their technology is their anatomy: smelter guts, grown circuitry, bacteria carried generation to generation like a craft no individual understands. From orbit they read as sprawl, as factories with chimneys. At ground level, the chimneys resolve into baffled nostrils that exhale once, slowly, and condense their own breath into frost that falls straight down and is eaten by the next animal behind.

If you stand among them long enough — they will investigate the pressure of your steps with whiskers the width of doors, but they will not flee from anything as small and brief as you — you understand that emptiness was only a failure of tempo. The plateau is not sparsely inhabited. It is inhabited at centimetres per hour, in pressure and static and conducted heat, in near-silence and near-darkness, by millions of tonnes of life that have learned to make weather, soil, and metal out of lichen and stone. The herd takes days to pass. The last Seeder goes over the rim. The induced lights fade under the ice.

In the footprints they leave behind, condensation is already beading. Something dark is already beginning, very slowly, to grow.
