From the plateau they are almost impossible to see.

White ground, white houses, and people the color the basin has made them: not pale the way an un-sunned person is pale, but dusted, silted, salt-bloomed. Their clothes are undyed at a distance — ochre clay cloth, gypsum-rubbed leather, fungus-fat worked until it shines grey — and their hems and sleeves and veil-edges are weighted with small smooth stones sewn into the seams. Nothing about them flaps if it can help it. A dust devil passes through a market crowd and a hundred garments tug, once, heavily, and settle.

You only pick them out by movement, and then all at once the archipelago is populated. Someone crossing a flat with a roof-stone held on each shoulder. Someone else kneeling too long by what looks like empty ground. A line of children in the bright, empty space between districts, each carrying one stone, setting it down, going back for another, in no hurry at all.

Close up, the place is written on their faces first.

They squint both ways. Years of light coming up off the floor as hard as light coming down have given even the very young a permanent, double narrowing, deep lines fanning not only from the outer eye but underneath it. Many wear a thin bone brow-shade tied across the forehead, and a lower veil of loosely woven cloth, not against the sun above but against its reflection. Their eyelashes are stiff and pale with salt. They blink slowly and often, and tear constantly in a thin, useful way — the basin gets into every eye and has to be washed out again. Old people are known by a milky filming that no amount of tearing clears. They do not go blind all at once. They go *white* by degrees, and long before sight goes they have shifted to judging the world by foot and hand.

Because sight, here, is unreliable anyway at noon. What is trustworthy is the ankle, the sole, the palm flat against a wall. Floors tilt a degree toward their cistern. Streets have intentions you feel in your calves. A bulge is coming days before it can be seen, a faint springiness under a threshold, a door arch that takes the shoulder differently. The basin-people walk as if listening through their feet: unshod indoors, in thick, soft, silt-muffled sandals out, placing each step whole-footed and quiet. They pause, unconsciously, in doorways. They rest a hand on a house as they pass it, the way you might steady a living animal. It is not affection, exactly. It is checking.

Their hands are their other eyes. Broad-palmed from plastering, fingertips perpetually abraded and healed and abraded again from salt brick and gypsum block, nails kept filed almost to the quick — a long nail is a sharp edge, and sharp edges are forbidden, impractical, unlucky; crystals seed in the crack and wind worries at them. They round things by reflex. Give a child here a lump of clay and she will not pinch it into points. She will roll it between her palms into a loaf. Give a man a tool to set down and he will set it with the edge turned to a wall. Their bone knives, their scrapers, their salvaged bronze gone green in a season: all blunted, all hafted smooth, all made to be dropped into silt without a clatter and found again by sweeping a palm.

They speak the way the town forces everything to speak: close, or not with the voice at all.

Raised voices die in the white bowl. Silt eats sound, bright curved air scatters it. So spoken language is an intimate distance thing, low in the throat, meant for someone within arm’s reach — indoors, at a cistern lip at dawn, in bed. It is a language full of small wet clicks and breath sounds that would be lost across a street, well-suited to naming fifty states of damp, a dozen kinds of salt growth, the particular sighing give of a floor that will rise tonight. For anything farther, they sign. Public talk is hand-talk, fluent, fast, performed high against the chest so it reads against a pale shirt and not the pale ground. Strangers who learn only the spoken tongue still think these are quiet people. They are not. At mid-morning a flat between districts can be roaring with argument — two water-wardens disputing a newly blackened pocket, elbows driving, fingers stabbing, children translating obscenities for each other behind their hands and collapsing in silent laughter. For district to district, truth travels by mirror: polished obsidian flashes from the towers, official, terse, impossible to lie in elegantly. Market layouts, devil warnings, cistern registrations, deaths. Everyone reads the towers the way you glance at weather. Private gossip prefers hands. Love prefers the voice, close enough to feel breath in the cold lower air.

The cold marks them too. This is a bright place that never quite warms the body’s core. Breath shows at street level at ten in the morning. House floors sweat under bare feet. They dress in layers even in glare, bellies and kidneys wrapped thick, heads shaded, and they carry their heat jealously. Fungus-fat is rubbed into skin at throat and wrist and the backs of knees, against cracking; it smells faintly of iron and cellars, and so do they, always, under the cleaner smell of salt and cold stone. Skin splits at knuckle and heel and lip and heals salt-white. Hair, where it is allowed to grow, goes brittle and greys early from the ends inward. Most adults keep it cropped and bound under cloth. The basin’s rodents are hairless for a reason, the grandmothers say, and imitate them sensibly.

There are no farmers here. There are scrapers, carriers, levelers, channel-keepers.

A person’s work is decided less by trade than by dawn. Everyone who can walk belongs, in those first cold hours, to a maintenance crew: their house’s, their cluster’s, their cistern-kin’s. Walls un-bulged, plaster re-rounded by palmfuls of wet clay, channels picked clean of the night’s crystal teeth before the thread of condensation can be choked off. Silt that has drifted knee-deep against the leeward side of a house in a single still night is carried in baskets, uncomplainingly, back out to the flats and dumped where the devils will take it up again. This is not Sisyphus to them. It is housekeeping at the scale of geology. No one expects a floor to stay level. Expecting it would be a kind of insanity, like expecting a goat to stay sweet-tempered or a pool to last until autumn.

Inheritance runs accordingly. You do not leave your children a house. A house is forty stones, some salt brick, roof weights marked with your kin-sign, a curve of wall that will be taken apart and reset twice in a childhood. You leave them a *place by damp*: a share in a cistern that beads reliably nine months of the year, a right to scrape the orange fungus from its mouth every third morning, knowledge of which of the black patches under the gypsum overhangs will darken in May and which is a liar that forms once in five years after rain. You leave them a marked set of roof-stones and a gecko-wall full of thriving tenants. Cistern-kin can be forty people who share no blood at all and will feud murderously in sign-language over a new seep; they can also be a grandmother, two sisters, and an adopted plateau boy, rich because their groove runs long.

Children are put to stone-carrying as soon as they can be trusted not to drop one on a foot, which is to say very early, and they learn the town in their shoulders. A reset is a festival of a sort: roof uncovered stone by stone, stored in the yard in family piles, walls coaxed down, floor broken and re-laid over fresh silt while the geckos retreat, complaining in soft chirps, to the neighbor’s wall and wait to be invited back. Children sleep through mild heaves without waking. They play devil-chase in the flats, reading bird-scatter and glitter the way plateau children read clouds, running just ahead of the pale columns to feel the cold tug and the smell of salt rush over them, scattering at the last moment with the exact, unimpressed timing of gulls. Their games have no balls that can roll away and nothing with corners. Their toys are weights, ropes, little mirrors.

Food is low, grey, and sufficient. Fungus-cake, dense as damp bread, scraped mats dried and pounded and re-wetted and baked on hot stones. Larvae from the morning pools, sieved in cloth before the flies rise, tasting of minerals and faintly of nuts. Beetles in season, crunched whole. Goat — milk, cheese, occasionally meat so precious the bones are boiled three times — is plateau wealth, brought down by traders who stay two nights and complain the whole time about the light, the silence, the floors moving under their bedrolls, and leave with headaches. The true feasts are water events: the first morning after a cistern has been closed all winter and is opened, everyone in the kin drinking in turn from a rounded cup, not because they are dying of thirst — dawn condensation is in the jars — but because stored water, water that *stayed*, is miraculous here.

Age changes a person’s district without their moving. The young are carriers and runners of messages through the flats at devil hours. The middle-aged are wall-hands and water-wardens, thick through forearm and calf, able to judge a bulge by laying a cheek to plaster. The old, filmed-eyed, coldest of all in their thick wraps, are kept — *kept* is the word, tender and practical — near the gecko-walls and the stores, where their hands remember channel shapes that silted up before their grandchildren were born. They navigate houses they can no longer see by the holes in the unplastered wall, counting gecko chirps at night for flies and fever-sign. When they die, their kin carry them up the long ramps at evening, out of the cup of cold air, and lay them in shallow stone cairns on the plateau where the wind finally has a voice. Nothing sharp goes up with them. Their marked roof-stones pass down. Their share of damp is registered by mirror-flash, and argued over by the next dawn, in hand-talk, vigorously, which is a form of mourning here.

They are not a solemn people. Solemnity would require expecting stillness. They joke in the close voice and sign the punchline bigger for the street; they name dust devils after in-laws and cisterns after liars and geckos — whose wall, whose chair-back, whose sleeping shelf — after ancestors whose habits they uncannily repeat. They keep a gecko in every sleeping room and would no more plaster over its holes than they would lid an uncovered pool at noon and call it private. A house without geckos gets flies; a house that gets flies gets fever; a house that gets fever gets emptied, stone by stone, and reset elsewhere, and somehow this chain is both sanitation and theology and neither word quite fits.

What they are, finally, is *daily*. Plateau visitors come down expecting the people of an ancient place to think in centuries. They think in last night and tomorrow morning. Did the channel close? Will the floor rise? Where did the devil put the market ropes? Is that patch darker than it was? Their permanence is not in anything they have built. It is in the unending, near-silent competence with which they take the built world apart and put it back, rounded, weighted, level for now, while swifts stitch the bright air overhead and something small and gypsum-colored watches, throat pulsing, from a hole in the wall they left for it on purpose.
