From above, during the day, you might not see them at all.

A figure crosses the plain between pits, bent under a basket, the colour of the loess itself — dust in the hair, dust worked into the wool of a coat, dust deep in the lines of the hands and the folds at the neck. Donkeys appear first, ears moving over a rim. Then children, running up the ramp too fast, shouting back down into the pit long before their heads are level with the ground. Then, for a while, nothing: fifty or a hundred metres of empty roof-earth, a threshing floor beaten pale, a plough turning thin soil, and underneath it all a town roaring softly in its holes.

Come down the ramp and they resolve.

They are not underground people in any burrowing, diminished sense. Their stoop, when they have one, is learned late — from tunnel ceilings, from lintels, from cave doors cut by shorter ancestors — and they straighten it out without thinking when they reach their courtyard, the way you straighten after passing under a branch. What is adapted is more specific, and slower. Eyes, first. Above ground, at noon, they squint to slits against the glare off pale clay and keep their gaze down or far. Below, their faces open. They read a room at a glance in light that would make a visitor pause at the threshold: who is sleeping in the back niche, which jar is sealed, whether that shadow in the stable cave is the goat about to kid or only the mule shifting in litter. Children learn the pit wall the way other children learn a street — this handhold dark with palms, this step dished deeper on the left, this cave mouth where the air smells of onions, this one where it smells of dung and warm animal.

Their skin carries the same double life. Hands and forearms and faces are sun-hardened, loess-coloured, scored with fine white lines where the dry earth cracks and heals, cracks and heals. Under the sleeves, where the pit shade has kept them, arms can be startlingly pale. Old people have the geography of a working life written across them in tones: dark fingers, pale shoulders, a permanent light mask around the eyes where dust and sweat settle thickest. Nobody is clean, exactly, because there is no water to waste on being clean, but nobody is unwashed either. They rub down with fine dry loess and ash. They work oil into skin before the cold months. Clothes are beaten against the pit wall until the dust leaves them in a brief yellow cloud, and for a few minutes a woven pattern shows — undyed wool mostly, brown and grey and off-white, a strip of madder red at a cuff, indigo gone nearly black — before the plain takes the colour back.

And they are loud the way a bell is loud, out of necessity and then out of habit.

Everyone shouts in the pit. Not in anger: in address. A question about beans thrown from a cave door arrives at the stable amplified off three walls. A goat answers. Six pigeons leave their holes at once and clap the air full of dust. A baby, startled, announces itself to the whole courtyard, and four people answer it before its mother does, because in these shafts nobody has to be told whose child is crying. By middle age most of them are a little deaf in the upper registers — sparrows go first, then the thin voices of small children at a distance, then whistling. What remains is the body of a voice, low and carrying. Old people speak like drums being struck through wool.

So the real language of the place happens closer in.

They talk with their whole faces, with shoulders, with hands laid flat on forearms, with a thumb pressed once or twice against a wrist, with fingers tapped against teeth, against a jar, against the wall itself to send a knock travelling through a room. A conversation at the market can be mostly touch conducted in a roar: price, doubt, agreement, insult and apology all passing hand to hand over the back of a melon while both parties shout pleasantries for the benefit of the walls. Because nothing said in a pit is private. The walls give it to everyone. They have grown up inside that fact the way fish grow up in current, and it shapes them more than the dryness does. In public — which is to say, below — they are indirect, ceremonious, almost elaborately imprecise about anything that matters. Feelings get stated as weather. Refusals arrive as stories about somebody else's grandfather. A person who wants to say a true thing plainly goes up.

Up on the plain is where they keep their silence, and their secrets, and some of their grief.

You will see two figures standing a long way from any rim at dusk, perfectly still, facing each other in the wind, too far off to be heard even if they were speaking at full voice, and that distance is the point. Lovers meet up there. So do people settling a debt they do not want the courtyard to carry. So does somebody who has just been told a death and wants the first minute of it unwitnessed. The wind takes the edges off words. The flat land takes the rest. Children are strictly forbidden this use of the surface until they are old enough to be trusted with it, and the forbidding is one of the few things ever fully enforced, because a child who learns to keep secrets below ground, in whispers, will grow up either lonely or dangerous. Better to learn the other discipline here: live your life where it echoes, and keep your face kind while it does.

Their days are not measured by a clock but by a patch of light.

It comes over the rim in the morning, lays itself down one wall, crosses the courtyard floor in its few hours, and climbs out the other side, and the whole population moves around it like cattle around water. Seedlings get it first, behind reed screens. Then the washing of jars, the sorting of grain, the mending done where eyes need help. Nobody works in it if they can help it — the pit exists to refuse the sun — but everybody works by it, shifting bowls and baskets and sleeping mats a hand's breadth at a time to stay in the moving cool. Rest happens where the shade is deepest in the hot months, work where the shade is merely kind. In winter the logic reverses in miniature and the sun patch becomes a hearth no one lights, old people and cats and the smallest children colonising it together in a slow, silent traffic all afternoon.

A household, down there, is hard to count, because it keeps adding members who are not people.

There is the fig in the courtyard — planted off-centre where the light rests longest, mulched with ash and manure, poured the last rinse-water of the day at its roots — and it is spoken of as a member, with an age and a temperament, and sometimes with a name that is just the name of whoever planted it. A tree that fruits well is an ancestor who stayed. A tree that fails is grieved without embarrassment. Around it, under it, through the whole pit in ordered density, the animals live at a closeness that would read as squalor anywhere with more space and is here simply population: goats in the side cave stamping and coughing in deep litter, a donkey asleep on its feet with one ear turned to the ramp, chickens working the manure heap with total concentration, cats coming and going from the grain caves with the air of clerks, pigeons in their hundreds in cut holes along the wall, exploding upward all at once when a dog barks and turning the shaft of sunlight solid with dust. The people move among them with an unhurried fluency that no visitor can copy — stepping where the floor will be empty in a second, ducking the exact height of a mule's swing, silencing a goat with a palm between the horns without looking at it. Children are raised partly by this traffic. They learn balance on ramps crowded with hooves. They learn the gesture language first from watching dogs ask and donkeys refuse. It is common to see a toddler conducting a serious, silent negotiation with a hen, both of them absolutely committed, neither giving ground.

They eat what the pit can persuade out of its own shade: broad beans and onions, peppers dried to leather, squash with leaves overlapping like hands, melons that are mostly patience, grapes that climb to the rim and get pruned by sheer dryness a metre out onto the plain, grain from the thin fields overhead ground between stones worn into shallow perfect bowls that are older, some of them, than the pits they sit in. Goat milk. Eggs. A pigeon, sometimes. A goat, on occasions whose importance is measured by how much of the courtyard has to be rearranged. Food smells — onion, smoke, roasting pepper, dung underneath it all — never quite leave the cave rooms, and neither does it leave the people. A person from one of these pits, met on the plain at a distance, announces their home courtyard to anyone who knows how to read scent: this one's fig leaves, that one's stored garlic, the tannin-and-litter smell of a stable with sheep rather than goats. It is one of the ways they locate each other in the dark tunnels, those low passages wide enough for a laden donkey that connect pit to pit beneath the undisturbed roof-earth, where the air stands still and smells of cool mineral soil and somebody else's dinner.

Water has taught them a deliberateness that looks, from outside, almost like slowness of mind, and is nothing of the kind.

They simply never do anything once with water that could be done three times, and never do anything quickly that the loess is willing to do slowly. Drinking water lives in sealed jars in the cave rooms and is poured, not splashed. Leaves are thick; growth is slow; the gardens in the driest months do not look desperate, they look considered, like the people tending them. Their hands, working soil that is dry dust to a hand's breadth and then dark and cool below, have a knowledge in them that they would have trouble putting into the shouting language: how far down this year's damp has kept, where an old root seam runs, when to stop digging. A bucket coming up twenty metres from one of the oldest wells comes up not full, and nobody expects it to be, and the person hauling it rests exactly as long as the rope needs and not one breath longer. Waste, here, is not a moral category. It is a description of people who do not last.

And nothing lasts, which they also know, and build for.

A new pit is a generational project: basketload by basketload in the dry season, the spoil spread so thin on the plain that the wind eventually carries it off and no mound ever admits what was done. A cave room is enlarged a hand's width a year. A marriage might begin as one new doorway cut into a courtyard wall, the lintel set, the interior left mostly earth until the couple, and then their children, have hollowed a home out of it at the speed of living in it. The dead do not get houses. They get carried up the ramp in the cool of the morning, out onto the land that has been roof and field and grazing and road, and if there are graves they are as shallow-marked and soon-eroded as everything else up there, because the town's memory does not live in monuments. It lives in thresholds worn into polished hollows. In handholds blackened by palms. In a grinding stone whose bowl fits a great-grandmother's motion so precisely that her great-granddaughter's hands fall into the groove without being taught. In the fig tree still giving fruit in a courtyard its planter never saw finished.

So the oldest inhabitants are not always the oldest people.

They are the trees, some of them. They are the worn stones. They are the pits themselves, long past the age of sharpness, slumping a little more at the corners each rare rain, settling toward saucer shape, so that an abandoned pit and a very old inhabited one are separated mostly by intention: people still arriving each morning to argue with the erosion, goats still being kept out of the beds, swallows still being argued with over the lintels. When the people finally leave, the succession is orderly and it is not an ending anyone here seems to find tragic. Pigeons first, in greater numbers. Then a fox in a fallen room. Then caper and untended pomegranate, splitting with fruit nobody picks, wasps and starlings screaming over the stained harvest. The inhabitants change species. The address remains.

At night they come into their final form, and you do not need to see them at all.

Oil flames sit deep in wall niches, small and mostly unnecessary. Bats pour out of the unlit tunnels and owls come in to replace them, shift for shift, like tenants who have an arrangement. Goats shift in the litter. Somebody laughs in a cave room and the laugh comes up the pit wall and out over the rim, bodiless, surprisingly young. Dogs, sleeping on the ramps where they can watch both levels at once, begin answering each other from pit to pit across the dark, each call taken up and passed along until the whole settlement has spoken once, in order, without a single human word. The people inside that relay lie in their cool rooms with the day's dust still in their hair and the deep earth's temperature holding steady around them, and arrange, already, the small movements of tomorrow: which jar, which root, which basketload of spoil, which hand's width of wall.

Above them the plain extends empty to the horizon, saying nothing at all — and that, for them, is not loneliness. That is the roof holding. That is the quiet room of the world, left open, where anyone who needs to may climb up and stand in the wind and finally, privately, say the unsayable thing. Then come back down the worn steps, into the shade, into the noise, into the dense green animal warmth of home, where everything they say will be heard by everyone, forever, and they have made a way of living that calls that not a burden but a courtyard.
