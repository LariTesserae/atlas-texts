From a distance you might mistake them for ants on the face of it — specks moving up the ramps, loads on their backs, pausing at the switchbacks. Up close there is nothing insect about them. They are slow, deliberate, and almost entirely preoccupied with weight.

Everyone in the Highworks carries something. A child carries a smaller child in a sling of loom-cloth. An old man carries a bucket with a lid chained to its handle. A woman goes up to market with empty sacks folded like fans on her shoulder and comes back down with soil, oranges, a live hen held close against her ribs. Nobody swings their arms idly when they walk. Hands are for balance, for loads, for catching at a railing when a mule pushes past on a ramp too narrow for both. To drop a thing is not clumsiness here. It is a moral failure that can kill two streets below.

So they are tied, clipped, knotted people. Tools hang from short cords at the belt. Cups are hooked to jackets. Toddlers on the open terraces wear bright harnesses, their lines running back to a parent's wrist or a ring set into the courtyard wall, and they learn the length of that line the way flatland children learn a road. Babies learn not to throw before they learn to talk properly. Throwing is un-taught, smacked out of small hands gently and constantly. What goes down does not go away. Down has a name and a garden and will come up after you.

Their bodies are made by the climb.

Even the base-born, who live their whole lives in the warm shade of the arrival yards and may never go higher than the dye-works, have calves like rope and a rolling, sideways gait from ramps and uneven tile. Higher up it sharpens. The true Worksfolk — the families who have been roofward for four, five, six generations — are barrel-chested and light-boned, long in the thigh, short in the torso, with broad feet splayed from a lifetime of gratings and hot stone. One leg is often visibly stronger than the other; the central ramps all spiral the same way, and a lifetime of turning takes its side.

They squint even indoors. Their eyelashes are pale with lime dust. Their hands are enormous for their size, not from vanity of work but from use: hauling, gripping iron in winter, testing a wall for damp, knowing by palm whether a cistern pipe is running true or knocking with air. Masons can hear a bad buttress. Pump-keepers put an ear to a shaft at night and tell you which level is stealing water. Children put their teeth to railings to feel the freight elevators coming.

And they chew, constantly, the small bitter leaves from the high terraces. It stains their teeth a faint green-black at the edges and keeps the ringing out of their ears on the upper levels. Newcomers get the tea and gag on it. Born Worksfolk suck the leaves while they talk, while they weave, while they sleep on the hot afternoons, and go a little restless and headachy without them down at the base where the air feels, to them, too thick to breathe properly. Soup, they call it. Gravy-air.

No one people lives all of the mountain, though — height makes countries out of cousins.

The Base folk are the loudest and the broadest, market people, mule people, soil-makers. They smell of dung and citrus peel and wet reed, and their skin is shade-pale under clothes despite the heat because the sun reaches them in strips. They make the earth everyone else stands in: they shovel out the condensation drains, turn the compost yards, mix ash and crushed brick and goat droppings and kitchen rot until it steams and sweetens, and sell it up-mountain by the sack with their family stamp pressed into the weave. It is dirty work and absolutely sacred. A soil-maker's daughter can marry anywhere. Her hands will be black to the wrist at her wedding and nobody will pretend not to notice.

The Middle folk are the majority, and the tangle of the place is theirs. Weavers with lint in their hair, printers with ink under their nails, teachers, launderers, bean-growers, electricians forever coaxing light into the deep interior corridors. They live in patterned shade under wires and laundry and vines crossing between towers, and they have developed a close, lip-reading way of speaking from working beside the looms and generators — leaning in, touching a forearm, saying half the sentence and letting the machines supply the noise around it. At rest they are sign-talkers, quick flickers of the fingers while a shuttle clatters. Their children know a hundred shortcuts through other people's roof gardens and are tolerated in all of them, so long as they step only on the stone lips of the beds.

The Top folk are another creature again. Fewer, leaner, wind-cut. The sun has hammered them dark and then bleached their hair, and white limewash lives permanently in the creases of their knuckles. They tend the fog nets, the orchards, the signal bells, the swift-eaves. They speak less, because the air is expensive, and more slowly, because the wind takes the first word if you are careless. They wear undyed wool even in summer against the glare, and wide hats stitched with little mirrors that flash warnings to the levels below when a net is being lowered. From their terraces the real mountains are a low blue irrelevance on the horizon, and many Top-born go their whole lives without descending past the middle markets. When they do, they walk bent in the heavy lower air, embarrassed and a little frightened by how close and loud and smelly everything is, how nobody looks up before stepping out of a doorway.

Marriages between levels are negotiated like trade agreements: which family keeps the cistern rights, whose planter wall will be maintained, how many sacks of soil come with the bride or groom, whether the children will be raised high enough to get the lungs for it. And yet people are always moving, a sack and a bedroll and a goat at a time, up a level for work, down a level for love, sideways into a new trade. A life is measured in addresses learned by the legs.

Their clothing tells you the day's travel. Anyone making a long vertical journey dresses in peelable layers tied at the waist — market cotton at the base, a mill-smock for the damp middle, a white shawl and gloves for the glare above — and carries a water skin flat against the spine. Nothing trails. Nothing flutters loose where a cable-car could take it. Pockets are deep and buttoned and everywhere; a Worksman's jacket, shaken out, yields string, leaf, nails, seed packets, chalk, a folding knife, dried fig, a spare hook, someone else's lost bell.

Seeds, especially. They are all involuntary gardeners. Their cuffs and hair and boot-treads carry mint, corn pollen, tomato, pine. They pick burrs off each other in doorways with absent-minded tenderness and flick them — not down, never down — into a planter. Every family maintains its wall and its green edge as a single duty; old women who can no longer climb sit all day in roof orchards shelling beans and watching for cracks, for soil slipping, for a fig root getting ambitious. To let your terrace shed is to let your house wound a neighbor.

That is why their dead do not leave.

There is nowhere to put them that is not already a home. The bodies are burned in the deep furnaces on the slow days, and the ash — human, goat, mule, it all returns — is mixed at the soil yards, rested, blessed by no single priesthood but by use, and carried back up in sacks. Grandmother becomes part of the bed she tended. Children eat tomatoes and know, unsolemnly, whose they are. A particularly beloved fig tree may acquire a name that started as a person's, and in time it is hard to say whether they are feeding the tree or visiting it.

Time, for them, is not quite days. The pumps lift water cistern to cistern on a seven-day cycle, and the week is a wetness arriving, a tank filling, a spout somewhere below beginning to overflow in a rhythm every child can name. Births are remembered by what was being built. “Born the year they capped the south buttress.” “Born when the nets tore.” A repair begun in a grandfather's youth and finished by his grandchildren is not a delay; it is the normal unit of intention. They do not expect the mountain to be finished. Finished would be a kind of death. A finished place cannot take another garden.

So they build at the speed of growing. A new room is also planted the same season. A new cistern gets its frogs moved in by hand, in buckets, by children who take the job with grave importance. A new eave gets its swift-holes before the tiles are set, because the swifts are not decoration — they eat the midges from the tanks and they tell the hour at dusk with their screaming, and a level without them feels wrong in the throat, too quiet.

They are not a gentle people, exactly. They bicker ferociously over overflow rights, over goat trespass, over who let mint get into the stairwell and choke the drain. Their lawsuits can run for thirty years over a gutter. But they will turn out at the bell, whole streets of them, with ropes and mats and buckets, for a cracked tank on a stranger's level, because water lost is everyone's and a wall falling is a weather event. They understand, in their bones and in the bowl-worn steps under their feet, that the world is not underneath them holding them up. They are holding each other up, course by course, leaf by leaf.

Flatland visitors sometimes ask, breathless and bent double at a landing, when they first arrived here — as if there were a founding, a first family, a before. The Worksfolk find the question funny. They point at a stained wall, a tree splitting a planter, a girl leading a mule over a roof that is also a street that is also somebody's ceiling.

“We didn't arrive,” they say. “We're still putting the place under ourselves.”
