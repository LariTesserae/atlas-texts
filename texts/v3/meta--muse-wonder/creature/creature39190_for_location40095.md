They do not hurry. That is the first thing you notice about them, and after an hour down in the pit it is the first thing you understand.

Four thousand people live in a town you could cross in nine minutes, and none of them cross it quickly. They walk as if moving through shallow water, even on the dry streets, even in the coolest hour before what passes for morning. Shoulders low. Steps short, placed rather than taken. A pace that looks lazy from the rim and is, in fact, exact arithmetic: hurry builds heat in the body faster than the hot, still air will take it away. Children have to be taught it. New hires have to be taught it, usually after the first collapse on the switchback stairs. The pit-born have it in the way other children have balance.

They are plant tenders, and the tenders' families, and then everyone else a town of four thousand accretes around its tenders — cooks, riveters, plumbers, teachers, midwives, lift-operators, moss-harvesters, registrars of shade. But the plant sets the shape of all of them, in shifts that never let the town sleep whole. Half of them are always awake. Half are always behind wet curtains, breathing slowly in rooms that never fully cool. Kitchens are always warm with somebody else's breakfast. Stairs are always occupied and always, somehow, making room.

Their bodies are not strange. Twelve years is not long enough to make a new kind of human, only long enough to make the same kind unmistakable.

They are wet, for one thing. Not dripping — the pit is a dry place, they will tell you that, down where the heat is forty-four degrees and shirts hung indoors never finish drying. But skin keeps a sheen that isn't quite sweat and isn't quite condensation. Forearms glisten in the amber household light. Hair, worn short by almost everyone or tied up in cloth against the neck, lies dark and flat. Faces are flushed low down, around the cheeks and throat, and pale higher up where the sun touches them for forty minutes a day at most. People who work the middle terraces, in the falling rain that never lands, get a particular stippled look to their shoulders in summer — not freckles, exactly, but a map of where a thousand evaporated drops have left their mineral behind. Salt blooms on skin the way it blooms on concrete. Left alone it crusts; they wipe it away with the backs of their wrists, absently, all day, the way people elsewhere brush off dust.

Their eyes do a visitor's work in reverse. Visitors duck, expecting a ceiling, and blink in the green and orange dimness and see nothing. The pit people move through the pipe galleries at a walking pace without seeming to look at anything. They have learned the town the way you learn a house in the dark — by handrail and by heat, by the change in the air where a cistern sweats into an alley, by the smell of a doorway breathing damp. They can read a room by its lamps: sodium in the yards, green along the galleries, the stuttering blue of the welding bays through steam. Coming up to the rim blights them for an hour. They stand at the top squinting, courteous, faintly embarrassed, holding a hand up not to shade their eyes but to test the wind, which they do not quite trust. Air that moves on its own feels unaccountable to them. Air should be pushed.

They speak low. It isn't reverence and it isn't secrecy; raising your voice is work, and work is heat. Whole conversations happen at a volume visitors keep leaning into. A dispute on a gallery — and there are disputes, over water rights, over a new walkway riveted across somebody's only daylight, over whose planter has rights to a new green seam three levels below a rerouted cooling line — sounds from two meters away like two people discussing the weather. They have a talent for conducting an entire argument in the placement of a bucket. Buckets are chained to railings by address, under particular leaking joints, and moving a neighbor's bucket six inches is a statement everyone on the street can read.

Their hands are the most educated part of them.

They know, without looking, which handrails are handrails and which are pipes, and of the pipes which are merely warm and which will take skin. They know the lagging on the great engines by texture, asbestos cloth under sheet iron. They scrape bright moss out of pipe joints with spoons, delicately, because the moss is tea and poultice both, bitter, and good for the soft infections that breed in the sauna-pockets where a cool cistern meets a hot wall. They pollinate the bean troughs by hand. The beans grow pale and stringy in their lines under the reliable drips, leaves nearly white, and there are no bees, no flies, no wings anywhere in the pit to do the work, so a paintbrush passes from household to household along a terrace, and people do the flowers one by one in the coolest hour with a concentration that looks, the first time you see it, like prayer. Maybe it is a kind of prayer. The pit has no animal life at all, and they are very aware of doing, themselves, all the small moving things that elsewhere would be done by something else. Nothing scurries when they lift a crate. Nothing crosses the light. They are the only warm, quick things in town besides each other, and they know it, and it makes them gentle in odd places — around the bean flowers, around sleeping neighbors, around children.

Because privacy, down there, is a practice, not a wall.

Houses share walls with cisterns and transformer sheds. Ladders are bolted through other people's kitchens. A steam line goes through the laundry's second floor and out a stranger's bedroom window, and neither household considers this an intrusion worth naming; the laundry gets heat, the bedroom gets cheaper rent, the pipe gets tended from both sides. So they have customs where other towns have doors. You announce your steps on a ladder. You look at the rungs, not the room. You learn not to hear what a thin partition makes it impossible not to hear, and the not-hearing is so complete, so collectively performed, that it becomes a real silence — the same silence that falls, entire and synchronized, when the felt-wrapped bell strikes once for shift change and four thousand people stop at the same moment. Visitors find that silence unnerving. The pit people rest in it the way you rest in shade.

And shade is serious. Shade is surveyed, deeded, inherited, argued over in courts up at the rim. So is water, which is a more complicated inheritance than money because it falls upward, in a sense — born on the cold loop under the rim, caught in the drip-zones, tasted and sorted. Everyone down there is a sommelier of their own roof. The first drip after a cooling cycle is thrown away, iron and machine oil. The second is for washing. The third, from a clean pipe, patiently waited for, is for drinking, and they can tell from it whether the north boiler is running lean, whether a joint three levels up wants reseating, whether the moss is getting into a line. A child who can call the boiler from a mouthful of roof-water is praised the way a child elsewhere might be praised for reading early. Taste is not a luxury sense in the pit. It is instrumentation.

Their food comes down sealed — tinned, dried, frozen, into cold boxes straight off the freight lift — and the meat has never been anything they could picture walking, which for the pit-born is literally true. A twelve-year-old down there has never seen a dog. Has never been startled by a bird crossing the noon disk of sun, that hard white forty minutes when everything metal goes untouchable and the whole town ticks and pings as it expands. They have words for kinds of condensation a surface child would need a paragraph for, and no word of their own for rain, because rain is the grey veil on the middle terraces that frays and vanishes before it reaches the hot roofs, and it is not weather, it is just the town breathing out and failing to reach itself. They sit out in it anyway, in the coolest hour, on iron balconies among the bean troughs. Everyone does. Old people whose knees have memorized every permitted route from bottom to top and can no longer climb it. Pregnant women trading up a floor for venting week, because you do not sleep on the lowest level then if you can help it, and somebody can always help it — that is also a custom, a quiet circulation of the vulnerable toward the bearable. Babies, born damp and kept damp, who fall asleep to the pressure of the engines in their teeth and wake when it changes, and whose mothers can hear a boiler's mood shift through a sleeping child's breathing.

They do not keep things that cannot stand the keeping. Paper goes soft in drawers, so records that matter are stamped into tin or kept at the rim. Salt left out becomes a stone, so salt is used fast and communally. Tools are never left where the noon disk will find them. Clothes are cut loose and made of stuff that rots slowly, because nothing ever dries and everything always does.

They are, to a visitor, unimaginably clean and unimaginably crowded and very, very slow. They touch each other constantly — a hand at an elbow on a shoulder-width alley, a palm flat and brief against a back to pass on a stair — and hardly ever embrace, because embrace is heat. They love at arm's length and argue at a murmur and mourn, presumably, the way they do everything else, in shifts, half the town holding the other half's hours.

At night, when it is night up above and only the pit glows orange and green from below, its little weather turning over in the dark, the rain that never lands falls a little further before it vanishes. Then the pit people come out onto the middle terraces and are, for a while, not tenders of anything. They sit with their wet curtains pushed aside and their bean plants ticking with condensation above their heads, listening to water bead and gather on pipes they could name in their sleep, feeling the plant breathe under their feet through the young black rock. Their faces turn up — not to any sky. There is no sky to speak of, only the rim, the shade-net, the cold loop beading in the dark. They turn their faces up the way they have learned to do everything in the pit: just enough, and no more than the heat allows, toward the narrow band of the world where their weather is born, and they let it almost reach them.
