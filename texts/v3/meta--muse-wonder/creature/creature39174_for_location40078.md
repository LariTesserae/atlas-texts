You would not know them first by seeing them. You would know them by the bubbles.

A silver thread rising in front of a house that is not quite a house, breaking in a steady rhythm under a lily pad. Another along the pale line of the old causeway. Another, in a sudden brief flurry, from the fish-pens below. Where the bubbles go, someone is working, walking the bottom in that slow, leaning stride the flooded streets require, a hand's breadth of green water moving over sunken stone.

Then a head pushes up into an air-bell, and the town becomes people again.

They are not very different from the hill people their ancestors were — that is the first surprise, and then the second is how thorough the differences are once you stay. Barrel-chested, all of them, even the children, even the very old, with the deep, quiet breathing of people who count their air without thinking about it. They speak seldom underwater, obviously, but above it, in the bells, they are loud with the ease of people conserving nothing. Their voices have grown used to resonance. Even a whisper comes back off leaf and water with a roundness to it.

Their skin is brown from the mountain sun on top and strangely pale below the chin, where the lake keeps them. It is almost always wet. It wrinkles less than you would expect — the cold, and some oiliness to them, a slickness worked in by duckweed and pond-life and long immersion, so that water beads and slides rather than soaking. Old people get a greenish cast along the forearms and the backs of the calves, where fine algae takes hold in the creases and will not quite scrub out. They do not much try. A green grandfather is a grandfather who still works the lower terraces.

They are webbed, but only just: a low crescent of skin between the toes, longer and more prehensile toes than yours, made for gripping the scoured causeway stones and the root-felt of the garden baskets when their hands are full. Their fingers are long, the nails thick and pale. Their eyes, in air, squint against the thin mountain light — they wear broad lily-leaf shades tied on with fibre when they must work the surface at noon — but put their faces under and the eyes open wide and stay open, unblinking for minutes at a time in water that would make yours stream. Generations of children have learned the streets by sight before they learned them by touch.

They swim before they walk, in the practical sense. A baby is born in a bell, which is also to say half-born in the lake: the mother braced in a hammock at the waterline, her body below, her head in the warm plant-smelling air, the midwives in a circle around her with their own heads up and their hands down in the water where the work is. The child's first breath is bell-air, green and close. Its first sleep is in that same hammock, rocked by nothing visible. By the time it can hold its head up it is being ducked, gently and daily, for longer and longer; by five it is pearl-diving in play after mussel shells along the market stones; by ten it is expected to clear its share of weed from the sluice, holding on to the lashed logs against the outflow with its feet while it works.

No one here lives dry. That has to be understood. Dryness, to them, is a temporary and slightly exposed condition, like being out in wind. They come up into the bells to talk, to eat together, to make love, to shell water-chestnuts and mend nets and sing — the bells are their hearths, their parlours, their chapels of gossip — but much of life is spent trailing below that bright line: bodies in the cold lake, in fibre kilts and woven chest-bands that do not drag, weighted a little at the waist with smooth stones when they want to walk rather than swim. They tend the terraced baskets of arrowhead and wild rice. They coax young horsetail stems, thigh-thick and hollow, into the bow of a wall, tying them while supple with root-fibre and talking to the plant as one talks to a slow animal. They repair a roof by encouraging it. They begin a house when a child is born — sometimes when a child is only hoped for — because the house will be ready, roughly, when she is grown, and not before. To live here is to be always in a relationship of patience with a building that is also a relative that is also a vegetable.

Cutting is violence in their ethics, and rare. Stone adzes, bone saws exist, for lily-rhizome beams and for work that cannot be persuaded, but a severed main stem is a hole knocked in someone's home and a piece of the town's lung removed. They would rather reroute a walkway. They would rather raise a floor again, as their ancestors raised thresholds, fingertip by generation, and let the lake have the lower room for fish. The sluice-keepers — a duty that rotates through every household, performed by hand every morning — are the town's most trusted officers, because the whole moral world is in that narrow outflow: clear it enough that the inlet fall does not drown the bells, never widen it, never, lest the water fall and the houses die roots-first in the air.

Their food comes to them in the same medium they do. Trout drift through the streets in loose glittering processions and shelter in the under-house pens; the people take enough, and let enough escape, and both parties understand the arrangement as an old treaty. Snails from the terrace ponds, cress from the cold inflow, water-chestnuts cracked between the teeth in the evening bells, mussels pried from the causeway and eaten raw with a face made at the green taste. In the market bell, over the drowned market circle, trade is done head-to-head in a happy din, with wooden clappers struck to call a shoal in for inspection and hand-drums of fish skin sending their deep irregular beat down through the water so that a buyer three houses away knows fish are being sold before she has heard a voice.

Which is to say the other inhabitants cannot be separated out.

The trout are neighbours with routes and preferences. The newts live in the warm margins of the bells, sliding over children's ankles, unmolested. The mussels crowds the walkways filtering the light. Frogs sit on the roof-pads in late summer by the thousand, singing through the houses; a frog on your pad is thought companionable, a sort of porch-sitter, and children name the big ones. Diving ducks commute straight through drawing-rooms and are cursed affectionately. Leeches are the tax, picked off and flicked away without drama. Herons are the watchmen: a heron standing motionless for hours on a shallow ruin is part of the town's information, and no one sets a new weir without first noting where the herons have chosen to stand, the way you might consult an elder who says nothing and knows the water.

And under all of them, through all of them, the town itself is alive in a slower tense. The houses grow a thumb's width in a lifetime. The root-floor thickens over a decade. A leaf-canopy closes, opens, closes again above a sleeping nook. The people tend it, prune it by bending, breathe the air it traps, eat what it shelters, and are in turn held up by it — literally; more than one old woman does her evening visiting supported in the buoyant press of stems, half-floating, shelling nuts into the water for the fish. When someone dies, the body is weighted with causeway stones and laid in the old stone rooms in the deep green below the houses, where the white roots are already coming through the doorways, and over years the town quietly takes them into its structure. Their grandchildren swim past and point: that tangle, that one, that was her wall.

At dusk they gather most visibly, if you know to put your face under: dozens of heads in the bells in golden circles, bodies trailing below into shadow, talk and drum and frog-song coming up through leaf and water until the whole lake seems to be speaking in its sleep. Above, from the crater rim, there is only the fall roaring, the smoke flattening from a reed chimney, a scatter of pads on dark water. Below, the bubbles keep rising along the streets in their silver order, and the town — people, plant, fish, root, and stone — goes on living in the lake it refused, very slowly, to leave.
