You would not meet one of them by seeing them first.

You would be walking a causeway you believed to be empty — counting, as everyone here must, the warmer slab where a cable crosses under the slag, then the cool braid of the seep, then the long hot exhalation of a boiler-house through a field of standing water — and the crowd would resolve around you without ever having approached. A pressure in the sinuses from a bat you did not startle. A hand, warm and entirely damp, laid flat for a second on your forearm to let you know a person is passing on the narrow side. A smell: iron, cane-sugar, skin that has not been dry in weeks, milk.

They live the way their works are built. Scattered, never clustered. A pump-house, a family. A transformer yard, a widow and her sister's children and an old man who sleeps with his ear to the fence so he can feel the current change. A gas-plant siding where the tanker drivers keep a bunkhouse raised on brick piers, its floor always ankle-deep in small white shrimp after a flood, which no one sweeps out because the shrimp eat what would otherwise rot.

They were not born eyeless. That is the first mistake an outsider makes, feeling for it. They are born with eyes like anyone else, and for the first few weeks the eyes open and search in the way infants' eyes do, unfixing, aching without knowing why. Then the lids learn. By the time a child can hold her head up she keeps them mostly closed, not squeezed, just rested, the way you would rest a hand you aren't using. Adults open them sometimes out of habit — in anger, in orgasm, waking from a dream of the kind they all have and never describe except to say *it was bright in the other way* — and for a moment there is a wet, unfocused, perfectly intact eye that sees nothing and does not expect to. It does not frighten them. It frightens only people newly arrived, who still think blindness must be a surface over a missing thing.

Sight, for them, was never taken. It was demoted.

Their skin did the work instead, and did it slowly, across a life and then across generations of lives lived without leaving. It is not that they are naked — they wear clothes, work clothes, canvas and rubber and patched wool that never quite dries and takes on the temperature of whoever is inside it — but the skin underneath has become an organ of reading so fine it has changed the shape of their days. The backs of the hands first. The forearms. The soles of the feet, which they keep bare on the causeways whenever the slag will allow, and the slag, walked by bare feet for decades, wears down in the middle of each road into a smooth warm trough exactly one person's width. Then the cheeks. Then, in the oldest of them, the whole scalp under thinning hair, lifting a fraction at a draught you cannot feel, at a rat crossing twenty paces ahead, at a cold place coming around the corner of a pump-house at less than walking pace.

They know each other that way before they know voices. Voices do not carry here, and they have adapted to that with a closeness that would be unbearable elsewhere. To speak to someone beyond arm's reach is to shout into wet clay. So they don't. They speak into a shoulder. Into the side of a neck, lips nearly touching, the words felt as much in the recipient's collarbone as heard. Children learn language as a vibration before they learn it as a sound. Lovers argue in complete silence a room away from sleeping elders, hands pressed flat to each other's ribs, and feel the argument perfectly. A mother can call her child from inside a house without raising her voice at all: she puts her palm to the warm iron of the pump housing and taps. The thud goes through the causeway, through the floor piers, through the mud. Small bare feet come back through water that bears them because they have never learned to hurry across it.

They are never in a hurry. It shows in their bodies. They are not tall — the heron-things in the flooded fields stand taller than any of them — and they are not built for distance on land. They are dense. Soft-looking until a hand closes around a chain or a flywheel spoke or the slick neck of an eel the length of a leg, and then there is a slow, total strength that does not peak and does not slip. Their movements have the patience of the machines they tend: one great effort, then a long rest while the consequence travels. They lift. They wait while the water sighs somewhere underground. They lift again. A visitor trying to help will always be early for the second stroke.

Their hands are the most changed. The fingers are a little longer than you expect, or seem so because the nails are kept very short and the pads are broad and perpetually wrinkled from wet, like the feet of something that lives in a river. They can tell cane from cable insulation from a sleeping bird's back without pausing in a conversation. They can tell, sifting a handful of ditch water, which mollusks are banded too heavily with copper to eat and which are only sweet with it. They sort bolts in trays in the gas-plant office by thread-pitch in the dark while discussing paperwork dated next spring, and never reach for the wrong one.

They eat what the dark can make without light. Cane sugar scraped from the sweating joints and boiled into a black syrup. Fist-sized snails grazed off mineral crusts. Frogs, in the season that is always almost arriving, eaten whole and soft-boned. The red-black algae in its brief years, harvested with flat paddles and dried on boiler housings into something that tastes, they will tell you seriously, like remembering meat. Fish that breathe mud, thumped gently against a shin and taken by the gills without a struggle, as if curious to the end about the heat of the hand. They do not eat the heron-things. Not from reverence, exactly, and not from fear. They leave them their fields, and step around the metronome sweep of those blind bills, and the herons in turn have learned the exact height of a crouching person and sweep above it. It is an arrangement older than anyone's grandmother and no one remembers negotiating it.

Their homes smell of all of this, cooking and condensing. Condensation is a domestic fact they build around. Anything cooler than a body weeps, so anything they love they keep warm: tools wrapped in oiled cloth and slept beside, books — they have books, swollen, soft as fungus, read by fingertips over ink that has bled into topography — kept in flat tins on top of transformers, the paperwork, always the paperwork, kept meticulously in a dry cabinet in the one office that is heated past comfort just to preserve it. They write constantly. Logs of strokes. Logs of water levels that agree to be measured only sometimes. Births, entered by the midwife's hand guiding the father's hand guiding the pen. Deaths. The appearance, one year, of a new pump-house on a known road, entered in the same level hand as a change in the weather, its concrete curing, its flywheel already turning, its outflow already populated, its forms in order, signed in a hand no one recognizes. They do not forge it and they do not question it. They staff it. A younger son who had been sleeping three to a bunk walks out along the causeway he has walked all his life, finds the door where the door now is, and hangs his tools on the peg that fits them. In a month he has a wife there. No one says the basin built it for itself. They say the roster was short.

That is how you must understand what they are: a roster. The industry did not bring a town and the darkness did not drive it away. It brought shifts, and shifts became families, and families became the only way the pumps could be inherited. They keep the water just below the land and the land just above the water, an agreement maintained one slow stroke at a time, and in return the country keeps them at exactly skin temperature and feeds them from its ditches and never, ever lets them be startled by anything approaching fast.

Except the cold places.

They call them shadows for lack of another word, and they have rules the way they have rules for still water: do not stand in one longer than a breath; do not speak into one; step aside for one on a causeway even if stepping aside means the mud. A child is taught this before she is taught not to hurry on a pond. And they go quiet — not fearful exactly, but attentive in the whole pelt — when one pauses, as they do, against the side of a running machine, as if listening.

The old workers have a theory they will only tell you after you have stayed long enough for your own eyes to stop aching. It is not told as a ghost story. It is told as a matter of staffing.

When a person here has spent a whole life reading heat — the thread of the cable, the braid of the seep, the wall of the boiler a field away, the small mobile warmths of rats and children and lovers crossing ahead in single file — then heat becomes, by the end, the thing the person is *mostly made of noticing*. The body gets slower. The flywheel time between breaths gets longer. One winter-that-is-not-a-winter they are found in their bunk cooled through, which here is so rare that the condensation on the walls makes a sound, and by the time the family has gathered shoulder to shoulder there is simply less of them to bury than there should be. The mud takes what is left very quickly. Earthworms, toads, the crabs moving sideways in their thousands — nothing here is wasted.

And for a while afterward — a year, a decade, the time it takes a wave from the lagoon's great sleeper to cross the basin — the places that person used to tend run a fraction cooler. A pump-house wall loses its warmth on one side. A stretch of causeway thins in the damp. It drifts. It pauses by machinery it once maintained as if checking the stroke. It leaves a scour in the mud that nothing colonizes, the way a foot leaves a shape in wet clay by withdrawing.

They do not call that death. They call it going on the long round. They step out of its way respectfully, hold their breath, feel their chests afterward forget for a moment whether they have just inhaled — and then they tap the housing, and go back to work, and write it in the log.

Children born now are born a little readier. That is the quiet fact no one makes a doctrine of. The fontanelle stays tender longer. The skin of the inner wrist is so sensitive that a newborn will turn, blind and days old, toward the warmer of two hands. Their ears are not larger, but the fine hair inside the shell of them never dries, and they start, in their sleep, at bats.

Someday, if the basin is left to finish what it is doing — if the pumps keep sighing, and the new houses keep being remembered into being beside known roads, and no one comes with a light that works — they will not be a people who live in the dark.

They will be the part of the dark that has learned to maintain itself. Warm, dense, unhurried. Moving along the causeways in single file, nose to tail like the rats in the canals, hand to shoulder, reading the country through their skins as it reads them back — while underneath them, through clay and slag and cable, a wave set moving before they were born is still gently arriving, and will still be traveling onward, long after they have cooled and thinned and gone to pause, listening, beside the machines.
