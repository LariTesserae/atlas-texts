Down there, nothing gets to live alone, and nothing gets to live dry.

The first lives are on the causeways. That is the only country. A narrow, built-up strip of crushed rock and clay between black sumps and brown ponds, and everything that needs feet, wheels, roots, a nest, has to fit itself onto it, under it, or in the warm shallow margin just off it.

The people come down the haul road in the mist before the light towers have been turned off. By eight their shirts are stuck to them. By nine the inside of their ear defenders is wet. The pit holds its own weather a few degrees warmer than the fields on the rim, windless, smelling of diesel and hot algae and wet limestone, and it gets into them the way it gets into everything else: boots softening and rotting out in a season, skin staying pruned at the fingertips, paper curling in the office trailer, a green bloom coming up on anything left still for a week.

They do not spread out. There is nowhere to spread. They live their working lives nose-to-tail like the parked excavators, shoulder to shoulder between pump-houses on skids, fuel bowsers, pipe stacks, cable reels, pallets of geotextile going soft at the edges. They step from board to board between trailers. They know, without looking, which water is ankle-deep and which identical-looking water will take them to the waist.

Noise has changed the shape of their society. The pumps — six big diesels and the submersibles ticking down in the sumps — never stop, and the sound is not so much heard as inhabited, a pressure in the teeth and the soles of the boots. Over it the conveyor squeals, water falls in curtains from bench to bench, backup alarms bleep, pressure washers hiss. So the crews have become a close-talking people. Conversations at six inches. The rest in hand signals, in nods, in a lifted palm. At lunch, with the canteen door shut and steam on the windows, they still have to lean in. They are loud even when they are quiet, because everything about them has had to rise to be heard over the place.

And they are not, by any count, the majority.

The frogs got there as soon as the first cut filled, and they have not left a wet place unclaimed since: leopard frogs and bullfrogs and chorus frogs in the sumps, in the tyre ruts, in the flooded conduit trenches that were supposed to carry cable and now carry tadpoles. At dusk they stop being separate animals and become one continuous roar that competes, fairly successfully, with the diesels.

The birds have parcelled out the machinery. Swallows nest in the open ends of the stacked pipe, under the dredge gantry, in the eaves of the pump-houses, and commute all day through exhaust fumes. Coots have taken a discarded cable spool for a reed bed. Herons and egrets stand in the settling ponds only a few metres apart, in a stillness that looks professional, as if they too were on shift and waiting for a signal. In the morning a few thousand blackbirds can lift off a single pond at once and for a moment the air above the causeway is all wings. Turtles haul out on floating pallets to warm themselves. Water snakes pour off the riprap when a truck passes and are gone before the ripple settles. Dragonflies stitch back and forth at windscreen height, ticking off hard hats. Midges hang in columns over every pond, marking where the warm air sits.

Even the fish arrived uninvited, washed in with river water in the first flood, and now turn in the warmest basin in water so shallow their backs break the surface as they move, navigating a lake that has not officially been allowed to exist yet.

It is an opportunistic, amphibious population, and after a while the distinctions start to look administrative. Humans nest in trailers raised on concrete blocks; swallows nest in pipe. Egrets occupy the drowned fence-line at the east end, white bird after white bird, doing exactly what the surveyors do on their sinking platform — standing, watching water, waiting for it to make up its mind. Everyone here lives on a perch, on a levee, on a floating or temporary thing, packed into the narrow band between too-wet and not-yet-drowned. Everyone is making noise to hold a place in the noise. Everyone moves slowly for creatures in such a loud place: a damp patch on a bench on Monday becoming a pour by next month, clay heaving a millimetre at a time under a pad, gypsum needles pushing out of a fresh wall overnight, frogspawn becoming frogs becoming a chorus before the engineers have finished measuring whether the floor will ever bear weight.

At night the people mostly go back up to the rim, and the place reveals who its permanent tenants are. The light towers stay on in the mist. The pumps keep their throb going through the empty causeways. The frogs take the dark. The herons stay at their posts. Something is always condensing, seeping, dripping back into itself, and the sumps rise a finger's width by morning, patient whether anyone is there to pump them or not.

What lives in the pit, finally, is not a settler but a holding company — a dense, mixed, temporary nation of things that are exceptionally good at arriving early and thriving in the interval. Creatures that can breed in a trench, nest in inventory, sun themselves on freight, raise young in water that smells faintly of fuel, and sleep, work, hunt, and call inside a sound so complete it behaves like another kind of flood pressure against the walls.

They are all waiting, without quite admitting it, for the place to finish deciding what it is. In five years a foundation, they say. In ten, a reservoir with an intended shore. Until then the inhabitants belong to the in-between: not quarry, not lake, not building site, but a new low wet world cut open eighty metres below the farmland, its systems still all laid out on the surface because there is no inside yet for them to go in — and already, on every causeway, in every pipe and sump and shallow, completely, noisily full.
