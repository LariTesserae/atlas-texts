At first you don't see her. There is already too much life that isn't human to sort through — the frogs reattaching themselves to the tile after the surge, the minnows making their permanent bright circuit of the valve wheel, the swifts folded up damp in the dark between the dome and the fern — and she is standing so still, in water to her sternum, in the one place where stillness is possible.

One hand rests on the valve wheel. That is how you know.

The wheel is described as polished by palms, and it is. Not by the palms of the dead, not only. Her hand is there the way a hand rests on a rail on a ship, without gripping, feeling the minute tremor come up through the iron from the turbines far below. When the next surge gathers — you can hear it change pitch under your feet before you feel it — her fingers tighten slightly, her other hand finds a root of the fern, and her whole body braces at an angle, knees bent, head lowered. Not to be still for the flush. She has learned the rule in her hips.

She is old, and then, looking longer, not provably old. The steam has done to her what it has done to the labels and the gauge faces. Edges are gone. Her hair is long because nothing up here cuts it, and it is not so much grey as colorless, plastered in ropes down her back and shoulders, green at the ends where algae has taken hold in it. When she turns her head, small things move in it. Not lice. Midges. A thumbnail frog, translucent, clinging just behind her ear with its throat pulsing, mistaking her for tile. She does not brush it away.

Her skin above the waterline is flushed permanently from the heat, beaded always with condensation, the rain from the dome running down her face as if she were another riveted surface that sweats. Below the waterline she is paler, and you feel more than see the difference when the pool clears between surges: her legs are incredibly strong and very still, standing in the shallow bowl worn into the inspection ledge, her feet placed exactly where feet have always been placed. The bowl fits her. It may by now be mostly hers.

She wears what is left of a tender's vest, or she wears its shape. The cloth went years ago to a soft, saturated felt that clings and rots and is patched, if patched is the word, with woven fern fiber and strips of something that might once have been a shirt brought up from below. It is hard to tell where garment ends and growth begins. A vine — not the flowering vine on the pipe, a thinner opportunistic cousin — has made a loop through a buttonhole and rooted in the seam at her shoulder. She lets it. Everything up here is let, until it has to be tended.

Because she does tend. That is the life, insofar as it is a life apart from the pool's.

She tends the fern the way you would tend a fire in winter. She keeps its roots in the water. After each flush, when the root-curtains have been tossed and tangled and sometimes torn against the standpipe grating, she wades the three impossible paces through chest-deep water and sorts them with patient fingers, freeing snails, guiding the pale tips back down into the dark. She prunes — with her teeth, with a sharpened shell, with a flake of rust — fronds that lean too far and would block the portholes, because the fern must be lit as well as wet. She carries handfuls of swift-lime from under the nests and packs it around the root-crown where it splits the insulation. She talks while she does it, not words, mostly, or not words in any language that survives the dome's echo. The sound she makes sits under the frog chorus and finds its level.

If the fern is keeping the air alive, she is keeping the fern.

She reads the gauges that have no numbers. Three times in an hour — her hour, which is counted in surges, eight or nine surges to a turning — she puts her cheek near the brass and watches the needle tremble in its habit, and taps the glass once with a fingernail grown thick and yellow as a snail shell, and listens to the answering tick through the pipe. She knows by the valve wheel's groan, by how far it ghosts around on its own during the flush — an eighth of a turn, a little less in cold weather from below — by the taste of the air on the back of her tongue, electric or flat, whether a pumping station two thousand feet down is laboring. Nobody reports to her. She reports to nobody. The hatch above her was welded shut a century before she was born. Still she keeps the watch the room was built for, because keeping it is the shape her days fit inside.

She eats what the pocket decides to feed her. Frog spawn in season, scooped in two fingers from the back of a tile, tasting of pond and iron. Minnows, the slow ones, the ones who have nibbled too much rust and go dreamy near the surface — she takes them in a cupped hand without looking, and eats them whole and alive the way the eels do, because cooking is unimaginable here; there has never been fire in this air. Once, rarely, when an eel tangles badly in the roots and injures itself against the grating, she will haul it up, forearm-length and coppery and blindly furious, and bite behind its head with a quick efficiency that is not cruelty, and share the rest with the water. White petals of the vine-flower, on the days the pumps shift pitch and it blooms, are bitter and make her briefly, giddily drunk; she laughs then, alone, and the dome throws the laugh back at her enlarged until the swifts shriek in answer. She drinks the rain off her own forearms. She is never thirsty and never dry.

She sleeps upright, wedged between the horizontal trunk of the fern and the curve of the wall, water to her chin, head tipped back into a cradle of roots, rising and settling a hand's breadth with every surge through the night that is not night — only the portholes going white, going grey, going dark, going white again with cloud. The small frogs climb her in the sleep hours and attach themselves along her collarbones to sing. Her body temperature has learned the pool. To touch her hand on the valve wheel is to be surprised that she is not warm stone.

What she is not any more is a person who could live below. That is plain in the way she breathes: shallow, wet, contented breaths, close to the surface of the pool where the fern's exhalation and the rising mist mix thickest. The thin clean air outside the portholes, the air that cut your lungs on the summit stairs — she remembers it the way she remembers the labels that used to be on the pipes, as something legible once that has dissolved off the world. When a cloud takes the summit and the glass goes white and there is no above and no below, she lifts her face and breathes deepest of all, and the fronds lift with her, and for that while she is not a keeper or a tender or a woman at all but simply the largest animal in a small, warm, hidden weather.

Valley people, hauling inspectors up the standpipe in the slack minute — back when inspectors still came — used to ask, through the water, shouting ear-to-ear over a surge, when she was coming down. She would smile with all her teeth gone green at the edges, and put their hand on the valve wheel so they could feel it turn by itself, and say the thing she still says to you, to the frogs, to nobody, whenever the water rises and the rain turns to a sheet and the whole dome fills with hammering:

"After this one. Hold on. After this one."

Then the surge passes. The eels settle. The snails resume their clearings on the glass, and she lets go of the root, and takes up her hand's place again on the polished rim, and stands in the bowl her feet are making, and waits — not trapped, not resident, in the way of the fish — for the next seven minutes to arrive.
