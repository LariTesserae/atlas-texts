If you are admitted to the Hatching Shelf, you will hear her before you see her.

Not her voice, exactly. A tap. A shift. A small, deliberate clink of boot against new stone, repeated too regularly to be walking. Then humming, from somewhere else than you expected. Then counting aloud — *seven, eight, turn* — and the sudden bark of laughter when a rail answers her from across the bowl as if it had understood the joke.

The animals are not the residents here. They are loud and numerous and entirely at home, but they are tests, guests, early attempts. The frogs in the pools, the flightless rails, the six finger-hoofed grazers, the daylight bats — they were brought to see if the place can hold animal life before it can properly hold a forest. They can all be lifted out again.

She can't be, not anymore. Not easily.

She is the keeper. That is not quite her title — the hut by the south pylon has a printed sign that rewrites itself, and on wet mornings it has called her Warden, Monitor, Attendant, and once, for a whole afternoon, Mother — but keeper is what the work has made her.

She is built, now, around never being still.

She stands the way other people stand only when they're waiting for a late train and trying not to show impatience: weight rolling from heel to toe, from left foot to right, knees loose, a little unconscious dance that has no steps and never ends. The Shelf shuffle, though there's no one else there to name it with her. Even when she's kneeling by a crack with her instruments forgotten in her hands, watching it close with that fine mineral ticking, something in her is moving — fingers drumming the damp stone, head nodding almost imperceptibly to the dripping from the overhangs, one ankle turning slow circles so the growing ground doesn't get ideas about her boot.

She learned it quickly, she'll tell you, if you can keep up with her long enough to hear a whole story. The Shelf taught her in the grammar it uses for everything. Her first week, she fell asleep reading on the bench and woke with her boots taken to the ankle and a bright seam of quartz already sterilizing her laces. She had to be cut out by her own drones while the glass frogs screamed encouragement from the central pool. Now she sleeps in the hut in a slung net that never stops swinging, and a small keeper-drone nudges it through the night with patient, irregular taps, and she wakes anyway every hour or so — bright-eyed, unsurprised — to flex her hands before the bedding can acquire a crust, to shake the fine white salt out of her hair.

Her hair is always damp. Everyone's is, inside. Hers has adapted by giving up. She keeps it cropped close enough that the builders can't get a purchase on it, a dark wet cap against her scalp, beaded always. Her sleeves are damp to the elbow. Her skin takes the mineral sweetness of the runoff and keeps it; on hot afternoons, when water evaporates off her forearms standing on a new terrace, it leaves the same ephemeral white maps it leaves on the stone, and she absently licks a line of it away while she's thinking, as other people bite a nail.

She tastes the air constantly. That's most of the job.

The maps in the hut rewrite themselves. The pylons shepherd the builders. The drones lift seedlings out of the stone's affectionate stranglehold and carry them to nursery crates. All of that proceeds without her. What she does is notice the difference between a ridge that is merely settling and a ridge that is *confused*. She puts her palm flat on warm basalt that is still slightly yielding, like bread crust, and listens through her bones to the deep stressed groan going through it, and decides whether it is learning how much weight it is supposed to carry, or learning it wrong. Then she talks to it. She talks to everything. It is partly to make noise — quiet is stillness, and stillness is an invitation here — and partly because she believes, in a practical, unsentimental way, that a landscape in draft needs to hear what finished things sound like.

So she narrates. She tells the tiered pools where they went yesterday, when they drain and reform two terraces lower. She describes the course a stream attempted at breakfast before the field of new stone drank it, walking the vanished bank with her shuffling steps, so the water still running a little while underground, muffled and stubborn, has something to follow. She explains rain to the new spines during the rehearsals, when the veil tightens its lenses and borrows thunder through the pylons and hammers the bowl for twenty violent minutes — she stands out in that, sometimes, soaked to a second skin in seconds, shouting not in fear but in something like coaching, while the rails bolt for the hut and the frogs go berserk with joy.

Her voice is hoarse at the edges from years of competing. Near the pools at dusk, when the transparent frogs begin and you can see their hearts beating inside them like small red metronomes, she doesn't even try. She's fluent, by now, in the pauses. She'll conduct a whole conversation with a visitor in the spaces between cricket-sawmill whines, starting and stopping mid-sentence, utterly unbothered, her feet never ceasing. People who knew her down below remember her as restless. Here that restlessness has finally been employed. She was a fidgeter, a pacer, a woman who did her best thinking walking up and down small rooms and drove desks and lovers quietly mad. The Shelf took the flaw and revealed it as a qualification. She was admitted because she could not keep still. Then it made sure she never would.

The builders have learned her, in their trillionfold patience, the way they've learned the goats' hooves and the rails' favorite rubble. Her initials, if she were foolish enough to scratch them, would probably fill in. But a cut on her knuckle closes while she's still looking for a cloth — *tick, tick* — and extrudes its bright seam. Her boot prints linger a fraction longer than a stranger's before rising and smoothing, as if the ground keeps checking. Her boots themselves have to be broken out of a thin stony skin every morning, a small resurrection she performs with a mallet and strong language, sitting — no, half-standing, one foot raised, then the other — on the hut's buzzing steps.

She has nothing that expects to stay. Her clothes are soft, unpocketed mostly, because things left in pockets accrete. Her tools are brightly colored so she can find them in the hour before the stone climbs their handles. Her bench is everyone's bench and therefore mostly hers, and she is diligent, even fond, about moving it the moment she gets up. *Remember just in time,* she says to it, patting its wet back like a goat. She has moved it so many hundreds of times that there is a kind of dotted trail of her attention all over the Shelf, a history of conversations and measurements and sudden sleeps she didn't take, written nowhere.

Because the ground won't keep writing. It proofreads. That is what she loves about it, and what will eventually dismiss her.

In the evening, when the light goes amber through the mesh hundreds of feet up and the pools hold the color a moment longer than the air, she walks the bowl one more time — ten minutes across, if you keep moving, which she does — checking which terrace sang true under the day's rehearsal, which pool is practicing a new course, which seedling needs lifting before the stone loves it to death. Somewhere a new spine throws its first real shadow, a hand's breadth higher than at dawn. Somewhere under it, water is already finding a way through. She hears both. She puts a hand out, not quite touching, and does her shuffle in place, pleased and proprietary and already a little bereaved.

She knows, better than the maps do, that she is as temporary as her flags on their dissolving stakes. She is here for the childhood of a place — to keep it warm company while it is still beneath its remembered sea, to be loud while it learns to be loud, to move so it learns that things upon it move and are not to be kept. One day the pylons will power down, the veil will disperse like a held breath let go, and the Shelf will be left alone in the hard mountain sunlight to begin wearing away.

On that day she'll walk out through the shimmer and her ears will pop the wrong way, and the thin clean air will feel like not enough air at all, and she'll squint like a newborn. Behind her, for the first time, her footprints will fill in at exactly the same speed as anyone's.

Until then: tap, shift, hum. Another terrace. Another telling. She keeps moving across the unfinished ground, and the ground, attentively, keeps not keeping her.
