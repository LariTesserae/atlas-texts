Nothing animal lives there.

That is the first true thing, and it stops being sufficient almost immediately. There are five plants in the pocket — five only, widely separated on the black ash — but *five plants* is a way of counting them that belongs to a visitor. From inside their own timescale, they are less like five individuals in a room than like five organs that have not yet agreed to become one body.

They do not drink. They have never been thirsty in any way you would recognize. Water would be an interruption, a cold foreign accident. Their roots do not search downward the way roots are supposed to, feeling blindly for moisture. They flatten as they go, thinning into pale filaments that enter the cracks in the basalt and brighten there, not because they are burning but because burning is, for them, a form of contact. Heat is food. Pressure is food. The slow grinding stress of a mountain still pushing itself up through the crust is food.

What rises through them is not sap. It is clear and viscous, a mineral oil that moves with insulting slowness, and in the oldest stalk there is also a bright thread of molten salt that ticks as it heats and reheats. Days pass inside a stem. A signal, a meal, an intention — if those words can be stretched thin enough to cover them — takes most of a season to travel from root to leaf.

The tallest is the furnace-fern.

When it is fully extended it stands three meters high, which makes it sound like a tree fern until you get close and see that the resemblance is only architectural. Its fronds are thick translucent plates, amber-colored, veined with copper, each one hinged at the midrib by a strip of living bimetal the plant grew for itself. Heat curls it. Cooling begins the long process of snapping it open again. One gesture takes most of a day. Most of a day of almost invisible coiling tension, and then a sudden silent unfolding, large enough to stir the dry air and set the other leaves faintly chiming against one another.

That snap is not just movement. Crystals in the hinges discharge as they flex, and sometimes, at the peak of the gesture, a blue arc jumps from the fern's tallest tip to the ivy on the back wall. It is thin as spider silk and gone instantly. It is also the nearest thing this place has to pollination, to sex, to a visit. There are no insects to carry anything from plant to plant. No birds. No wind worth naming. So they have learned — not learned, *arrived at, the way water arrives at downhill* — to throw lightning at one another across the empty spaces.

Below the fern, close to the floor, the rosette grinds.

Its outer leaves are heavy with silica, edged with stone teeth, and they rotate around a central hollow at roughly a degree an hour. You would not see it move if you watched. You would have to leave, remember, come back, and notice that the teeth had advanced. It is chewing the mountain. Patiently, in place, it wears raw basalt down into graphite-colored ash — the first soil this peak will ever possess, made before there is anything that needs soil. From its center rises a faint exhalation of sulfur and iron vapor. The fern takes that vapor in through pores on the undersides of its fronds. What one exhales, another breathes. Nothing is wasted because nothing can be replaced.

The ivy owns the hottest wall, where the seams in the rock glow dull cherry.

It should not be able to climb, and in a sense it doesn't. It extends. Its vines are hollow, jointed like bamboo, like pipework, and inside them something pistons back and forth with the daily expansion and contraction of the stone itself. Every few hours comes a deep hydraulic sigh and a soft mineral *click* as a section lengthens by a millimeter and locks. Its leaves do not turn toward the light falling now. They turn toward the heat the light left in the rock yesterday. It lives slightly behind the present, feeding on aftermath.

And it smelts. Vapor drawn up from the deep crack condenses inside it and gathers, bead by perfect bead, in the axils of its leaves: copper, tin, once something too soft and pale to quite be gold. A bead grows for weeks. Then it drops with a clear musical *tink* — you will hear it a moment before you see it fall, because sound in the pocket arrives with a small unnatural courtesy, ahead of its cause — and lands in a groove the rosette has worn in the floor. Over months the tilt of the still-growing mountain conveys it back, a fraction of a millimeter a day, toward the central crack, where the heat cracks it open and releases its metal as vapor again, and one black seed like a ball-bearing.

That is their reproduction. That is also their digestion, their conversation, their economy. Vapor to leaf to bead to groove to crack to vapor. Fern to ivy by lightning. Rosette to fern by breath. A closed loop with no outside, turning stone into ash, heat into electricity, electricity into seed.

The other presences fill out the circuit in the long bare stretches between — younger, older, quieter versions of the same experiment, stalk and plate and grinding leaf, all of them green only in the way oxidized copper is green, all of them throwing back the sourceless daylight in amber and metallic flashes. Look at them for long enough and the categories start to fail in a particular order. First they stop looking like plants and start looking like machinery: millstone, generator, pipework, condenser, furnace. Then that fails too, because no machine was ever this patient, and no machine makes seeds. Then they look, finally, like parts of a single slow animal that has distributed itself because it cannot afford to move — a lung up high, a stomach on the floor, a circulatory system bolted to the wall, skin made of leaves that remember where the sun was when they were germinated.

Because they do not live in the present. That may be the strangest thing about them, stranger than the molten salt or the lightning. The light in the pocket arrives at the angle the sun will have in twenty minutes, and a cloud's shadow crosses the floor before the cloud crosses the peak, and still the plants lean toward a slightly different summer, the season stored in them at germination. They grow toward where the light *was*. Their bodies are aimed at the past while the room illuminates them from the future. They occupy the gap between the two, which is perhaps why nothing about them hurries. For them, cause has not entirely separated from effect yet. The rules arrived late and have not finished eroding this place into consistency.

They have no defenses because they have never had an enemy. They have no flowers because there has never been an eye to attract. They are lush — absurdly, overgrown, tangled, dripping with leaf — but the lushness is not generosity. Nothing here is for eating. Their leaves are glass and copper and boiling salts, beautiful the way a still furnace is beautiful: heavy with stored work. If you touch the ivy it will blister you, not out of malice but because your temperature is simply incompatible with the conversation it is having with the rock.

Nor can they use you. That becomes clear the longer you stand among them, sweating water that vanishes instantly off your skin, heart beginning — horribly, gently — to try to sync itself to the ivy's hours-long pulse. You cannot pollinate them. You cannot feed them. You are too quick and too wet and too cool, a brief weather event in a room calibrated to geology. They are not ignoring you. Ignoring would require that you registered as an interruption. The fern completes its day-long gesture. The rosette advances a fraction of a degree. Somewhere a bead cools toward its eventual fall.

And they have never died. Individual leaves must fail, sections must lock, seeds must crack — but death as an event, as a boundary, has not been invented here yet. Matter just changes its arrangement in the loop. Metal becomes vapor becomes bead becomes seed becomes vapor. Stone becomes ash becomes the floor the next root will grip. The pocket is embryonic not because its inhabitants are young — the oldest stalk may be far older than the seam that lets you in — but because they live in a state before living and not-living have pulled apart into separate countries.

One day the seam will seal. The mountain will finish this increment of itself, and the five of them will go on for a while in their enclosed noon, trading breath and sparks and ground stone at ever slower intervals, until the heat shifts deeper and a frond locks half-open mid-gesture and does not complete it, and a vine sighs and does not click, and the loop, very gradually, becomes architecture.

For now, what lives in the hidden room is not a creature, not quite. It is a rehearsal for one. A garden before soil, an industry before hands, a single distributed being learning — at a degree an hour, a millimeter a sigh, a seed a season — how to circulate itself forever in the heat, with nothing to eat it, nothing to bury it, and nothing, yet, to mourn it when it stops.
