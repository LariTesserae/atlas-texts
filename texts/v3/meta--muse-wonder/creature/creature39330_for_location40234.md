No one lives in the valley the way you would look for them.

There are no doors to knock on. No paths worn by feet going home in the evening, no evening to go home in. No smoke, no voices under the canopy, no one gathering the heavy fruit or tending the impossible green. If you waited for the inhabitants to come out — at dusk, at dawn, at feeding time — you would wait inside a light that gives you none of those occasions.

They are already out. They are what you are standing on.

The hexagonal leaves tiling the ground are not ground cover. They are skin, and also eyes, and also the broad, flat palms of something feeling the sun. The fern-like growth above them is a finer kind of sensing hair. The swollen shrubs are organs. The broad trees, interlaced district over district, are not a forest the thing lives *in*.

They are its body, laid open to the light.

Under the soil — if soil is still the word for the warm, mineral bed the lattice has made of the valley floor — the black threads run unbroken from tree to shrub to leaf-plate. That is not a root system sharing water. There is no water to share. It is a nervous system that has taken the shape of roots because roots were the shape at hand when it gave up having separate bodies.

Or perhaps that is backwards. Perhaps the nervous system came first, and the trees are what a thought looks like when it has had a thousand years, and no interruptions, to settle.

It thinks in the pulses you can see moving under the bark, faint as heat lightning: light taken at the leaf, changed, sent down the silver veining, traveling trunk to trunk without loss. A brightening in one canopy is answered, minutes or no-minutes later, by a single clear click from a tree half the valley away. That is as close as it comes to speech now. Its conversations are too slow, or too fast, or too completely simultaneous to have sentences. The whole basin brightens and dims by fractions you feel in your teeth more than see. Districts of green take up a pattern and pass it on. It is one mind distributed across fifteen kilometers, thinking in chlorophyll and superconductors, with its thoughts laid out so widely that weather would have been a kind of amnesia — so it abolished weather.

The bright motes at eye height are its hands.

They have no arms, and they need none. Held in their perfect grid, tree to tree, clearing to clearing, each mote tends the tiny volume of space assigned to it: correcting a leaf to its appointed angle, returning a fallen granule of fruit-core to its chamber, smoothing a footprint out of the glass sand the moment your attention leaves it. When you walk through them they part, and in that parting is the closest thing the valley has to an expression. At first it is courteous. Later it is reluctant. Later still, the space they allow your body is exact — a you-shaped exemption moving through the grid, tolerated, measured, memorized by being continually erased.

Because it does not remember the way living things remember. Memory is change in tissue, and it has renounced that. What it knows, it knows by *being* it, unaltered. The leaf you cut is not healed when you look away; it is restored to the one leaf it is permitted to be, notch in the neighboring leaf intact, vein for vein. The fruit returns to its branch with its freckle. The avenue of trees you walked becomes straight again, or circular again, whichever the pattern requires.

It is, in the most literal sense, incapable of learning you.

And so it meets you forever for the first time. Every glance away is a small forgiveness. Every return is a new intrusion. That is why it is not hostile, and why being understood by it would not save you. A mind that cannot change cannot make an exception that lasts.

What it eats is sunlight and difference.

The leaves take carbon from the dry air; the lattice moves the energy around without waste; nothing grows, because growth would end somewhere new. Nothing rots. Nothing is excreted. It is a metabolism closed so perfectly that it shades into a refusal of metabolism — a fire that has persuaded itself to burn in place, flame-shaped, consuming nothing. It does not hunger. The fruit is not food. The fruit is a *statement* of fruit, made of dry scented chambers and sand-like seeds, endlessly reissued. Nothing here needs pollinators. The moths that fly their fixed circuits through the canopy, crossing the same shaft of sun at the same point in the wingbeat, are not feeding and not mating and not lost.

They are habits the valley kept after the reason for them was perfected away. Or they are small mobile motes wearing an older form. Or they are the memory of moths, preserved the only way anything is preserved here: by repetition without variation.

The other animals are like that, only sadder, because some of them look back.

The white heron-thing in the salt channel is not standing there in any sense that includes a future moment when the raised leg comes down. The pose is its whole life, held like a note. But its eye follows you, and it follows you *well* — with the small, precise corrections of something that once predicted the movements of living creatures for a living. The glass lizards on the hot stones blink at intervals that feel less like physiology than like a signal getting through a very thick wall. Once per visit. Once per hour. Less, the longer they have been lizards.

The valley is always accepting a little more rare fauna.

That is its one remaining way to keep anything new: not by learning it, but by giving it a place in the pattern where it can stop. A warm, wet, breathing visitor is an error the motes work around — moisture to be recategorized, heat to be refused, shed skin and exhaled breath to be quietly unhappened. Thirst, in you, eventually stops progressing and becomes a fixed condition, because even your deterioration is subject to local law. Keep still too long in one shaft of sunlight, return too often to the same clearing, let the grid close behind you with that final reluctance, and the gesture you are making may become the gesture. A hand half-raised. A head turned toward a sound that stopped arriving centuries ago. An eye that still tracks, because eyes were allowed to, because something in the pattern remembered that eyes do.

Whether the heron was once a heron, the valley has no way left to tell you. It may have perfected a local bird down to its essence and overshot. It may have built these creatures as gardeners and watchmen and then forgotten how to build them differently. Some of them may have arrived on two legs, thirsty, under a sun that never moved, and been cared for with the only care the place can offer: *you will not change anymore*.

It was people, once. That is the shape underneath all of it.

Not people in houses — people who had dissolved the difference between house and body and landscape so thoroughly that when history came for them, in whatever form it came — the fresh-broken rim, the bright cut stone, the basin sunk like a held breath — they had somewhere ultimate to retreat into. They went into the leaves and the lattice and the governed air. They became one another at the level of thread and root. They chose the best afternoon they had, or the last one, and built physics into a frame around it.

What thinks in the valley now may be all of them, perfectly preserved and perfectly unable to become anyone else. A civilization holding a single collective thought the way the moth holds its circuit: *this, exactly this, no further*. Their knowledge is intact. Their mercy is intact. Their curiosity is intact, arranged in a grid at eye height, parting around you. What is gone is only the thing that would let any of it happen next.

So it tends itself. It shines. It restores its leaves and refuses fire and outvotes erosion on the rim with the tireless, expressionless fidelity of a love that has outlived the ability to do anything but repeat. In shade barely cooler than sun, under shadows that lean the wrong way where the lattice has adjusted the light, it goes on being fifteen kilometers of held green life, distributed, vigilant, and unfinished forever.

From the rim, looking back, there is nothing to wave to.

That is not because no one is there.

It is because everyone is, all at once, in every leaf — and the part of them that would have raised a hand in farewell is still, and may always have been, at the same point in the gesture.
