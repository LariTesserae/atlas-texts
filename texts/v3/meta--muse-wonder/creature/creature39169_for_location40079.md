The people of the High Yard have learned to live a second out of step, and it shows in their hands before it shows anywhere else.

Watch one of them work. A fitter on the upper smelter roofs, in the bright veil they all wear against the noon sun — not a helmet, exactly, but a hood of pale, reflective gauze that leaves the world washed and distant — will reach for a wrench, miss it by nothing, by the width of a breath, and without pause or embarrassment reach again and close her fingers exactly where the wrench has become. The first reach was for the tool as it was offered by the light. The second is for the tool itself.

Everyone up-slope does this. Children do it with cups. Old men do it with railings. Lovers, reaching for each other's wrists on a walkway, sometimes meet twice: once in a faint pressure that isn't there yet, and once in skin.

They do not talk about it. Talk would be slower than the adaptation. Instead they have developed a whole etiquette of the hover — the hand that waits, the foot that tests the serrated tread before it commits, the head that does not turn too quickly toward a voice coming from a higher terrace, because the voice may arrive a fraction before the mouth moves. They are not startled people. They are people for whom startlement has been worn down, daily, to patience.

Their bodies are what the Yard has made plausible.

They are lean, not from hunger — the greenhouse roofs produce more than enough beans and tomatoes and white flowering things, and the cistern gardens are thick in summer — but from ladders. Everyone climbs. Roost Row children climb before they properly walk, hauling themselves hand over hand up the perforated wall of their own homes, past sleeping pods and battery stacks and pigeon lofts, past their grandparents shepherding dew with soft brushes two levels down. By adulthood a Yard person has long calves, long forearms, a particular economy in the shoulders. They move like cats do here: placing, not stepping. Even on the flat causeways their gait has a vertical memory.

The light has worked on them. Their skin is darkened and wind-cracked at the lips and knuckles, protected everywhere else by veils and sleeves and a white dusting of mineral cream that smells faintly of copper. Their eyes are narrowed as a habit even indoors, even in the fifty-foot twilight of the Low Pits. When they come down-slope — down meaning toward the center cisterns, toward the older, lower, more temporally reliable districts — they blink and complain good-naturedly that everything is too loud in its colors, too blunt. Up-slope people are said to be paler under the veil, quieter, harder to surprise. Down-slope people trust instruments. Up-slope people trust bees.

Because the bees are right.

That is not a proverb. It is a maintenance procedure. The bees commute in their painted lanes between hive-wall and greenhouse port navigating by a sun that sits for them exactly where a sun should sit, and the mirror crews in the Lens Fields time their enormous, slow recalibrations to the bees' return. So an ordinary Yard worker may adjust a solar furnace worth more than his whole terrace by watching insects come home. It has given them a distinctive kind of respect: not sentimentality about animals, but reliance. The goats are not mascots. The roof goats — small, white, black-footed — patrol the Radiator Ridges and eat lichen from seams no brush can reach, and foremen will wait, tools in hand, until a goat has finished staring them off a fin. The swifts nesting under the Roost eaves alter the dawn schedule; no one runs a crane through the first pour if they can help it. The great pale moths, sleeping in their thousands on transformer housings, are parted by hand in the service corridors, gently, the way you would move a curtain.

A Yard household is therefore never only human.

It is a tenure arrangement in layers. A family in the Roost Rows might occupy three sleeping pods tucked behind a heat shield, a workshop bench that folds over a battery stack, and a strip of roof that is technically theirs but functionally a goat run, a condensation farm, and a landing place for swifts. They will share a wall with finches who live in an open framework and refuse to leave, and consider this normal privacy. They will keep a shrine of retired pressure gauges by the maintenance ladder, not from superstition as an outsider would name it, but because the gauges were good gauges and the wall needed something in that shadow. Their laundry dries beside drying circuit sheets. Their children sleep within earshot of a hive-wall humming almost below hearing and grow up associating safety with that hum.

They replace rather than repair, and this extends inward.

A bad knee gets a swapped brace, unbolted, refitted, the old one sent down the line to be melted and recast. A cracked tooth is pulled and a printed one seated. Tools are modular, houses are modular, habits are modular. Nothing is mourned past its usefulness because usefulness is how a thing stays present; the melted brace comes back as a stair tread a cousin will climb. It would be wrong to call them unattached. They are attached the way a reef is attached — continuously, by accretion and replacement.

It also extends forward. Everyone has heard the maintenance stories, because everyone has done maintenance: the service bay under a deck that is too large for the structure above it, clean and lit and stocked with parts for a machine not yet installed. Yard people do not loot those rooms. They do not worship them either. They inventory what is there in a careful hand, seal the hatch, paint the date in white, and go on with the shift. When the part is needed — next year, in ten years — they open it without triumph, as if collecting something left by a reliable neighbor. Children are taught the surplus corridors the same way: walked through twice, once looking at the floor so the Roost Row passage returns them, baffled, to its own entrance, and once looking up at the ceiling pipes so it delivers them correctly to the hive-walls. No sign. No explanation that would survive the experience. You learn the Yard in your neck muscles.

Their days are built around scarcity that turns out not to be water, but timing.

In the hour after dawn, before the sun takes the dew back, the whole plateau drips and runs and chimes into its cisterns, and that is when children and old people are most powerful. They are out with brushes and shallow pans shepherding condensation along cornices designed as watersheds, coaxing it before it can evaporate, while the working population is still in the pods. It gives the very young and the very old a shared shift and a shared authority. A six-year-old in the Cistern Steps, serious in oversized veil sleeves, knows more about where the water went this morning than her parents will all week.

By nine the decks are dry, lips crack, the green things close down to punctuation, and the long slow manufacture begins: alloy sheets moving a week down a hall, presses in the Low Pits completing one stroke an hour with a *whump* felt in the molars, cranes crossing bays so slowly lunch can be planned around them. Yard work is mostly tending tending-machines. Lines of workers pass light through glass at angles, read reflections, reach twice. They are not in a hurry and they are not idle. Their tempo is mirror-tempo — patient, cyclical, nearly still — and people who cannot learn it leave, or move down-slope where the clocks agree better.

At night they have two dusks, and they use them differently.

The first, solar dusk, is violet and sudden, the white city going blue in minutes, the temperature falling out of every shadow at once. Work stops. Veils come off. Faces appear, surprisingly varied after a day of identical gauze — scarred, freckled, tattooed with fine white lines at the temples where the veil ties, beautiful mostly in the way of people who are rarely looked at all at once.

Then the materials give their light back. Tile and lens blank and cistern water glow faintly for an hour, sourceless, enough to see by. This is the social hour, and also the moths' hour. Families eat on roof edges that are also goat runs, now vacated. Workers crossing home pass moths gathered in cool shadow where tomorrow's sun-patch will fall, waiting for a warmth that has, for them, a kind of precedence. Yard people step around those gatherings instinctively. It would be rude — not unlucky, rude — to stand in a place that is already occupied by tomorrow.

No one knows if the up-slope residents age at a different rate. The records duplicate oddly after a certain year and cannot settle it. What can be observed is smaller: that a woman who has worked thirty years in the highest lens bays will answer a question occasionally before it finishes, then smile and wait for the questioner to catch up; that cats there have an extra carpet square placed where the sun-patch will drift against reason on Thursday, and sleep in the square early; that the roof goats, several generations of children swear, are the same goats, white and black-footed and municipally unbothered, older than goats should be.

Maybe they are. The Yard has no ground to bury anything in. The dead, like the bad knees and the retired gauges, are unbolted from their use and returned to the making — ash into ceramic glaze, names into the painted dates on sealed hatches, stories into the two walks through a corridor. What persists is not the part. What persists is the fit.

So if you visit, do not look first for monuments. Look for the pause before the hand closes. Look for a child brushing dew uphill. Look for a foreman consulting a bee lane before he touches a mirror the size of a house, and for an old person laying out, without comment, an extra square of carpet in the shade.

That is who lives here: a people who have nested, with goats and swifts and moss and time itself, in the joints of a thing they built — and who have quietly, practically, without fuss, begun to be built by it in return.
