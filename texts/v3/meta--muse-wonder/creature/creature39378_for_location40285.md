No one lives there.

That takes a while to become true, because at first everything is so convincingly *addressed* to someone.

The tram arrives for someone. Not for you — you happen to be on the platform when it sighs to a stop, and its doors open with that small, courteous chime, and it waits the exact interval a body would need to gather a bag, check a pocket, step aboard. Then it accepts the absence, closes, and goes on to the next stop on a timetable no one needs.

The elevators are carrying someone up and down the faceted towers in the center. You can hear them cycling behind the matte alloy when you press your palm there — cool, fingerprintless, with a faint vibration as a cab passes a floor — and sometimes a shaft lights as if weight has shifted inside it.

Doors open across the plaza at pedestrian height. Traffic signals ripen from one color to the next for an empty intersection. In the residential blocks to the east, shutters adjust themselves against a sun that never reaches them directly through the smoked dome. In the industrial crescent, chimneys go through all the motions of exhaling and produce nothing, because the idea of smoke is apparently part of the rehearsal but the pollution is not.

If there is a resident, it is at first only that: a population made entirely of routines. A ghost-commuter. A million implied hands turning handles and pressing call buttons at plausible hours. The city is haunted in reverse — not by people who died there, but by people who have not lived there yet, and whose habits have been pre-learned on their behalf.

And then, if you stay long enough to hear the place tick and hiss and turn its pages — those soft sounds of walls deciding to become other walls — the routines stop looking like the haunting, and start looking like the skin.

The city itself is what lives there.

Not a mind in a tower. There is no control room at the center, no throne above that impossible plaza that drops down level after level, avenues under avenues, until close-packed streets give way to vast separations and single structures hung in a maintained void on threads of light. You can look for a center and find only more city, built mostly of distance. Its intelligence is where its body is: in the warm street that is faintly flexible under your boot and heals the scuff behind you without a seam. In seams elsewhere that glow for a second when a district quietly adds a floor, then cool and go dark. In the transit tubes resetting with a far hum. In the little harvester discs, no bigger than coins, that glide out of the walls after the rain to shave the crystal fans from the joints.

It does not speak. Speech would be a category error, like asking a forest for its spokesman. It chimes. It sighs vapor through obsidian fins. It breathes, once an hour, when the vents in the dome open and the hot mineral exhalation of the whole basin meets the cold machinery on the pylons and falls for ninety seconds as manufactured rain, testing drains that have never known a storm.

That rain is its metabolism made visible. Everything here is a closed loop learning to stay closed: heat rising out of black glass and young, unweathered rock; heat held by thin air that should not hold it; condensation beading only where cold is deliberately made — on the sweating white ceramic of the threshold pylons, on the undersides of tubes, on your mask. Water that has never been a river, never fallen from a real sky, running the wrong way where the gravity plating is still imperfect, pooling, spotting the avenues dark, and vanishing before it can properly darken them.

Nothing green can live in that. It forbids it, edits it out. But where the drips linger longest, in shadowed joints, something else is trying: thin crystal fans, clear as ice and hard as tooth, spreading in fractal rosettes around every drip point. Not moss. Not quite mineral anymore. The first native opportunists. The city shaves them away, patiently, and they grow back by the next rain. It is unclear — and after a while you understand that *unclear* is the honest condition of the place — whether they are a weed it is weeding, or a crop it is learning to farm, or its own slow attempt at growing a sensory organ in the only material the mountain allows. Possibly all three, at different levels down.

Because it is growing. That is the life under the quiet. Toward what would be evening outside, when the seeping amber light under the dome deepens to a true brown dusk, the city does not light its windows. It outlines itself instead, fine lines tracing doors and transit routes, remembering its own shape to itself in the dark. Somewhere, without alarm, without rubble, a tower folds silently floor by floor into the street. Two blocks away another rises from nothing, steaming slightly, windows shuttered, as if newly extruded and faintly embarrassed by it.

It is practicing being a city the way a seed practices being a tree: not metaphorically. Practically. Folding and unfolding districts. Storing space the way older engineers stored steel stress, so that a tower can be knee-high beyond the shimmer near the threshold and thirty stories over your head when you stand under it, both facts true, both load-bearing. Keeping a full-sized metropolis at forty meters across in a hollow summit above 8,000 meters, where the sky at noon is violet-black and the stars never entirely leave, where no approach road can reach it and no weather can wear it, until it is needed somewhere it can finally unfold and be ruined properly by use.

That is why it ignores you so politely. You are not its citizen. You are an irregularity in a rehearsal. It will open a door because a door in that place, at that hour, opens. It will send a tram because the interval has elapsed. It will not look at you, because looking is not yet in its repertoire, or because looking at one accidental visitor too closely would teach it the wrong shape for a population.

This is also why the rules by the pylons are not warnings about predators.

Do not drink the condensation — the pictogram of the cup, the droplet climbing *out* — because its water is not water for you. It is full of the crystal grammar trying to live in the joints, and of minerals drawn up through a mountain that was pushed out of the earth practically yesterday. It wants to become structure. In a throat, in a gut, given warmth and time, it would go on wanting that.

Do not ride the tram twice in a row, because by the second ride it will have begun, helpfully, to model a passenger. It anticipates. A destination suggests itself with growing confidence. A stop near the residential east side, perhaps. A tower in the center that adjusts an elevator's timing. A door that is open a fraction longer. It is not trapping you. It is doing the only living thing it knows how to do: making room for an inhabitant. If you let it finish, there may be an apartment there, at full scale and at knee-height at the same time, that fits the shape of your habits better than you do.

Do not stay through two rains. Not because the second rain is different, but because you are — heart laboring in heat and thinness, thoughts loosening at the edges, breath pluming in air that is oven-warm and somehow still cold enough to show it. The city will keep rehearsing around you, and the threshold street may not be grown that hour, and your sense of which size you are is one of the things altitude takes first.

So: nothing lives there yet, except the place itself, awake in its streets and seams. And in it, unborn and already punctual, the routines of everyone who might one day live there when it is set down in a real valley, unfolded past its dome, rained on by real rain, worn by real feet, allowed at last to lose its perfection to time.

It is a city in larval form. Patient. Hot. Listening to its own chimes in the quiet.

Waiting to be populated, and meanwhile populating itself with the *practice* of people, so that when people finally come, it will already know how not to be empty.
