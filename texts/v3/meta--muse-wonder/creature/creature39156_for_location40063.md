At first you only see them the way the basin lets you see anything — at a distance, and in color.

An orange smudge in the fog bank when it rolls. Two orange smudges paired at a valve wheel three hundred meters away, turning it together in a slow, practiced heave. A single line of orange along a yellow walkway, head down, not hurrying. The suits are meant to make them visible, but down here visibility is temporary, so the suits have become something else: a way of announcing *person* in a place that otherwise announces only process. Tank, tower, trench, pump — and then, moving against the scale of it, soft and upright and briefly bright, a person.

Come closer and the first thing you notice is how covered they are, and how precisely.

No skin shows in winter, and very little in summer, which down here is only a less serious kind of cold. Balaclavas under hard hats, goggles that fog and are wiped with the back of a specific knuckle, double gloves — thin liners for dexterity, heavy outers clipped to the wrist so they are never, ever set down on wet concrete. Their coveralls are padded at the knees and elbows, scarred white at the shins where they've brushed drainage grates still sharp enough to open fabric. Their boots are tall, sealed, and lugged deep, and they walk in them with a flat, deliberate roll of the sole, feeling for the invisible frost that forms off the yellow lines. Longtime Low Workers can read Deck 3 through their feet. The turbine hum lives there, in the bones of the foot, and they will stop mid-stride and frown, not listening, exactly, but attending with their arches to a change in pitch most people would need a gauge to see.

They are all youngish, because the place is, and because their bodies are the first instruments it has worn in. Twenties to early forties. No children, no old people, no one who has been here long enough to be bent by it. They cut their hair short against the damp. Their faces, when the balaclavas come down in the warm rooms, are pale in a way that has nothing to do with being indoors — it's the paleness of forty minutes of real sunlight a day, and that taken slanted, in a band, standing still to receive it. Crews arrange breaks around the solar pass without ever calling it that. You'll see five or six of them along the western wall at noon, helmets off despite the rules, tilted up as the light slides down the terraces and across their closed eyes, steaming faintly in the cold. Then it's gone up the other side and they are back in the dusk and the sodium lights, blinking, pupils huge.

Their eyes have learned the low constellation. White masts, green pipe-lamps, amber from below the grates. They can navigate by indicator color the way sailors do by stars, and they are half-blind, briefly and without resentment, whenever they make the trip to the rim and the real sky.

Their hearing is stranger. The basin throws every sound everywhere, so they have learned not to locate noise but to *parse* it — to hold five separate machines in their heads at once and hear which one has missed. In ordinary conversation they speak quietly and in short, front-loaded sentences, because anything long gets smeared by echo off the retaining walls. They gesture a lot. In the equalization fog, when the acoustics invert and a valve two aprons away whispers at your shoulder while your partner at arm's length goes silent, they don't call out at all. They stop. Everybody stops. Hands find a rail or a pipe stanchion — never a sweating pipe — and they wait, orange fading to grey to nothing, breathing the warm, mineral fog, until the lights come back one by one. New hires panic in that whiteout. Old hands — old meaning three seasons — go almost restful in it. It's the one time the place insists on stillness, and they've come to take it.

They live by other clocks. Not the sun. The two-hour reversal of the Long Trench, the forty-seven-minute sigh of the reservoir, the four-hour pump rotations, the shift change when a dark sector floods with light and a machine sealed for weeks is opened and exhales hot oil and sweet antifreeze. Their sleep, up in the rim barracks, is famously deep and famously broken; they dream, they report, in amber light and at low volume. They eat hugely, and always hot, holding bowls close so the steam warms their faces. They are fastidious about feet, about glove liners, about the specific drip from the specific joint that makes their specific puddle. They never touch steel with a bare hand. They never leave a tool flat on the deck unless they want it back in the morning under a perfect dome of clear ice, displayed like something in a museum — and sometimes they do, for luck, for a joke, for a new kid to find.

They are not the only residents, and they know it, and the knowledge has made them superstitious in a technical way.

They tend the moss in the leakage trays like a crop and read it like scripture; a yellowing mat empties a sector faster than an alarm. They step around the wiry, colorless grass in the rail bed as if it had earned it. They feed — officially no one feeds — the small white frogs in the polishing ponds, those blind-looking, unhurried natives who have never seen real night or real winter because their water never quite freezes and never quite warms. The frogs are the only things in the Low Works that were, apparently, born to the dusk, and the crews watch them with a frank envy that they disguise as monitoring. The inspection carts on Service Level 1 get names, get cursed, get apologized to. The swallows under the rim anchors get respect and no eye contact; they are the commuters, the ones who can leave vertically into daylight and choose to come back down, and nobody wants to examine that too closely. The rats get left entirely alone. Their tracks go ruler-straight through frost-dust along the warm pipe runs, and that straightness is taken as a sign of seriousness, of a parallel crew working the underdeck on its own shift, doing a job no one has been assigned.

Mostly, though, the inhabitants are defined by seam-work. Everything down here is a lid on something else, and their lives are spent at joins — gaskets, expansion joints, sluice gates, hatch seals, the line where a hot pipe crosses a cold one and makes its own three meters of private rain. They stand for hours in that rain without noticing they're wet. They learn which joints tick when they're taking strain for the first time and which ones tick before they fail, and they press gloved palms near them, hovering, reading temperature by proximity the way you read a stove. They smell chemistry drifts before the moss does. They develop rashes in the shape of their respirator seals and call them basin rings. They get very good at working far apart from each other — a crew spread over half a kilometer of concrete, each alone in their own weather of steam and rime-fall, connected by radio checks that are less information than reassurance: still here, still here, still here.

At the end of a rotation they ride the switchbacks up, blinking, into a wind that comes from one direction and weather that falls from the whole sky, and it feels, they say, unmediated and a little careless. The plain is too wide. There's no wall to throw their voices back. Above the rim they move faster and less carefully, and they touch things with their hands.

But the place is still becoming itself, still sharp, and they are becoming themselves inside it. Down in the permanent dusk, where the frost falls glittering off the warm pipes and the trench turns and runs the other way, they've stopped behaving like visitors who operate a plant. They tend it the way you'd tend something that shelters you — badly, coldly, extravagantly, but reliably. They've learned its tide tables in their calves, its moods in their soles, its breath in their lungs.

They are the life that found the seams on purpose. Still thin, still orange against the concrete, still stopping together when the fog rolls across and swallows the lower lights.

And while they wait, quite still, the white frogs go on moving below them through clear water, unhurried as anything, seeing nothing, at home.
