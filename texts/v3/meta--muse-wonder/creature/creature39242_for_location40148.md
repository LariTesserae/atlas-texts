You would know them first by what they haven't done.

No path worn between the kitchen door and the water jars, though they cross it a dozen times a day. No snails crushed along the north wall. No web left swept away, no fern pulled from between the stones. The stepping stones keep moving — a hand's width here, turned over there — because the feet that use them are still learning, and are honest about it.

They go barefoot, of course. Not as a custom brought from somewhere else, but because the courtyard taught them in the first week. Their soles are stained the color of the ground, dark silt worked into the lines of the skin, under the toenails, never quite washed out. They walk the way the cat crosses the roof at night: one considered step at a time, weight put down slowly, ready to take it back if the earth gives too much underneath. In the morning, before they lift anything or mix anything, they make a circuit of the courtyard just feeling it — where the damp has risen in the night, where a brick course has settled true, where the fig roots have pushed a stone up another fraction.

They are builders, but that is not quite the right word for most of the day. Building is the brief part. It happens at first light, while the air is still cool enough to hold and their hands don't stick to everything. One of them holds the wooden mold steady. Another presses in the silt — river mud, straw, water carried in pot by pot through the passageway — presses it again if it slumps, turns it out under a palm mat. They raise a wall a course at a time and then stop, even if their arms are not tired, even if the room is only waist-high and the green roof poles are already cut and waiting and beading sap. To go on would be to watch it lean by evening. They learned that here, not before they came. You can see the lesson in the one wall they didn't: it has a belly in it, slight, forgiven, left standing as a reminder.

The rest of the time they wait, and waiting has become a skilled activity in them. They sit in the moving shade and watch where the sun patch travels. They check whether the pool in the low corner has shrunk a finger's width or two. They notice when the vine has gained a knuckle's length along its string, when the basil needs pinching back, when a gecko has changed its hunting place on the unfinished wall. Their eyes have adjusted to smallness. Nothing loud has trained them here — the thick new mud swallows the town outside — and so their voices have lowered to fit the courtyard without any of them deciding it. They talk close, and not much. A basket set down softly is often announcement enough.

They are not old inhabitants of anywhere, in this place. No one has died here. No mortar bears their grandparents' thumbprints; the handprints in the bricks drying under the mats are their own, still sharp-edged. They arrived when the quarter was still being twined out over drained marsh, and everything they do shows they do not yet trust the ground to be land — nor quite distrust it, either. They do not plant close, because they don't yet know what the damp corner wants to be in the dry season. They leave wide earth empty between the stones. They keep the mint and basil imprisoned in jars by the kitchen door and trimmed into tidy mounds, as if a few disciplined plants will stand for the garden they aren't ready to commit to the soil. And yet they let the pioneers stay: the moss, the thumb-sized ferns, the single reed. If it arrives on its own and thrives, they take that as information, not intrusion.

Their bodies have taken on the climate the way the porous water jars have. Cloth never quite dries on them; it hangs soft and heavy, smelling of wet clay and fig sap. Their hands smell of basil because they brush the plant every time they pass the kitchen doorway, absently, for the pleasure of it. By late afternoon, in that greenish underwater dimness the walls make, their skin is warm and slightly damp to the touch and they move even slower, doing the last small jobs by feel — shuttering a window with woven reed, hanging a door straight on its leather hinge, turning a tile stack to dry evenly.

They live in the one finished room with the swallows in its eaves, and the other rooms rise around them at the pace the silt allows, intended for people who are mostly still themselves: a child who has only ever known this unfinished address and thinks a house is something that grows a wall at a time; kin who have not yet arrived from wherever the town's people came from; the older versions of themselves, who will need shade on a different side of the courtyard. They are building forward without being sure of the shape. That doesn't seem to frighten them here. The dragonflies patrol an empty depression as faithfully as a pool, and they have learned to do the same — to tend a place for what it will hold.

At night they listen, barefoot still, to frogs clicking in the wet corner, and know without looking whether there is standing water or only damp. They know which jar sweats fastest, which stone rocks, which wall stays cool longest after noon. This is their knowledge so far: not inherited, not written, held in feet and fingertips and the daily disappointment of a brick pressed too wet. They are as new as the mortar, and as disproportionately alive as the fig — putting down roots fast and shallow in ground that has only just stopped being riverbed, lifting the stones a little as they go.
