They are not tall people.

You notice that first when someone stands up from a pool-rim to let you pass on a street that is also a stair that is also a drain. They come up to your shoulder, perhaps — broad through the chest, deep through the back, legs short and bowed a little inward at the knee from a lifetime of walking sideways on wet terraces, feet set down flat and whole, never heel-first. They do not stride. They place. Even the children place. A foot seeks the warmest, driest, least grown-over inch of stone, tests it with the toes through the felt of the shoe, and only then takes weight. Indoors they go barefoot on stone warmed by water running underneath it, and their soles are horn-thick, yellow-white, cracked in patterns that fill with mineral until the cracks themselves look veined.

Above the clouds, the air does not give enough. So their bodies have learned to take more of it. Wide nostrils, flared a little even at rest. A barrel ribcage that does not seem to settle between breaths. They breathe slowly while they work furiously, which is disconcerting to watch — hands flying in a basket-chain, ash flying, a reed gate hauled and dropped, and all the while the shoulders barely moving. At night you can hear that breathing in a house, under all the dripping: seven, eight people and the animals behind the curtain, breathing in no rhythm at all, each one thrifty.

Their faces are burned and frozen at once.

The sun up here does not warm so much as cut. Below the line of the goggles the skin is dark copper, polished, split at the cheekbones and across the bridge of the nose with fine white scars where ice and glare have opened it year after year. Above the line, where the woven reed and polished bone slits sit all day, the forehead is paler, softer-looking, almost indoor skin. It gives everyone, when they push the goggles up at dusk, the look of having been masked. Eyebrows and lashes go white very early — not with age, with crust. Mineral water dries on them in the wind. Old men blink slowly under little white eaves of their own.

Their eyes themselves are narrow whether they squint or not. Inside, after the bowl's triple-reflected blaze, you would think them half asleep, until they look at a pool and tell you by the color of it — a certain emerald thickening at the edge, a milkiness in the current — that it will overflow on the north side in an hour, that the fingerlings will rise, that you should move the drying cheese. They learn water the way lowland children learn faces. A girl of five can put her ear to ice and distinguish, in the ticking, between ice that is merely settling and ice that is about to let go three terraces above her house. She will not be able to explain how. She will pull your sleeve — they are always pulling sleeves, tapping elbows, nudging with a knuckle, patting the stone twice — and point.

For they are a touching people, because they are a silent one.

It is not that they have no voice. Indoors, under a roof thick enough to drink the sound, they murmur freely, and laugh into each other's shoulders, and sing to babies so low you feel the song in the bench through the felt more than you hear it. The language, when you catch it, is breathy and full of clicks made with the tongue against the teeth, sounds that do not carry. But outside, working, in the full bowl where sound flies up to the cornice, they speak with their whole upper bodies.

There is a flag-language for across the town — undyed wool snapping on poles: *this channel, not this one; close the lower cistern; your roof is taking water*. And there is the nearer, richer hand-talk, constant, peripheral, carried on while the hands are otherwise busy. Elbows jerk: danger above. An open palm pressed flat and slid sideways: let it flood, don't fight it. Two fingers walking down a forearm: someone is coming down with a load. They sign while carrying baskets on their heads, while hanging from ropes, while holding a child. Infants are carried against the back in stiff, mineral-crusted slings and learn to sign before they speak, their small hands patting rhythms on their mothers' necks. *Hungry. Cold ear. Bird.*

To a stranger they can seem, in the rush of the melt-pulse, like a single animal with many limbs. This is not metaphor to them. When the warning goes up — flag, hand, a swift-shadow river suddenly veering — they pour out of doorways narrower than their own shoulders and turn without discussion to tasks that have obviously been done a thousand times and will be done differently this time. No one gives orders above a murmur. Seniority shows in position: the oldest water-keepers stand stillest, up on pool-rims where they can see three levels at once, signing small and infrequently, each sign rerouting five people. The strongest young men and women take the underwater chipping, hanging in harnesses under eaves and bridges, antler picks wrapped in felt, working submerged because water swallows the sound and because travertine is softer before it hardens in air. Their arms come up white to the elbow, steaming faintly, pruned and gleaming. Children, who are everywhere and underfoot and somehow never in the way, run with ash still hot from the dung-fires, with horn cups, with armloads of dry felt to pack a sluice. Babies sleep through it in roof-niches. Dogs weave between legs, nosing a calf hard when a person hasn't noticed a sign — trained to urgency without a single bark, apologetic and insistent as a hand.

Work has made their hands into something separate.

Fingers short, nail beds broad, knuckles enlarged, the skin of the palm laid over with callus that itself deposits mineral in the creases until their lifelines show white. They chip, scrape, lift wet stone, handle freezing water and hot ash in the same minute, and their hands register temperature slowly, painfully late, which is both gift and danger. You will see a woman lift a kettle by a handle that would take your skin off, frown at the smell of her own burning felt glove before she frowns at pain. You will see grandfathers whose outermost finger joints have stiffened and fused slightly crooked — not only arthritis, though there is that too, early and universal — but a life of cold water and mineral that seems to be turning them, very slowly, to the material of the town. Their clothes do it first.

Everyone wears the crust. Inner layers are felted wool, undyed or dyed a dun that hides staining, softened with animal fat and body-warmth. Outer layers — wide aprons, hooded over-tunics, gaiters laced to the knee — are deliberately stiffened. They stand in spring mist to get them wet and let them dry in sun until travertine stiffens the fibers, season after season, until an old coat can stand up by itself in a corner and has to be chipped at the armpits and knees to be worn again. It is armor against wind, glare, scraped stone. It makes them move with a faint, dry whispering, fabric against fabric like leaves, never a clatter. Their hats are wide felt discs with bone-slit goggles tied beneath the brim. Even indoors people keep a crust of white on their shoulders, like snow that will not melt.

They smell of the place compounded: wet wool, dung-smoke, cheese rind, warm mineral water, the iron tang of algae drying, animal musk. Individuals underneath it smell surprisingly clean, because they are always, unavoidably, being washed. Water comes through walls. It drips organized into horn cups but drips nevertheless. Floors are sluiced by rerouted springs once a day, whether a family planned it or not. Children are born into steam and wiped with warm felt, and for the first days of their lives their skin is an unmarked pink astonishment in a house where everything else is white-rinded. Then the crust begins, the sun begins, the cold begins to polish.

They eat quickly, communally, and with intense attention to occasion. Most days: dried meat softened in hot spring water, hard cheese shaved thin, bitter crack-greens, tea made from cushion-plant tips that tastes of resin and stone. But they live ready for the feast that lasts an afternoon — a pool warming fast enough to hatch a cloud of insects, the fish fry rising silver to eat them, swift eggs gathered from the unchipped holes in the eaves. Then fires are lit roof to roof, women sign across terraces with floury hands, and a whole quarter turns out to scoop and roast and eat with their fingers, goggles up, faces shining with grease and sun. Food goes first to the chippers working wet, second to children, third to the animals, because animals are heat in winter and dung is fuel and marmots under the floor keep the drains open. The old eat last and least in a thaw-feast, and make a performance of not minding, pressing the best morsels on a daughter-in-law with a hard, affectionate thumb.

For families are stacked like the houses. Three, sometimes four generations behind one low door, sleeping on the stone benches around the walls in order of age, youngest nearest the fire-hood and the animals, grandparents nearest the inner wall where the water runs warmest underneath and the stalactites have to be knocked down over their beds most often. Privacy is a curtain, a turned back, a conversation signed inside a sleeve. Courtship is largely a matter of whose hands you find yourself trusting on a rope. Marriages are announced not by any ceremony loud enough to name, but by two people beginning to chip the same doorway wider and to carry their felt to the same bench; the neighbors, who have noticed weeks earlier, start leaving small useful gifts in the new channel-niche — a bone goggle polished extra clear, a cup, a pair of tiny felt shoes crusted only very lightly, in hopeful miniature.

They are tender people in a hard register. They touch foreheads to greet. They carry their old on their backs down the steep channels rather than let them risk the wet bowl-smooth stone. They keep the gold-spotted salamanders in the cisterns and greet them by name — every house has two or three, drifting pale in the dark water — and a house that loses its salamanders quietly borrows a neighbor's bucket at dusk until new ones appear, because sweet water is not a thing to be without, and luck neither. They tolerate ibex on the roofs and shoo them with pats, not sticks. They mourn their dogs by laying them, wrapped in undyed wool, in a dry crack above the springs where mineral will take them fastest.

Death, for people too, is not shouted over. No wailing rises. When someone dies in the night — and most deaths come quietly, lungs giving up the thin air at last, a heart, a fall on the ice no one heard — the family signs roof to roof at dawn, flags droop rather than snap, and work in that quarter re-routes itself around a stillness. The body is washed in the warmest spring, dressed in the least crusted clothes they own, which are still stiff enough that dressing is a kind of building. They are carried down only if the cloud below has lifted enough to see the burial terraces on the path, or kept, felt-wrapped and cold, in the ice-bearded north room until it does. It is said that the very old can hear the cornice in their sleep, and some elders refuse to be carried down at all and are placed sitting in wall niches above the town, where the spray will seal them gradually into the cliff they spent their lives chipping away. Within a generation they are less a face than a suggestion of one, a brow, a folded pair of hands, white upon white. Children sign to them as they pass. Good morning is the same sign as keep watch.

So by dusk, when the pools skin over and the swifts pour silently home, you will see them on the roofs that are other people's ceilings, sitting close together in the last reflective light, goggles pushed up, faces lifted, doing the small evening work that can be done sitting: mending felt, scraping the day's white rind from tools, chipping each other's coats at the shoulder where the crust has grown too thick for comfort, picking ice from each other's eyebrows with careful thumbnails. Someone murmurs and three people laugh into their hands. A child falls asleep mid-sign against a knee. Below their feet, through stone, spring water ticks and hurries toward its nightly freezing. Around them the town is growing its thin new skin of stone, and they are already, absentmindedly, reaching for the pick to keep it from closing over them.

They do not look conquered by the place. They look maintained by it, the way it is maintained by them — kept open, kept narrow, kept warm enough, by continuous attention. Nothing about them is finished either.
