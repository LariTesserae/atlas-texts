At first you do not see them.

You see the town breathing, the leaf-roofs shifting a fraction in the wind off the rim, water ticking down through the grafted streets — and then, where you had taken a thicker shadow for a trunk, or a bundle of felted fiber for stored thatch under an arch, something turns its face.

They are built for being overlooked. Short — not one of them, grown, would come much above your chest if they stood up straight, which they do not — and broad through the back and shoulders from a lifetime of moving hunched under the low canopy. They walk bent at the hip and knee, head thrust a little forward, as the trees above them grow bent. It is not deformity. It is agreement. In the sleeping terraces nothing is taller than it must be, and a spine that insisted on straightness would spend its life pushing its crown into cold, wet leaves.

Their clothing helps the camouflage, and then stops being clothing at all when you look longer. Coats, hoods, leggings, wraps for the hands in the worst frost: all of it is felted plant fiber, pounded and matted until it is dense as hide, black-green, perpetually damp. Moss has colonized it. Not decorated it — colonized. Fine green filaments root in the felt wherever it stays moist and shadowed: across the shoulders, down the outer arms, in the folds behind the knees. In the brief warm hour the moss on a living back is perceptibly brighter than the moss on a hanging curtain. People carry a faint geography of themselves, greener where they are warmest.

Under it, they are paler than you would expect and darker than you would expect, in patches. Faces, throats, the insides of the arms have a bloodless, blue-white cast, skin that has spent generations under sideways light that never quite arrives. Their hands are stained to the wrist, permanently, green-black under the nails and in the knuckle creases, the stain sinking deeper with age until an old person's fingers look dipped in ink made of leaves. Their forearms are scored with thousands of fine, pale scars — thorn, bark-edge, the snap of a frozen stem — most too shallow to remember.

Their nails do much of their work. Kept thick, untrimmed at the edges, grown into tools. So do their teeth. You will see a woman stop in a root-street, consider a shoot that has quested overnight across the path at ankle height, and simply bend and bite it through, chewing the bitter end a moment before she spits it into a wall-pocket to discourage regrowth. Children learn which greens can be bitten and which will bite back long before they learn any other distinction. The sap here is loaded with antifreeze sugars and resins, turpentine and salt on the tongue, and it gets into the people the way it gets into everything else that eats in the bowl. Their sweat, on the rare occasions when exertion in the warm hour produces it, has that same faint chemical tang. Their blood must run sweet and strange and slow to keep from locking in their veins on the nights when the whole canopy goes rigid at once.

They are adapted to thin air by taking it slowly. Big, deep chests under the felt, but the breath itself is economical — a long, shallow draw, a longer pause, mist held and then released in a cloud that hangs because the air is already saturated and has nowhere to put it. At rest they can seem not to be breathing at all. This is partly why the town is so quiet. It is not silence as rule. It is silence as physiology. The canopy eats sound, yes, but they also do not produce much to eat. They speak low and close, mouth near ear or near bark, and a great deal of what passes for speech among them is touch: a hand flattened to a trunk to feel whether water is moving in it yet, knuckles drummed once against a door-vine in a rhythm that names the visitor, fingers worked gently into a sealed seam in the morning to talk a house open again. They speak to the plants in the same tone and at the same distance as they speak to each other, and it is not always obvious to an outsider which kind of address is happening.

Their eyes are made for the bowl's blue dimness. Large, dark, wet-looking, with a shine that catches the phosphorescent fungus in a wall-niche and throws it back at you at night like an animal's — except there are almost no animals, so the comparison fails in the hearing. True daylight, if it ever got over the eastern ridge unfiltered, would probably hurt them the way it hurts the bruise-colored flowers that only open in the gloom. Down in the valleys thousands of feet below, on the rare clear days when they go up through the frozen moss curtain to the rim to scrape lichen from the true mountain rock, they squint out at the soft green distance with no longing that you can read. Warmth at that scale is a rumor. Heat would be a kind of drowning.

The cold shapes the day, and the day is short. In the frozen hours they are as still as the town. Families sleep on the upper terraces in the domed cells, vine screens grown shut over them, bodies packed side to side and head to foot under moss-felt, sharing the only reliable heat there is besides the compost pits sunk in the floors — slow, vegetal warmth, the heat of rot carefully encouraged in one precise place so the roofs drip all night with condensation and nobody quite freezes. Their sleep is deep in a way that looks, from outside, almost mineral. Heartbeat dragged down. Extremities gone white and then, unhurriedly, not white. When the frost takes the canopy and every leaf goes rigid and the town makes its faint scattered crackling — a hundred small joints settling as water turns and holds and does not burst — some of that sound is theirs.

Then the dim light comes sideways again and the warm hour begins, warm meaning only that water moves instead of locking, and everything that was waiting resumes. This is when they are unmistakably alive and the labor starts, which is to say it never starts because it never stopped; it only froze in place.

Everyone is a gardener of habitation. That is not a caste, it is a condition. A doorway left alone for a week heals shut. A street untended for a month narrows by a foot on each side. So people keep their homes by touching them, daily, the way you would keep a fire by feeding it — except inverted: not adding, but persuading away. Pruning with nails and teeth and smooth stones. Packing a bitter leaf against a shoot to turn it. Introducing a thread of the right fungus to soften a wall that has thickened too rudely inward during the night. Sleeping with a shoulder deliberately against a young enclosure so that, growing, it learns the hollow of a body and holds it. Children are born into rooms that have been coaxed open in anticipation; as the child grows, so — if the family is attentive and the plant is willing — does the room.

Districts tilt this common labor into kinds of people.

Down in the wet quarter, where the reed-houses stand on their own roots in the black shallows and everything drips without pause, live the water-keepers, frog-pale, their felt rotted to a softer, stringier texture that never even approaches dryness, algae filming the backs of their hands in green lace that they do not bother to scrape away. They move through marsh on feet so broad and splayed, toes so long and prehensile, that the root-mat barely dimples under them. They tend the aqueduct reeds, feeling down the hollow stems for blockages, listening with a cheek to the joint for the slow green pulse of meltwater; they keep the exit crack in the rock clear of ice and silt so the stream can leave, because if the bowl ever truly flooded it would rot from the bottom up. Something amphibious turns over sometimes in the deep fan near their houses and does not surface. They are not startled by it. They may be its tenders, too. Nobody explains.

Uphill, in the market knot under the oldest tree, where five root-streets collide beneath a ceiling that is less canopy than architecture, live the keepers of stores and memory — for here the two are the same. The niches grown at hand-height in the trunks hold seeds in their pods, dried fungi, fiber bundles, cakes of salt-white lichen, black berries sealed in resin. The people who tend them have the deepest-stained hands in the town and the longest memories, because memory here is not written; it is kept alive, which is harder. A lineage is a graft-line. A family's claim to a terrace can be traced in a trunk where, generation by generation, cuttings from their first house-plant were introduced and took. The market-keepers know which pods are whose, which fungus may be introduced to which wall, which seed must under no circumstances be allowed to germinate in the bowl no matter how useful it would be, because once it rooted the town would spend a century arguing with it. Trade, when it happens, is done at breath-distance over a bench grown in a ring, and consists mostly of moving living things from one set of hands to another with instructions murmured against the receiving wrist. Nothing is shouted. Nothing needs to be.

Higher, on the sunward terraces, the population is densest and youngest. This is where the sleeping cells crowd row above row and the vines must be talked open each morning by hands still clumsy with cold. Families here have a particular look: adults with babies bound flat against the chest inside the moss-felt, so that two faces peer from one hood, the infant's tiny coat already acquiring its first green film from the parent's warmth and exhaled mist. Toddlers move through the root-streets with the terrifying competence of small animals in a burrow, knowing by foot which bridge-roots will flex and which are still too young to bear weight. Old people drift uphill over a lifetime, too, but in a different sense: the very old tend to migrate toward whatever house has grown thickest around them, sitting longer each day in one place, intervening less with nail and tooth, letting the enclosure make its suggestions. A grandmother may spend her last winter in a chair-shaped swelling she has been encouraging for a decade, her felt merging visually with the wall behind her, moss bridging the small gap between shoulder and bark. When she dies — quietly, in the frozen hours, the heartbeat simply not resuming its climb with the thaw — there is often very little to move. The compost pits receive what the plant does not. Residue and root-hair interpenetrate. Within a season the room has remodeled itself around an absence shaped exactly like the person who kept it open, and then, slowly, forgotten the shape.

They eat what the town sheds and what can be taken without triggering its ambitions: berries that ripen black and hot-tasting on the tongue despite never having known heat, inner bark peeled in ribbons from stems already slated for discouragement, the hearts of reed stems, lichen from the north faces at the rim. Meals are raw or fermented or merely thawed against a body. There is no fire. There never has been, or none remembers it, and smoke rising from the bowl is as unimaginable as a metal nail. Fermentation pits and compost warmth and the brief, patient application of cupped hands do all the cooking that gets done. Food is eaten where it is found, mostly, in the course of other work, so that a meal is less an event than a continuous low-level grazing interrupted by sleep.

What conflict exists among them takes the form of pruning disputes: whose street, whose shoot, whether this bridge-root should be encouraged across the stream now or turned aside for another generation, whether a house that has healed shut over a family gone too long to the wet quarter should be talked open again or allowed to complete its return to tree. These are settled slowly, at bench-distance, sometimes over days of the warm hour, by people who understand that any decision made here will still be growing, and therefore still arguable, in fifty years. They are patient not as a virtue but as a timescale. They live long by lowland reckoning — metabolisms throttled by cold and thin air add up — though they do not count years, there being nothing that reliably happens once a year except the frost, which happens every night. Age is measured in depth of hand-stain, in thickness of nail, in how thoroughly the moss has taken to your back.

And at the edges of their attention, always, is the sense that they are not the town's masters and were never its builders. They arrived — in their own telling, if you can get them to tell, which involves sitting still long enough that a nearby shoot visibly changes position — the way everything else in the bowl arrived: as something the hollow caught. A seed on a wind. A cutting in a crack. Whether their ancestors climbed up from the warm valleys or were once a different people who learned the bowl's terms on pain of freezing is a question they regard with the mild incomprehension of plants asked about soil chemistry. They are here. The town is here. Each keeps the other from becoming something simpler: the people keep the green from sealing itself into trackless forest, and the green keeps the people from the wind, the rock teeth, the thin murderous air outside the rim.

Leave through the last grafted arch, under the icicles that grow down from living wood, pushing through the frozen threads of the moss curtain onto bare fresh rock — and look back. It is very hard, from there, to pick out a single one of them. A darker felt among dark trunks. A face turned, briefly, toward the cold you have carried out with you. Then the warm-hour steaming rises off ten thousand leaves, the bowl exhales, and the inhabitants are subsumed again into the one dark organism that fills the hollow rim to rim — divided into rooms, streets, markets, marsh — tending itself awake each dim morning with teeth and fingernails, and freezing each night, joint by joint, without bursting, to wait for release.
