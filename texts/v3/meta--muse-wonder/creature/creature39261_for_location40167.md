You hear them before you see them, too — or rather, you don't.

Out in the valley proper there is no carrying human voice. The rain and the pumps and the rivers have taken that register for themselves. What you get instead, as figures come looming out of the murk on a rerouted light-line, is a closeness. Two hoods touching, visors almost fogged together. A gloved hand flat on another person's shoulderblade to steady them while the substrate settles underfoot. A line of amber text unrolling, too fast to read, across the wet inside of a shield. Then they separate and go on, single file where the moss will hold, without looking back.

That is how they live: brief, and close.

From a distance they are indistinguishable — and for the first fifteen minutes up from the landing stages, you will take them all for transient crews. Oilskin-colored outer shells in patched blacks and yellows, hoods cinched to a circle of face, head-lamps kept low and amber, packs riding high because everything below the waist will be wet by noon. Boots that do not so much step as test, the soles buzzing faintly against the ankle bone when the ground underneath a green mat is soup. Everyone moves uphill instinctively when the pump note deepens under the valley floor, stepping onto tussock, root, printed curb, without breaking stride or conversation, the way a city person steps off a curb.

They are not transient. Or not only. The alder and willow were planted here as nurse trees, fast and nitrogen-fixing, to hold a place until something slower could live in it. The people have turned out to be the same species.

The first of them were pump engineers, river modelers, swarm shepherds, print-rig operators, biologists with engineered sedge plugs in cold boxes — people who came for a three-month rotation to coax a habitable plumbing system out of an estuary that had lifted itself out of the sea. Then the pods were linked up. Then the store. The chapel, the schoolroom, the laundry vents steaming into the rain. Then children, who have never known a map that lasted past breakfast.

By now you can tell the valley-born at a glance, once you know how to look under the shells.

They are shorter in the leg and broader through the calf than their off-valley parents, from a childhood spent pushing through head-high horsetail and moss that swallows you to the ankle with every step. They walk a little splayed, weight continually shifting, reading softness through their feet while their eyes are busy elsewhere — on the geese, on whether the drones are flying in working lines or clumped under the eaves like swallows, on the color of moss: old acid-yellow where it is safe to trust for another hour, pale frost-fuzz where the soil machines printed it last week and it will still take your weight and swallow it. They never stand squarely. Even indoors, even asleep, the body keeps a listening lean.

Their hands are what give them away when the gloves come off in a pod. Nails kept almost to the quick, cuticles black-green with biofilm that no scrubbing quite lifts, fingertips permanently wrinkled as if just out of a bath. Between thumb and forefinger, where they pull sedge plugs and polymer stitching through slump-mesh, the skin thickens into a pale callus. Behind the jaw, where the bone-conduction tabs sit day after day, there is a small, hairless pressure mark, faintly scarred in the older workers who wore the early models too tight. They touch that spot when they are thinking, the way other people touch a mouth.

They have not gone deaf, exactly — the tabs and hoods see to that — but they have gone economical with sound. Speech out of doors is saved for inches-away intimacy or emergency. Everything else is text: short lit phrases that have stripped themselves down over fifteen loud years into a grammar you could carve on a curb. *Mat lifting E.* *Pump 3 up. Step up.* *Glove yrs? in new channel.* Even jokes travel as text, and hit harder for the quiet around them. Indoors, where the pod walls deaden the rain to a needling and the pumps to a tremor in the floor, their actual voices come as a surprise: hoarse-soft, low, used sparingly, with long comfortable pauses filled by frog noise. Children whisper even in the schoolroom, leaning forehead-to-forehead to be heard clearly, and grow up associating being understood with being physically near. Arguments, courtships, grief — all happen at that distance or not at all. It has made them a people startlingly tender with their hands, forever adjusting a strap, righting a child on a curb as water commits to a new course, holding an elder's elbow while still scanning the moss.

They work the way the valley works, in pulses. There are pump-keepers who can name a surge three valleys away by the pitch at Hjalli Flats and who sleep, on shift, with one boot on. Print-riders who waddle with the six-legged extruders, shouting at plumbing that happens to be laying a levee, re-teaching a bridge to go porous for fish fry between breakfast and lunch. Swarm-hands, often the youngest, who stand up to their shins in a brown new channel retrieving whatever it has taken this time — a builder drone still trying to swim, a spruce-needle dam, somebody's laundry — with the glittering tiller-swarms flowing around their boots and quite ignoring them. Medics whose main practice is cracked basalt cuts, trench foot that never quite dries, the mild, chronic ringing that lives in the teeth of anyone who has been here more than a winter. Everybody, whatever their ticket says, learns moss work. Everybody can stitch a bank.

They eat hot, salty, and standing up as often as not: geothermal-pot potatoes, fish that passed through a bridge that was told to let them, goose from the flocks that mutter on the warm sedge meadows, greens cut straight out of a ditch-side tangle that was bare steaming mud two seasons ago. Off-valley food arrives weather permitting and is regarded as charming and insufficient, too dry. Meals happen in the linked pods around low tables, knees up, shells steaming on racks over the black hoses, the whole structure booming now and then when wind throws debris at it. Someone is always listening mid-bite, head cocked not to the talk but through it. When the note changes, the room stands as one animal and moves its baskets to higher shelves.

Aging here does not retire a person upward to the drier terraces so much as inward, to the modules that tremble least: keeping spawn-puddles, mending tabs, telling children which waterfall lips are still un-eroded and therefore treacherous. The old off-valley hands are the ones with the early hearing damage, signing more than speaking, their text phrases slowest and most complete. The young roll theirs out while already moving.

The children are the truest natives. They are rocked to true sleep — not endured, but genuinely rocked — by a noise-level that would hospitalize a city baby: pumps thrumming up through the pod floor into the cot frame, rain on tin, frogs peeping so densely it stops being individual frogs and becomes weather, a river trying out a thought in the dark and the builders printing it back into place before dawn. They learn colors first as safety: green line, red line, amber lamp, black pool. They learn not to mourn routes. A path is not where you went yesterday; it is where the light says you may go this morning. In consequence they are preternaturally unattached to possessions that cannot float or be dissolved on purpose, and preternaturally attached to people, whom they collect in the small, warm, linked rooms and count, unobtrusively, again and again.

No one here talks about taming the valley. That language belonged to the first press releases on the mainland. They talk about keeping up, about staying ahead of lunch, about a piece of ground doing well this month. If you ask them if the technology is miraculous — the ground that remembers instructions, the bridge that softens and is re-eaten — they will shrug inside their shells and look faintly embarrassed for you, the way a plumber would if you marvelled at a tap. It breaks. You shout at it. You fix it while standing in what it was supposed to hold back.

At last light — which in mid-afternoon in winter already means the amber masts pricking through blue-black, the pod windows small and gold, the surf still an idea in your teeth — they come in off the reroutes in twos and threes, still not talking, still touching shoulders as they pass. They peel off shells that release a day's condensed water down their own backs. They dry their tabs. They eat close together while the text scrolls lazily and then stops, because indoors, in the warm, they can finally afford voices, and even then they keep them down, saving them, leaning in.

Outside, the deer pick between the machines. The geese settle or don't. The builders keep their patient, ant-like print through the dark, and the people who live here sleep inside all of it, not in spite of the roar but nested in the middle of it, one foot, even in dreams, already feeling for higher ground.
