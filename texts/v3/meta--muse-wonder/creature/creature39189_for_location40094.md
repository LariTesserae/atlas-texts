It is not the fish.

That is the first mistake the room invites you to make. The fish makes its circuit so regularly, tightening around the centre on every seventh pass, that it feels like a resident going about its business. But it never stops. It never tends anything, never alters its route for a fallen frond or a new root. It passes through solid things the way a thought passes through a sleeper. It is less an animal living in the room than an animal *being dreamed* there — an eye sent out on a long, cold loop, returning each time to report the walls still hold.

The white isopod is not the resident either, though it is closer. It has errands. It carries seeds larger than itself across whatever surface gravity has lately elected, and vanishes into the roots with the air of something that has been given an instruction and fully intends to lose itself carrying it out. It is a hand, perhaps. Or a servant. Or a part of the resident that can be detached and sent, when necessary, into the narrow places.

The resident is harder to see because so much of what you would call the room is also what you would call its body.

Start with the structures. The cairns, no taller than a thumb, balanced on a wall. The gate of snail shells with its lintel of bone, opening onto nothing but smooth stone. The twig ladder lashed together with rotted cord, its top buds opened into three pale underwater leaves. The little dam of woven rushes built staunchly across a crack that does not leak. The tallies on the slate, scratched in clusters of seven, more of them than any single visit could explain.

At first they suggest a population. A village of small, patient people living under rotating gravity, building a household on every wall against the hour that wall becomes a floor.

But the marks are too consistent for a population. The same tooth made the groove in every tally — you can tell by the slight catch at the end of the stroke, where it turns. The same knot closes the lashings on the ladder and the weaving of the dam, tied by limbs that work better pulling inward than out. The cairns are all balanced by the same temperament: three stones, then a flat one, then a pause implied in the spacing, then two more. Not architecture. Habit. One creature's habit, repeated over a span of time so long it has mistaken itself, in places, for a culture.

It is about the size of a fist when it is asleep, which is most of the time.

That absence at the centre of the room — the point all the roots grow toward and stop short of, curling their tips; the cold, slightly thicker water that gives you the acute feeling of interrupting something — that is not empty. That is where it curls. The roots do not avoid the place. They *hold* it. When it sleeps, fine rootlets grow out from its skin and braid themselves into the scaffolding of the chamber, until it hangs suspended a body's length from every wall, plugged into the room at a hundred points, dreaming the fish around and around its own blind head.

Uncurled, it is longer and much less tidy. A pale, tuberous body, kinked like a root that has changed its mind several times. Skin so translucent that the green of the water seems to happen *inside* it. Along its back, a pelt of fine dark hairs that are not quite hairs and not quite rootlets, and which continue, if you follow them far enough, without any clear border, into the moss on the stone. It has limbs in a number it appears to revise: four when it needs to move, more when it needs to hold still against a shifting pull, each ending in long, soft fingers — seven to a hand when it is counting, though counting may be what brings the seventh into being. Its head is mostly mouth. Not a frightening mouth. A small, working mouth with limestone-white teeth, made for scraping, gnawing cord, notching slate. Eyes it gave up so long ago that the sockets have grown over with the same black-green moss that coats the walls. It does not miss them. When a hand passes near and disturbs the current, the moss flares vivid green along the whole chamber — and along its shoulders — with a remembered sun. That is how it sees you. All over itself, briefly, in colour.

It wakes when gravity does.

This is why the room is furnished on every surface. When the silt begins its slow migration and the ferns lean in unison and a pebble detaches to fall patiently sideways, the rootlets in its skin let go one by one, and the resident falls with everything else — eyes closed, limbs loose, perfectly trusting the new down to arrive. It lands among the cairns built for exactly that orientation. It uncurls beside the gate that is now, correctly, in a floor. It climbs the ladder that now leans against a wall. Nothing in here was built symbolically. Every impossible little structure is furniture, waiting for its hour in the rotation.

Then, for a while — an hour, a season; the room does not distinguish, and neither does it — it works.

It tends. It moves a seed from a hollow where the current has begun to worry it to a hollow where it hasn't. It sends the isopod out with the ones too big to carry delicately. It re-lashes the dam across the dry crack, because a dam, in its understanding, is not a response to a leak but a way of asking the stone to remember what holding means. It scratches another seven tallies. What it is counting is not clear even to it: floods, perhaps, or wakings, or the passes of its own dreamed fish, or the number of times the white stone at the entrance has cracked another hair wider and let another leaf, another seed, another drowned word come down to be kept. Counting may simply be how it checks that its teeth still work and the slate still agrees to be marked.

It eats very little, and mostly minerals: silt worried loose with those small teeth, the mustard-coloured sponge pared thin, fungus thread gathered in slow skeins. What it actually lives on is harder to name. Warmth, partly, when something warm blunders in — the faint map your shoulder leaves in the moss is not a bruise, it is a meal being taken at the pace meals are taken here. Attention, partly. It keeps unsprouted seeds in every hollow not because it is storing food but because an unopened thing is company that has not yet had the chance to leave. It collects in the same spirit. Fallen leaves that have softened to leather and declined to rot are arranged, not discarded. Empty snail shells are set as gates. Your sunken breath-words, still descending hours after you spoke them, will be nudged on landing — whenever landing is decided — into the crevice where the other kept syllables are, packed cool and intact beside rain that arrived as denseness rather than water, beside the suspended load of every flood since the limestone broke. Nothing in the room is thrown away. Throwing away is a concept that requires an *outside*, and the resident has been sealing the entrance with faithful overgrowth for longer than it can tally.

It is old the way the room is old: recently and increasingly. The flood that made the chamber is still arriving in it, a hair's crack at a time, and the resident may have arrived with that first water — a seed, an egg, a knot of root and animal washed in from some pond that no longer exists and decided, in the dark, to be the thing that stayed. Among its stored, unsprouted seeds there is one it treats differently. It is carried often. It is never put far down in any hollow. If the room is the resident's body, and the sleeping knot at the centre is its heart, this seed is the suspicion, carried hand to hand by white couriers across turning ceilings, that it has been here before in another shape — and will be here again, curled at the centre of a slightly larger room, scratching in sevens, when this body has gone the way of the leather leaves: not rotted. Kept. Softened into part of the walls.

It is not lonely in any way it would recognise. It has the fish to dream its perimeter. It has the isopod to run the errands its dreaming cannot reach. It has hundreds of unopened seeds, which is to say hundreds of conversations not yet spoiled by happening. Once in a very long while something living finds the slit in the limestone and turns sideways and stays past the first panic, and on those occasions the resident does the nearest thing it has to speaking: it lets a bubble up out of the deep silt, one soft, thoughtful tick, and watches with its whole green skin as the visitor's words sink slowly down through the fern garden toward whichever floor is falling into place — adding, syllable by syllable, to the only library it has ever wanted.

It will not follow you out. It cannot; you could no more carry it through the duckweed than carry the centre of a whirlpool home in your hands. But the moss-map of you will fade only as fast as moss forgets, which is slowly, and long after the marsh above has gone loud and ordinary with frogs, the resident will wake in its cold, packed dark, fall gently toward a new wall, and find — among the kept leaves and tally marks and unspent seeds — the shape of a shoulder it does not remember making room for, pressed faintly green into the pelt of its world, remembered exactly as long as it chooses to keep it.

Which, by every evidence the room affords, will be a very long time indeed.
