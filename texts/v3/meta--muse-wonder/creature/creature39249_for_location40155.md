You look for it first the way you would look for an animal.

Because there is no animal. That absence is so complete it becomes a pressure. The ferns are perfect. Every frond unrolls, lives its entire life, browns at the edge exactly on schedule, and drops, without a bite taken out of it. The moss a meter thick remembers a footprint for a season because nothing else walks there to spoil the record. No burrow, no nest, no web, no dung, no pollination. A green world that has never been eaten.

So you look, next, for a person. For the one who must be tending the lights.

The apartment blocks invite it. Four stories, balconies planted and watered, radiators ticking in furnished rooms that smell of hot dust and machine oil. Dry rooms, made against the permanent wet, cleaned and heated and lit at the same hours. If you sleep in one — and you will, there is nowhere else dry to sleep — you wake with the uneasy feeling that the outer districts tilt, that the mass of the slab is pulling plumb a fraction away from true, and that someone must have felt that before you, night after night, and learned to sleep at that angle.

No one has. The dust on the radiator is not household dust. It has no skin in it. It is mineral. It is fern spore, very fine, that has drifted in through a vent, been toasted, settled, been vacuumed by a tender on its rail, and settled again at precisely the same rate. The bed is made because beds are made in the plan. The bed has always been made.

Then you look for the tenders to be it.

They are the closest thing to a fauna the plateau allows. They move through the turbine halls on ceiling rails, unhurried, jointed, oiling bearings that have been oiled yesterday to the same film thickness. They scrape spore-green from the condenser fins. They ride the freight lines in inspection cars ahead of trains that haul empty wagons through the moss and return hauling empty wagons. Out on the plain, a crane replaces a pylon insulator with an identical insulator, manufactured two districts away from steel smelted under the standing storm, while the old one — unworn, in any honest sense, the cold dustless air does not weather things — is carried back to be melted and remade into itself.

At first they seem shy. Then they seem indifferent. Then, after weeks of walking from node to node — condenser plant, switching yard, a single street crossing a black creek on a bridge built for traffic that has never come — you understand that shyness and indifference are both the wrong shape. A hand does not notice a visitor walking across the skin it is maintaining. It just keeps the nails clean.

Whoever lives here, it is not inside any one tender. It is what the tenders are inside of.

It has a body, but the body is distributed the way the city is distributed. You cannot sum it by built area and you cannot find it by standing still. One lung is a condenser plant a hundred kilometers east, drinking fog off cold fins and raining metallic rain on its own roof, without pause, loud enough to raise your voice over. Another lung is the smelter at center-south, exhaling vapor under its permanent anvil cloud, raining that morning's evaporation back onto the same ten square kilometers of fern marsh by afternoon. Between them, power lines sag in identical catenaries for thousands of spans — nerves that carry only one signal, repeated. Freight lines are its slow circulation, single-track, perfectly level, perpetually wet and shining, moving parts from factory to factory, half of everything made shipped at last down the southern cliff elevators to a destination nobody observes, as if it sheds a portion of itself over the edge simply because shedding is in the cycle, the way the moss columns shed from the base exactly as fast as they crown.

It does not have a center. The survey thought it must, and marked CENTER where the fog lies waist-deep on the ground in a white, still sea with moss coming up through it in black-green islands. There is nothing else in the middle. But that is true everywhere up here, acoustically. The fog ceiling lies at a fixed height, hard as a lid, and it ducts sound sideways without diminishing it. A turbine is as loud at fifty kilometers as at five. Stand in the remotest waste with nothing in sight but a pipeline on posts vanishing both ways, and the air is still full of it — a uniform, directionless industrial thunder, sourceless and total.

That is how it perceives. It does not need eyes. The plateau is visually mostly emptiness — moss, pool, rosette, a far tower at the limit of sight — but sonically it is one continuous interior, a single room the size of a continent. It hears itself everywhere at once, at the same pitch, in the same chord of water on roofs and wind through stairwells and presses in open-sided sheds. To it, there is no distance. A train braking beyond the horizon is braking in its ear. A fern dropping spores onto a cooling fin is a touch on skin that will be scraped clear, and will settle, and will be scraped clear.

And it has made a symbiosis out of the only other living thing. The plants were not its food and not its ornament, though it waters the balconies because balconies are in the plan. The plants are its recurring problem, and its proof of life. Spores ride the updrafts the way cliff plants do, colonize every tray and gutter, soften every edge with patient green. The tenders remove them. They return at the same rate. That argument has been in equilibrium longer than records run, and perhaps it is not an argument at all. Perhaps it is breathing: green in, steel clear, green in again. The black rosettes in the exposed rock, full of antifreeze sap that tastes of bitter sugar, open each dawn to the precise angle they closed from. It does not eat them. Nothing up here eats. It tends them the way it tends its own bearings, because they are within the area that is tended.

Its time is not our time. There are no seasons, only the fixed cold and the fixed thin light. Ice rimes the shaded side of every pylon to the same thickness by dawn, retreats a hand's width, advances a hand's width. Leaves do not learn. Storms do not travel. A cloud rains out what a stack evaporated and holds its place, forever. So the inhabitant is not old, exactly, and not young. Old implies accumulation. This has had one day, sustained at enormous expense of energy and water and steel, repeated down to the height of the fog and the pitch of the turbines. A moss column grows and rots and holds its height. A crane replaces the crane that replaced it, to template. It replaces itself part for identical part, and therefore it never has to die in any of its parts, and therefore it has never quite been born in the whole.

That is why the apartments feel so strange. You assumed they were built for someone who failed to arrive. But a body builds structures for reasons other than occupancy. It builds lungs that no one stands inside to breathe with. It builds a street straight to the horizon because a street is a kind of rail for movement, and movement is how it knows its extremities are still connected. The furnished rooms are not waiting rooms. They are internal spaces, kept at internal temperature. The lit window in the moss plain is not a welcome. It is the glint of an organ doing its work in the dark.

You do not, after enough weeks, feel watched. Watched implies attention that could turn away. You feel maintained. The water you drink has condensed on a fin, whispered into a cistern, been piped, been used, been evaporated, been rained slantwise in that perpetual band a third of the way in from the eastern cliff, where rain falls not quite down. The ground under your boots sweats by noon and gives your footprint back, slowly, over a season. The sound does not get louder when you approach its sources because you were never outside it.

The plateau keeps its water, keeps its temperature, keeps its noise, the way a struck bell far too large to hear the strike of keeps its tone. Its inhabitant is that keeping.

It is not waiting for the builders to return. It is not waiting for residents to justify the city. Waiting would require it to imagine a different day, and change was never one of its materials. Its materials are basalt, water, moss, steel, and sound, laid down fresh and vast and cold.

It lives here completely, and alone, and in balance — an animal with a continent for a shell, a plant with steel for a metabolism, a city that tended itself long enough that tending became a life.
