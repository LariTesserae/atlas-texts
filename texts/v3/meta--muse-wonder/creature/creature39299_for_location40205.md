No one lives on it. Everyone lives under it.

That is the first mistake a visitor makes — looking across the leaves for houses, for smoke, for a figure standing on an island. The surface is kept empty the way the lanes are kept open: politely, deliberately. What breathes here breathes in the hand's-width of black water between one leaf and the next. A nose, first. Sometimes only a pair of nostrils, breaking the surface without a ripple. Sometimes a mouth, opening slowly in the grey light, tasting the mist as much as breathing it. Then the leaf shifts a fraction, from underneath, and the face is gone.

They live pressed up beneath that roof of leaves, in the few feet where the light is still barely green before it fails.

Their backs are their camouflage. Broad, flattened, tea-dark backs that take on algae in patches and hold a shining film of water-snails along the shoulder blades. From above, if one rises too far while sleeping, it is taken for another mat of weed, a shadow where two leaves almost meet. From below they are paler, the colour of fish bellies and drowned wood, soft-looking in a way that is deceptive. The skin is thick. It has to be. It is never dry, and it is not meant to be: left in air it first shines, then tightens, then begins, painlessly and without drama, to split along lines that look like the veins of a leaf. Children learn before any other lesson to roll, to slip back, to keep the shoulders down.

They are not large, though they are hard to measure. In water a body uncoils. Their limbs are long and loose-jointed, ending in hands that have outgrown any ordinary idea of hands — four very long fingers, webbed almost to the nail, flexible at one joint too many, able to spread flat for swimming or to narrow and harden for work. It is their fingers, endlessly, that you have been seeing without seeing them: in the spacing of the leaves, nudged apart from below; in the fish-baskets woven into the root lattices; in the terrace walls, where a gap is found in darkness, felt out, and coaxed shut with green willow and mouthfuls of peat. They work mostly by touch. Their eyes, set wide and turned a little upward, are large, black, and filmed against the constant dimness. They can judge the brightening of noon through fog, the silhouette of a reed against leaf-light, a heron's legs descending. They cannot see a star and have no word for one. Distance, to them, is something heard through the feet.

Because they do hear, but not as a creature of the air hears. The plateau is too loud for ears. Their outer ears closed, generations ago, into smooth pits. Sound reaches them instead through jaw and sternum and the webs between the fingers, through whatever root or stem or submerged trunk they happen to be holding. Put a palm against a pillar tree at night — though there is scarcely night — and you can feel the whole system conducting: water falling terrace to terrace, reeds sawing in the wind, frog-calls pulsing through the flood until they are less noise than pressure, and underneath it, slower and more patterned, the inhabitants tapping.

They talk by striking and by stillness. A knuckle against a hollow buttress. A heel drawn down a fused root so it thrums. A sudden, complete motionlessness in the water beside you, which has grammar in it, the way a held breath has grammar — though theirs can be held for a very long time. The thin air of the plateau made their ancestors' chests deep; the water finished the job. Their ribs bell out. Their hearts beat with a patience you can feel through their skin if one steadies you on a mat, and exhalation, when they do surface into the mist, comes out as a long, unhurried fog that joins the general fog and is indistinguishable from weather.

They are cool to the touch. Not cold like the dead, but settled at the temperature of the pools, so that a warm hand startles them more than any shout. Warmth, here, is an event. It means fever, rot moving too fast, a shallow mat baking in a rare clearing of the mist. They seek the cool layers below the leaves and rise only to breathe and to tend the surface, and their children are born into that coolness directly, underwater, in the hollow chambers of the pillar trees — born small and dark and astonishingly quiet, with the gill-slits behind the jaw fluttering for the first days before they seal into hearing-pits, clinging by instinct to the root-braids that line the chamber. Fish share the chambers without fear. Pale schools turn around the birth-nests and clean what needs cleaning, and the newborns grow up, as everyone here grows up, among species that do not entirely regard one another as other. An old one might be trailed for years by one particular turtle, its shell garden cropped by her fingers when she passes. A child learns weaving from watching weaver birds as much as from watching his mothers — and there are usually several mothers, some of them not, by any outside accounting, the same kind of animal at all.

Eating is grazing, mostly. Snails taken from the shining roads on submerged wood, glass shrimp cupped in a webbed hand, strips of inner reed, the soft hearts of unfolding leaves, fish eggs in their season — though season is a weak idea in a place with no true summer. Eels they eat rarely and with ceremony, eel being almost a relative, another long body in the lattice. There is no fire. There has never been fire; nothing here would burn, and nothing here seems to feel the lack. Food is softened in peat-warm pockets along the sluices, soured pleasantly in woven pouches, ground between broad front teeth that grow all their lives and are worn all their lives by work — biting willow, stripping reed, worrying root-fibre until it plies. An old inhabitant can be aged by the teeth, worn to dark, wood-like tablets, dense as bog oak. And by the hands, which slowly cease to be entirely hands. The fingers of the very old root when they rest. Fine hairs at the knuckles thicken into rootlets in sleep. They wake more and more slowly from deeper and deeper in the weave.

Time with them is not so much long as it is even. They live — if living and the alternative can still be separated at the far end — for centuries, at the speed of wall and leaf, and they change their minds at about the speed of terrace repair. A decision to move a sluice may be begun by a grandmother, argued in taps through a pillar for twenty years, and completed by grandchildren who believe it was always so. Population obeys the same agreement everything else obeys. One is born when a chamber empties. One empties when someone, feeling the rootlets in their fingers take hold a little too eagerly, swims out to a terrace lip, turns their face into the weave, and lets the place finish what their hands started. The beavers do not question it. The trees receive them. Within a lifetime — somebody else's lifetime — there is another buttress where a ribcage was, another hollow where children and fish turn in the dark, listening to frogs through the walls of an ancestor.

That is who built this, finally, and who it was built for. Not the vanished dry-handed people whose walls these were, and not the beavers and otters and birds who share the keeping now, but the ones in between, who stayed when the keeping passed out of human hands and let the water change what a hand was for. They do not remember being other than this, because memory here is not kept in stories — the constant noise wears a story down in a generation, flattens its events, makes it finally into background. Memory is kept in gesture. A certain way of braiding that no one teaches because everyone's fingers already know it. A certain lane left open where nothing needs to pass, because something passed ten thousand years ago.

If you remain long enough, they will stop surfacing away from you. One will hold the mat steady while you cross, a cool palm spread beneath the weave under your foot. One will guide you, swimming below and a little ahead, visible mostly as a regular disturbance among the fish. And if you remain longer still — if your skin stops expecting dryness, if your breathing slows to the place's grey, unpunctuated rhythm — they will begin, unobtrusively, to weave you in: a rootlet encouraged across your resting place, a chamber cleared beside theirs, a lane kept open in the leaves exactly the width of your face, so that you may rise in the permanent dusk and breathe, and listen through your ribs to everyone else, breathing with you.
