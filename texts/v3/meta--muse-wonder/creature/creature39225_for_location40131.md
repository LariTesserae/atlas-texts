First you feel it. 

Not through the air — the air is useless, it is already full — but through the soles of your feet, through the spring and drum of the mat. A pattern. Not a herd yet, not even movement. A pattern repeated at intervals, deliberate enough to separate itself from the random tremor of gust-fronts in the reeds: three low beats, a drag, three low beats again. Something ten kilometres away is saying where it is, and saying it only to the ground.

Then, an hour later, against the tin-cut horizon, a darkness that is not a shadow. It moves low and wide against the light, and it does not resolve, the way your eyes want it to, into legs and a body. It resolves into a *hull*.

That is the creature that lives in the open here. Or rather, that endures being open.

From a distance it looks ballasted, almost ballasted on purpose — as if the plateau had made an animal by taking weight very seriously. It is no taller at the shoulder than a man's waist, but it may be three metres long and nearly as broad across the hips, a low, wedged mass kept down out of the worst of the wind. It does not stand up into the weather. It presents the weather with a slope.

Its head is the lowest part of it, and the widest. A blunt, armoured prow, slung between its forelegs, with the eyes set not forward but far to the sides and high on the skull, under bony hoods. The eyes are small for the size of the beast, amber, almost perfectly round, and filmed with a polarising shutter that clicks down in sections against the noon glare — you can see the facets of it working, darkening in bands, until the world must be reduced for it to horizontal strips of manageable brightness. It sees movement at the edge of the reed-sea very well. It sees detail poorly. On a plateau where the horizon is ten billion leaves shivering with reflected light, detail is a liability. What it needs to know is: what is moving, and is the ground under it drumming?

It does not listen for the answer. Nothing native here does. Its ears are sealed plates of horn, smooth as river stones, with only a faint seam where an opening was, in some ancestor, begun and then abandoned. Sound reaches it — must reach it, the roar presses on everything — but it reaches it as pressure in the breastbone, as a buzzing in the teeth, as weather inside the ribs. It lives in the same deafening world you do, and it solved it long ago by simply not treating the air as a place where news could happen.

News happens underneath.

Each foot ends not in a hoof proper but in a sled: a broad, flattened runner of horn, slightly upcurled at the front, grooved beneath, spreading its considerable weight across the root-mat so it does not punch through into the red darkness below. The runner is also its ear. The whole underside is lined with a dense, naked pad, grey-pink and deeply creased, exquisitely sensitive to vibration. Standing still — and it can stand still for an hour, head down, shutters half-closed, apparently asleep in the full scream of a reed company — it is reading. Reed tremor. Fire tremor, far off, a deeper wrongness in the floor. The discharge-crackle of the black mirror-fields transmitted as a staccato prickle through the mat. Others of its kind, moving, grazing, signalling. A predator is not a shape it expects to see. A predator is a brief, wrong syncopation in the drumming of the herd.

When it moves, it moves with unexpected economy. Those runners do not lift so much as skim and settle, skim and settle, a gliding, deliberate gait that makes very little surface noise and a great deal of ground sense. A walking herd lays down a continuous braided sentence in the mat: direction, speed, number, the presence of calves, alarm and the cancelling of alarm. Individuals sign it as they go, stamping a forepad twice to mark a change in the electric fields ahead, dragging a hind runner to warn of a patch of mat thinned by fire. They have, laid down this way over generations, a whole inherited cartography you cannot see — safe crossings between discharge points, old mill-tree parishes, glades where the grazing after a burn will be sweetest in nine years' time. It is not written anywhere. It is pressed, repeatedly, into a living floor that remembers pressure the way peat remembers stems.

Close up, the body explains the climate at every point.

The coat is not fur, not exactly. It is overlapping scales of horn, each the size of a hand, each slightly cupped, growing from the shoulders backward like the slats on a mill-tree's sail-leaves. Wind passes over them with a faint, dry clatter. Reed blades — those glass-edged leaves that open your skin without seeming to try — glance off them. Fire-sparks glance off them too. Beneath the scales, in the sheltered seams, the skin is dark and oily with the plateau's resinous chemistry, so a cut seals itself almost at once with a bitter amber film and the animal barely pauses. It smells, at close range, not like an animal at all. It smells of turpentine and hot coins and peppery peat, the smell of the whole continent, perfectly worn.

It is built cold. The sun lays heat down and the wind takes it away again, and its answer is not to generate warmth wastefully in a body exposed on all sides, but to be a low, dense economy: heavy bones laced with iron and manganese taken up from the seed-shot it eats, a slow heart you can feel through the mat when it lies down — *thud, thud*, a smaller, animal version of the bellows-roots pumping around a mill-tree — and a gut that runs the length of it like a dark refinery. It eats what the mill-trees expel: the dense seed, rattled down into the storage bulbs, cracked by its flat, continuously replaced molars into a flour it digests without free water. Resin is not poison to it. Resin is solvent, medium, drink by another name. What moisture it needs it takes at dawn, kneeling awkwardly on its sled-feet in the hollows of the mat, cracking the night's frost with a slow pressure of its prow and licking the melt directly from the wax-sealed chambers of the roots before the sun can steal it. Its wastes are dry pellets, metal-rich, and the mat takes them back immediately. Even its breath is thrifty. The nostrils are vertical slits that seal against dust, pollen, seed-blizzard and spore-smoke, opening in short, timed draws on the lee side of the head. It never calls out. It has forgotten, anatomically, how to waste breath on air no one is listening to.

Yet it is not solitary, and it is not silent to itself.

Herd life is conducted at close range, by touch. You will see two of them press their armoured foreheads together in the middle of a gale, scales clicking, and stand that way, one drumming a forefoot softly against the other's runner, the exchange passing foot to skull to foot without a sound the wind needs to carry. Calves stay literally in contact with their mothers for the first season, cheek to flank, learning the ground-language as a physical dialect laid against their own bodies. A calf is born not on the surface but *in* the mat, in a chamber the herd worries open with their prows over several days — down in the muffled red interior of the root-world, where the roar is reduced to a vast surrounding pressure and the resin smell is thick as syrup. It arrives blind, earless, naked-skinned, in the only quiet place on the continent, and it lives there for its first weeks, drinking a resin-thick milk and listening with its whole unarmoured body to the pumping of the world. When its scales begin to harden and its shutters grow in over its eyes, it chews and shoulders its way up, by degrees, into brightness and noise so total that it must be a kind of second birth, survived simply because by then it already knows the only language that matters, and the language is under its feet.

It does not fear fire. When a reed-sea flash-burns — and from inside a herd the warning is not the smell or the light but a sudden, accelerating bass note in the mat, a tremor that becomes a command — it turns side-on, lowers its prow, and moves at that unhurried skim along the edge of the burn, cropping the heat-cracked seeds the flames have opened and snapping up insects flushed stunned from the mat. Its scales shed the sparks. Its shuttered eyes shed the glare. The calves travel in the lee of the adults, in a pocket of slightly slower air, reading the evacuation entirely through their feet.

It does not tend the mill-trees, and it does not worship them, and it would not recognise either idea. But its life is fitted into their parish the way a hand fits a worn tool. It eats their seed-fall. It manures their bellows-roots. Its herds, drumming the same paths for uncounted generations, keep the mat compacted in routes between tree and tree that the roots can follow underground. When it dies — usually simply by lying down, one day, in a glade, the slow heart missing a beat in the floor and then another — the scales slump, the mat sags, and the burrowing things come up from inside the world to take it down into the dark in pieces. Within a season there is only a slight, springier hollow, greener than the glade around it. Within a decade the mat may decide, in that spot, to rise.

Stand downwind of one at noon, which is to say stand where the wind has already taken its smell a hundred kilometres away, and watch it read a landscape you cannot hear. Head low. Shutters ticking against the white sky. A forepad lifting, settling, lifting again — tapping out something unhurried to a companion you haven't yet felt arriving. A creature that gave up the air entirely and put its faith in the ground, and was right: massive, patient, deaf, articulate in its feet, armoured against a forest with no shade and fed by factories with no builders. Not separate from the plateau's process, but the part of the process that learned to move — slowly, on sleds, from mill-tree to mill-tree, drumming its news into a continent that is always, always screaming back through everything except the one channel it learned to speak on.
