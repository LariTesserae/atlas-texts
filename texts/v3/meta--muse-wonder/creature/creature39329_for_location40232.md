The people of Merrow do not look like mountaineers.

That is the first surprise, after two days of climbing to reach them. You expect something lean and sharp and wind-burned, people made for peaks. Instead they are made for flatness at altitude — for standing still, at four thousand meters, in wet air, and waiting for water to arrive sideways.

They are, on average, shorter than the lowlanders who trade with them, and broader through the chest and shoulders. Not stocky, exactly. Built around the lungs. Generations born on the Planum have learned to live on thinner air without thinking about it: they breathe slower and deeper than visitors, they can work a whole shift in a roasting shed or haul a bucket train across roofs without pausing for breath, and their resting hearts are famously unhurried. Cheeks stay ruddy even in that silvered, sunless light. Hands and feet tend to be large, quick to warm, quick to redden. Children are born small and stay indoors for weeks in the steamy upper rooms, wrapped close to a parent's chest, learning the altitude before they learn the cold.

Their skin is pale in a way that has nothing to do with delicacy. It is cloud-pale. Freckles are rare, sunburn is a lowland injury they talk about with disbelief, and almost everyone over thirty has the fine, netted scarring of old chilblains on knuckles and ears. Their eyes are used to diffusion. They squint less in fog than other people do in sunlight; loud, direct noon in the valleys below hurts them, makes them shade their faces and complain, sincerely, that everything down there is too *edged* — shadows too black, sounds too clear, the sky an aggressive blue. On the plateau they can judge distance by a change in dripping. Down below, they get headaches.

They are almost always a little damp, and they have stopped regarding that as a misfortune.

A Merrower's clothes are not meant to keep water out entirely. That would be a losing, rotting proposition. They are meant to manage where the water goes. Heavy wool underneath, worn for years until it fits like a second, felted skin. Over it, oiled canvas and waxed cotton in dark greens, browns, blacks — colors that do not show moss-stain. Hoods are wide and stiffened so they shed to the shoulders, not the face. Sleeves are cut to channel runoff away from the hands when working overhead. Many people wear a shallow copper or tin gutter at the collar, a personal joke and not a joke, that funnels clean mist-water into a flask at the hip while they walk. Boots are broad-soled, soft, almost silent on wet stone and peat, laced high. Nobody wears anything that clicks if they can help it.

Their hands tell their work before they speak. Nails kept very short. Palms callused not from gripping, but from wringing — sponges, cloths, moss, laundry, nets, hour after hour. Fingers extraordinarily sensitive to angle and temperature. A condenser-keeper can run a bare hand down a finned wall in full fog, in the dark, and tell you by the pattern of beading where a seam has scaled, where lichen is starting, where the tilt has sagged a degree. Factory workers from the western sheds have faintly darkened fingertips for years after they leave the sorting floors, iron dust worked into the skin from the washed bog mud. Older people carry a permanent ache in the larger joints that is better than any barometer; the town jokes, without really joking, that you consult a grandmother before you consult the windmills.

They organize their day not around the sun, which they may not properly see for a week, but around the cloud tides.

A household wakes when its gutters begin to tick. That can be three in the morning. Children go up in the half-light with sponges and buckets, a chore so ordinary they do it arguing, half-asleep, barefoot on cold slate that they know by toe. Adults check sluices, unroll nets between chimneys, open or close the great angled planes on the roofs depending on which way the white river is crawling. When the mist is in, everyone works. When it lifts for the two pale hours after midday, the markets erupt under canvas, trams run double, laundry is carried down from the drying-lofts, repairs that need sight get done in a hurry, and people stand in lanes blinking and talking faster than usual, as if clarity itself were a kind of festival. Then the evening inundation comes, and the town packs itself away again, roof by roof.

It makes them profoundly maintenance-minded. In Merrow, wealth is not what you have built. It is what you have kept clean, angled, unclogged, and ready. Old families who own the hundred-meter condenser walls by the mills inherit not water, but a surface and the obligation to tend it. Newer cooperatives, who string nets and share roof rights, measure a good member not by strength but by reliability in fog. A lazy person is not someone who rests — everyone rests when the air is dry — a lazy person is someone whose slate was dirty when the cloud came through. That is the local form of waste. People will forgive debt, bad temper, even theft of tools more readily than they will forgive a neighbor who let good mist pass over a fouled fin, or who stripped moss from someone else's north wall and left the stone to freeze and split.

Because sound behaves treacherously in saturated air, they have become a listening culture, and a close one. They stand nearer to each other than lowlanders find comfortable, often with a hand lightly on a forearm, because a voice at arm's length on an open roof can arrive muffled and drowned, while a whistle from across town can land suddenly in your ear as if whispered by a ghost. So they speak low, and they learn early to read mouths, shoulders, hands. There is a whole small vocabulary of gestures for use in thick mist: this sluice, your roof, open, close, listen, pool rising, come down. Children ring handbells for the lammergeiers at the rim not out of excitement alone, but from training — from infancy they are taught to *announce* what cannot be reliably seen.

Noise after the evening mist is not merely rude there; it is understood as theft, because quiet is a tool. In quiet you can hear whether your cistern frog has gone silent, whether a gutter is filling evenly or spilling at a joint, whether a bog pool beyond the lane is about to overtop its hollow and send a bright thread of water through a workshop door. Merrowers will stop mid-sentence, mid-step, mid-shift, heads tilted, not listening to the silence but into it. Visitors find this unnerving. To the town it is literacy.

They eat like people who trade metal for food and grow what the cold allows in the meantime. Lowland grain and dried goods come up the long road in exchange for tin, copper, iron; almost every meal is built around something preserved, something hot, and something sharply flavored to cut the damp — bitter greens, fermented things, dwarf-willow and bog-herb teas, sour berries kept from the short summer. Roof gardens produce in tubs and sheltered beds, tended by people who coax growth toward drip-lines rather than sunlight. A good gardener in Merrow is not the one with the sunniest plot, but the one who knows which fin weeps longest after the cloud has gone. Food is cooked in the lower, warmer floors, eaten in the dwelling floors above, and the steam of it is not wasted: it rises into drying clothes, into sleeping rooms, into the moss on the inner walls. A house, like a person, is expected to drink and dry in cycles, never to seal itself completely.

Their social life is dense, because their buildings are. Three, four generations can live stacked in one of those rounded black-stone houses — workshop below, elders in the middle where it is steadiest, children near the roof where the sponges and buckets and pigeons of errands are, cistern-frogs singing faintly under everyone's feet. People know whose roof leaks by sound, whose nets are up late, whose factory cough has worsened, whose moss has sporulated early. Privacy, in Merrow, is not distance. It is tact. It is pretending not to have heard through wet stone what you certainly heard.

That closeness can make them seem reserved to strangers and intensely loyal to each other. They are slow to adopt a new technique unless it has survived a full season of cloud, quick to repair an old one that has. They distrust sharp solutions. They build around the ancient boulders not because they worship them, but because the boulders have proved, over lifetimes, that they hold cold, anchor moss, break wind in a useful place, and do not move when everything else swells and drains. At sunset you will see people — factory hands on their way home, old women with market baskets, children supposed to be elsewhere — lay a palm flat against one of those stones, absently, as they pass. Checking it. Greeting it. Taking its temperature against the evening.

They are not a dramatic people. Their virtues accumulate: patience, exactness, a tolerance for waiting that looks like idleness until the mist arrives and the whole town moves at once without needing to be told. Their vices are the same virtues soured — stubbornness about an inherited angle, feuds over a meter of north-facing wall that can last thirty years, a reflexive suspicion of anyone who talks loudly, builds high, or wants to drain a hollow permanently instead of learning its rhythm.

Leave Merrow for the lowlands and they become homesick in odd ways. For flat horizons. For weather you can hear coming for hours. For the moment at dawn when the cloud sinks below the rim and the town stands dripping above a white sea, belonging to no visible earth at all. They miss the cold that is not cruel, the light that does not insist, the feeling that height has nothing to do with rising above anyone.

At night, when the trams stop and the factories sink to a few amber windows, they are mostly invisible — which is how they prefer to be described. But they are awake in shifts behind those stone walls, listening to roofs tick and fins weep, to pools rising by fractions in the dark hollows outside. Ready, not because they conquered the plateau, but because they learned, over generations, how to keep their surfaces clean and their ears open, and to hold still, like the old stone under them, while everything wet and living moves across.
