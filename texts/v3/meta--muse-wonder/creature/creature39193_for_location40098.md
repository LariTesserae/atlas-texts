You hear them before you see them, too.

Not individual voices at first. Through the stone, below the bend in the shaft, the forty-one resolve into a single raised weather — a town permanently trying to be heard over a river that is not in the room. Then, as your eyes learn the amber, doubled light, they separate.

No one down here is dry. That is the first thing their bodies have agreed to.

Their skin is pale in a way that has nothing to do with delicacy. It is cellar-pale, lamp-pale, the color of things kept from the sun in a wet place: undersides of stones, roots that never found soil, the blind crickets in the corners. Veins show blue at the wrist and temple. Hands stay faintly pruned even at rest, the fingertips wrinkled as if everyone has just finished washing. They oil themselves — skin, hair, lips, the leather of their belts, the wood of their tools, all with the same thrifty lamp oil — so there is a soft sheen to them in the lantern light, and nothing, person or object, is ever quite matte.

They keep their hair cut close. Long hair would never dry; it would mildew against the neck. Women and men alike wear it cropped or bound up tight under cloth caps, and children go shorn until they are old enough to manage the oiling themselves. Clothes are sacking and dark wool woven on the second-tier looms, cut loose because tight cloth rots faster against damp skin, and weighted at the hem so it hangs instead of clinging. Everything smells of the town: smoke from the one brazier, iron from the drain, wet wool, oil, bodies. A newcomer from above arrives smelling shockingly separate — of bread, of dust, of open air — and within a week the distinction is gone. That is how you know the chamber has accepted them.

They are not large people, though whether the place has bred them smaller over generations or simply trained them so is impossible to say. They move stooped on the upper tiers, one shoulder habitually lower, ducking stalactite teeth and shelf-corners without looking. On the main street they stand straighter and walk with a peculiar, flat-footed certainty, placing each bare foot on the slick flags exactly where a thousand feet have dished the stone. Their soles are broad and hard as leather. Their toes grip. Toddlers learn to walk on the planks over the drain before they learn anything else worth knowing, and they learn it the way children above learn stairs — by falling, but here the fall is only ever ankle-deep and black and appalling, and everyone laughs at full volume, and the child is fished out and stripped and hung, briefly, like the washing.

Because nothing can be whispered, they have become a people of large, readable faces.

Conversation is conducted forehead almost to forehead, mouth to ear, often with a hand flat on the other person's chest or jaw to feel the words arrive. They shout constantly and without embarrassment, and so their voices are extraordinary: deepened, roughened, set far back in the throat, built like millers' and quarrymen's voices. A girl of twelve can make herself heard across the market — which is to say across seven paces and a hanging blanket — without seeming to strain. The old lose their hearing early and evenly, which the town treats not as an affliction but as a promotion. The truly deaf become confessors, judges, keepers of the shrine. You can shout your debt, your affair, your heresy directly into the mayor's ruined ear, and it goes no further. Privacy here is not quiet. It is finding someone who genuinely cannot take your words away with them.

Everything important is therefore announced. That is not a contradiction; it is the whole civic architecture. Marriages are bellowed at the rain-basin so the copper carries them. So are apologies, prices, births, accusations, the settling of the eternal dispute over whose washing drips onto whose shelf. To try to lower your voice is to draw every eye in the room. A whisperer is either sick or conspiring, and in a chamber holding exactly forty-one breaths, conspiracy is a kind of bad air.

They are forty-one. Always forty-one.

Ask and they will count for you, cheerfully, pointing up the tiers: the hammer-man and his two apprentices who are not his sons; the loom-women, four across the second level, whose shuttles keep a clacking counter-rhythm against the roar; the ladder-keeper, who alone is licensed to work the bucket-pulley and who sleeps beside the shaft in the only spot where the sound thins, and is therefore considered a little strange, a little touched by silence; the basin-keeper, timekeeper and town crier in one, who scrapes the pale slime from under the indoor rain and empties the copper clock and knows by its ring whether the water behind the north wall is running high; grandmothers enthroned on ledges the size of tables, mending, shelling beans, ruling three generations stacked above and below them within arm's reach. A jailer, though the jail is only a dry-ish corner shelf behind an iron grille salvaged from the drain, where the offender sits for a day in full view of everyone and is shouted at, hourly, with great formality, about what he did. A mayor, chosen not for wisdom but for carry — though the two, down here, are assumed to be related.

One birth, one departure. One death, one arrival down the ladder with a bundle on their back, blinking, already losing their above-smell. They do not discuss it as an edict. They discuss it as drainage: the room holds this much smoke, this much noise, this much hunger, and no more, the way the drain holds its finger-depth of water without ever rising. Who leaves, and under what sorrows, is announced at the basin like everything else. The dead go up in the bucket, wrapped in sacking their own looms made. The living who go up go the same way, and are spoken of afterward in the present tense for a very long time, as if they were only in another tier, just out of lantern reach.

Their children have never seen the sky, and regard it the way children above regard the deep sea: as a true thing that belongs to stories and to the insane. They know bats the way they know angels — rare, frantic, sacred visitations that stitch the lantern light twice a year and must be caught in a sack and returned to their own country. They are raised fortissimo. Lullabies are sung at a roar directly into a baby's ear, one enormous hand cupping the small head, and the babies sleep eventually with their whole bodies, mouths open, trusting the noise the way other infants trust a heartbeat. Silence, to a child of the vault, is not peace. Silence is what would happen if the aqueduct stopped behind the north wall, and no one down here, young or old, believes they would survive learning what that sounds like.

So they put their ears to that wall. That is the shrine: not an image, just a stretch of weeping brick a forearm from a buried river, where the roar comes through stone into the bone of the skull. They go there to pray, to marry, to think. And they consult the eel.

Everyone feeds it. Crumbs are set aside with a seriousness reserved for nothing else that cannot be eaten by a person — a crumb for the forty-second citizen, the pale, uncounted one under the planks, seen only as a snout at one grate and, weeks later, a tail at another, longer than belief allows. Old people will tell you, forehead to forehead, deafeningly, that it was here before the first shelf. That it will be here after the last. That a town that cannot grow and cannot whisper and cannot ever be dry requires at least one inhabitant exempt from all its rules, moving slowly its whole life through black water beneath their feet, seen in pieces, never entire.

In the evenings — the bell from above says it is evening; the flames, trimmed to their agreed height, do not change — the town does not quiet so much as gather. Cooking smoke finds the flue-crack. Someone sweeps water toward the drain with a twig broom, as someone always does. The looms stop. The hammer stops. Forty-one voices go on rising against the water, announcing the small, complete news of a main street, a market, a marriage, a theft, a birth coming that means a departure coming.

They are not waiting to be rescued. That is the last thing the place teaches you, and it teaches it through their faces, upturned to the lanterns at every level, wet, oil-sheened, shouting with laughter at a child pulled dripping from the drain. They have built a formality, a dignity, a whole jurisdiction of drips and ledgers and looms inside one room at the bottom of the world they need, and they maintain it plank by plank, knot by knot, voice by voice, in perfect, permanent balance with the stone.
