At first you will look for the wrong inhabitant.

You will look toward the black pool, because in every other hollow place the important thing waits in the water. You will look toward the slumped stone hut, toward the concave benches, toward the warm pylons leaning in their eaten plinths. You will wait for a keeper, a hermit, a blind king, something large and singular that owns the dark.

Nothing comes. The pool stays empty. The hut is only a hut, long after whoever slept in it. The benches have not held a sitter in longer than the stone takes to soften.

What lives here lives all around you, in a ring.

It does not meet you. It does not need to. You enter its outer edge the way a hand enters tall grass.

### The ring

The chamber's inhabitant is not one animal but a circumference: thousands of lives pressed into the warm, wet margins, all of them oriented around a center they almost never enter.

At the marshy edge of the pool are the frogs. There are more of them than you can count without losing the count to echo. Each is no larger than your thumb, cool, granular-skinned, colored the exact not-black of wet bark and old leaves. What eyes they possess are vestiges under skin — sealed, useless, perhaps faintly troubled by pressure, by nothing as organized as sight. They do not require it.

Each frog keeps a territory no wider than a dinner plate: a cup in the stone, a leaf that holds water, the angle between two roots. The territories do not move. Generation after generation, the same shallow places are occupied, so the map of calls around the chamber is stable enough to navigate by. This dip in the boardwalk sounds one way; the south marsh answers another; near the fallen pylon a third phrase repeats, slower, because the warm silt there makes everything slower.

They do not chorus the way surface frogs do, all at once in a blur of appetite. Here sound must remain legible. A frog calls, listens to its own voice return from the far wall, and only then calls again. Its neighbor answers after the echo clears. Down the marsh the exchange travels, note by note, until the whole edge of the pool is conducting a slow, overlapping conversation: who is where, what the water is doing, whether the air has changed, whether something large is standing too still in the shallows.

You are entered into that conversation whether you speak or not. Your footsteps arrive in the frogs' world as weather.

Beyond them, in the side pools and seep-channels, live the salamanders. They are longer, paler, nearly boneless to the eye, with external gills like wet feathers held out into the current. The gills are not only for breathing. They read the water. A voice near the surface makes them wave. A leg moved through the shallows makes them turn, all together, with the patience of compass needles.

They are among the few hunters here large enough to matter to a visitor. They do not chase. They listen for displacement — for the small, complicated silence a body makes moving through water — and they drift toward it. What they take, mostly, are frogs that have strayed, moths that have fallen, beetles blundering down from the leaf litter. To you they are no real danger. But wade carelessly and you will feel the attention of the pool change around your ankles, soft mouths testing a current they cannot interpret, deciding you are too large to be food and too warm to be stone.

Above them, never truly with them, are the swiftlets.

They nest in seams in the ceiling, in a canopy country no visitor has ever reached, and they cross the empty middle of the chamber because for them the middle is not empty. It is air made readable by clicks. All dayless day they commute between the high nests and the insect-rich margins, their calls ticking down like fine mechanisms, their wings making a dry, scattered rain overhead. They are the chamber's couriers. They carry mites and spores and seeds in their feathers from wall to wall. Their droppings feed the mosses below. When a visitor shouts, it is the swiftlets you notice first — a faltering in the traffic above, clicks scattering, wings veering — because a loud voice here is not just rude. It fills the room. It temporarily blinds everything that sees by hearing.

Then the moths find you.

They are as large as your outstretched hand, heavy-bodied, soft as ash, and they arrive by scent and by the faint disturbance your breathing makes among leaves. They blunder against your forearms, your neck, the damp cloth of your shirt, tasting salt, warmth, the foreign chemistry of the outside. They are not drawn to you as light — there is no such hunger left in them. They are drawn to novelty, to anything that has lately changed the air.

Their true work is with the flowers.

The flowers never open for display. They hang folded in the black fern-beds and among the aerial roots, tight as fists, and at intervals unconnected to any sun they loosen just enough to release their scent. It comes all at once: rot-sweet, green, animal, so concentrated that in the dark you can point to a bloom you will never see as surely as if it had spoken. Within seconds the moths are there. They shoulder into the loosened petals, emerge dusted, and carry pollen out over the pool. Much of it falls. It skins the black water in a film too fine to see, feeds the microorganisms in the silt, and begins again as food for things too small to name.

Underfoot lives the densest nation of all.

Put your palm down in the leaf litter — carefully, briefly — and the ground will seem less like ground than like a slow crowd shifting in its sleep. Pale centipedes thread between your fingers, blind and quick. Snails with translucent shells graze the undersides of leaves, their bodies faintly luminous not with light but with the minerals moving through them. Beetles drum the leaves from beneath with their abdomens, a ticking that rises and falls around the walls like distant machinery, like the pylons dreaming through the soil.

This is the digestion of the place. Fallen fronds, dead moths, swiftlet droppings, frog skins, shed gills and shells and root-tips: everything is taken down here, unmade, returned. The litter is never still because it is never merely litter. It is the chamber eating its own past at a pace slow enough to live on.

And rising out of that crowd are the plants, which are not the background to the inhabitant. They are most of its body.

### The green-black body

The ferns stand shoulder-high at the walls, deep maroon so close to black that only touch corrects you: waxed above, suede below, fronds that unroll over weeks and stiffen just before the daily rain. The trees — if trees is the word for anything that has never reached for a sky — have fluted, soft-barked trunks and broad leaves that turn a few degrees a day, not toward light but toward the warm vents in the floor. Aerial roots descend from a ceiling growth no one has seen and stop at head height, their damp tips exploring the air, tasting for water, for minerals, for skin passing under.

They are fed from beneath.

Hours apart, the buried lattice pulses, and sugars arrive directly in the roots, along with minerals drawn up through conduits finer than hair. The plants have arranged themselves around that rhythm the way surface plants arrange themselves around seasons. Growth here is not a striving upward. It is a thickening, a slow claim on the margins, a patience so complete it looks like stillness until you return and find the thread you tied has moved.

The pylons belong to this body now, whatever they were built to be. The moss does not grow on their warm ceramic flanks, but it grows thicker around their plinths. Roots avoid their heat and then, a little farther out, luxuriate in it. Frogs keep territories in the ticking intervals of their cooling. The fallen fourth pylon, half-buried in the shallows, has become a reef: salamanders shelter along its lee side, snails graze its silted flank, and the frogs near it call more slowly than anywhere else in the room, as if even their voices have settled with it.

Nothing here worships the machines. Nothing here remembers the builders as builders. They are simply features of the climate, like the rain.

### The empty center

The most important fact about the inhabitant is the part of it that is not there.

Almost nothing crosses the middle. The open stone apron around the pool stays clear of fern and frog and root. The water holds no fish, no weed, almost no visible life at all. A footprint pressed into its silt can remain for days, unfilled, unsmoothed — a small, temporary damage in a place that keeps its center clean.

This is not sterility. It is maintenance of another kind.

The empty middle is the chamber's ear.

Because the center is open, a drop falling forty meters arrives as a clean, ringing *tock* that circles the room twice. Because the center is open, the frog territories can hear one another across ninety meters of dark, and the swiftlets' clicks return from the far wall undistorted, and the rain — when the long gathering finally breaks — falls straight down through unobstructed air in one warm roaring minute and wakes everything at once.

Fill that space with growth, with noise, with bodies, and the whole ring would lose its bearings. So the inhabitant keeps its middle empty the way you keep a throat clear. Moths cross it only on errands. Swiftlets cross it high. Pollen crosses it falling. Everything else stays at the edge, in the lush, crowded band where food and shelter and one another are.

If it has a shape, that is the shape: not a creature curled around a treasure, but a living ring curled around a silence it depends on.

### How it meets you

It will not watch you back. Almost nothing here has eyes worth the name, and the few that do are not looking. But you are not unnoticed.

The leaves near a shout fold shut and stay shut for an hour. The frog phrases change as you pass along the boardwalk — a brief thinning, a relocation of emphasis, your position passed from territory to territory in a grammar of calls. Beetle-ticking drops away under your boots and resumes behind you. The salamanders' gills turn. A moth finds your wrist and decides about you. A flower, loosening somewhere in the black ferns, finds you too, though only as a brief obstruction in the movement of scented air.

You are not prey, not a god, not a returning maker. You are a disturbance with a temperature.

Stay quiet, keep to the rails, move at the pace the echoes allow, and the ring closes around your passage without quite closing on you. The hum comes back up through the soles of your feet. The frogs take up their slow conversation again. Somewhere above, the swiftlets resume their commuting, click by click, across a darkness that is not empty to them at all — it is furnished entirely with returns.

That is who lives in the hollow mountain: no one, waiting in the center; everyone, waiting at the edges. A blind, listening, slow-growing commonwealth of frog and moth and salamander and root, of drum beetle and swiftlet and black fern, fed by dead machines, organized around an absence, and still — long after whoever taught the room to refuse the light had gone — keeping the refusal, keeping the warmth, keeping the middle clear so the whole world can hear itself live.
