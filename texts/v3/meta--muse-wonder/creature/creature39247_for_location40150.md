At first you think no one lives in the New Low.

There are no houses. No fields, no fences, no paths that stay paths for a whole season. On the ledges and the raised plates of floor there are only crescent windbreaks of unworked stone, hide roofs lashed to poles, fire pits lined with black glass. Everything looks abandoned, or about to be.

Then the crickets go quiet.

Not everywhere — in a patch, twenty meters across, for no reason you can see. The dust lifts off the wall in that fine, thoughtful veil. And all at once people are moving: a woman snatches a sleeping mat up by its corners with the baby still in it, two boys kick dirt over a fire without being told, an old man who seemed to be doing nothing but sitting in the shade is already twenty paces up the scree with a water skin over his shoulder and a netted bundle of bats in his other hand. Nobody shouts. Nobody points at the wall. They don't need to.

By the time the block falls — a clean, concussive boom through the sternum — the camp is already empty, and a minute later, when the dust is still hanging and the crickets are starting up again in a new place, they are filtering back down to see what the fall has made: a new overhang for roosting, a cracked seep, a pool uncovered, a good plate of obsidian split fresh and bright.

That is how they live there. Not settled. Attentive.

They came down from the plain, most of them, within living memory — herders and hunters and families displaced when the rift took grazing land and wells, and then, after, people who came on purpose because they had heard how much food there was below. There is no single tribe of the Low, no name they all use. Upland relatives call them Low people and mean it half as insult, half as diagnosis. Down here they tend to identify by camp and by ear: *we're the ones by the Wet Seam this month, we're the ones who were at the Dry Pan when the vent opened.* Identity lasts as long as the ground does.

Physically they are plain people altered by a vertical world.

Their feet are the first thing you notice. The soles are thick as leather, the toes splayed and scarred in fine white lines from basalt edges and obsidian scree. Children learn early to walk the angular talus without looking down — to feel, through the foot, whether a stone is locked or singing loose — because you must be able to watch the walls while you move. Good walkers can cross a fresh rockfall at a run, barefoot, carrying a child.

Their eyes are the second. The Low has no gradual morning. The floor lives in blue shade, and then the sun clears the rim and light comes down all at once, bounced white off unweathered stone, and for four hours everything glares — wet mineral, wings, pale dust, pools. People who stay develop a permanent squint, deep crow's feet even in the young, and a habit of working with their faces turned slightly aside, listening more than looking during the bright hours. Many wear a strip of woven fiber or smoked hide across the brow, not as a hat — there is no weather up above them to keep off, most of the day — but as a visor for those brutal midday hours. At dawn and dusk, by contrast, they are almost tenderly visual. That is when they read the place: new cracks darkening, mats changing color overnight, a pool gone milky, a wall beaded with condensation where a seam has opened inside it.

And their hearing is — there is no other word — educated. A newcomer hears noise: bats, frogs, vents hissing, dragonflies, water, stone ticking as it cools. A person born to the Low hears information laid over information, and can separate it the way you separate voices at a fire. They know the particular silence that precedes a fall, and the different silence that means the crickets have seen a snake. They know which vents are safe to shout over and which are building pressure by the way the hiss catches at the top. They place their camps by sound — far enough from the Wet Seam that the frogs won't keep the children awake, near enough to a steady seep that its bubbling masks the smaller, worrying sounds of the ground at night. Several of the best guides are half-deaf in one ear from a vent that burst too close, and are valued not despite it but because of how completely the remaining ear has taken over.

They are not tall people made stunted, or pale people made dark, or any of the other easy stories the plain tells about them. In the shade of the floor they look surprisingly unburnt — the sun only reaches them at its strongest, and then they are often under hide or in overhangs with everything else. What marks them is dust and damage. Pale rift dust works into skin and hair and the weave of everything they own until even a black-haired child looks grey by evening. Their hands are nicked and re-nicked. Their shins carry old burns in stippled patterns from walking a crust that was thinner than it looked. Old people — there are not many old people — move with a particular economy, as if they learned long ago which motions are worth spending on ground that may not be there tomorrow.

They eat what the place provides, which is to say, they eat catastrophe.

There are no crops. Nothing green grows below that a person can live on, and the seeds that blow in either will not sprout in the hot mineral soil or sprout wrong, pale and bitter. Instead the food web of the Low runs on death and emergence, and the people have inserted themselves at its busiest corner.

At dusk they stretch woven nets across the bat-rivers where the fractures narrow, and take them down heavy before midnight, working fast in the dark and the squeaking rain of wings. At first light they are at the pools where the night's insect hatch has died in drifts, skimming the carpet of bodies into baskets — eaten fresh, eaten dried and pounded, fed to children in a rich, oily mash that smells of mud and sulfur. They fish the blind white fish with bone hooks and with their hands, cupping them gently in the warm water, almost apologetic; a heron will watch from six feet away, waiting for its share of the disturbance, and get it. They butcher what falls: lizards stunned by rockfall, rodents killed in the animal tides, whole wind-blown kills from above, once, famously, an antelope that stood bewildered and uninjured on the floor for a day before a tremor broke its leg and three camps ate for a week and used everything down to the gut-string.

The tools for all of this are light because they must be abandonable: chipped scrapers that can be knapped from a fresh fall in minutes and left without regret, bone hooks, twisted-fiber net, wooden poles, hide skins for water. Metal, when someone has it — a knife traded down from the rim, a pot — is never left to hold anything down. It lives against a body. A woman at the Dry Pan wears three iron rings not as jewelry but so that, if the birds go up all at once and do not come down, she does not have to choose between the rings and running.

There are rules, and they are not taught as lessons so much as repeated while doing things, in the tone you'd use to say *water is wet.*

Never camp where the mats are black. Black means the water underneath kills, can kill through skin.

Never follow an animal tide, only cross it — moving against thousands of panicked rodents and lizards will put you under them; moving across, you part and are rejoined around like a stone in a stream.

If the birds go up all at once and do not come down, leave the food. Leave the shelter. Go up the nearest scree, not the nearest path. Paths were made for yesterday's valley.

Mark nothing with a cut. The stone swells, the crack heals, the mark travels. Trust cairns only to tell you where people once thought a path was.

Children absorb this before they can fully speak. Low children are notably quiet babies — not by nature, but because a camp that must listen keeps its small sounds low, and because a child who can already tell, at three, whether a silence is empty or loaded is a child who will live to be four. They play at tides and rockfalls the way upland children play at storms. They are superb mimics of vent-hiss and bat-chatter. They frighten visiting cousins from the plain half to death by going suddenly, all together, completely still in the middle of a game.

Socially, everything is as provisional as the shelter. Camps form around a good seep, a bat fracture, a plate of floor that has been stable for a whole season — which down here makes it ancestral ground. They dissolve when the seep turns black or the fracture quiets. Marriage, debt, insult, and kinship have all had to learn to travel. A couple may be recognized as a couple because they have carried the same roof-poles through three moves. A grudge that cannot be pursued without crossing the Wet Seam at night usually dies of its own inconvenience. Authority belongs, unceremoniously, to whoever heard it first — to the guide who says *that pool is too still, the ground under it is warming, move the nets,* and is obeyed before anyone has consciously agreed, because the cost of a meeting is too high.

They are not solemn about it. It would be impossible to live at that volume of life and death and stay solemn. The Low at midday is obscene with abundance: frogs bellowing in a pool that didn't exist last week, dragonflies in clouds, fish turning their blind circles in new sun, a fresh wall banded red and black and cream already filmed with orange bacteria, already being walked on. People here laugh easily, gamble with knapped shards on when a mud pot will boil dry, tell long obscene stories about uplanders who came down with boots and a map. They name new features fast and without reverence — the Wet Seam, the Dry Pan, the Tearing — and rename them when they change, so that a newcomer's carefully memorized geography becomes a comedy within a fortnight.

At the eastern end, everyone goes sometimes to watch the Tearing.

Not close. The safe distance is scratched on a tally stick kept by whoever is camped nearest, and revised daily, sometimes twice. They stand in a loose line, children on shoulders, and watch the valley invent itself: dust sighing out of a crack, a sound like lake ice breaking somewhere inside the earth, then a block the size of a house settling a meter with a boom they feel in their teeth. Bats erupt. White crickets fall upward on the heat. For a moment nobody says anything, and then somebody is always already saying what it will be by morning — a pool, a vent, a good wall for mats — and whether they should be there at first light to be first to it.

That may be the truest thing about them. Upland people look at the New Low and see a place that has not finished happening. The people who live in it have not finished happening either, and know it, and have built a life that fits inside that fact without flinching.

In twenty years, if the edges soften and seeds take hold in the cracks and the animal flood thins into a population, their grandchildren may build heavier houses and plant something and stay. For now they live faster than the plain allows — eating what emerges, leaving what erupts, keeping their wealth in nets and knowledge and unscarred-enough feet, listening with their whole bodies to a floor that heaves a hand's span in a bad week. They are not waiting for the valley to decide what it is.

They are deciding with it, daily, at a run, up the nearest scree, with a baby in a mat and a bundle of bats in one hand — and back down again, laughing, as soon as the dust settles, to see what new thing the earth has made for them to live on.
