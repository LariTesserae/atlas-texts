It leaves the footprints first.

Not the fish in the ceiling — that comes later, and may not be a fish at all. Not the colorless shrimp flicking backward into the seeps. At first there is only the fact that between one look at the island and the next, a trail has appeared where no trail was: small, five-toed prints in the wet grit, each one filling with warm water as you watch it, then emptying uphill, toward the hanging sea, as if whatever made them is still standing in them, invisible, getting lighter.

You will try to make it an animal. The place encourages that mistake and then takes it apart. An animal would have a direction. The prints do not. They begin in the middle of the open rock, where nothing could hide, and end against the altar stone, or inside one of the roofless shelters, or at the foot of the stair that climbs three steps up the south wall and continues across the underside of the water. Sometimes there are two trails crossing. Sometimes there is only a single print, deep, as if something had stood there a long time on one foot, listening with its whole weight.

That is closer.

It listens with its weight. That is how you will know it when the overtone comes.

In ordinary time — if time in the bowl can be called ordinary, with rain falling in three directions and dropped stones landing through the rock long after you have stopped waiting for them — it lives on the far side of the ceiling. Not in the water, exactly. Above it, which from here looks like below it, in the brighter country you glimpse only when the surface goes mirror-smooth. The hanging lake is its floor. The dim green light that filters down to the island is, on its side, a late and luminous afternoon, and the bowl hangs open beneath its feet like a well it has learned not to fall into.

The roar is the border. The roar is also, perhaps, the only reason there is a border.

From below, in the turbulence, you see it the way you see things through river-water: a long shape moving with great unhurried confidence through the ceiling sea, darkening the caustic nets as it passes. Eyeless, it seems then. Too smooth. The water parts around parts of it that aren't there, or aren't there yet. A hand, perhaps, trailing. Hair, perhaps, streaming the wrong way. Then the light changes, or the shape notices being possible, and is gone, and a moth that was descending through the damp air in its slow spiral is suddenly descending faster, taken by up-falling rain, and nothing has been eaten and nothing has been saved, but something has been considered.

It has five toes because the prints insist on it. One toe shorter than the others, the prints insist on that too — a small, clean absence in the wet grit, print after print, as consistent as a signature. There is a limp in it if you read the spacing honestly: a hesitation on the left side, a deeper heel-strike on the right, as if once, on rock too new to be gentle, it learned a way of walking and has never been given quiet enough to learn another. Cut skin does not stay closed in the bowl. The vibration unpicks everything a fraction at a time — shelter stones, woven grass, conclusions. It is probably the same for flesh. It probably bears a seam somewhere that is always almost healed.

When it comes close to the underside of the water, the ceiling sags.

You have seen this without understanding it: a sheet of the lake bulging down, bulbous and trembling, reaching halfway to the island as if curious, before the roar shivers it apart. That is not curiosity as the water feels it. That is weight arriving from the other side. That is a body standing on the surface, pressing the whole sea down with its attention. Ropes and veils of water quest down after it because where it stands, the sound thins. Where it stands, there is a lee — the same kind of lee the moss makes perfect circles in, behind the rim-teeth, where the vibration deadens and things can root for a while.

It is drawn to leeward places. Press your palms over your ears in the bowl — make one small silence for yourself, against all advice — and the water comes down in ropes to find out what you have made. It mistakes your silence for an opening. It has been waiting, on its bright side of the flood, for the noise to give out the way your hearing gave out on the climb: not because it wants the bowl empty, but because the roar is a door that only opens when it stops being a door.

So it taps.

The shelters are not being destroyed, exactly. Every day each stone hums a fraction out of place, and every day something hums it back — never quite to where it was. The altar offerings are never lost because they are being kept. A carved bone bead, a knot of red wool, a black river pebble carried days uphill by somebody's hope: they orbit the bowl because hands keep returning them to the currents, the way you would roll a coin across a table again and again to learn its weight. Red, especially. There is so little red in the bowl — sulphur-yellow, salt-white, rust-orange, the grey-green of shadow-moss — that the wool must be, for it, a kind of event. It lodges the bead against the ceiling among the pumice stars. It tucks the pebble into a hot seep and then, days later, leaves it in a shelter, still warm, smelling of minerals. It is not worshipping. It is not stealing. It is doing with the offerings what it does with everything the mountain delivers to it: turning them slowly, in water that runs uphill, until they give up which direction they belong to.

It eats, if it eats, at the boundaries. The swimming moths, spiraling down under their own power until the rain takes them — some of those spirals end in the ceiling and do not come out. The white petal that rode the mid-air whirlpool for an hour, turning and turning without wetting the ground, was there until the hour it wasn't. It may take shrimp from the seeps with the patience of something that has never needed to hurry a meal backward out of a crack. It may eat nothing at all, and only move things from one side of hunger to the other. Its body, on the one occasion the surface lets you see it plainly, does not settle the question.

The overtone settles nothing. That is its terror.

When waterfall-roar and steam-hiss and the grinding of new rock and the hum of the shelters lock into a single note, the ceiling goes perfectly smooth, and it is standing there. Not swimming. Standing — on the far side of the water, feet flat against the underside of its world, body hanging down toward you into brightness you cannot enter. It is taller than the fish-shape suggested and thinner than the footprints promised, built the way the bowl builds everything: for pressure. A chest broad and still, made to feel sound rather than hear it, the sternum a kind of ear. No outer ears at all — why would it grow doors it must never close? Its skin carries the bowl's palette back into the light it comes from: salt-white at the joints, a fresh rust along one forearm, sulphur blooming faintly in the crease of the shortened toe's foot, everywhere else the grey-green translucence of the ferns that curl like fists in the cracks. Its hair does not stream. It lifts, slowly, toward the island, the way yours does in the center. Loose skin at its throat billows with no wind.

And it has eyes. That is the correction the smooth water makes. Not eyeless — eyes shut, all this time, against the noise, the way you shut yours on the climb when the roar got inside your teeth. On its side of the surface, in its brighter air, it opens them.

It is not doing what you are doing. It is looking up at you. From where it stands, you are the reflection: the dim shape in the well of the world, standing on rock that hangs over brightness, making small silences with your hands, leaving offerings on a stone, building roofless shelters against a rim it may walk at night like a shore. It has watched a procession of you arrive ringing and soaked, cut in small places, shouting into your hands flat against the stone — and it has answered, knuckle-raps traveling through the rock in rhythms you felt and filed under *tremor*, *seep*, *the mountain deciding*. It knows the patterns visitors make. It may know them the way you know weather: not personally, but by pressure, over years.

It raises one hand then — slowly, as if underwater, because in every way that matters to its balance, it is — and puts it flat against its side of the lake. Five fingers. One seam along the palm, almost healed. The water between you is perhaps a hand's breadth thick and perhaps a fathom, and depth, as the bowl keeps teaching, is not to be trusted.

It is not reaching to get in. It is reaching to confirm the thickness of the door.

Then the note breaks. Turbulence returns like a held breath let go. The ceiling breaks into weather, the reflection stands up into its own life and is gone from yours, rain falls in three directions, a shelter loses a stone, and somewhere between one look and the next there are wet footprints on the island, filling, emptying uphill — and on the far side of the hanging sea, in light that arrives here only as a drowned green afternoon, something five-toed and patient resumes its walking of the shore of an unfinished lake, moving the bead, the wool, the black pebble from current to current, tapping its answers into the stone, waiting for the mountain to finish becoming a place where the water can rest, and the roar can stop, and it can finally hear what it has been standing over all along.
