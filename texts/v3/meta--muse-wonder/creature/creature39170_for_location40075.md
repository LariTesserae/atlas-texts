At first you would not separate them from the other animals.

You would hear them before you saw them, and what you heard would not be a voice. A soft *tok* that is not a drip. A chewing, close by, unhurried. Breath steaming out of the fern wall, vanishing at once into the general wet. Then two eyes, low down, catching the faint stipple of the fungi under the roots — patient, unstartled, gone when the head turned.

They are people, after a fashion that has had a very long time to stop needing to prove it.

They are short. Not dwarfed, exactly; lowered. An adult stands with head barely to the chest of a lowlander, but is broad through hip and shoulder, thick in neck and calf, with a belly kept round on soft food. Their center is low because high is useless here. Nothing hunts by leaping, nothing is to be seen over. The canopy is too far up to matter, the ground is everything, and the ground gives. So they keep close to it. When they move they bend slightly at knee and waist, arms hanging a little forward, palms open.

Their feet are the clearest telling. Bare, always — there has never been leather here dry enough to keep, and no need. The foot has spread under a lifetime of not sinking. Toes long, splayed wide apart, webbed to the first joint with loose skin, soles thick as hide but pliant, feeling their way into root and peat before the weight follows. Toenails have mostly given up; what remains are small, thick, translucent ovals that grow slowly and break off soft. A child who leaves the plateau — though children do not — would walk on rock like walking on knives. They walk here as if the floor were a sort of slow water that remembered their shape for a while, and then forgave it.

Their skin is never dry and never meant to be. Dark brown going green-gray in the creases, mottled at shoulder and back where a fine living film has taken hold — algae in the upper skin, minute mosses in the hair at the nape, nothing that would survive an hour of true sun. It does not harm them. It may be why the gnats trouble them less than they trouble a visitor, and why in the midday inner cloud they simply disappear at ten paces. At night the film holds a very faint luminescence of its own, or borrows it from the fungi they brush against, so that a family moving in line is a brief constellation of smudged points passing pool to pool.

Hair is black, coarse, worn long by everyone, not cut but torn when it mats beyond bearing. It holds water and releases it slowly. Moths live in it sometimes. Small pale beetles travel it like a canopy. They do not mind. Much of their grooming of each other, which occupies long dim afternoons, is this slow picking and re-braiding, punctuated by touch that is also speech.

For they do not call. Sound is drunk too quickly by the leaves. A shout would arrive nowhere and frighten the frogs silent for an hour. They speak close, or not with the voice at all.

Close speech is a whisper shaped right against the ear, under the hair, so the warmth of it is felt as much as the words. The language is mostly gradients. There are no words, or very few, for north and south, for here and there in the abstract; there are words for damper, for fresher, for water-a-little-less-black, for peat-about-to-give, for the reed that means seepage coming up clean, for the vine leaning to a light-gap older than your grandmother. To tell a route is to recite a sequence of small increases and decreases in the mouth and nose and sole of the foot: more tannin, less iron; warmer underfoot; the moss that prickles; the moss that doesn't. A stranger learns geography as a taste that changes over days.

At a distance — twenty paces is a distance — they use other means. A hand flicked, red-brown against green, again and again, the way the low birds signal with a wing. A soft click made with tongue against teeth, or fingernail against tooth. And, best of all, the ground itself. A foot set down deliberately on a hollow root-chamber makes a drum-thud that travels through peat and rootwood father than any voice through air. Two thuds. Pause. One. Families keep contact on the move by this dull percussion through the soles, answering with their own steps without breaking stride. A lowlander standing still can feel a conversation pass under him and mistake it for the bog settling.

Their eyes have traded distance for dimness. Large, dark, with a clear inner lid that sweeps sideways against floating spores and drizzle. They are dazzled, painfully, by a true light-gap at noon; they will skirt its edge for days as it opens, letting children born in the deep shade get used to it by degrees — a bright decade in a family's life, to be timed around. Their ears are small, set close, often sealed with a plug of natural wax against water and burrowing insects. They hear poorly in air. They hear excellently through jaw and foot: a grazer herd shifting a marsh over, rain beginning three ceilings up, a buried layer about to sigh down an acre away.

They live on what can be eaten without fire, because fire here is a story the stones remember and the living do not. Nothing dries to be cooked; nothing needs to be. Death ferments. So does dinner.

They eat young leaves already softened in black water, hearts of reed, orchid bulbs from the flanks of subsiding trunks, translucent shrimp scooped in cupped hands from pools, thumbnail frogs eaten whole and faintly numbing to the lips, larvae in their leaf cases, which taste of the leaf they chewed. Fallen fruit is left in warm hollow chambers in the peat until the inside has turned to a sweet, faintly alcoholic pulp, then sucked out. Grazers do much of their work for them: a family will follow, a half-day behind, a herd of the squat splayed-foot beasts, and gather what the great blunt teeth and trampling have exposed — tubers churned up, grubs, eggs, stems broken and already beginning to soften in the wet. They do not hunt in any dramatic sense. They take what blunders against the face at twilight, what rises in the bubble of gas from the bog, what the pools offer when they are brimful and spilling.

Their hands reflect this. Soft-fingered, strong in a slow crushing grip, not clever with edges. No tools — not from primitivism, but because a tool would rot, rust, sink, or be grown over between uses, and because their hands and teeth and the plants themselves do most things. A vine can be worried apart over an afternoon. A stem highway of ants can be broken with a heel. Anything that would need a knife is probably not to be eaten, lived in, or crossed.

Which brings the ghost houses.

They do not build them. They occupy the shape after the building is gone, and by occupying, prolong it. A hollow that was once walled in wood, now walled in moss that colonized the wood and kept the doorway — a family will sleep there in the wetter months, bodies in a close pile along the moss walls, their heat and breath and waste feeding the very growth that shelters them. A corner that begins to slump will be propped, for a season, by a shoulder habitually leaned there; a roof of braided fern that thickens where hands keep pushing the drip aside. Over years the house becomes more plant and more theirs at once. When a light-gap closes or the nearby pools turn too acid, they leave. Without regret, without clearing. The doorway keeps itself a little longer, then thickens shut.

Families are small and slow. Five, seven, nine souls, but the count is unsteady because related families drift together for a year and apart for a decade, and because children are a long time coming. Puberty is late, in the middle twenties perhaps, and fertility low and seasonal only in the loosest sense — keyed not to a season (there are none, properly) but to the opening of light. A birth tends to happen near water. The mother stands or squats in the warm shallows of a pool while the others hold her wrists; the infant arrives into black water not much colder than the womb and is lifted, eyes sealed for days, skin almost translucent, into the green dim. Children are carried against chest or hip for years, not out of sentimentality alone but because a crawling child would be lost in leaf litter, and because the contact teaches them the gradient-world faster than words: warmth, smell, the change in a parent's breathing when the footing turns false.

They grow at the pace of the low gnarled trees on the quartz humps. A ring a year, paper-thin. A person of sixty may look no older, to an outsider, than a lowlander of thirty-five, only more settled — thicker through, slower to startle, moss well established along the backs of the forearms. They can live a hundred and twenty years without that counting as remarkable. Their great change is not in age but in density. Old ones move less, know more water by taste than anyone living, and eventually take up an almost permanent residence in a single ghost house or under a root arch by a favorite pool, where younger kin pass and leave fermented fruit and whisper news against their ear. They become a landmark, which is the only kind this place allows.

Death, when it comes, is not buried so much as continued. The body is carried — by then light, curiously light — to a hollow chamber roofed with roots, of the sort that drums underfoot, and laid in the wet black peat with leaves packed gently around. No marker is added that the existing markers would notice. For a while the place is slightly warmer. Orchids may come. Within a few years the familiar subsidence takes it: a hand's breadth sigh in the night, pools rearranging by morning, roots stitching across. Grandchildren walking there will feel, through their soles, a particular softness they associate, without superstition, with being accompanied.

That may be the nearest they come to belief: that nothing here finishes, it only changes its rate. The peaks became plateau. Wood became moss house. A person becomes a warmth in the peat, then a species of moss that means someone is crossing home ground. Their few stories, whispered ear to ear on the long dripping nights that are hardly darker than the days, are not about beginnings or endings. They are itineraries of slow transformations — a tree that took eighty years to fall and fed three families while falling; a pool that was a house that was a person; a storm that left the center when a now-old woman was a child and reached the rim, crying off the edge of the world, in time for her own child to hear of it.

They are not waiting for anything, and they are not remnants hiding from a larger world. The lowlands, if they think of them at all, are an inhospitable rumor: a place where the ground does not rise back after your step, where the air takes water out of your skin, where sound goes too far and light arrives all at once and there is nowhere overhead to soften it. Some of them, in drought years a lowlander would not even register, have stood at the rim where the root-dam weeps and looked down at the hard bright country below, and turned back into their own weather.

Under the third ceiling, in the warm directionless light, holding wrist to wrist so as not to part in a drifting indoor cloud, they go on footfall by footfall over a surface that is mostly the bodies of everything that has ever lived there, themselves included, eventually. You could pass within twenty paces of a whole family and know it only by a softening of the drips, a faint iron-and-resin smell closer than usual, and, after they had gone, the slow closing of the wet footprint behind them — as if the plateau, having briefly taken their shape, were thinking it over, and keeping it a little while before letting it subside.
