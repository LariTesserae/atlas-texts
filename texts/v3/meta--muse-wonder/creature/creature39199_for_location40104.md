They are fewer than the town is built for, and they know it in their bodies. They live scattered across a grid meant for thousands — a family alone on a dormitory balcony built for laundry lines for twenty, a night-shift fitter with a whole numbered street to himself, children whose addresses sound metropolitan and whose nearest neighbor is a vacant lot of blackberries. So they have spread out the way water spreads in a shallow pan. Every household claims a little more ground than it needs. Gardens spill. Workshops annex empty foundations. Someone's tomatoes are growing in Cinema Lot 3. Someone else keeps bees behind the clinic, paints the hives silver like the hydrants, and swears the bees hum a third below the fans.

Most of them came for the Condenser, or were brought by someone who did. There is no other reason to be in the basin, and they are untroubled by that. Work is not what you do between your real life; work is the weather you live inside. Fitters, electricians, filter-washers, compressor men — it is still mostly men on the heavy crews, and they correct you mildly if you assume it — drivers for the tankers, the small clinic staff, two teachers, a storekeeper, a priest who doubles as the plant's safety officer and wears his ear protectors over his collar. And the painters. There are always painters. A disproportionate caste, universally respected. They go out in threes with scrapers and pots and wage the town's one declared war, against rust, and they are winning because there is so little yet to defend and because they never, ever stop.

You can tell a lifer — the town is too new for the word to mean old, but they use it anyway, two years, five — by how they stand when they talk to you. Close. Not intimate-close, plant-close. Forehead almost inclined, one ear turned a fraction toward your mouth, voice pitched not loud exactly but *projected*, from the diaphragm, the way actors do in old films. Everybody talks like they're addressing a small public meeting. Everybody enunciates. Consonants survive here that die in other places. Children are startlingly clear speakers, and startlingly loud at table, and nobody tells them to use their inside voice because there isn't one. Inside is also loud.

And by the protectors, of course. Orange, yellow, acid lime, worn up on shift and down around the neck off it, on a cord, like medals, like rosaries, like the necklaces of some bright plastic tribe. People fiddle with them while they think. People can tell each other's at a glance in the mess hall — taped, initialed, chewed by a particular toddler, repainted by a particular painter. Newcomers get issued a color and feel naked without the weight after a month. Old hands sleep with theirs under the pillow, not for use but for nearness.

Their hearing is the town's open secret and its quiet accounting. The clinic does the tests in a padded closet that is the only truly silent room for miles, and the audiologist — the most confided-in person in the hamlet, more than the priest — keeps the graphs in a drawer where the paper nevertheless curls. A notch at a certain frequency, year by year. A wife who says *you didn't hear the kettle again.* A foreman who reads lips so smoothly you only notice in the seven minutes, when he keeps reading them out of habit while your actual voice comes through. They are not bitter about it. They joke about selective deafness as a marital technology perfected by engineering. But they bring their children in early and often, and the children wear their protectors up, even in the playground, with the unselfconscious discipline of kids who have been taught to wear boots in snake country.

Because everything here is damp and everything here is thirsty, which makes a particular kind of careful person.

This is the central education of a child of the basin: you can be wet to the skin and still not have a drink. Fog on your face is not water. Roof-catch in your barrel — with its lid, with its inevitable frog thumping softly at night like a heart in the wall — is for washing, for hair, for the gray, thrifted purposes. Blue tokens are for drinking, counted out at the store, kept in a chipped mug by the sink in every kitchen, spent with a deliberation that looks like stinginess and is really just form. The drip from the main ducts, ice-cold, beaded, sweet-looking, singing as it falls, is forbidden absolutely and in a tone adults use for almost nothing else. Copper. It will make you sick in a way that builds. So they learn thirst as a kind of literacy: to read which water is which by where it has been. To watch most of what their parents make all day, cleaned and balanced and sealed, grind out through the rock cut in tanker trucks for other people's towns, and wave at the trucks without irony. It was never promised to them. It's the product. You don't eat the shoes in a shoe factory town.

It makes them precise. It makes them a little ritualistic about small abundances. Bath night is an event. A full kettle is announced. The gardens on the outflow side — where the spent air sighs forever cool and wet, and the manager's predecessor's tree ferns have made a green cathedral over the duct supports, and someone's escaped orchid has become everyone's orchid by cuttings — are tended like a lending library that pays you back in jungle. You do not *own* a gunnera with a leaf big enough to stand a child under. You have custody of it for a season. Cuttings move house to house wrapped in damp newspaper that never dries out anyway, and thrive, because the whole town is a greenhouse with a noise problem.

Their houses testify. Paperbacks swell and then hold their swollen shape, as if they'd read themselves. Photographs are kept in plastic sleeves. Salt lives in the fridge. Shoes live on racks off the floor and are mourned anyway, blackening nobly. Everyone has excellent rain gear and nobody entirely trusts it, and everyone's handshake is a little rough from work and a little soft from perpetual moisture, a combination you come to recognize. Laundry is hung at noon, in that brief, blue, burned-off hour, with military optimism, and brought in at evening still holding a memory of damp, and worn anyway. Nothing smells quite clean here. It smells like hot metal, low-tide fog, green things growing too fast, and people who have accepted that.

They keep time differently.

Ask someone when they'll be done and they may say *two cycles*, or *by the third quiet*. Ninety minutes up, seven down. Their day is not an hour hand; it's a lung. The whole town inhales noise and then, briefly, exhales it. Babies who will not sleep in a silent motel room in the downcountry towns sleep like stones through the roar and stir, fretful, in the sudden hush — mothers learn to rock a little harder through the quiets, to hum on purpose. Long sentences, delicate news, apologies, proposals, the punchline you want to land exactly right: saved for the quiet. Teachers build it into lessons with an instinct no curriculum taught them, holding the hard explanation, the poem, until the compressors sigh down and thirty clear child-voices can suddenly hear themselves think. In the mess hall, conversations don't so much pause as *drop*, gratefully, half an octave and half a volume, and faces change — oh, that's what you sound like. Strangers are revealed. So are spouses.

It would be wrong to call those seven minutes sacred. They call it *the quiet* the way you'd say *the store* — definite, singular, shared. But people do arrange to be outdoors for it. They do step onto balconies with tea. In the quiet you can hear the town's second population take over its shift: the dripping from every eaves in counterpoint, swallows reeling out of the warm ducts, frogs tuning up in the culvert and the year-round puddle under Tank No. 2 and a hundred barrels, crows having one last argument on the intake towers, a heron complaining at the holding pond, deer picking over the warm pavement on feet that click. And the people listen back. Not sentimentally. Competitively, almost. *Hear that? Chain on the north gate. Hear that? Your boy, laughing, three streets over.* Their deafness has made connoisseurs of them in the gaps. They can identify, blindfold, whose generator, whose loose shutter, whose radio left on in an empty dorm. They hear the owl beyond the last street as a town hears a bell.

They are not shy either. There hasn't been time, and there aren't enough of them, and the deer in the dormitory corridors licking condensation off the pipes have set a general tone. People walk into each other's unfenced gardens. People borrow and don't quite return. Children range the empty lots in a pack that crosses age lines because there is no other way to make a pack. Adolescence, in a place this small and this loud, is endured mostly in the outflow jungle and the skate-park-turned-frog-pond, in a first pair of protectors painted black with marker, in learning to drive the water truck a year before the law downcountry would allow and being waved on by everyone because the law here is largely who's watching and whether the road through the cut is clear.

They are waiting, supposedly. That's the official shape of their lives: the grid will fill, the cinema foundation will be a cinema, there will be a second school, the reservoir will be finished and the tokens retired. Downcountry management sends plans. The plans get pinned up in the mess hall and curl in the damp. And yet if you stay a season you notice nobody is holding their breath. They repaint hydrants that serve no houses. They number addresses on lots with nothing on them and keep the numbers legible. They teach their children the names of streets that end in fern walls. The maintenance of the unbuilt town is not optimism, exactly. It's closer to the painters' war: you keep a thing up because keeping it up is the job, and the job is the place, and the place is — this comes out slowly, over projected conversations and sudden quiet ones — home in a way the dry towns that drink their water mostly are not.

They roar all day to make, drop by drop, a surprisingly small amount of anything. They have learned patience from the machines and from the alders adding a foot a year and from the gullies learning their courses down a cut that was raw rock when some of them arrived. They speak loudly to be heard and save their truest voices for the gaps. They live, wet and rationed and half-deaf and green at the edges, in the middle of a loud, dripping machine for turning fog into a future — and in the seven minutes when it stops, when the swallows pour out and the fog comes visibly spiraling back in and somewhere an owl calls too loud and too close, they do not look like people exiled at the edge of an industry.

They look like people who have been put, deliberately, inside an ear — and have learned, together, how to listen out of it.
