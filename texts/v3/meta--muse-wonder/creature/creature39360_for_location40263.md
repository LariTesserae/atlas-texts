It would be easy, at first, to say the swifts live there.

They are the first thing you can point to. Hundreds of them packed into the solution holes in the limestone wall, pouring out in one shrieking braid, turning inside the field as if the rim of the bowl were the rim of the world. They own the height of the place. They make the air seem cut.

But they do not quite live there. They rent the wall. Their wings are made for long weather, for distances — they just cannot make themselves cross the meniscus when they are hunting, because inside it the insects are thick and slow and confused by sound arriving from the wrong direction. The swifts have learned the confusion. That is all.

The frogs might be next. The copper thumbnail frogs in the leaf mold under the tree ferns, each one ballooning a throat sac absurdly out of proportion to its body and calling as if it intended to be heard on the plateau. Down among the millipedes and the orchid roots and the dark circles of leaf mold, the frogs are a ground-floor nation. They lay their eggs in the living soil between the plants. They dislike the warm stone path in daylight and will not cross it. Their chorus rises near the fig tree in the evening until the fig leaves tremble.

But the frogs could live in any wet crack, given the water. So could the millipedes. So could the tree ferns. The beetles are closer to belonging — the castanet beetles down by the fallen pylon, clicking in synchrony with the field pulse, their shells grown over generations into little tuned boxes, hollow-backed, resonant. They have clearly been shaped by the hollow in a way they could not be shaped anywhere else.

Still, the beetles are only keeping a time that something else taught them.

You notice it first in the lizards.

They are translucent, almost glassy, with the organs showing through in faint, pearly smudges. They sit on the sunlit stone near the cups, utterly still except for the quick, independent swivel of their eyes. A dragonfly passes. The lizard launches.

It should fall. That is the simple part. It does not fall at once. It hangs.

Not gliding — there is almost no wing, only a frill of skin along the ribs that opens at the moment of the leap. Not floating, exactly. It stops in the air near a pylon, where the standing wave is strongest, and for a beat too long it is simply there, suspended at the node of a sound you cannot hear cleanly because the field folds it back before your ear can place it. Then the mouth, already open, already full, closes, and the lizard drops in a neat arc to the stone and is still again.

The juveniles do that. A second, two seconds. Hunting practice.

The adults never come down.

That is the thing that lives there.

You don't see one until the dawn pulse, when the pylons bead and the mist precipitates in sheets out of empty air. Then, for a moment, the water finds an outline that isn't a plant. Something long is hanging above the fallen pylon, at head height and then higher, turning very slowly in the acoustic current. The mist pearls on a back. A ribbed frill opens and closes, not flapping, not straining — rippling, the way a curtain ripples over a vent. For an instant there is a shadow on the stone with nothing visible to cast it, and then the sun burns the mist off and the animal goes back to being a distortion, a place where the dragonflies inexplicably change course.

Over a morning you learn to find them by indirection. There are perhaps a dozen. Perhaps fewer, and the field's habit of rehearsing a sound a fraction before it happens makes each one seem doubled. They occupy fixed stations, one above each pylon and the others at lesser nodes where the folded sound crosses itself against the bowl wall. They have territories, but the territories are not on the ground. They are volumes: a tilted lens of air above the fig tree, a slow spiral over the stone cups, a flat, patient ceiling near the south wall where the listening horns in the old shelter point up and have been pointing up, unattended, for decades.

A grown hanger is the length of a person's forearm, then half again as much in tail. Its body is flattened, not like a lizard pressed down to stone but like something pressed out by pressure from above and below. The spine is a pale cord. The ribs have grown hollow and slightly separated, and when the noon crest builds and the cicadas make the whole bowl uninhabitable to conversation, you can see those ribs move in sympathy with pressures you feel in your teeth. Its skin is translucent when young. In the adults it clouds to a milky, opalescent white, except along the belly, where it stays clear enough that a swallowed dragonfly shows as a dark, struggling knot working slowly backward.

It has almost no eyes left. Two wet, black beads, sunk under the skin at the sides of the head, useful perhaps for telling noon from midnight. What it has instead is ear, all the way down. A membrane runs the length of each side, pleated like the inside of a shell, and the frill along its ribs is fringed with filaments so fine the dawn droplets snag on them. It hears the way other animals breathe: constantly, without alternative. It knows a moth — those furred, deliberately silent moths, the only quiet things in the hollow — not by sound at all, but by the hole the moth makes moving through the chorus. It knows a swift by the scream arriving from the wrong direction and corrects for the field's lie without thinking. It knows you, down on the stone path, by the footstep that meets you before you feel it in your boot.

It has never heard the world without that rehearsal. Outside the meniscus it would be deaf in the way a fish out of water is blind.

And it would fall. This is not flight. The animal is heavier than the air and knows it. What holds it is the old machinery's accident — phased sound, layer on layer, building pockets of pressure in the bowl that never quite dissipate. The adults have learned, over whatever slow count of generations the pylons have been running, to find those pockets with the frill and lie on them the way a gull lies on a cliff updraft, except the updraft never moves and never stops. They make minute adjustments all day: a tilt of the tail, a ripple passing from shoulder to hip, one pleated membrane cupping harder against the wave. At night they sink lower, into warmer air, and drink the droplets that fall upward again, catching them on the filaments of the frill and drawing the skin through the mouth in slow, combed strokes.

The water does something to them. Their blood carries the faintest blue charge from the nanomachinery in the cups, and after a heavy pulse an adult will sometimes give up its station and describe a slow, attentive circle in the air, round and round the bowl at wall height, head cocked membrane-first toward sounds that haven't fully arrived yet. The young ones do it in spirals and lose the wave and tumble, catching themselves badly on the stone. The adults do it like listening is a place they can go.

They breed during the surges.

When the whole system locks — condensation beading out of clear air, frogs and cicadas and beetles rising to one tempo, swifts pouring from the wall in a single body — the nodes in the field tighten, and stations that are meters apart the rest of the day are suddenly, acoustically, adjacent. The hangers leave their volumes. They slide along standing waves that exist only for those thirty seconds, frills fully open, hollow ribs sounding a note so low you register it as the pylons dimming. Mating is brief, blind, conducted entirely by pressure on the membranes. Then the system loosens, the rhythms stagger apart, and each animal must find its own air again or fall.

The young are born falling. That is not a metaphor. A female carries two, sometimes three, until they are long enough to show through her clear belly like commas. When they come they come in a surge if she can time it, dropping from her station with their frills sealed wet against their sides. They have the length of the fall to open. Most do. They catch a lower, weaker node near the ground — and that is why the juveniles hunt from the stone and hang only a beat too long, working their way up year by year, station by station, learning a map that is not drawn anywhere except in their own ribs. The ones who miss the catch hit warm limestone among the frog burrows. The beetles find what the millipedes leave within the day. A footprint in that soil stays indented and flooded for days; a small body does not last the afternoon.

The old ones die still holding their place, for a while. When a hanger's heart stops, the frill stays open out of stiffness, the hollow ribs stay tuned, and the field goes on lifting a body that no longer adjusts to it. Dead, they turn very slowly above the pylons, sometimes for days, giving off in the noon crest a faint, pure note as the cicada-pressure plays across the ribcage. The other animals ignore it. The swifts fly around it. Eventually the filaments dry, the membrane tears at one pleat, and the body sideslips out of its node and comes down — or, on a wet night when the droplets are falling upward, rises a little first, uncertainly, before it falls.

That, finally, is who the shelter was for. Not a keeper of the pylons; the pylons never needed one. An observer, someone who grew ceramic listening horns warm as skin and pointed them at the south wall's empty air and stayed long enough to understand that the emptiness had residents. The cot frame is empty, the crate is still sweating cold, because the work was only ever listening, and whoever did it eventually left the hollow to the one animal that could not leave with them.

Up on the plateau the wind combs grit over stone that finished changing eons ago, and there is nothing to see.

Twelve meters down, in a room of folded sound, a dozen blind, translucent animals lie on air that should not hold them, drinking rain that never belonged to a cloud, hearing your next footstep arrive before you take it — native, at last, to a place that was built by accident and then, patiently, by them.
