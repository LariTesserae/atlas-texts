From below you would not see them at all. You would hear them first, long before the rim.

Sound spills over the lip of the crater the way the smoke does — a clank of donkey bells, goats somewhere impossible, a woman calling and another woman answering, hammers ticking, pigeons bursting — all of it echoing down the smooth outer folds of the mountain, so that the place sounds larger and closer than it is. Then, on the switchbacks, you begin to meet them coming down through the red dust: boys, mostly, barefoot, driving the morning train to the seam-spring halfway down. They are small, brown with dust, unhurried even downhill. They shout at the donkeys not because the donkeys need it but because at this height everything needs pushing, even a voice. Their chests work visibly. They stop often, without embarrassment, hands on knees in the thin air, and let the animals stop too.

That is the first lesson of the people who live in the bowl: they have not conquered the altitude, they have negotiated with it. They are not tall. Generations in that saucer of trapped, warm air have made them compact, broad through the ribcage, deep-breathing, slow-hearted. Their movements have a deliberate incompleteness to them — work done sitting down wherever possible, weaving, grinding, scraping hides, mending harness, shelling and sorting and beating, all of it at floor level, on low stools, in doorways. They rise for a task, do it in a short, efficient spell, and settle again. Old men deliberate for whole mornings in the same patch of shade, shifting only as the rim-shadow moves. Children, who cannot be made entirely economical, run the lanes in bursts and then flop panting against walls while lizards regard them without moving.

They live in green half-light, and it has marked them. For most of the day the rim steals the sun, and the fig crowns lean together over the roofs, and the hanging cloth — madder-red, indigo, undyed wool — dims what light remains. Their eyes are accustomed to amber gloom, to finding a stair by the feel of its stone dish, to knowing a passage by its smell: woodsmoke in this one, tanning urine in that, hot corn, crushed herbs, goat. At noon, when the light falls straight into the crater for its brief hour, they squint and shade their faces like people coming up from a cellar. Walls blaze white, leaves flare, and work pauses. Babies are carried into doorways. The old monkey by the main cistern closes his eyes. Then the shadow returns and everyone blinks back into their real day.

The dust is on them always. It is red, powdery, warm, and it gets into the weave of clothes, into hair, into the lines of hands and feet. Since water is too precious to wash away what will return in an hour, they wipe rather than wash — a dampened corner of cloth across the face in the morning, leaving clean tracks; hands rubbed in ash and grit after work; feet simply brushed before bed. Their soles are broad and horny as leather. Their skin takes on the color of their houses, sun-dried brick and basalt and dust, burnished in places the way the door lintels are burnished: forehead, cheekbone, the back of the neck where a carrying strap rides, palms polished by metate and loom and rope.

Everyone here knows water by sound before they know it by sight. They are born in houses that have a dark mouth under the floor, and as children they learn to lie awake before dawn and listen to the cistern drinking: a slow, irregular plinking from the roof gutters, drop by drop through bamboo and copper, each house with its own small tune. A mother can tell by that sound, without lifting the stone, how deep her water stands and whether the night dew was generous. A thrifty household sounds different from a wasteful one. And waste is remembered. To spill a cup is not a sin, exactly, but it is a story — *the summer Ana's boy knocked the jar at the fig tree steps* — retold because water is the town's one true wealth, carried up on donkey-back in sloshing skins, caught from mist by air-plants, sweated out of stone at night. Jars of urine stand neatly by doors for the tanners and the trees. Pots shine from scouring with sand. Nothing is sluiced, rinsed, poured away. Their patience is not an abstract virtue; it has been trained into them cup by cup.

It has made them close, in every sense. Roof touches roof, and so does life. There is no privacy of walls when the crater hands every sound back — a mason's tap three lanes over, hides being beaten, corn grinding house to house in that slow gritty tide, a couple quarreling, a child learning its letters aloud, someone singing to bees in the wall-hives. People modulate not by whispering, which the stone betrays anyway, but by timing, by curtains, by turning a face to the wall. They know each other acoustically the way other peoples know faces: by a rooster's pitch, by a particular donkey's bell, by whose goats have got onto whose thatch again. Gossip travels across the roofs faster than a boy can run the lanes, women calling news while their hands never stop on the stones.

And they are rarely alone among their own species. Chickens negotiate underfoot in the market. Lizards hold title to the sunny sides of walls and are never disturbed. Pigeons pour out of the holes left for them in the upper houses and settle again in a cooing that underlies everything, and their droppings go to the figs, as everything goes somewhere useful. Dogs sleep in the exact centers of passages, confident of being stepped around. At night bats come out of the rim over the lamp-lit roofs, and the people go to bed early under that soft erratic flight, partly because oil is dear and partly because the day's work begins in darkness, listening for drips.

They eat what thrives without a stream: corn ground daily, beans, figs fresh and dried, prickly pear fruit and pads, agave hearts in lean years, goat cheese, eggs, now and then a chicken that has stopped laying. Food is seasoned with ash, herbs from wall cracks, honey from the house-walls. It is not a hungry town, but it is a measured one. A fig dropping with a soft thud onto thatch is heard, and fetched. A cistern falling an inch a week is discussed like weather.

Time, for them, does not move in the valley's way. Nothing on the mountain seems to be changing any longer — the peak long gone, the slopes rounded to their final curves — and the town has taken the same shape. A wall falls and is rebuilt with its own stones, in the same place, by the grandchildren of the man who built it, who now sleep in the room their grandparents were born in, above the cistern their great-grandparents cut. Roofs are rethatched a strip at a time across a season. Loom patterns repeat. Names repeat. A girl learns the exact note her roof makes in rain by sleeping where her mother slept. To leave is possible — the donkey path goes down, boys see the valley — but to leave is to leave the only shade, the only gathered water, the only walls that know your sound. Most come back up by evening, breathing hard, carrying something metal: a hinge, a knife, a bell, bright and prized among all that wood and clay and fiber.

So they remain, thick and noisy and rooted as the figs over their roofs: a small, dense people in a dim warm bowl at the top of a worn-out mountain, entirely surrounded by sky and entirely without water to spare, listening to themselves echo off ancient stone — and listening, each night, a little before dawn, for the slow plink under the floor that tells them they can live another day.
