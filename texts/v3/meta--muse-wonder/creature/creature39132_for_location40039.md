If you descend into it — and descent is the only way in, hand over slick hand through the layered leaf until the hard sky is gone — you do not meet its inhabitants one at a time. You meet them as pressure.

First, at the top, where almost nothing stays: the brief fliers. Swifts and swallows and iridescent insects that live their whole bright lives in the forty meters above the canopy and only dip down when a storm shears them sideways. They are pale underneath, frantic, unadapted to the dark. They do not belong. They visit.

Below them the valley begins to keep things.

In the high canopy, in that green twilight where leaves are black-green and bottle-green and their undersides flash maroon and copper, live the crashers: troops of long-limbed, heavy-browed monkeys, or things doing monkey-work so completely the distinction stops mattering. They never fully land. They sleep each night in a new crook of branches, they give birth holding on with three limbs, they die, often, by falling and being caught halfway down by the next story of growth. Their hands are black to the wrist — stained by tannin, by hammering, by a lifetime handling wet bark — and clever in a way that never builds up. Down on the limestone anvils where rock shoulders through the floor, the old females carry nuts up from the seeps along with smooth river-stones that fit the palm, seat the nut, and hammer. Crack. Eat. Drop the stone. The young sit shoulder to shoulder, shoulder blades touching, and learn by stealing the split halves and getting cuffed. A particularly good stone may be picked up for years after its owner dies, recognized by hands that watched her. Then it sinks into the leaf-mold one wet season and no one mourns it. There is no pile. There is no shrine. Knowledge here lives only in watching eyes, and dies unless it is copied body to body.

They are courteous before a fruit-fall, suddenly. That is how you know it is coming. And when a mast tree ripens all at once in the dark they arrive screaming, fighting, drunk before they have eaten, while below them the rest of the valley arrives too.

Because below the monkeys, occupying the trunk-space, is everything that flies without daylight. Moths big as two hands, dusted grey and brown and blind-looking, crowding any paler bark. Beetles the size of thumbs blundering leaf to leaf with their wing-cases open, smelling of resin and rot. Bats in their thousands that at dusk untangle themselves from hollow trunks and pour through the forest in a river twenty minutes long, bending around trees without thinning, stirring the air so thoroughly that a pale fungus waiting all day in the gloom fruits at their passing — a brief white snow of spores smelling of yeast and old blood. Birds you will never properly see live sideways to this traffic, tucked into tangles that merely stop the drip: they announce themselves only as sound, a single fluted whistle from nowhere, a boom that shakes condensation out of the leaves, a burst of maniacal laughter directly overhead from a throat you cannot find. One glossy, crow-like bird among them fishes termites with a thorn held crosswise in its beak and throws the tool away the instant the soldiers swarm. No nests persist. Eggs are laid in warm leaf-mold and guarded by a body, not a building. Anything built would be eaten, rotted, or shaken apart in a week.

Lower still, where the light comes only in solid dusty columns that die before they are useful, the plants themselves are inhabited. There is very little standing water on the floor, so the frogs live up inside the architecture: in bromeliad cups, in curled leaves, in the inch of rain held by a split seed-pod. Hundreds of kinds, or one kind in hundreds of tempers. Green and glassy, orange-toed, throat-sacs pulsing, calling from inside the leaves so the plants seem to be shouting for mates, for territory, for rain. Their droppings feed the throat of the plant that holds them. Their refusal to sit in a leaf is the surest sign its water is foul. Around them geckos click on bark. Lizards bark, unexpectedly loud. Small striped mammals — solitary, long-snouted, forever levering up bark with a wedged stone they immediately abandon — snuffle through the damp for grubs. Snakes go through the litter making a sound like poured sand.

And the litter itself is not ground. It is a population. Ants make living roads up trunks and across gaps on bridges of their own clasped bodies, and where an ant road crosses a slug trail there is a brief silent war that ends with both routes redrawn. Termites soften fallen giants until in a month a tree is only an outline, in three a soft green hill of fern and fungus. Cicadas and crickets keep up the continuous sizzling hiss that is not background but weather — a pressure on the eardrums, the texture of the air itself. To live here anything with ears has learned to hear *through* it, the way you learn to see through rain. The frogs pulse above it. The howlers roar above that and shake the water out of the canopy. It is a map, if you know how to read it: frog-pitch dropping where water is ahead, monkey courtesy where fruit will fall, a sudden predatory sweetness in the smell where pulp is splitting.

On the floor proper — thin, absurdly rich soil over young, sharp rock — move the large ones, mostly at night. Something hoofed and heavy-shouldered roots up tubers, ploughing through understory, eating fast and noisily and never alone for long. Because something else, also large, prefers the game trails it makes. That one waits in absolute stillness, breathing through its mouth so the breath makes no whistle in the damp, and it is the only true silence in the valley. When it moves, the chorus stutters: a half-second hitch rippling outward as frog, insect, bird, and monkey freeze in sequence. That tear in the sound is its shape. Everything small has evolved around it — around listening for the place where listening stops.

Nothing here is built for permanence, because the valley is not permanent. Quakes rearrange the seeps where clear mineral water beads, fills a handspan, and sinks again. A ridge-face slumps and opens a bright sun-wound, and a hundred species pivot to exploit the light before the canopy closes it. Roots pry fresh cracks wider at night; you can hear the rock ticking as it cools under the animal noise. So the inhabitants grow indecently fast, breed in leaf-mold and plant-cups and the crooks of branches, replace themselves before rot can finish, and move — always move — with the feeding fronts as they pass like weather from mast tree to mast tree, brawling hectare by hectare: parrots screaming down, pigs underneath, wasps drunk on split fruit, butterflies lapping at the carpet of pulp, predators drifting patiently at the edges taking the distracted.

They are not quiet, or still, or careful, because those strategies belong to emptier places. Here emptiness on the floor is not absence but preparation, a stone may be a tool or a weapon or an egg-case or asleep, and survival looks like being loud in the right register, wet always, saturated in color even though no one can quite see color — waxy red, bruised purple, thick yellow, copper undersides flashing as a signal in the wind — and moving with the front instead of against it.

Go in and they will not, at first, seem like separate creatures at all. They will seem like one continuous, deafening occupation, layered from swift to spore, that tears down its own scaffolding while it builds it, eats its houses, forgets its tools, and holds itself together — over rock not yet decided on its final shape — largely by volume.
