They hear the town before you do, and they hear it differently.

From the wet stair they will know — before any pipe is visible in the twilight — whether the boiler is warming or cooling, whether the filtration tanks sighed early, whether a valve three tiers up has begun to stick in a way that will matter in a month. A child born by ELBOW 9 can put a palm flat to a retaining wall and tell you if the car is coming round the second tier, not by the sound, but by the soft increase of hum in the iron under the skin. Diagnosis is not a trade here. It is how conversation starts. *It's holding,* they'll say, about a pressure line, a marriage, a knee, a pregnancy, meaning the same thing in each case: it will keep, for now, if no one makes it hurry.

There are perhaps two hundred of them. Perhaps fewer in winter when some of the younger ones go up for surface work and do not come back until the light hurts them less, perhaps more in flood season when everyone is moved in together on planks. No one keeps a census that agrees with the city above. Above, on paper, there is a maintenance crew of eleven. Below, eleven would not feed the cats.

They are not troglodytes and they are not blind, though both stories are told on the surface by people who have seen one of them come up the stair at noon. They are pale in a particular way — not sickly, not translucent, but rinsed. Sun does not brown them so much as bewilder them. Their forearms show blue where a surface laborer's would show tan. Their hair, worn short by most and tied back wet by the rest, dries all day and never quite finishes. In the boiler room in winter, the older women sit with their hair spread over the warm tanks while the moths collect, and for an hour the air smells of clean wool and hot iron and human scalp, the driest smell in town.

Their eyes have made an arrangement with the place. In the perpetual twilight after rain that passes for day, they move without lamps through yards of wet stone they know by drainage pattern, by which grating sings under a wet foot, by the difference between a drip that will fall on the left shoulder and one that will fall on the right. Look up the center light shaft with them and they will not squint at the bright coin of sky; they will look slightly aside from it, at the dark around it, where the bats are watching back. When they must go above on business they wear goggles cut from the same thick green glass as the chapel windows, and they come home with headaches behind the eyes and a ringing quietness, and they sit a long time on the lower steps with their trousers already rolled, letting the cool air coming up find them again.

Because that is the uniform, more than any municipal green or gray repainted coat, more than the oilskin aprons in the pump house or the polished rust-red varnish that has got into the creases of every working hand: trousers rolled. To the knee, to mid-calf, according to the season and the tier. Skirts are hitched and pinned. Children go bare-legged as a matter of course. Feet are broad from catwalk grating and stone, nails kept very short because damp makes them soft, soles unembarrassed by an inch of perfectly clear water standing over iron plate. The lowest houses are islands for weeks in spring and their people come and go by plank with a shopping basket on one hip, stepping without looking, without haste, while black eels investigate their ankles and are gently, apologetically, nudged aside with a toe. An eel in a street is a colleague on an errand. You do not startle it.

They speak low, but not from meekness. On the upper tiers in daytime no voice is raised at all because of the breathing ceiling in the unlit gallery, thousands of bats folded into a second vault that must not be poured out before its time. The custom follows them down even where it isn't needed. They leave pauses in the middle of sentences that surface people keep trying to fill, listening — to a pump changing cycle, to a heron landing statue-heavy at the pool margin, to whether that new drip is one drip or the start of a weep. Nothing here needs to happen quickly ever again, and their speech has taken the shape of that. Arguments are long and contain a lot of weather. Courtship is mostly a matter of showing up with dry kindling, or remembering which cup catches which north-wall seep, or being entrusted, one evening, with feeding the granary cats from the night pumpman's own tin because the night pumpman has decided to sleep beside a human being instead of beside the turbine for once.

Work arranges the generations. There are the pump people, a loose priesthood of families who can read the one remaining turbine the way surface doctors read a chest, who know the boiler's tick as it cools and warms across a day is a language with perhaps forty words, all of them important. There are the machinists in the long quiet shifts, turning valves and impellers on a lathe at a pace a body can sleep beside, restoring cast pipe that came down the freight ramp scarred and will go up again months later without anyone above remembering where it has been. There are pipe-walkers who patrol the circumference with wrenches as thick as their arms and tin cups for new weeps. There are eel-keepers — usually old men, usually widowed, volubly cranky — who keep the meat account for the pool, apologizing out loud, by name sometimes, for any eel taken from a pipe, and replacing it formally so the intakes stay clean from inside. There are two women at LOWER SUMP 3 who keep the fern altar by the warm outflow and are consulted about fevers. There is a keeper of the fig tree on the third tier who has, for nineteen years, successfully prevented anyone from fixing the burst main its roots live in, on the grounds that the tree now bears pale, enormous leaves and, twice, fruit like cold green lamps, and roots are a kind of pipe anyway.

Children belong, in practice, to the tiers. A baby is born, most often, in the chapel made from the switch-gear cabin, because its green-glass windows make even the work-lamps gentle and because the midwife — currently a VALVE ROW daughter with forearms like rope — says nothing born under green light cries for long. Babies are carried in slings against damp wool and learn the pump sigh before they learn to fear anything. Toddlers are set down, without discussion, a brick's height above any floor, on principle. School is the market when it isn't flooded and the pump house gallery when it is: letters stenciled first on pipes, arithmetic taught in gallons and minutes and feet of rise, history taught as plumbing. *Your great-grandmother decided not to abandon this,* they are told, at ELBOW 9, at VALVE ROW, as other children are told about wars. It is not presented as a marvel. It is presented as a housekeeping decision that got out of hand in the best way.

They eat what can be kept a tier above rats and bargained down a freight ramp: grain from the second-tier granaries the cats formally guard, salt meat, dried things, groceries that arrive with the coal on the single electric car, which they pay for mostly in repaired ironmongery and in the city's continued, unexamined ability to drink. From down here they take sparingly and with ceremony — black fish from the shallows, never the blind white ones, who are left to their slower country in the deep pipes; frogs on certain evenings, individual and spaced, not a chorus; figs when the tree consents. The herons who spiral down the main shaft at dusk are not fed and not shooed. They are neighbors with fishing rights, older in the town in spirit if not in fact, and a boy who once threw a stone at one was made to stand still in the pool margin until his legs went numb and he understood statues from the inside.

Time here does not run wrong. It runs three minutes slow by spring, and agrees with the pumps, and that is enough. Shift change is not a bell — there are no bells — but the bats pouring up through the shafts for twenty minutes, darkening even the dimness, and the whole iron lace of catwalks answering very softly under people going home. Flood season is not dreaded and not celebrated. Furniture rises a shelf, a tier; the market migration happens with the unhurried competence of laundry being taken in; silt is swept afterward into patterns — spirals, pipe-maps, the silver routes of snails enlarged in mud by children with brooms — and left until the next water edits it. Someone's grandfather dies, most often near the boiler where it is warm and his joints have finally stopped negotiating, and he is not buried, there being no earth that is not accounted for by water. He is kept one night in the green chapel while the turbine breathes. Then the car carries him, and his people walking the circumference with their heads uncovered under the blinking tell-tales, up the freight ramp in the early hours, to whatever plot the surface still reserves for the maintenance crew. Eleven plots, officially. The surface groundskeeper stopped counting long ago. It seemed rude, and anyway the grass there stays strangely, beautifully damp.

They are not cut off. That would require effort in both directions. A girl from LOWER SUMP 3 went up at seventeen because she wanted to see weather happen all at once, and came back at nineteen with surface money, sunburn peeling from her nose, and a horror of overhead noise she described as *everything arriving without passing through anything first.* A boy stayed up, sends down batteries and novels swollen before they're opened, is spoken of fondly and without mystery, like a cousin in a drier country. Love and leaving both happen. What does not really happen is arrival. Surface people can visit — a city inspector every few years, in boots too new, talking too loud on the upper tiers until a child, politely, puts a finger to her own lips and points up at the sleeping dark, at which the inspector goes very quiet too and remembers it for years. But visiting is one thing and adjusting is another. Skin adjusts. Eyes adjust. Or they don't, and the stair takes you back up.

At night, when the surface grates are shut and the lamps go to half, the town reduces to points of amber in doorways and they reduce with it: silhouettes crossing three tiers up, a couple wading home through clear water with their shoes in their hands, the night pumpman doing his rounds with the cat tin, someone singing, very low, a song whose rhythm is wrong for dancing and right for a lathe. If you stand among them then you understand that they are not hiding down here, and they are not trapped down here, and they are not waiting. The reservoir finished failing into its shape long ago, and so have they. They live in the cool, wet hollow of the city's heart and keep it beating at a pace blood can match — rolled up, unhurried, one hand on a warm pipe, listening in the pause between one drop finding its level and the next for the eel turning, the turbine breathing, the fig leaves ticking with moisture, the people they love moving softly on the iron above them in the dark.
