She is the only thing in there that is not the place itself.

At first you might not count her as an inhabitant at all. The room is so empty, and she is so much the color of it when she is still — wet grey fur darkened by the damp, pink skin showing through where the fur thins — that she reads as a shadow in the architecture. A dimple where three ribs meet, low on the wall. A slight thickening there. Then the thickening breathes.

She is shrew-like, but not a shrew. Small enough to lose in one cupped hand, if she would ever allow it, which she would not. Long in the body, low, trembling at a speed that seems too fast for the warm slowness around her. Shrews burn like that, living an hour ahead of themselves with hunger, and she does too: even asleep she shivers in small, constant adjustments, nose working, whiskers fanning and folding.

She is blind. Not eyeless — there are two dark places under the translucent skin of her face where eyes started and then were covered over, sealed by a thin gold-pink membrane veined like the walls. She does not need them in a place that makes its own daylight. She navigates by the other weather in the room: the pressure of mist arriving on her whiskers a fraction before it arrives, the creak of a root growing through the outer flesh telegraphed through the rib under her feet, the smell of a vein about to bead — green, iron-sweet, hotter — before it does.

Her snout is long and mobile, almost a small trunk, naked and intensely pink and always wet. It never stops testing the air. Her ears are mere ridged folds tight against her skull, better at feeling sound through bone than catching it from the air, which is useful here, where sound is unreliable and often early. Her forepaws are surprisingly hand-like, pale, five-toed, the thumbs not quite opposed but capable of pinching, holding. Her hind feet are broader, splayed, with soft pads that grip the living wall even where it sweats in its cycles and turns slick. Her tail is bare and segmented, pink-grey, and she curls it around root-hairs for balance when she crosses what should be the ceiling and is, for her, just another floor.

Because gravity in the center is weak and pulls toward the nearest wall, it barely argues with someone her weight. She does not distinguish up from sideways. She walks the vault as easily as the ground, pauses upside down — from your point of view — to drink from a cup-leaf, her whole body adhering, her fur hanging faintly toward the middle of the void rather than toward you.

The dimple is her home in the way a hollow in a cheek is a home. She has made it more itself. Into its edges she has woven lengths of fallen root-fiber, cut to surprisingly even pieces, tucked and knotted and rewoven as the wall grows and shifts the shape underneath her. There is no pattern you could copy. It is not a nest built by instinct repeated ten thousand times by ten thousand ancestors; it is one animal solving damp and slope and a rib that keeps lengthening a hair at a time. It compresses under her when she sleeps and springs back sparsely when she leaves. A few black seeds, pea-sized, she has pried loose before they could embed — or perhaps collected after a fall that never happened — are stored along one edge, hard and faintly ticking. She noses them sometimes, does not eat them, rearranges them as if counting in a language that is mostly touch.

And she has the tool.

That is the thing you keep returning to. Not the blindness, not the wall-walking. The tool.

A thorn snapped — not chewed, snapped — from near the entrance slit while it was still glassy and new. She broke it at a length just longer than her own forearm and then committed an act of patience entirely at odds with her frantic metabolism: she wrapped the blunt base again and again with fine root-fiber until it was thick enough to hold, and sealed the wrapping with sap that she coaxed out and allowed to harden between applications. The handle is ugly. It is also fitted, unmistakably, to her paws. Darkened where she grips it. Worn smooth on one side.

With it she feeds.

There is a particular vein that swells along the rib beside her dimple after the mist cycle. She climbs to it — three quick dashes, a pause, a dash — and sets the thorn tip against a seam you wouldn't have noticed. She taps. She pries with a little twisting motion of both forelegs, her whole spine braced against the wall. The plant resists, then yields a bead of milky fluid, opalescent, smelling of starch and crushed stems.

You hear the scrape of the thorn a heartbeat before you see the pale shaving curl away from the wall. It doesn't startle her anymore. If anything, she works by it. She lifts the tool, stops — intent, whiskers still — and only when the ghost of the scrape reaches her does she actually move her paws. She is living slightly ahead in the room's loose time, listening to her own actions arrive. When the bead wells, she laps it directly from the vein with a tongue quick and narrow as a moth's, then taps the wound closed again, smoothing the sap over it with the flat of the handle. Not always. Not tenderly. Practically, the way you would close a tap so it does not drip all night.

She uses the thorn for other things: to sever a dangling fiber, sawing; to lever a spore case open; to scratch a route through a place where a new pore has opened and makes the wall too slick. She keeps it tucked into the weaving of her dimple when she sleeps, sharp end out. When she wakes — which is often, in brief burns between short sleeps — her paw finds it before her nose lifts.

She ignores you completely if you learn how to be ignored. Stay still, breathe shallowly, let the room take up your breathing and practice it back from behind the wall, and she will go about her rounds within a foot of your boot: down the rib, across the floor that gives and firms under even her slight weight, up to drink condensed water one mouthful at a time from the cup-leaves. The cloud passing above that you register as a twenty-second dimming, she registers earlier, freezing before the light changes, her fur already flattening for cooler air that has not cooled.

Move suddenly, though, and the room betrays you. Your shift becomes a low drum-roll off the far wall, and she is gone — not run, gone, pressed so flat into her dimple that the woven fibers close over her back and she becomes the wall again. She will stay there long past the time the sound has stopped, because for her some part of it may not have happened yet.

No mate comes to her. No young are hidden in the fiber. No trail of droppings or bones marks a territory; the floor heals, the plant absorbs, the air is filtered almost clean of her except for the warm, mammalian fug that gathers briefly around her sleeping place: milk, wet fur, old sap on small hands. Whether she found the slit last spring before the root arched shut, whether she was already in the soil when the bulb swelled around a pocket and simply adapted to being inside a germinating thing, whether the plant grew her tolerances the way it grew its light — there is no sign to read. She is solitary in a way that seems complete rather than lonely. The room accommodates her because she is small, and warm, and does not try to make it permanent.

That is perhaps why she belongs here more than you ever could. She does not speak, and so the room cannot complete her voice. She does not build beyond what one night's damp requires, and so when a seam lengthens she rewoves a single strand and goes back to sleep. She expects the tap before the touch.

And she will not last with it. When the bulb finishes this brief experiment with emptiness — when the walls, sighing cell by cell, begin at last to meet in the middle — she will either find the slit thinning with new root and force herself out into the cold leaf-litter and the ordinary dark, tool left behind or clutched absurdly in both forepaws, or she will stay flattened in her dimple while the plant does what it does with any soft thing it encloses.

For now she makes her rounds in the sourceless afternoon, pink and grey and utterly focused, hearing her life happen a breath before she lives it, lapping sweetness from a wound she opens and closes in a room that will outgrow her.
