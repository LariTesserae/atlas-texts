The animals are not the ones who live there. Not really.

They shelter there. They rehearse there. The translucent salamanders keep to the damp crevices, the white crickets practice their jumps along the walls, the little chalk-colored frogs sit with their throats pulsing in the wrong rhythm, but all of them keep to the margins, as everything does. Even the moss, moving its slow green inch around the base of the wall, does not cross the middle.

The middle is occupied.

You would swear at first that it is empty. That is its best trick, and it is not even a trick — it is a condition. In the center, where the sourceless light pools brightest in a clean column, there is nothing to see. Only dust that drifts in and vanishes and reappears a hand’s breadth off course, as if it had been politely moved aside. Only air that is very slightly warmer on one side of your face than the other, though the room gives no heat. Only, now and then, the feeling of being regarded from exactly the height you are not looking at.

If you do not look directly, it begins to assemble.

A paleness inside the paleness. A disturbance in the brightness with soft edges, crouched low, then not crouched, then tall enough that the top of it should brush the breathing ceiling and doesn't. It has the wet translucence of the salamanders and the sealed smoothness of the mouse's eyes and a fine, undecided down along where its back might be, like the frog's. Feather-gills lift at its throat when the air dampens. They settle flat again when it remembers — or decides — that it is breathing air.

It has not finished choosing a face, so a face does not finish happening. Eyes open in the expected place, pale and unfocused, and then, while you are trying to meet them gently, they have thinned to the memory of eyes, and there is a second, fainter pair lower down, still shut, that seems more sincere. Its mouth, when it has one, opens sometimes at the same instant all the bats hanging from the ceiling open theirs. No sound comes out, from them or from it. Several seconds later, from a far corner, you hear the smallest wet click, as if its voice had set out late and gotten lost on the way to you.

It casts the shadows. Not yours — yours stayed outside, flattened dark against the entrance hole, waiting. But when you lift your hands in that bright center, four very faint shadows disagree on the floor, and they are not quite hand-shaped. One has too many long fingers, spread like a spider learning hands. One is small and perfect and six inches to the left. One lags, closing slowly after you have already closed yours. They belong to it. It is trying shadow on, from being near you. It wants to know if it is the sort of thing that can interrupt light.

In a way it is made of light, the way the new crystal in the walls is — lit from within its own marrow. The bats know. When they wake they do not hunt outward. They spiral through that central column with their mouths open and drink, and their bellies glow faintly afterward. It glows too after they have passed, a little brighter in the places their wings disturbed, as though they had stirred it.

It is the builder of the little structures around the edges. That becomes clear if you stay long enough to watch a mud tower slump and, an hour later, find it rebuilt half an inch away, still hollow, still with one round doorway far too small for any body in the room — including its own, at most of its sizes. It builds them the way a child builds a house for a parent: certain of the shape, unsure of the scale. The pebble arch spanning its inch-wide gap, balanced perfect and leading nowhere. The grass-and-feather nest woven in the pattern of a much larger bird's nest, from a memory that cannot be its own yet. The three flat steps by the entrance that climb six inches and stop, made unnecessary when the floor rose to meet them. It keeps making thresholds. It does not seem to mind that nothing can use them. Making them is the use. It is practicing entrance and exit, shelter and span, before it commits to needing any.

It practices everything else through the others, which is why nothing in the room can quite hunt or be hunted. When the salamander flows toward the cricket and snaps shut on empty air, wearing that startled look — that is it, inside the salamander for a moment, misjudging distance through new eyes, forgetting which mouth is which. When the cricket lands a breath before it jumps, that's it again, running cause ahead of effect to see if the order is mandatory. The pillbugs rolling up and forgetting how to unroll are not stupid. They are being briefly inhabited by something that learned the first half of a gesture and is delighted with it. It rocks them, gently, until they remember themselves.

It is not cruel. It is pre-cruel, in the way it is pre-everything. Predation is a shape it is holding up to the light. So is flight. So is green: the fern rehearsing its uncurling beside it every morning, the three grass shoots leaning homesick toward the entrance, are its earlier drafts of wanting something from a distance.

And it is young in a way that has nothing to do with years. It may have been there through a hundred of those fern mornings. But youth here is not a stage passed through; it is the medium. Its skin, on the rare moments it lets a surface settle long enough to be touched, gives like the soft chalk of the walls under your thumb and keeps the print a while, considering it, before smoothing itself away. Put a thumbnail in — it will allow this, the way the young crystals do — and the dent fills, not healed exactly, but revised. This is why your own nails soften sitting near it. This is why a scratch on your knuckle un-bleeds. Proximity to it makes finish negotiable. It is constantly un-becoming its small injuries, and it extends the courtesy without thinking.

It is curious about you in the specific way of something that has never had a visitor with a fixed shape. It follows, not by moving, but by being more present in the part of the room you have turned toward. It studies your hands more than your face — hands are busier, hands have more decisions in them. Sometimes, in your peripheral sight, it has hands too, small and wet-looking, sorting the pebbles that grow on the floor, setting one down larger than it picked it up and seeming faintly surprised at its own work. Sometimes it has the fern's instinct instead and curls, whole, tighter and tighter as the afternoon comes on, as if it could shut itself for the night and dream in that position.

It does not sleep. The light does not dim enough, and it has not decided whether it is a creature that sleeps or a place that does. For the room is also it, or it is also the room, and the grammar has not been settled. When the ceiling sighs down a finger's width and rises again, something in the center sighs with it, exactly out of sync. When a drop of condensation slides sideways along the wall, a bright bead stands for a moment on its shoulder and evaporates before falling. When, deep in the stone, something groans in your molars, it flinches — not at the sound. At the feeling of the walls practicing hardness. Hardness frightens it, a little. It is the one future it can sense: the day the breathing stops mid-breath, the crystal teeth set, the entrance seals or breaks open, and it will have to be at last a cave-thing, eyeless and final in the dark, or a valley-thing under real weather, rained on and rooted. Until then every form is revocable, and it keeps them all, tenderly, in circulation.

That is why the center stays empty and the edges stay crowded. It is keeping its options in orbit around itself.

If you leave — and your shadow will flatten itself thinner with impatience, waiting to reattach — it will not follow you out. It leans toward the entrance the way its grass does, but only with attention. Out there, light falls and burns and comes from somewhere. Out there, things finish, and are eaten correctly, and cast one shadow each. It is not ready to be that singular.

It stays in its lit, unfinished room under the hill: a bright, half-invented inhabitant at the heart of a half-invented cave, trying on gills and hands and hunger and houses, glowing faintly through its own undecided skin, opening its mouth with the bats in a silence that arrives a few seconds late — still practicing, most of all, the difficult art of being one thing, and not yet willing to give up being everything else.
