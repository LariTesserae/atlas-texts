Nothing human lives in the Governor Pit. The divers only visit it, tethered and in pairs, and they time those visits the way you would time a call on a dangerous animal — in the twenty quiet minutes when the levels have equalized and the water, for a little while, is not going anywhere.

What lives there lives by that interval, and by the violence on either side of it.

Begin low, where the light has least to say. The floor is iron plate, buckled, patched, silted over with rust and brick dust and coal-black sediment, and it ticks. Shore crabs walk it on tiptoe, green-brown, their claws held clear of the muck, picking a slow living from the edges of the mussel beds. Their feet make a faint metallic sound on the plates that carries farther than it should in the water, a small irregular percussion under the thump of the pumps next door. Starfish lie flattened over the patch plates and bolt heads, arms following the seams, bodies taking the shape of whatever iron they cover, eating at a pace so slow that, to a diver hanging above them, they seem like another kind of corrosion. Only if you come back week after week do you notice they have moved, and that the mussels under them are gone.

The mussels are the pit made flesh. They pave the lower walls and the flanged feet of the columns in black cobbles of shell, so dense in places that the engineering brick disappears entirely under them. Twice a day, when the tide forces itself under the north gates and the room becomes a river — current hard enough to pin weed and tools and unwary divers against the south grating — they shut. When it slackens, they open. Thousands of them, in unison neither taught nor decided, simply resumed. In the slack they filter, and filter, and the green water that came in opaque with silt after the rush clears again from the bottom up, until a diver can see across the eighteen metres from gate to catwalk and all the suspended matter has passed through living bodies and been laid down as a finer, darker silt upon the plates. They are why the pit is not a drowned cellar full of rot. They are its kidneys, its patient second set of pumps.

Above them, the walls acquire other tenants according to what the water gives them. Barnacles whiten the gate frames where the flow is fastest and food arrives whether they want it or not, their plates opening into brief feathery fans between one surge and the next. Sea anemones — dull red, olive, the colour of wet brick gone soft — have taken the pipe flanges along the south wall and the lee sides of the columns, wherever the current breaks and enriches. They sway together when the turbine begins to turn, tentacles combing the same water, over and over, unhurried by scarcity or plenty because in the pit both arrive on schedule.

The water column itself belongs to the mullet.

A loose, perpetual school of grey mullet circles the two rows of iron columns, round the turbine housing, back along the north wall, round again, turning all at once for no visible signal — or for a signal so small it is only pressure changing against their flanks. They have the columns memorized the way the divers memorize the line: not as a map, but as a sequence of nearness in the gloom. When the gates begin to tremble and the chains sing, the school tightens, angles itself, and lets the current carry it into the sheltered eddies behind the columns rather than fighting to hold a place in the open room. They grew up with gratings for doors. Some of them came in small through the harbor grating, in through the hairline under the sluice gates on a summer tide, and have grown too thick in the body to leave the way they entered; the pit is not a station for them anymore, it is the whole geography of their lives, eighteen metres long.

The bass know this and use it. Two, three, sometimes five sea bass hold station in the lee of the big turbine drum where the current folds and slows, hanging almost motionless, fins making minute corrections, watching the mullet go round. They do not chase often. In a room this size, patience outperforms pursuit — something weakened, something stunned against the grating, something dislodged from the mussel beds will eventually come to them on the water. They are heavy, well-fed fish, with the calm of animals who have never been angled for and have learned that the big shapes that descend occasionally on lines, trailing bubbles and amber light, are neither food nor, if left alone, threat.

At dusk — which in the pit means only that the green columns from the harbor grating fail and the two caged lamps on the west wall take over, their light going browner and more local — the eels come out.

They live in the dead conduits, in disconnected pipe runs and cable ducts that end at blank flanges, in darkness inside the darkness. By day you may see a head at a pipe mouth, tasting the current, and nothing more. By lamp-light they emerge whole, long and unhurried, and move through the machinery as if the machinery had been arranged for them: under the catwalk, over the control stand, between the brass levers, along the chains until the chains vanish down into silt cloud. An eel in the pit does not swim so much as pour itself from one shelter to the next. They hunt the crabs among the mussels, take small fish in the turbine’s shadow, and vanish — completely, impossibly — into apertures a diver would swear were too narrow, when a hand reaches or a torch beam steadies on them. If anything down there can be called a resident in the human sense, with an address and a tenure, it is the eels. Generations may have lived and died without ever seeing open sea.

Then there are the seasonal lodgers, who remind the rest that the room has a sky somewhere. In summer a single kelp frond finds its way in and fastens somehow to the ladder and spends the season streaming from it, reaching, not metaphorically but literally, toward the grating light that is not quite enough to live by, bleaching slowly at the tip. Sea lettuce glows a luminous, improbable green in the same thin sun near the east wall. And in autumn the cuttlefish drift through: solitary, hovering over the submerged catwalk on undulating fins, changing colour against rust and brick and verdigris as they pass, big-eyed and somehow more alien than anything else in the pit precisely because they behave as if merely passing through a landscape, when everything else behaves as if there were no other landscape to pass to. They stay days, sometimes weeks, hunting in the lamp light, and then they are gone out under the gates with the ebb, and the mullet resume their circuit through the volume the cuttlefish briefly made thoughtful.

Even the plants that are not quite plants belong to the rhythm: brown algae bearding the lamp housings, stringy growth trailing from the lamp chains, brightening and dimming as the current combs it one way, then the other. The lamps burn underwater year in, year out, and life has simply incorporated them as two small, permanent, amber suns around which moths will never gather but around which, at night, small translucent things do — specks and larvae and drifting feed, drawn out of the dark, and the eels and bass attending at the edge of the glow.

So the pit is governed again, after all. Not by the iron balls in their encrusted cage above the turbine — though they still stir and clank faithfully when the tide runs, regulating nothing, remembering everything — but by the same pressure they were built to answer. Inhale: the gates complain, the chains lift, the mussels close, the mullet tighten their circle, the bass sink deeper into lee, silt blooms up off the floor and the lamps shrink to smudges. Exhale: the room reverses, the turbine sighs around the other way, weed tears loose from the south grating and tumbles north, and the crabs come out from under the plates to see what the water has rearranged for them. In between, in the astonishing twenty-minute quiet, everything opens, feeds, filters, courts, repairs itself a little, and the water clears to that thick green translucence in which shapes resolve late and gently, as if nothing down there had ever needed to hurry.

It did not need soil, or shore, or dry land at any stage of the tide. It needed only a hard surface to hold to, a reliable current to eat from, and holes too small for anything to be entirely trapped except by growing. The sea provided all three, and moved in — not as an invader flooding a human room, but as an inhabitant finishing one: softening the edges, silting the numbers off the pipes, paving the iron with shell, turning a chamber built to control the tide into a small, complete, breathing piece of the thing it failed to control.
