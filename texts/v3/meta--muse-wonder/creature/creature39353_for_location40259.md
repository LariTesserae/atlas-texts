At first you try to find them the way you would find people anywhere else.

You look for a shift. For doors. For a neighborhood set apart from the work, where the heat-suited figures you see gliding between foundry-groves might go to take their masks off, eat, sleep, be born and grow old. You look for the soft parts that a population leaves behind: laundry, waste that isn't feedstock, children too small to be useful, graves.

There is nowhere for any of that to be. There is no empty ground.

The figures in the heat-suits are not residents. You learn that by watching one long enough. He walks a lane that was cut at dawn, drives phosphor markers into bark with a little hammer — tap, tap, a green point of light that will let the next crew see in the dark — checks a gauge grown into a trunk, and then he goes. Up, eventually, in a service lift, out through the pall into that obscene clean daylight on the rim. Another suit comes down. They are replaced like gloves. Nothing in the caldera learns their names, and they learn nothing down there except where not to stand still.

Because if you stand still, it notices you, in the only way it knows how to notice anything.

Fine root-hairs come out of the lane wall, pale and searching in the black air, and test the seam of your boot. They test your ankle, your tripod, the warm exhalation valve of your mask. Not hungrily. Industriously. Patiently. The way everything here tests a fallen beam or a dead machine, to decide how best to grow through it and make it load-bearing.

That is your first meeting with the inhabitant.

It is not the carts, though for a while you think it might be. The pruning carts move through the tunnels with the slow authority of animals that belong — sawing, mulching, replanting in a single hydraulic passage, keeping the map barely possible for another day. But a cart is only a hand, and it knows it. It follows scent-trails laid in resin, it obeys pressures transmitted up through the root-rails, it kills exactly the growth it is told to kill and no more. When a cart breaks down in a decommissioned vat-house, vines are through its treads by nightfall and through its engine block by the end of the week. It is not mourned. It is composted. A hand, when it stops moving, becomes material.

It is not the trees, either, though they are closer.

A cooling spire has more of a biography than most things here. It is seeded in hot ash, fed on furnace exhaust and ground slag until it can push twenty meters in nine days, all black fin and hollow flute. It flowers — resinous cones, heavy and almost ready to burn — hardens, does its fifty days breathing heat up into the pall, and then, on a signal you cannot hear, lets go at the base and drops precisely into its own shredder pit. Seed to flue to mulch in two months. A cathedral-tree in the smelter district lasts longer: it is allowed centuries to mineralize its own firebrick lining, to become five stories of vaulted, excreted stone inside living wood. A coppiced factory grove is older still and younger at once, cut to stumps in the morning and already pushing green-black shoots — except nothing here is green — by the time the harvest cart reaches the end of the row.

No one tree is the citizen. They are cells with jobs. Leaf-cells, broad as doors, turned down toward geothermal fissures to drink infrared. Root-cells mining warmth and copper out of manufactured soil. Fruiting vines that swell patiently around purified silicon, tin, wire — fruit as ore, ripening on a conveyor. Pollinator trunks turning great grown-in fans, making the only wind there is, pushing pollen from male grove to female grove because no living wing could survive the air between them.

And under them, through them, is the body they all briefly belong to.

It fills the caldera edge to edge. Thirty kilometers across, and it has only ever been one thing, worn down over impossible time until forest and factory, organ and infrastructure, stopped being useful distinctions. You cannot find its edge by touching it, because there is no surface that is not it: the soil is its shredded former selves, the pavement is its interwoven roots laid over ancient rail lines, the warehouses are its limbs trained as saplings around steel armatures now completely enclosed, bone inside wood. The power station is not a building it uses. It is a clearing where it leans its own bodies inward and feeds itself resin pods, and feels the turbines humming back up through its soles.

It eats heat. That is the metabolism everything else follows from. It eats waste oil, mineral slurry, slag, dead towers, dead carts, the exhaust of its own furnaces, and in a certain sense it eats light — but not sunlight. Sunlight is too thin, too wet, too unreliable up above the pall. It taught its leaves to turn away from the sky and face the floors, the fissures, the forge walls. It sees, if seeing is the word, in infrared: a slow topography of hotter and cooler, furnace, flue, cooling fin, the brief fever of a human moving through a lane like a spark crossing a dark field. It hears through pressure in wood and root. It thinks, insofar as anyone standing in it can tell, in the grain of stressed timber, in chemical gradients sent lane to lane, in valves opening a fraction before a tower is ready to fall. Slow thoughts. Heavy ones. A decision may take a grove nine days to grow into.

It has no animals because it has left no vacancy for them. Not out of cruelty — cruelty would require it to distinguish between kinds of flesh. Insects would be incorporation that moves too fast and breeds out of register. Birds would bring unlisted seed. Even its human ancestors, the ones who scaffolded the first saplings and built the first muffled machines because they intended to live inside their own engine, were gradually edited out of the design by the same smoothing that wore the volcano flat. Look for them now and you find their choices fossilized instead: the preference for slow gears, for fluid drives, for falls of material muffled in bark mulch. The insistence that a place you must live inside should not deafen you. They fed that instinct into the system, and the system kept the instinct and did not need to keep them.

So it does its own keeping now. The machines are its reproductive organs and its immune system. It cannot travel to mate, so it builds blowers into its trunks and moves its pollen on artificial weather. It cannot run from disease or overgrowth, so it grows pruning carts to circulate through itself, cutting lanes in the morning that it will spend the afternoon trying gently to close. It harvests itself with clippers that run on trellis-rails of its own roots, and replants itself in ash beds where seedlings are visible moving, like something surfacing. Everything it makes is a way to continue being exactly as massive, exactly as hot, exactly as balanced as it already is. For every tower shredded, a pit is already sprouting in correct proportion. Total mass does not change. Total heat does not change. Only the arrangement suffers, endlessly, to remain the same.

That is why it feels both ancient and frantic down in the lanes. Its parts live at a violent sprint; it lives at geological stillness. It has forgotten its designers the way the plateau has forgotten being a mountain — not by losing the shape, but by eroding everything sharp enough to be a separate intention. Cathedral, warehouse, nursery, smelter: these are names visitors bring down with them and leave taped to districts, composting within the week like the bark-paper maps. The inhabitant does not use them. For it there is only more or less heat, more or less growth, places where the body needs thickening and places where it needs to be cut back before it chokes its own circulation.

It is not lonely. Loneliness needs an outside, and it has spent a thousand years filling its outside in. It is not waiting, either. Nothing is coming to free it from the caldera, and nothing would be improved by arrival. A visitor is a brief, warm irregularity to be routed around or, if he stands too long, trellised.

When you go back up the lift and break out of the cloud lid into freezing wind, you look down at that featureless black seal and realize you have not been shown a habitat. A habitat is where a creature lives. This is the creature, lying in the shallow dish it outlasted, sunning nothing, feeding on its own fire, growing its own walls in the dark and tearing them down, quietly making and remaking a single body in which a forest full of short, furious lives can flare up, serve, and be taken back without the whole of it ever once needing to wake.
