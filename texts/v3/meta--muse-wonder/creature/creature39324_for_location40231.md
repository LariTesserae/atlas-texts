The people of Ambel Low are wet.

That is the first true thing about them, and after a day in the root-wall it becomes the most important. Not rain-wet, not river-wet. Citizenship-wet. Their hair is always darkened and clinging, their cuffs always dark a thumb's width farther than they were an hour ago, their skin at collarbone and ankle always shining. The hem is where Ambel signs you first.

A visitor fights it. An Ambel-born never learned to. Children pad through the market in the morning with water beading on their foreheads before the heat has even properly arrived, perfectly at ease inside the warm hand of the air, wearing shirts of woven leaf-fiber that drink overnight and sigh it back all day, trousers cut wide for canal-wading, moss-slippers they have usually kicked off somewhere and will have to apologize to later — the slippers, being alive, wander toward damp and have to be coaxed back with a particular sweet smell made behind the tongue.

They are human, mostly, in the shape textbooks would admit. Two arms, a talent for argument, roughly three hundred of them across forty towers. But "mostly" does a lot of work in Ambel, the way "trunk" does. When every tap delivers water that fell somewhere else in the city a second from now, when your bedroom walls are vascular and your well is the same event, plant-wise, as your neighbor's cistern three streets and two canals away, then the boundary of a body gets politely theoretical. They share water. They share light, filtered down through tower crowns. They share a microbiome with the mat, the frogs, the canal silt. An old woman will press a wet thumb to a child's scraped knee and then to the plaza fern and consider the wound, administratively, handled — and she is not entirely wrong.

Their feet tell it. Sole-hard, greenish in the creases from chlorophyll that never quite washes out, toes spread from a lifetime on springy uncertain ground. A floor in Ambel is a decision the plants are still making. So they walk the way sailors do, loose-kneed, always slightly braced, reading the mat through their arches. A native crossing a floating plaza knows the market crowd the way you know weather — by pressure changes underfoot. The bounce talks.

They navigate by everything except sight. Maps expire at sunset because the canals move, the bridges throw themselves elsewhere, a tower decides overnight to lean its shade across a different street. So Ambel children grow up asking directions out loud, constantly, of everyone: towers, frogs, herons, each other. It produces adults who gossip the way other towns maintain roads. It is not idle. "Your cousin's kitchen has moved two chambers sunward, the crabs are unhappy by the north pilings, don't take the sweet-smelling bridge, it's lying" — that is infrastructure reporting itself through mouths. Their ears are educated in a way outsiders find eerie. They can stand in a roaring market at noon — bark popping, beams being argued upward, canal-crabs clanging the root pilings, three hundred people trading futures at full volume — and tilt their heads and say, *that* canal, the third one, is humming in the wrong key today. A native does not have a good sense of direction. A native has a good sense of *relationship*.

This is because nothing they live in was built. It was requested.

A family does not own a tower. A family has an ongoing, sometimes generations-old agreement with one. They tend it, bribe it with nutrients, sing to its plumbing frogs, keep its herons respectful, and in return the tower grows them rooms — fluted, many-chambered, smelling of sap and rain, windows irising open at dawn. Inheritance in Ambel is not things. It is standing. Your grandmother may be dead forty years, but the southwest tower remembers whether she was fond, or punctual with fertilizer, or a liar about pruning. Grow up rude to plants and you will spend adulthood trying to lease a decent kitchen from a vegetable that has heard stories about you.

So they learn manners before almost anything else. You may not cut. Anything. Not wood, not cloth, not a conversation, if you can help it. You persuade, you negotiate, you bribe, you wait, you go around the long way while making encouraging noises. Children learn the chemical register of apology before they learn letters — door-apology, bridge-apology, the specific olfactory grammar for "I stepped on you with my full weight and I am genuinely sorry," which has to be exhaled in a warm breath across the bark while thinking it, really thinking it, because the plants read chemistry and chemistry cannot be bothered to lie. Ambel adults consequently have an honesty problem in the outside world and an argument culture at home of operatic sophistication. They argue beams upward with ropes and drums and rhetoric. They argue rooms into existence. A marriage proposal in Ambel traditionally includes a detailed position statement on which direction a shared tower should lean for the next ten years; courtship is mostly two families and one very large plant negotiating a lease in public, with flowers.

Time gets soft around them and they have stopped pretending it doesn't. Step into a growth field at dawn to plant a kitchen, spend a lazy hour inside, step out one minute later with the smell of new wood still ticking off your sleeves — do that twice a week for thirty years and chronology becomes a loose suggestion. Ambel-born keep ages the way they keep maps: locally, temporarily. The seed merchants at market are the worst, or best; a woman who looks fifty may have spent cumulative extra years inside other people's unborn bathhouses, and her apprentice grandson may actually, by strict external count, be catching up. They trade in this without flinching. Three seeds of cold storage. A week of shade in a particular direction. The pollination rights to a roof. A room that hasn't happened yet, carried home in a pocket like an egg with opinions.

Work, here, is mostly midwifery for things that already remember being finished. The plumbers are very old women and very large frogs, and it is genuinely unclear in most partnerships which is senior. The women read pressure by frog-song and correct it by persuasion and nutrient slurry; the frogs sing the codes up through the vascular dark where no human ear fits. The canal-crabs, dog-sized, placid, municipal, dredge in armor that clanks musically against pilings and accept payment in minerals and being publicly thanked. Turtle-porters carry goods through canals at a pace everyone respects because impatience with a turtle, in a city that forbids cutting corners literally, is a character flaw people can smell. After dark the bats go out dusted gold and run the scent-network off the night-flowers, and someone standing downwind of a tower can receive tomorrow's weather in a breath — petrichor coming, citrus for clear heat, a worrying thread of mildew underneath — delivered to a population that receives its news through the nose and trusts it accordingly.

They eat what agrees to be eaten. Fruit that drops. Leaves that have been talked into surplus. Things the mat offers up after storms of spores. Because the first law is negotiation, meals have an etiquette that can take a while: a good cook in Ambel is part chef, part diplomat, part perfumer, and a bad one gets a reputation that follows them through root systems. Their flavors run green and bright and faintly fermented, everything kissed with standing water and sun-warmed sap, and they eat communally at hours dictated less by clocks than by when the plaza has decided, today, to be a plaza.

Nocturnally they do not so much sleep as take turns. The towers close their windows one by one, never all at once, never dark entire, and the people follow the same rhythm — households dozing in staggered chambers while sap-light ticks green-gold along the vascular lines and somebody, always, is awake to hear a new root testing a canal. Ambel-born are light sleepers with excellent dreams, raised from infancy inside a building that is dreaming about them back, in the slow plant way, remembering their adult shape in its seeds.

And underneath the noise, they are — this surprises visitors most — calm. Frantically busy, yes. Restless redecorators of a city that redecorates itself first, loud traders in futures, chronic askers of directions in streets they have lived in all their lives. But the basin underneath them finished happening a million years ago, and some of that finishedness has soaked up through the mat into their bones. They can afford turbulence. Nothing has to be preserved against a deadline. A beloved room can be un-grown into a seed and carried in a pocket through a bad year. A mistake can be talked back down its own timeline. Their dead go into the mat without ceremony or panic and come back, imprecisely, as a tower's fondness for their great-grandchildren.

So they spend the world's saved-up stillness the way their market spends noon light: all at once, joyfully, in public — three hundred wet-footed negotiators and their frog plumbers, heron inspectors, crab dredgers, turtle porters, dragonfly couriers waist-long and iridescent overhead, ten thousand animal colleagues and forty towers that are also landlords and relatives and memory, growing and un-growing and flowering in a bowl of warm light, brief as weather, and rooted, rooted, rooted in the oldest quiet there is.
