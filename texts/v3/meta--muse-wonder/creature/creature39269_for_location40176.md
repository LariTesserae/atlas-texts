At first you would take it for one of the tadpoles.

There are tadpoles everywhere in the bowl, at every stage, in the tea-brown sheet-water that is never deeper than your hand. But this one is too large, and it does not hurry. It rests in an inch of water with its belly spread flat against the warm mud, forearm-long, perhaps longer, its body the width of a flattened leaf. Its tail is broad and translucent, veined like a lily pad, and it keeps up a slow, almost absent-minded undulation even when the rest of it is still — not quite swimming, just keeping the water moving over itself.

If it has a color, it is whatever it is lying on.

Young ones are glassy. You can see the dark thread of the gut through them, the heart knocking, a greenish clouding under the skin where algae have taken up residence in the tissue itself. As they get older — though older is a difficult word for them — they cloud over with the place. Ochre from the mud. Moss-green from the light. Brown-black along the spine where specks of leaf-mold have settled and stayed. Some have actual moss beginning at the shoulders, a fine bright fur between the shoulder blades, where a frog of another kind might have a pattern. It does not bother them. Nothing that grows on them seems to bother them. Their skin is less a boundary than a tenancy.

Three pairs of external gills rise behind the head, soft, branching, fern-red when the water is clear and darkening to rust when it is silty. They wave constantly, delicate as tree-fern fronds, and small fish and insect larvae shelter in their shade and pick at what collects there. Between the gills and the moss and the algae living inside the skin, it is hard at a glance to say where animal stops and planting begins — and the longer you look, the less the animal helps you. It will sit for hours, days, in the posture of a submerged leaf, gills fanned, tail fanned, eyes — two small, lidless, black beads set almost flush with the head — reflecting the green gloom without focusing in it. In light that has no edges and casts no proper shadow, eyes are not worth much. It sees brightening and darkening. It sees movement close by as a smudge in the water.

It feels the rest.

It has never lost the lateral line of its larval life: a faint seam down each flank, exquisitely sensitive to pressure, to the footfall of a crab through root-mat, to a dragonfly's feet dimpling the surface film ten body-lengths away, to the whole bowl's constant minute trembling as worms and beetles and burrowing things work the mud. When a beetle crosses a leaf above it, it knows. When a frog lands, it knows. Much of its world arrives as vibration in water that is barely there.

It breathes, mostly, without bothering to. Through the gills, through the thin wet skin, through the lining of a mouth that opens and closes in a slow, idle pulse. There is no dry air to protect itself against, no need for a watertight hide. It could not survive dryness — its skin would take it straight in, and lose it straight out again — but dryness is a theoretical country. Its skin never dries here. Water condenses on the cool of its back the way it condenses on a frog's, on a leaf's underside. It lives at the exact temperature of the bowl, neither warmer nor colder, its blood running at the speed of that unchanging heat: steady, quick, unseasonal.

And it walks.

That is the surprise, if you watch long enough. It is not a fish that has strayed into the shallows. Its four legs are long for a larval thing, splayed wide, ending in toes absurdly elongated and webbed almost to the tips, toes that spread its negligible weight the way a snowshoe spreads a man's. It can cross the thinnest film of water over mud without breaking through, padding from puddle to connected puddle with a soft, sucking deliberation, each foot placed flat and peeled up again. On the root-mat, where the interlaced roots bounce slightly underfoot, it moves with a kind of loose, sprawling confidence, clinging with the undersides of its toes when the mat tilts. It does not leap. It does not burrow, exactly. It presses, and the soft ground gives, and it settles in.

It never finished growing up, which is why it is built like this.

Somewhere in its ancestry there was presumably an adult form: a proper salamander, lungs developed, gills resorbed, skeleton hardened, skin thickened for a life up banks and under logs. That adult no longer happens. It cannot happen, here. Metamorphosis needs a signal — a pool shrinking, a season turning, a stress of drying or cooling — and the bowl withholds every signal there is. The water never leaves. The heat never drops. The leaf-pools never empty. So it stays as it began, enlarged. Its bones remain largely cartilage, soft as the mudstone. Its teeth are tiny larval pegs, good for snapping up worms and mosquito larvae and small snails whole. Its feathery gills stay fanned out into its maturity, because maturity, when it comes, arrives *inside* the larval body.

It breeds as a tadpole that never became anything else.

There is no courtship season. There is no season at all. When an individual is large enough — and fed enough, and not yet eaten — its body simply ripens, male and female outwardly indistinguishable, both glassy-bellied, both slow. Eggs are laid in ones and twos, continuously, in the warm shallows, in the flooded axils of leaves, under the edges of the root-mat: clear jellied beads with a dark center, stuck to a root hair, a submerged stem, sometimes to the motionless tail of another of its own kind. They hatch into miniatures of the parent, already green-tinged, already fanning invisible gills. Many are eaten before they hatch. More are eaten after. This is not a tragedy in the economy of the bowl. It is the economy. It lays because things hatch; things hatch because it never stops laying.

It eats the way it is eaten: constantly, at background level. A snap at a passing flatworm. A lunge — startlingly fast, the one fast thing it does — at a tadpole of a genuinely frog kind. Hours of nothing, during which a fallen fruit beside it ferments and boils with flies and is slowly reduced, and it turns its head a quarter-inch and begins, dreamily, to pick the flies off one by one. When big leaves that trap insects fold shut and hold their catch softening for days, the salamanders learn the places of those leaves and wait underneath, taking what drops. They graze, too, in a way no self-respecting salamander should: scraping algae and bacterial film off roots with those peg teeth, swallowing mouthfuls of the bright green scum that skins the still water, digesting the algae in it and absorbing the rest into their own tissues for the thin photosynthetic subsidy it gives them in the gloom. Animal, plant, scavenger, prey. The categories sit loosely on a skin everything else lives on.

For everything does live on them, given time.

An individual that avoids the herons picking through the shallows, avoids the large fish in the wider lily flats, avoids the crabs and the other, hungrier salamanders, does not become an elder in any stately sense. It becomes slower, and broader, and more planted. The moss on its back thickens. Orchid seedlings — no larger than a grain — have been found rooted in the mucus along its spine. Mites move through its gill-fronds like birds through a canopy. A treefrog may spend its whole small life within a hand's span of one great, still salamander, eating the gnats that are attracted to its warmth, laying eggs in the permanent puddle of its footprint where the mud has filled and stayed filled around its weight. It is a surface, in a place where every surface is a garden. It is also, obscurely, one of the bowl's engineers. Its padding and pressing and wallowing churns the upper mud back to softness before the mud can settle; its grazing nips off tree seedlings at the cotyledon stage along the water's edge, thinning back the very saplings that might one day lift a higher, cooler canopy. It helps keep its own world young enough to never trigger its own completion.

Nothing about it ever hardens. When it dies — usually in pieces, usually taken — what is left redistributes within hours. When one dies whole, untouched in some root-hollow, it does not so much rot as get edited out: snails at the skin, worms up from beneath, the white threads of fungus stitching it into the mat, until within days there is a salamander-shaped richness in the mud and then not even that. No bone bed. No shell. If its soft body presses into fresh mudstone and the mud, against all the local odds, begins to set, it might leave what the broken rocks show now and then: the perfect, curling impression of something embryonic, fully formed and never finished, in stone that never quite became stone.

At night — which is only the green light thickening a degree, the frog chorus trading one set of voices for another, the temperature not moving at all — they are, if anything, more numerous. The sheet-water cools a fraction against their backs and beads there. Their gills glow faintly rust-red in the duckweed. They move out across the flats in the dark in their dozens, unhurried, tails swaying, feet silent on the water, traveling miles over weeks through water never deeper than a hand and never once finding a shore.

They have nowhere to go. They have never had anywhere to go. The bowl is not a stage on the way to being an adult landscape, and they are not a stage on the way to being an adult animal. Both have agreed, without ever deciding, to stay exactly here: warm, dim, saturated, teeming, begun.
