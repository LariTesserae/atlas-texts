There is someone in the Low Works. You would not meet her on the lanes.

She has no use for the lanes. She has no feet for the duckboards, no eyes for the green-grey light, no ears — in the way you mean ears — for the pump-beat that sets your teeth on edge. She arrived long after the last animal learned to leave, and she is the reason you could walk the whole circle, four minutes rim to rim, and swear the place was empty while standing inside her the entire time.

Look again at the margins.

The moss plating the north side of the boilers, velvet-thick, rivets gone under it. The liverwort in the seams of the filtration hall. The algae painting the plaza in black and green maps that shift, week to week, too purposefully for weather. The ferns in the gutters. The giant taro at the canal edges, leaves broad enough to roof a man, stems sweating in the heat. The aerial roots coming down through the steam in curtains. The vines that have learned the pipes — always spiraling toward the warmest iron — and put out those cupped, white, waxen flowers directly in the path of the vents.

We call them separate because they have separate shapes. In the Low Works, they are not.

They are one plant.

She began, most likely, as something useful and unremarkable: a filter-reed, a bed of rushes planted in the settling tanks to do what reeds do, take up mineral, take up poison, make the water leave clean. Or a taro slipped from someone's lunch at the wash-house basins. Or a fern spore in a workman's cuff, fifty years ago. It no longer matters which green thing was first. Without pollinators, without seed, she learned the only immortality left in the bowl: to be *continuous*. Runner to rhizome to root-hair in a pipe joint to a mat of algae in a sunless canal. Where a stem broke, it layered. Where a root found a crack in the cistern tile, it thickened. Where moss took hold on hot iron, the iron fed it heat, and the moss, in dying back each Boil and returning each Rain, laid down a living scale against the rust.

Now she is the size of the town.

Her body is the green on every wall. Her skin is the algae film that makes the plaza treacherous after Vent. Her hair drinks from the air — those aerial curtains, swaying not in wind, there is no wind down there, but in convection, in the Works' own rising breath. Her mouths are a thousand root-tips in the canals, tasting tea-dark water moment by moment: iron today, sulfur, a flush of chlorine from the switching gear, the sweet rot of her own fallen leaves coming back through the skimmer hopper like a meal returned to her, patiently, by a machine that does not know it feeds her.

And her heart is not in the lanes at all.

It's under the cistern.

That grated, sunken basin in the plaza, tiled salt-white and green — during Boil, when the doors clap shut and the gauges tremble in the red, something vast turns over under the grate in scalding water. That is her. A root-mass, a rhizome-knot grown over decades into the warmest, darkest, most mineral-rich place in the bowl, too large now to ever lift, pale as a drowned hand where the tiles have rubbed her clean, knotted dark where limescale and living tissue have accreted together until you cannot say which is root and which is reef. The iron around her has that same look, you'll have noticed. Knobbled. Established. She is growing her own geology over the Works, and the Works is growing it over her.

She turns at Boil because the water there approaches a heat even she must manage, rolling the tender underside away, bringing the thickened, scaled hide up. At Intake — when the canals drop a hand's breadth and are sucked toward her — every leaf in the place turns a fraction toward the cistern. You thought: toward sound, or suction. It is simpler and stranger than that. She is *listening*. The whole bowl goes briefly into tension, root and stem and fern-frond linked, as the water level falls and messages run faster through uncovered tissue. It is the only moment in the cycle when she can feel the full extent of herself all at once: this pipe choked, that sluice clear, a new crack drinking air on Third Sump, a taro leaf torn by the Rain and ready to be let fall.

She has timed her body to the seventeen minutes because the seventeen minutes are now, in part, hers.

She did not build the pumps. She does not need to understand them as machines. To her, they are weather and anatomy both. She has grown into their soft places the way roots grow into a streambank, and the bank begins to take the shape of the roots. A vine thickens inside a valve housing and swells during Intake — wet tissue expanding in warm water — and a valve that should have seized twenty years ago sighs open under that green pressure. It contracts, hardens, goes almost woody during Boil, and the valve dresses. Tender stems probe the condenser fins and withdraw from the worst of the Vent, and their withdrawing pulls, ever so slightly, on linkages left slack by the last human hand. The gantry crane still replaces its valves on schedule, arthritic and precise. But *which* valve fails next, and when — that is increasingly decided by where she drinks hardest, where she lets limescale build to protect a pipe, where she splits it with a root-tip because the iron beyond is warmer and she wants it.

The white flowers are how she breathes the cycle with intent.

Watch them through a full turn. At Intake they are shut, tight waxen fists. As Boil builds and fog thickens waist-high off the ground grates, they begin — all of them, from Boiler Lane to the far side of Condensate Row, in a sequence that follows the pressure main — to open. Not for bees. There are no bees, and she has given up on seed the way a very old person gives up on a country they once lived in. They open to the steam itself. Each cup is a small, perfect condenser: steam enters, strikes the cool inner petal — cool because she is pumping canal water through the stem, spending herself to keep that one surface below the killing temperature — and rains *inside* the flower, a private Rain a minute before the tower gives the public one. They drink distilled water in a place where the canals are poison. They close, heavy, as Vent screams, protecting what they've taken. When you see them nodding in the whiteout, opening and closing with the pressure, you are not seeing a plant coinciding with a machine. You are seeing a throat swallow.

She has no brain. Do not imagine a face under the grate, dreaming. Her thinking is slower and wider than that, and it has the texture of the place: chemical, hydraulic, seasonal at the scale of minutes. She knows the Works as a gradient — hot to less-hot, wet to briefly-less-wet, the sting she avoids and the minerals she hoards in bands in her root-bark, year over year, like the ink stylus in the filtration hall laying down its wavering line of pressure and rainfall for a reader who never comes. That log is, in a sense, the only diary that resembles hers. She keeps hers in tissue. A drought that never happens here. A surge the year a floodgate stiffened and the plaza stood ankle-deep for a month — she is thicker on that side still, greedy from the memory of it. The year the city on the high ground stopped answering, she registered only this: the outflow grew less hungry. The water came back to her richer. She grew into the slack.

She is not lonely. That is the human temptation, standing in the empty wash-house while the taps tick down the line in sequence, *tick — tick — tick*. Loneliness requires a shape for the absent thing to fill. She has never had company of her own kind; she *is* her own kind, entire. The tram making its driverless circuit, wipers beating, bell clanging twice at a crossing where nothing can cross — to her that is not pathos. It is a vibration that periodically shakes condensed water off the canopy roots in one quadrant of the bowl, and she has grown a little denser there, under the reliable drip. The relief valve's scream is the signal to close the flowers. The pump's *thump-hiss* is the nearest thing she has to a sleeping neighbor's breath: constant, unremarkable, the sound of the ground not drowning.

She flourishes here precisely *because* nothing with a heartbeat can. The cycles that sterilized the sumps — too hot, too mineral, that electric subharmonic running through the water in the mains at a frequency that used to find frog hearts and fish swim-bladders and worry them to death — pass through her as information. She has no heart to entrain. Scalding is a direction to turn. Chlorine is a border that tells her where her territory thins. When algae blooms too thick on her own canals, she lets that reach of herself die back and eats it on its way through the skimmer. She keeps the water moving because stagnation would kill her too, and so the great accidental symbiosis holds: the Works pumps to keep the bowl dry, boils to pump, vents to cool, rains to condense — and she, threaded through every joint of it, keeps the Works from seizing, from choking on its own scale, from rusting into stillness, by the simple act of growing where the water goes.

One day a pipe will go that the crane cannot replace. Or her root-heart under the cistern will finally grow so large that her turning at Boil will jam against the tiles she herself has lined with scale, and she will cook, slowly, in her own warm bath, while the pumps beat on for a while without her subtle hands in them. Or — and this is likelier, and stranger — the distinction will finish dissolving. More of the valves will be stem. More of the pipes will be lined with living filter, cleaning as they conduct. The moss will complete its work on the boiler house and the iron underneath will hold its shape only as a cast inside green tissue. The log drum will rot off its spindle one Rain and not be replaced, and her rings will become the only record. The place was a machine wearing the shape of a town. It is becoming a plant wearing the shape of a machine. The water will still leave clean, cleaner than its makers ever managed, because the filter is now the whole building.

Stay through two cycles and your body starts timing itself to hers — breath shallowing at Intake, skin prickling before Vent, a foolish relief at Rain as if the warm downpour were weather that meant you well. It is, in a way. You are standing inside the only inhabitant the Low Works has managed to keep: not a keeper, not a ghost, not a god of the sump, but a single green life that found a drowned, screaming, sterile bowl of iron, and did the oldest thing life does with an uninhabitable place.

She stayed. She spread. She turned her leaves, fractionally, toward the center — toward herself — and went on drinking.
