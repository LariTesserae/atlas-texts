At first you would not see it as an animal at all.

You would see it as an absence. A small, clean patch of silence travelling slowly across the floor of the rift, through a noise so dense it has texture. Where it passes, the crickets stop rasping. The frogs stop mid-*tink*. The water boatmen go still under the surface of the pools. For a radius of perhaps three meters around it, the whole turning loop holds its breath.

Only then, if you stayed — and staying, there, means being climbed by crickets and tasted by mites and beaded with condensation until you are no longer quite separate from the stone — only then would your eyes, useless as they are, begin to sort it out of the dark by its lack of movement against movement.

It stands in the shallowest edge of a pool, on legs too long for its body.

From a distance, in country with light, you would call it a heron. It has that patience, that stilted, deliberate grammar of the body. Down close the resemblance thins and keeps thinning, until something else, more belonging to this place, comes forward instead.

It is perhaps a meter and a half tall when it lifts itself fully, though it rarely does. Most of the time it folds. The legs hinge backwards and then forwards again, pale grey joints wrapped in skin so thin the tendons show like cords under wet paper. Its feet are enormous, splayed wide over the bacterial film so as not to break it — four toes in front, each longer than a human hand, each ending not in a claw but in a soft, flattened pad, and a fifth toe set far back, almost a second heel. It does not stand *on* the ground, exactly. It listens through it. Every pad is dense with pressure sense. A frog calling twenty meters away arrives in its feet as a distinct location, a direction, a size. A crab shifting in its pothole. Rain beginning to thread from pool to pool. The whole valley, for this animal, is a map drawn continuously in vibration, redrawn with every wave of calling that runs the length of the rift and makes the walls briefly, faintly, phosphoresce blue.

It has no eyes.

Where eyes would be there are two shallow, sealed pits, overgrown first by translucent skin and then, in older individuals, by a fine beard of the same white fungal thread that hangs under the overhangs. It does not need to be cleaned of it. The fungus does no harm and may do some small good, drinking condensation before it can chill the skull. Its whole head, in fact, is less a face than an instrument for being still. Long skull, narrow as a trowel. A beak — if it is a beak — the color of wet slate, not hard all the way through but layered, slightly yielding at the edges, made for slipping into water without a splash and closing on something soft without a sound. Its nostrils are slits that close underwater. Its ears are not holes but broad, featherless patches of skin behind the jaw, stretched tight as drumheads, mostly kept slack. Opened fully, the frog chorus would deafen it in minutes. So it listens selectively, tightening and loosening the skin, filtering the world down to one pool, one frog, one heartbeat at a time. When the great wave passes and the air itself rings, it lowers its head between its shoulders and goes, for those seconds, entirely deaf by choice, and navigates by foot alone.

The body between the legs and the head is small, almost an afterthought, and kept close. It is covered not quite in feathers and not quite in down — pale, hair-like filaments, cream and rust-colored where bacterial film has stained them, that trap damp air against the skin and hold its slow heat in. Underneath, the skin is milk-white and translucent like the frogs'. In a young animal you can see the dark mass of the gut, the paler flicker of the heart. In an old one the skin thickens with scar and calcite dust and guano worked into the filaments until it is genuinely stone-colored, stone-textured — which is why the crickets do not merely fear a shape. They fear a piece of the valley that has inexplicably begun to move.

It is warm-blooded, but only just, and only sometimes. For hours it can let itself cool to the temperature of the pool it stands in, heart slowing to a beat every few seconds, breath shallowing to nothing visible, becoming stone in every way that matters to its prey. Then, when it strikes — head unfolding down in one unbroken fall, beak entering the water exactly where a frog called half a second too late — it warms from the inside out with startling speed, blood rushing to the neck and legs, filaments lifting. A strike, a swallow, a brief shiver all down its length to shake the water off. Then the cooling again. The patience again.

It eats frogs principally, because frogs are the valley rendered edible: film become cricket, cricket become frog, frog become this. But it is not specialized to the point of fragility. It takes crabs when the rains join the pools and the crabs make their clattering migrations and cannot hear it coming over their own noise. It takes salamanders when they rise, though rarely, and only the smaller ones; an old salamander in a deep pothole and an old one of these standing over it will sometimes hold that arrangement for an hour, neither moving, until the water changes and both dissolve separately into the dark. It takes fallen swiftlets from the bone alcoves. It takes, very occasionally, a cricket drummer distracted at a good stone — and the other crickets remember, and that stone stands unused for days.

It does not call. Not with its throat. Its throat is for swallowing, and for a low, felt thump it makes once or twice a year by inflating a sac at the base of the neck and striking the surface of a pool with its whole chest at once, a sound more pressure than noise, that carries through water and wet limestone farther than any *tink*. Mostly it speaks, if speaking is the word, with its feet.

Two of them meeting at a pool do not display, do not sing, do not, as far as any watching thing could tell, acknowledge each other at all for a very long time. They stand at opposite edges. Then one shifts its weight, toe by toe, in a pattern — a slow tapping pressed into the film and the shallow water, making rings that go out and touch the other's pads. The other answers, after a pause, with a variation. Back and forth, sometimes for the length of an entire frog-wave and through the hole of quiet after it, patterns building on patterns, negotiated entirely through the soles. Territory is arranged this way. So is courtship. So, apparently, is a kind of contest: two males at a flooded terrace in the wet pulse of the year, tapping faster and more intricately through each other's feet until one falters in the rhythm, steps wrong, breaks the surface loudly, and folds himself down and away into the dark while the crickets, tentatively, begin again around the winner.

The eggs — two, usually, grey as pool water and speckled like flint — are not nested so much as entrusted. The female scrapes a shallow cup not in mud, for swiftlets own the mud here, but in a dry guano drift against a boulder in one of the higher alcoves, where decomposition keeps the drift faintly, steadily warm, and lays them there, and then simply stands over them for weeks, cooled almost to stone, eating only what wanders within reach. The chicks hatch blind in the sealed-pit way of their kind, naked filaments plastered flat, legs already too long, feet absurdly large, paddling at the air. Their first act is not a cry. It is a tapping, weak and arrhythmic, against the shell fragments and the warm drift — and their mother answers it, toe by toe, before she ever feeds them, as if the pattern has to be established before the animal is. They beg afterwards by imitating, imperfectly, the *tink* of a small frog, a trick they will keep into their first year and use to draw real frogs within reach of their still-clumsy strikes. Adults never make that sound again. Something in the growing throat forecloses it. To call like prey, after a certain size, would be to lie to the whole valley, and the valley runs on the honesty of its noise.

They are solitary but not scarce, spaced the length of the rift in overlapping listening-ranges, each one known to the others as a recurring moving silence in the map they all share. A young one dispersing walks the whole valley at the pace of a tide, stopping wherever the chorus will tolerate its stillness, learning which pools restock fastest after rain, which drumming stones draw crickets thickest, which sinkholes at the southern end breathe cold air up in a way that means flood is coming hours before the streams join. If it survives — and many do not; lizards take chicks, deep floods take eggs, a mistimed deafening at the height of a chorus can leave one permanently half-sensing — it may live there for thirty, forty years, growing scarred and fungus-bearded and slower to warm, adding its silence each day to the same few terraces.

And when it dies, it tends, insofar as an animal with no eyes and no ceremonies can be said to tend, to die in the dry alcoves. Whether it seeks them or simply stops, at the end, in the last places it stood as a chick does not matter much to what happens next. The beetles come up out of the bone drifts. The films creep. The filaments go first, then the translucent skin, then everything soft, until what remains is the disproportionate architecture of it: the long listening toes, the folded legs, the narrow skull with its two smooth, sealed pits. Another layer of small white geometry in the drifts, picked clean, incorporated.

Nothing about it leaves. Nothing about it ever needed light. Stand in its patch of silence long enough and you understand the rift's simplest rule from the inside, the way the crickets understand it, the way the frogs with their glass voices understand it in reverse: the noise is the living surface of the place, loud and total and turning over and over on itself — and this quiet, stilt-legged, foot-listening thing is what the noise is *for*, and what it is watched by, and what, in the cool damp dark at the bottom of the world, keeps it honest.
