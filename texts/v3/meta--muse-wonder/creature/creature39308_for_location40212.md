At the lift-head, they are hard to read at first.

The cage comes up out of the cloud and into the thin, hard light, and they are simply there on the platform — a dozen figures standing much too close together, very still, their shadows cut short under them. From a distance they look broad and blocky, almost clumsy, wrapped in padded coats and copper-banded vests despite the heat rolling off the stone. As you get nearer the proportions resolve and unsettle you. The breadth is not fat, and not mostly coat.

It is chest.

Every man and woman on the High Table has a ribcage like a barrel set into the body, deep front to back, the sternum pushed out, the shoulders carried high and back to give the lungs room to work. Their necks are short and thick. Their diaphragms move visibly when they breathe, a slow bellows-motion under the ribs that never quite stops being conscious. Watch one at rest and you count it without meaning to: in for four, hold, out for six, a little shudder at the top where the air gives out and the body has to wait for what it has taken to be enough. Children do it faster. Old people do it deeper, with a faint click in the throat.

Their blood does the rest. They are pink in a way lowlanders are not — not sunburnt on the surface only, though they are that too, freckled and peeled and polished by glare across the cheekbones — but flushed from within, ears and lips and nail-beds a permanent, healthy red, the blood thickened and crowded with itself to drag oxygen out of air that will not volunteer it. Cut one and the blood is dark, slow, and reluctant to thin. Bruises last for months in the dryness. So does everything.

They move the way the factories move. A Table-born adult crossing a yard does not stride; they place. Heel, weight, breath, next step, unhurried even when the work is urgent, because urgency up here is a way to faint decoratively beside a furnace and have to be dragged inside by two other people who can ill afford the effort either. They lean into tasks instead of lifting them. They push with their legs and their backs, they use levers and counterweights and each other, forearms locked. When something must be carried between buildings it is passed hand to hand along a line of standing figures rather than hauled by one runner. A dropped tool is left where it fell until the breath to bend for it can be spared.

And they are almost always touching, or nearly. The air will not carry a voice ten paces, so conversation is a close art. Two smeltermen conferring will stand chest to chest, foreheads nearly meeting, each with a hand resting lightly on the other's forearm, feeling the small vibration of the words through coat and skin as much as hearing them. Public business is done by flags, by flashing shutters, by the ankle-high speaking tubes that snake through every town, but anything that matters — a bargain, a warning, a name, grief — is said at arm's length or less. Lowland visitors find them intrusive for the first week and then find themselves lonely for it. They have a hundred small tactile signs layered under the spoken ones: a squeeze for *wait*, knuckles brushed twice for *sealed*, a palm left flat against a sleeve for *I am listening, take your breath*. Lovers conduct whole arguments in a crowd without a word leaving the space between their hands.

They dress for sealing. That is the second thing you learn about them, after the chest: nothing precious is left open to the sky. Their coats close with overlapping flaps and waxed cord, not buttons. Their cuffs cinch. Their boots are soft inside stiff soles, laced to the calf, because a turned ankle a hundred kilometers from a town is not an injury so much as a slow sentence. Around the throat most wear a shallow collar of beaten copper or stiffened leather, not armor — a windbreak for the trachea, and an anchor for the veil. Out on the pans they pull down goggles of smoked glass set in leather, and a cloth mask that covers nose and mouth, dampened not with water, which no one can spare for such a thing, but with a smear of rendered licker-fat that catches the red dust before it can be breathed and tastes, permanently, faintly, of salt and iron.

Their hair they keep short or bound tight under caps. Loose things misbehave up here. In the afternoon charge, when the interior weather lifts the dust a meter off the ground and holds it hovering, an unbound braid stands out from the head and follows its owner like something curious. Table women laugh about a girl's first season working the mirror fields by whether her hair learned to point at the nearest heliostat.

Work shapes them more precisely than climate does. There are, broadly, three kinds of Table people, and you can tell them at a glance once you have been up a month.

The lift-head families are the smallest and the richest and the most scarred. They live where the cables come over the rim, in towns clamped to the cliff edge, and they work the great counterweight cages that trade ore and metal down for water and timber and lowland food up. The loads are balanced so precisely that a child could, in principle, check the figures, and never in practice allowed near them. Lift-hands have forearms corded from brake-wheels and a characteristic pattern of pale, hairline burns up the inside of the wrist from testing cable grease by touch. They are the only people on the Table who regularly see lowlanders, and it marks them: they speak a little louder out of habit, they know the prices of things that grow, they keep a green plant sometimes in a pressurized glass jar by a window like other people keep a saint, watering it with an eyedropper, apologizing to visitors for the extravagance and showing it to them anyway.

The factory people are the great majority. Smelter-tenders, mirror-creepers, still-watchers, press crews. They live in the low, dense knots of towns around the stacks, in houses half-buried against the daily swelling of heat, and their lives are set to cycles so slow that a single work shift can outlast a lowlander's whole visit. A mirror-creeper spends the day walking an acre of heliostats with a wrench and a polishing rag, adjusting each by a fraction, squinting not at the sun but at the small, patient movement of a shadow across a graduated plate. By evening their faces are striped with reflected light where the veil rode up. The still-watchers sit for hours beside vacuum bells taller than houses, listening — actually listening, ear to the cold plate — for the tick and settle of pure metal condensing out of vapor in the emptiness inside. They develop preternatural hearing in a place that discourages sound. They can tell a seal is going, hours before any gauge admits it, by a change in the note of their own blood in their ears.

They are a people of maintenance to a degree that looks, from below, like religion. Since nothing rots and nothing is wasted and everything cast up here is already flawed, a Table town is its own ancestor. A factory hand will show you, with real pride, that the hinge on his door was once part of the first lift cage in Orum, that his mother's kettle was a condenser plate, that the church bell — struck at funerals for its flash as it turns, not its note — was a crucible that cracked in his grandfather's firing and has been holy ever since. They mend clothing until the mending is the clothing. They name tools and inherit them. Their handwriting, on slate and beaten tin, is tiny, compressed, economical, because good flat surfaces are also inherited.

The third kind are the herders, and they are the strangest to the town-dwellers themselves. They follow the salt-lickers.

Where the town people have made smallness and closeness into a virtue, herders spend seasons walking single file behind slow, long-necked beasts across a pan with nothing in it, fifty kilometers between slag heaps, sleeping in pressurized tents that sigh all night as the hand-bellows work, eating what the bacterial crusts and the herd between them will provide. They scrape the iron-red and sulphur-yellow mats from the warm fissures into flat cakes they dry and grind; they milk the lickers rarely and carefully, a bitter, mineral fluid they mostly feed to children; they eat meat perhaps twice a year, when an animal dies in a way that lets them burn it in a portable solar box before the beetles claim it. Herders grow taller than town people and thinner in the face, their chests even deeper, their skin a uniform copper. They talk least of all and touch least of all and are considered, affectionately, half-feral. They understand the electrostatic storms the way sailors understand tide, by the humming of stones and the behavior of loose chain, and trains will stop in open desert to take a herder's waved flag seriously when they would ignore a town signal. When herders come into town to trade — refined ore from the slag-beetles, keratin scrapings, news — children follow them through the streets just to hear them breathe in the still indoor air, which they do like men surfacing.

At home, everything in a Table household is under pressure or under lid. Water lives in a riveted tank in the coolest, deepest room and is served from a tap that must be pumped back up after each use. Cooking is done in sealed vessels that hiss and clank; a stew cooks at a temperature a lowlander would call barely warm, for a very long time, and is seasoned ferociously with imported things ground to dust and used by the pinch — pepper, dried leaf, salt that did not come from the pans and therefore tastes of somewhere else. Baths are affairs of oil and hot sand and a scraper. The houses at night are not silent but sealed: bellows in the walls keep the inside air a fraction richer than the outside, doors double-doored, shutters shut against the ticking cold, whole families sleeping in one room not for warmth — the stone gives back its heat for hours — but for the shared economy of breath and bellows in the dark. They sleep on their backs, most of them. It lets the chest open.

Children are born in the deepest rooms of all, in birthing chambers kept at the highest pressure a town can manage, because birth is the one piece of work up here that cannot be slowed down, and it kills mothers and infants at a rate the Table people speak of plainly and without sentimentality, the way they speak of seal-failure. Babies are small, furious, and startlingly red. They are kept swaddled against a parent's chest for the first year — carried in the literal warmth and rhythm of a working lung — and old people claim, entirely seriously, that this is why Table-born adults can pace their breathing to another person's without thinking. Those who survive childhood grow fast in the chest and slow in everything else. They learn flags before letters. They learn to walk the mirror fields without looking up. They learn the local catechism of survival as a matter of manners: stand close, speak once, finish the task your hands are on, check the seal twice and then check it because you checked it.

By adolescence they have the stars-at-noon problem, which is not considered an illness. At the highest towns, and in the interior in summer, children report seeing stars in the blue-black midday sky and hearing a fine, continuous note that is only their own circulation. It passes, or they cease to mention it. Those it does not pass in become still-watchers, sometimes, or go out to the herds, or go — in the stories towns tell uneasily — further in still, toward the old cold works at the dome where no one lives and the floors are warm in winter and the herd animals will not graze. No Table person will stop an adult from walking inland with a sealed tent and a full skin. They will check his seals themselves, carefully, hand on his sleeve a moment longer than necessary. It amounts to the same thing as a blessing.

They bury no one. The ground is too hard and would keep them too well. The dead are burned at noon in the solar furnaces, quickly, in the white silent focus where metal separates, with the whole town watching the mirrors and not the body, and the ash — a handful, up here — is mixed into sintering dust and becomes wall. Someone you loved may literally hold up your roof. This does not strike them as metaphor.

Old age on the Table is mostly chest and hands. The barrel deepens as the spine shortens; the skin goes the color of beaten copper left in sun; the fine hearing of the still-watchers becomes a kind of deafness to everything but pressure and pulse. The old sit in doorways in the long evenings with their hands in their laps, breathing in that slow, shared rhythm, repairing small things by touch, watched over in turn by the feathered scavenger-dogs of the town who have learned which doorways vent warmth and sweetness at dusk. Table people feed those animals from their own plates without discussion. Kinship up here is not a matter of blood. It is a matter of what breathes your air and cleans your streets and can be trusted, over decades, with what does not decay.

A lowlander visiting for a season learns to walk slowly, speak closely, touch sleeves, and check seals, and goes down in the lift convinced he has understood them. What he has mostly learned is the shape of the place in his own lungs.

The Table people are what remains when that shape is inherited for thirty generations: a slow, red, deep-chested, close-standing people, patient as their presses, thrifty with breath and water and words, layered into their towns like the towns are layered into the stone — impossible to imagine anywhere else, and so completely at home in the thin, hot, silent air that when they finally do descend, which most never do, they stand in the rich lowland air at the foot of the cliff with tears running down their faces, overwhelmed, half-drowned, asking in whispers for someone to please stand nearer.
