It is not one animal that lives there. It is a density — a whole stacked, breathing population that has divided a single dark room between itself by height, by hunger, and by the hour of the day.

At the top, the ceiling is theirs.

Thousands of free-tailed bats, so close they no longer read as thousands. In daylight they are a single surface, brown-black and restless, stretched from wall to wall across the vault where you cannot see its end. A shoulder pushes. An ear flicks. A wing opens a hand's breadth and folds again against a neighbor's ribs. They sleep head-down in the heat of each other, and it is their heat more than anything that makes the chamber possible — a warm ceiling that meets the cold water seeping down through limestone and turns it to condensation, so that it is always raining, very slowly, inside a place where rain never falls.

They are the room's great commuters. All day they do nothing, magnificently, in public: digest, groom, nurse pups tucked into the crush, drop pellets that patter down through the dark. At dusk they become a river. One loosens, then ten, then hundreds, pouring up the shaft in a warm, leathery spiral, smelling of milk and ammonia and old fur, until the ceiling is suddenly thinner and the cave can cool a degree. Before dawn they return with the valleys in their stomachs — moths, beetles, flies caught far below in air this altitude could never produce — and what they do not keep, the room keeps. Guano, urine, dead young, eventually their own bodies. The bats feed the floor that feeds almost everything else.

Just below them is a second nation, louder in a different way.

Swiftlets cling to the upper walls in a band of ledges and cracks, each on a stiff little cup of its own saliva and feather, translucent as horn, glued to rock. They are smaller, sharper, more nervous than the bats, and they never quite stop announcing themselves. Their echolocation is a dry clicking, single ticks when they are settled, a frantic pattering when one leaves or returns and sets its neighbors off. In waves the whole wall starts to tick, as if the stone itself were thinking aloud, then settles again.

They leave first in the evening, because they need what last light is left. For nearly an hour they unspool up the crack, bird after bird spiraling into the brightness, brushing wings against bats still waiting for full dark. They hunt on the wing outside and sleep, breed, and die in here, and their droppings whiten the rims of the drinking pools and varnish the rock beneath their nests, dissolving it grain by grain.

The walls below the swiftlets belong to animals who never leave at all.

Cave crickets, pale, high-jointed, their antennae longer than their bodies and always moving ahead of them like a second, more honest set of eyes. They feel the room rather than see it — a boot, a fallen bat, a seam of damp guano, another cricket — tapping, tasting, retreating in sudden sideways leaps when the air changes. Among them, spiders hang in untidy webs strung ledge to ledge. Their silk beads with condensation. It catches gnats, moths strayed in from the shaft, the small flies that rise in clouds from the floor, and holds their wings long after the bodies are gone.

And moving through the lower walls, through cracks that look too narrow until it uses them, the centipede. Some as long as a forearm, segmented, rippling, utterly unhurried in the daylight hours when the bats are home and the room is at its fullest. At night, when the ceiling empties, it comes down. It hunts cockroaches in the crevices, crickets on the damp stone, whatever is slow or newly dead. It does not chase so much as arrive, with a certainty that suggests the dark is not an obstacle to it but a medium, the way water is to a fish.

The floor is where the population becomes uncountable.

It is guano, knee-deep at the center, fresh and black and steaming faintly on top, older and redder and grayer and finally pale and crumbly underneath, layered with wing cases, small bones, shed skins, seeds that never sprouted. It is warm from decomposition, and it never rests. Cockroaches in the tens of thousands turn it over, shouldering through it in a continuous, soft rustle. Beetles burrow into it and roll it away in balls. Millipedes thread its darker underside. Fly larvae work the wettest seams where the ceiling-drips land. Mites live on the cockroaches, on the beetles, on the dead bats, on each other, in numbers that stop being numbers.

If you stay still you can hear strata eating strata: chewing, burrowing, the minute collapse of a pellet dropped from thirty feet above, a wing opening and closing. Every few seconds something falls from the bats or the swiftlets. Every few seconds something below intercepts it. Nothing reaches the bottom unchanged.

That slow rain of food is why creatures can live here that have never seen the outside. In the shallow rock basins at the edge of the chamber — three or four pools of black, still water, kept alive only by snowmelt and condensation — blind shrimp drift and graze. Translucent, almost colorless, a backward jerk their whole answer to danger. They feed on the film that grows where guano meets water. Nothing larger hunts them; the pool is too small, too isolated. But almost everything else comes there to drink, crickets and centipedes and cockroaches and, at the dangerous changing hours, bats crawling down, so the rims are polished smooth by feet and tongues and flecked white with droppings that the next drip will undo.

At the margin of the margin, where the one blade of noon light sometimes touches the wall, there is a thinner, greener citizenship that holds on for only a few feet and a few minutes a day: wet moss, algae filming the drip-line, three or four ferns uncurling pale tips toward a light that will be gone before they can use much of it, lichen in rust and sulphur crusts just beyond. They are the only inhabitants who want the sun. The rest have traded it away for warmth, shelter, and each other.

And then there are the visitors who are not quite visitors anymore.

At dawn, choughs come down from the bright ridge — black birds with the outside still cold on their feathers — carrying snails and hard seeds they cannot open alone. They drop them, deliberately, onto one flat stone under the shaft, descend, and pick the cracked shells apart. Around the stone is a scatter of old fragments, generations deep. They did not build the anvil. They inherited the habit of it, bird from bird, and so the stone is kept a tool simply by being used as one. They leave again. They do not roost here. But the shells they leave stay, and get eaten, and become part of the floor.

That is the pattern of the whole chamber. Most of its citizens either commute or consume. The bats and swiftlets go out into the thin, brilliant, freezing world and bring it back as food and waste and heat. The choughs bring it back as broken shells. Wind brings dust and seeds down the crack. Meltwater brings minerals through the limestone. Everything that arrives is intercepted by something that lives permanently in the dark — cricket, spider, shrimp, larva, mite — and worked downward until it is guano, then heat, then food again.

So if you put your ear to the fissure, what you are hearing is not a colony. It is a crowded household with no empty rooms: sleepers packed across the ceiling, clickers mapping the walls with sound, feelers exploring your boots if you stand still long enough, hunters rippling through the cracks after the lights that never come on go out, and under it all a floor that is not a floor but millions of small mouths, digesting the mountain's one warm room and making it, year by almost imperceptible year, warmer and deeper and louder.

Outside, the summit looks deserted.

Inside, nothing is ever alone, and nothing is ever wasted, and nothing ever quite finishes being eaten.
