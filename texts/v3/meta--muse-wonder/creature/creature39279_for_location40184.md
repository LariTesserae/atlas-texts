Nothing here lives alone, and nothing here lives on the surface for long.

The first inhabitant is the mat. It has no head, no season, properly speaking. Wet, it is black and slick as oilcloth, a felt of cyanobacteria and iron-bacteria knitted together over the clay pans, breathing out oxygen in microscopic bubbles that catch what little light there is. Dry, it peels, curls, goes pale at the edges. It is food and floor and nursery at once. Everything else grazes it, lays eggs under it, burrows through it, or — in the bad weeks — peels it up and eats it.

On it, in their millions, are the snails. They are the color of wet granite and not much larger than a thumbnail, with shells so thick they seem mostly shell, a little grey meat tucked far inside. They move at the speed of damp spreading. Their silver trails cross and recross the pans until, in dim light, a whole flat looks faintly written over. When the water comes they unseal themselves from the undersides of stones, where they have waited glued mouth-down against the heat that never quite arrives. When it leaves, they seal themselves back. A snail here may spend most of its life shut. What it knows of the world is taste through the foot, and vibration.

In water a day old live the things that have waited as dust. Clam shrimp beating their frantic way through ankle-deep tea-brown water, brine shrimp upside-down and filtering, eggs that blew into cracks in the laterite ten, twenty years ago and hatched because this particular shower lasted long enough. Midges rise off them in columns, each column over its own pan, dense as smoke, turning without dispersing. They are food made suddenly out of almost nothing, and for three weeks there is too much of everything to eat.

And then the frogs come up.

They are the plain, briefly given mouths. A dozen kinds, maybe more, divided not so much by territory as by pitch, the way the seasonal people name the pans themselves. Each has found a frequency the flat stone will carry and the other frogs will leave alone.

Highest are the peepers, thumb-sized, bone-colored, who climb onto warm cobbles at the pans' edges and call with their whole bodies braced, toes spread wide to feel their own song coming back through the rock. Their note is thin and metallic and seems to come from nowhere and everywhere. Below them, in the open shallows, sit the spotted pan-frogs, green-black as wet mat, calling in rolling choruses that start with one male testing the water with his throat and are taken up, pan to pan, half a mile at a step.

The heavy toads keep to the deeper depressions. They float with only their eyes and nostrils breaking the surface, their sides working like bellows, and make the low note you do not hear so much as receive — in the sternum, in the fillings of your teeth, in the soles of your feet if you are standing in the sheet-water. Mother Pan is theirs. When they are in full voice the water around each male trembles in perfect rings and no smaller frog calls at all.

Strangest is the pale one, almost translucent, its organs showing as soft shadows, that never properly leaves the water or the mud beneath it. It burrows backward with fringed hind feet, eyes reduced to dark points under skin, and calls from underground through water-filled tunnels, so its song seems to rise out of the earth itself with no animal attached to it. In the dry season all of them go that way: backward, corkscrewing down through cracking clay, wrapping themselves in sacs of shed skin that harden against the loss of water, noses plugged, metabolism slowed to a patience that would look like death anywhere less patient. They sleep under the pans while snails click above them and wait, not for a date, but for vibration. Rain on stone, felt through a foot of earth. That is their alarm clock.

They are not deafened by themselves, though anything visiting will be. Their ears shut when they call — a muscle clamps, the great round tympanum stills — and they listen mostly through their feet and jawbones, through the water they sit in. A female does not find a male by direction; on a place this flat, direction is a lie the loudest pan tells. She finds him by the feel of his particular rhythm through the shallows, by the pattern the ripples make against her belly, distinct in the general trembling like one handclasp in a crowd. Their eggs are laid where sound and current will not strip them away: in black strings under submerged rocks, in foam tucked into the lee of a cobble, in the mouths of the stone bowls on the whalebacks.

Those bowls are used. One kind of small, warty frog lives his adult life almost entirely inside a resonator hollow, wedged in with his back to the stone and his throat to the opening, becoming an instrument larger than himself. His call comes out rounded, amplified, impossibly grave for an animal that would fit in a child's fist. Predators hear a toad the size of a dog. Females hear, perhaps, the stone itself proposing. He eats what the wind and the midge-columns bring to the lip of the bowl and in dry times retreats to its damp bottom, where seep-water beads even in a rainless week.

Around the edges of their noise work the professionals. Ibis and herons, grey as the light, stalk the shallows in loose, unbothered flocks, stomping rhythmically to startle shrimp up out of the mat — they hunt by sight and by the jerk against their bills, their ear-openings small, feathered over, nearly sealed. A rail, long-legged, reluctant to fly, runs pan to pan on toes that spread its weight over mud, snatching frogs with a speed that is purely visual. Snakes swim with their heads held high and their bodies writing S's in an inch of water; they have no outer ears at all to be ruined, and find the frogs by tongue and by the quiver in the water. All of them have learned the great synchronized silence, when a harrier's shadow crosses and a square mile of frogs cuts out at once. In that second the birds freeze too, heads cocked — not listening, watching for the movement that will inevitably come when one brave male starts again.

Everything here is adapted not to the wet, which is easy, but to the volume.

The seasonal people know this and come prepared to be temporary. Their dogs are stone deaf, bred or culled that way for generations, placid trotting animals who communicate by shoulder and hand-signal and sleep soundly with their chins on vibrating stone. The children lip-read. The adults have callused, slightly flattened fingertips from signing against each other's forearms in the worst of the chorus, and carry snail-shell earplugs on sinew strings as another person might carry a knife — always. Even their dead, carried out to the shallow graves at the plain's edge, are buried with the ears stopped with clay.

But they leave. They always leave. The birds leave in long dim lines when the pans begin to lace with salt. The shrimp vanish back into eggs, the midges into a crunch of bodies on drying mat, the snails onto the undersides of rocks. The frogs fray out from the edges of the plain inward, voices going hoarse, fewer each night answering from the stone bowls, until a single toad is croaking into water too shallow to carry it.

What remains is not empty. It is only turned down. Under the clay, in skin sacs, in sealed shells, in eggs like grains of pepper in the cracks of a pan, the whole population is still there, in the posture this place has taught everything that wants to stay: shut, low, patient, and listening through the body for the first rain to arrive, unobstructed, across the horizon, and start the stone ringing again.
