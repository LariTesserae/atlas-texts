At first you would say that nothing lives there, in the way a tenant lives in a house. The frog in its rock mortar is not the tenant. The sunbird is not. They are what the bowl permits for an afternoon — small, separate lives allowed to sit very still at the edge of a much larger attention.

The resident is behind the weeping wall.

You never see it enter or leave, because it cannot. Whatever it began as — and that beginning is far back, before the present ferns, before the channels in the rock were deep enough to lay a finger in — it found the hollow in the curve of the bowl when it was smaller, slipped in through some fissure under the pool, and stayed, and grew. The fissure is still there. The creature no longer fits through it.

Only its breathing reaches you.

The hollow is not empty space. It is mostly occupied. Something soft and dark and patient is curled in the flooded chamber behind the stone, pressed so close to the rock over so many lifetimes that the distinction has become administrative. Water runs over it the way water runs over everything here, in the same threads, year after year, and has polished it and fluted it as it polished and fluted the wall. Mineral fans have dried on the parts of it that break the surface of its black, interior pool: rust, pale green, a blind white where the sun, on the few days it can find the angle through the curtain, strikes and is instantly turned to steam. It has no use for eyes. It may have two, somewhere under that mineral skin, sealed and useless as the scar on the fern trunks. The whole bowl has become the organ it sees with.

For the bowl is its ear, enlarged to the size of a place.

The pool is the drum of it. That is why the rings behave wrongly. A drop falls, the surface receives it — and then the information passes *down*, through a narrow neck of water, into the chamber, and is taken up by the body curled below. The slow ripple you watch crossing the oval is not the drop's own energy spending itself. It is the creature deciding, at its own tempo, what the drop was. When a ring starts in the middle with nothing fallen, a soft *thoom* lifting the water from beneath, that is it shifting in its sleep, or swallowing, or turning some older sound over in the folds where it keeps them.

It keeps them separately. That is the strangest part, once you stay long enough to notice. Rings cross in the pool without interfering, as if each travelled in its own thin layer of water. Voices do the same. You speak near the back wall and the bowl does not give you an echo; echoes are careless, instant. This place is neither. It takes your sentence down into the rock, softens the consonants off it, slows it the way everything here is slowed, and returns it minutes later from a place that is not quite where you sent it — your last word arriving, unhurried and altered, after you have already stopped speaking and started, instead, to listen.

The ferns have learned to listen with it. Six trunks, spaced like pillars, crowns never touching — that is not competition for light. Light here is brief and vertical and they largely ignore it. They turn for sound. A new thread of seep finds a new flute in the stone, the pitch of the wall changes by a fraction, and the fronds uncruling that season come up leaned toward the change, beaded, attentive, the way a row of sleepers might all turn their faces toward a voice in another room. They are not the creature, but they have grown up inside its hearing, and they have taken the shape of it. Everything here has. The frog does not call, because a call in this bowl would be found, magnified, taken down and returned changed — would stop belonging to the frog. The snail leaves silver on bark and makes no noise at all. Life that stays here learns quiet the way desert life learns thirst.

What it eats is difficult to say, because it needs so little and takes so long to take it. Insects that blunder into the pool and soften there. Minerals in the seep, gathered, perhaps, through that vast wet skin. The chemistry the sun manufactures daily out of mist and leaf drip and orchid sugar, carried down on the feet of one furious sunbird. And, at long intervals, what people brought.

The hammerstone by the cleft is the clue to the arrangement, and the arrangement was never worship, exactly. Generations of hands ground something wet in the basalt hollow — seed paste, medicinal leaf, something bitter enough or visionary enough to be worth the climb — and a portion of it will have been pushed, not thrown, into the shallows at the pool's edge. You do not throw offerings to a listening thing. You place them where they can be received. In return, if you sat on the dry ledges at the rim where the roar resolves into a pressure in the sternum, and if you spoke — the names of the dead, a question, a sickness described carefully and close to the stone — the bowl would take your voice down into the one who lives in its wall, and after a time, hours or a night spent just outside the cleft, it would give the voice back. Slower. Softer. With the emphasis moved. People climb a long way, with almost nothing, to hear their own words returned to them as if by someone who has had time to understand them.

It is not wise, and it is not kind, in any human tense. It is merely *old*, in the tense of the lichen mapping itself in rings on the rim, a century to an inch. It survived the rockfall that scarred all six trunks at the same height; somewhere in the chamber there is a corresponding dent, grown around but never closed, which changes the resonance on the left side of the wall to this day. It has outlasted the generations who fed it paste, and forgotten their language, and kept their voices anyway, down in the layered dark, crossing each other without interference, going on arriving.

By day it does very little that you could point to. The mist thickens and thins — that is its exhalation, warmed off the wall and cooled back to rain on the ferns. The pool dims and lifts. A fat drop gathers for ten minutes under a leaf and falls and is received. At dusk, when the light leaves the bowl and the curtains go from silver to merely a sound in the dark, it shifts more freely. Then the *thoom* comes more often, unprompted, and the second reflection appears under the first — the displaced, stiller image of the fern crowns, seen not in water but through the curved, translucent surface of the creature itself as it rises a fraction in its interior pool and listens, with its whole submerged body, to the grove settling. The ledges grow damp. The moss takes a print, somewhere, and holds it, and slowly swells it away.

It is not lonely. Loneliness requires an expectation of company at your own scale, and its scale is now geological. It has the sunbird's fury once a day, the frog's single blink, the snails writing and erasing themselves on the bark. It has every sound the mountain has sent into the bowl since the bowl was finished — wind arriving as pressure, rain arriving as music, the rockfall still arriving on the left side — kept, sorted, endlessly returned.

You leave by turning sideways in the cleft, and three paces out the roar is cut clean, as if a door had closed. That is not the rock doing it. That is the exact boundary of the resident: the line where its hearing ends and the ordinary world resumes.

Behind you it goes on, unseen, warm, saturated, curled in the flooded dark at the heart of the stone — taking the next drop down into itself, and the next, and turning each one over very slowly, and after an interval no living person has ever waited out, giving it back to the empty, gleaming, cathedral grove, softened and changed, to no one in particular.
