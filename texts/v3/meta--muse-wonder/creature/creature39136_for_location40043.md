At first you do not see them at all.

That is not because they are hiding. In the bowl there is nowhere to hide — the light comes up off the limewashed walls and down off the ice and sideways off the cisterns, and lays itself evenly into every corner. It is because they are the same colors as everything else, and they move at the same speed.

A shape you took for a felted wall-panel stirs, and resolves into a shoulder. A bundle of turf on a roof lifts its head and is a woman picking cloudberries into her apron, a goat in felt hoof-covers cropping beside her without alarm. Two men are carrying a timber between houses on a path too narrow for the three of you, and already, without looking up, without a word, they have turned — one with his back to a trellis of peas, the other pressed politely into a doorway — and the timber has turned with them, as a single animal negotiates a burrow. You pass through the brief, warm smell of their breath and their clothes. They resume.

They are small people, though not uniformly, and not in the way of a separate race. They are small the way houses here are low and doors are low and stairs are steep: by long accommodation. Generations of ducking through the slot in the boulders, of passing sideways between a woodpile and a loom, of working stooped under a rope bridge or a terrace, have taken the tallness out of them. The tallest man you will see in a day’s walking stands perhaps five foot three in his felt soles. Most of the women are under five feet. They are broad in the shoulder and hand, deep-chested against the cold, short-legged, unhurried. A child of ten has the settled economy of movement of an adult elsewhere. An adult of forty has the unlined watchfulness of a child who has learned not to startle the birds in the eaves.

Their faces are hard to read at first because their faces do not do what faces do in direct sun. There is no single sun to squint against, so they do not squint. Their brows are smooth, their eyes wide open and habitually narrowed only a fraction — not against brightness, but the way one narrows the eyes to listen. The light arriving from below has given them a curiously shadowless look even up close: no shade under brow or chin, the whole features lit flat and pale and clear. Their eyes are mostly light — greys, washed blues, a hazel gone almost green — with pupils that stay small all day and, there being no night, all night too. They blink slowly. Frost collects sometimes on their eyelashes when they first climb to the rim in the coldest circlings, and they remove it for each other with the back of a warm finger, familiarly, without comment.

Their skin is pale but not sickly. It is weathered in a fine, even way, like wood worn to soap. Cheeks and the backs of hands are reddened by the thin cold; the rest of them is almost never exposed to it. They dress as the houses are dressed: felt over wool over linen, layer colonized upon layer. Tunics and trousers and long aprons of undyed or madder-dyed felt, soft as the soles of their shoes, in greys and browns and a dull moss-green. And as with the house-walls, the felt does not stay merely felt. Lichen takes hold at cuff and hem and the outer seam where a person leans habitually against a wall — pale rosettes, sulphur dusting the elbow, bone-white at the shoulder. The older a garment, the more it belongs to the light. The older a person, the harder it is to tell where the garment ends.

Their hair, kept cropped or bound close in felt caps against tangling on trellises and hoists, is fair gone early to white, or a dark that greys at the temples in the twenties. It does not seem to trouble them. Age here is not measured by hair in any case. It is measured by slowness, and by what one is entrusted to tend.

The young tend fast things. Children run the hoists, their weight no trouble on the rope bridges, calling down not with shouts but with a soft repeated clicking of the tongue that stops at the next terrace. They carry baskets of turnips, wet laundry, cut turf, a kitten, a pot of horsetails, from level to level in an endless patient circulation, and are everywhere underfoot in the manner of sparrows — expected, accommodated, never once tripped over. Boys and girls alike learn first not letters, not numbers, but stillness: how to sit on a granary ledge among the cats and not disturb the drying fish; how to pass a sleeping dog occupying the same narrow stair as a woodpile; how to hold a frog-colored frog in cupped palms until it decides its own direction. They learn to speak by speaking close. A mother instructs a daughter with her mouth three inches from her daughter’s ear, her hand already guiding the girl’s hand at the loom. A father corrects a son’s grip on a saw by laying his own fingers over the boy’s, no louder.

In middle life they tend growing things and making things. These are the gardeners whose spherical bushes and terrace beds you have been admiring without knowing you were admiring a signature. Each family — though family here is a loose word, more like a cluster of households sharing a cistern and a compost vault and great-grandparents — keeps a strip, a corner, a roof. Not owns it, exactly. Keeps it, the way the swallows keep the undersides of the bridges, the way the lemmings keep the gaps in the retaining walls. A woman knows her peas by individual plant and will move, not remove, a self-seeded fern that has come up in the wrong pot, carrying it cupped in soil down two terraces to the shaded place under the hoist where ferns belong. A man mending a retaining wall leaves, without marking or discussing it, the niches where the ptarmigan nest, and works a new stone around a snail trail as if it were a signature he must not efface. Their hands show it: nails short and black-edged with earth that never entirely washes out, knuckles enlarged, fingertips stained green in summer — which is to say, always — and extraordinarily deft. The finger-bargaining in the market is not a quaint custom to them. It is the visible edge of a whole language of hands. They point with chins, beckon with two fingers, agree, refuse, tease, comfort, and instruct apprentices largely in touch. When they do speak aloud, at conversational distance—which for them is under a foot—the voice is low and uninflected, a breath shaped more than a sound projected. They can hold a three-way argument in a doorway about the ownership of a goat, in voices that do not reach the goat.

What laughter there is does not carry either. It arrives as a sudden shaking of shoulders, an exhalation through the nose, eyes crinkling at last into a squint. Weeping is the same. Grief in the bowl is a person sitting down among the pots with her face in her felt apron while two others sit down, one on either side, and take up her work with their hands until she can take it up again.

The old tend slow things, and become slow things. Old men sit by the black stone table at the center, warming arthritic hands in the lens-light and softening resin for the young to use, saying little, their felt sleeves white with lichen. Old women keep the cisterns, skim them, feed the pale fish, know by the behavior of the water boatmen whether a pipe three terraces up has blocked. The very old occupy sunny niches in the house-walls almost like the ptarmigan — a bench let into the masonry, a felt cushion, a view across the bowl — and stare out with round, unembarrassed eyes at the traffic, saying nothing for long stretches, being consulted by a touch on the knee. They are not retired. Lichen and condensation and the settlement of stones are still work, and it is theirs. When one of them dies, which happens quietly, often in one of the short, bright sleeps that pass for night here, a room is dismantled and another room becomes possible. A bed is moved, not removed. Somewhere, usually within the same circling of the sun that outsiders would call a season, a child is born into the vacancy, and is shown, before it can walk, the place in the wall where its great-grandparent sat.

Sleep itself tells you most about them. Having no darkness, they do not sleep all at once, or all together, or for long. They sleep in shifts and snatches, in lofts under green roofs, in alcoves behind felt curtains, with shaped felt covers over their eyes — not to create night so much as to signal it, the way a hand over a child’s eyes signals a game. The city is never less awake than at any other time, and never more. A man rises from two hours’ sleep, steps in his felt soles around the woman who has taken his place at the loom, and carries her unfinished basket up a terrace as if the work were one continuous pair of hands occasionally changing bodies. They dream, they will tell you if you get close enough to ask, in light. Their dreams have weather in them, but no shadows and no loud noises. A dream of shouting is an ill omen. A dream of falling is interpreted, practically, as a warning about a hoist rope.

They are not a silent people because they are solemn. They are silent because they are crowded, and have been crowded for centuries, and have learned that sound is a substance that takes up room. To raise your voice is to occupy someone else’s terrace. To bang a metal pot is to startle swallows, wake a sleeper behind a curtain, set the roof-goats shifting in their hoof-covers, ruin a whole minute’s negotiated stillness between species. So they have bred the bark out of their dogs and the call out of their frogs and, as nearly as courtesy allows, the carrying power out of themselves. What remains is intimate and constant: a murmur at ear-distance, the tap of fingers on a market-cloth laying down white stones, breath, the settling of a dropped tool into moss.

And they live, finally, not as rulers of this packed and layered place but as one of its negotiated occupants. The sparrows have the eaves and are not encouraged into the granaries, where the cats have tenure. The beetles have the compost vaults and are thanked, in the practical form of peelings, for warming the roots above them. The goats have the roofs, the snails have the floor under the ferns, the bees have right of way — a person will stop mid-sentence, mid-step, and wait with lowered eyes while an enormous furred bumblebee blunders past her face. To live here is to know your niche the way you know the hollowed place in a step: by having been shaped to it, over a very long time, without resentment and without much conscious thought.

If you stay long enough you begin to notice that they look at you with a mild, puzzled pity, not for your clothes or your accent, but for your loudness and your shadow. You arrive casting one. You flinch at the brightness and shade your eyes with a hand, making a shadow where no shadow is wanted. You speak at ordinary volume and every head within fifteen feet turns, not in offense — in genuine startlement, as if a stone had clattered. You ask what time it is, and they look, courteously, at the frost forming and melting and forming again along the edge of a leaf, as if you had asked what time a pond is.

They do not want to leave. It is not that they are forbidden the plain; the young go up through the boulders for ice, for stone, for the occasional timber floated down the fjord, and come back down faster, you notice, than they went up, ducking gladly back into the bright bowl and its held, green, frost-edged noon. Outside, there is too much wind and too much horizon and no one close enough to speak to properly.

Inside, there is just enough of everything, and it is theirs in the way warmth is theirs at the black stone: shared, concentrated, passed hand to hand. They go on — planting, dismantling, replanting, warming their fingers, laying white stones down in silence for turnips — small and pale and lichen-flecked and close-voiced, perfectly fitted to the last ledge of the place that made them, and making it, handful by handful, in return.
