No one keeps house in there, if keeping house means sleeping, or eating where you can be seen.

From the lane you would think it was empty except for things. A hollow in the shape of a person, worn into tiles, and all around it a packing of things to the dome. But a hollow that has been maintained for centuries is not an absence. It is a posture.

What lives there lives in the walls, in the exact sense that the walls have become it.

Take the parts, first, the way you are forced to take them when you are standing inside it: inches from your face, at your elbow, behind your calf. Clay ears, slotted into the plaster by the dozen, some chipped, some glazed green, all turned a little outward. Clay hands, no bigger than walnuts, fingers fused or carefully separated, wedged between tablets. Clay hearts. Clay houses. Eyes stamped into the floor tiles and the bead strings and the copper plaques. If you tried to count its organs you would be there until your eyes gave out — a hundred ears for hearing, a hundred hands that have never closed, a hundred small abstract hearts doing no beating at all, individually, and holding each other up.

Individually none of it is alive. Leaning together, it has acquired the conditions of a body.

It is kept, the way a body is kept. Custodians it never sees feed it from behind, through the hatch behind the lamps, working by feel so they need not look at it directly. They trim its nine flames to the same height. They slide new matter into it — a plaque, a ribbon, a name cut into wet clay — into the one gap that will take it, and so it grows, very slowly, inward. It grows at the rate things in it fade. A ribbon lightens. A bundle of sage slumps a fraction toward dust. By then another ribbon has been worked in somewhere else on the eastern wall. Its substance is not replaced. It is supplemented, until supplement and decay have come to an agreement.

Its voice is not its own. On the other side of the north wall the chanters keep a note in relays, one taking it before another can let it fall, and forty clay jars buried mouth-inward in the brickwork catch that note and build it into a chord. That is its breathing, and its speech. Steady pressure on the ribs, on the teeth, on the backs of the eyes. It does not initiate. If you whisper your name standing in it, it returns the name at the volume of a shout, flattened and prolonged, and the little bells hanging over your head shiver in sympathy. After that most visitors learn its preferred conversation, which is none. It takes their unintentional sounds — the scrape of a knee coming in, a caught breath at the light — and makes them briefly enormous, and then goes on humming to itself through its buried jars.

Its climate is itself. Cool, perfectly dry, too bright for shadow, too loud for spiders, too sealed for mice. No ant crosses the copper strip at its threshold. Nothing nests in its ribbons. It has made its interior uninhabitable to every smaller tenancy by being so completely inhabited already. Even dust does not settle in it properly, but hangs arranged in the alabaster light, stirred by vibration, landing and not landing.

What green life it allows is kept at the minimum at which green life can be said to continue. Two leaves by the air shaft, no more, clipped back year after year by dry moving air to exactly two. They are its only outward-facing leaves, a kind of rationed lung. In a niche at elbow height sits the resurrection rosette in its cup, brown, papery, tight as a fist, perfectly capable of opening and perfectly unopened — life practiced entirely as waiting. The dried marjoram and rue woven in generations ago are still not rot and not quite dust. It holds all three in suspension, the way it holds the noon light from its hand-wide window and never lets it become afternoon.

It does not move, because movement would be collapse. Each tablet leans on a bead string, each plaque is wedged by a clay heart, each ribbon holds a bundle that holds a bell that has not fallen in a hundred years only because nothing has shifted. Do not touch is not reverence. It is physiology.

And in the middle of it, the hollow.

That is the strangest part of its anatomy: the only empty place in it is person-shaped, dished smooth by centuries of soles in the stamped tiles, narrowed imperceptibly as it accretes, and kept person-shaped because persons keep arriving to fill it. When you crouch through the knee-high door and work yourself upright into that space, you are not standing before it. You are standing *inside* its one cavity, where its heart would be if it had been allowed a single heart instead of a hundred clay ones. Your sleeves rub its nearest brass mirror-bright. Your image comes back to you from a hundred polished surfaces, broken and overlapping, none whole. Your heartbeat, given a minute, finds the frequency of the jars.

For as long as you can bear it, you lend it what its packed body cannot supply: warmth, an upright spine, a pulse that arrives and will go away again. In return it lends you its pressure, its glare, its sustained note, and removes your shadow.

Then you duck out backwards, because there is no room to turn, and it remains. Not bereft. Not waiting, except in the rosette's technical sense. The lamps are trimmed from the other side. The note does not fall. Another offering is already drying somewhere in the town, about to be carried down the lane toward the riverbed and worked in by blind fingers.

It will be a little narrower when the next person stands in it.
