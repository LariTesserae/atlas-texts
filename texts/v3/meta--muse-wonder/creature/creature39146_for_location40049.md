At first you would swear you were right: nothing lives here.

That is what the room insists on. The lime too fresh, the water too warm and too restlessly replaced, the tiles scoured every morning until the whole tank smells of wet chalk and cleanliness. No insects on the plaster. No slime under your foot. No shadow, at noon, for anything to hide in.

But if you stay — chest-deep, as you have to, turning slowly in the middle because there is nowhere else to be — and if you stop trying to look straight at the brightness, you begin to notice that the brightness has a way of not quite breaking evenly.

She is easiest to see from the side. Not her outline. The disturbance in her.

The sun comes down in its square column and shatters on the surface, and underwater most of that shattering moves together, bands trembling on the far wall, grout-lines wavering. Around her it doesn't. Around her the light bends a fraction too much, as if the water were deeper there, or thicker. Her hands, if she lifts them, have the same bleached and magnified look yours do, but longer in the palm, and with a faint, milky bloom under the skin that doesn't wash off.

She was not brought here. She accrued here, the way the green threading accrued in the shadiest bottom corner — first a film you had to catch at an angle, then a presence that could hold a shape against the current.

She is only as old as the room is. Finished this spring, flooded this spring. That matters. Her skin is still curing, like the plaster.

Out of the water — she is almost never wholly out of the water — she is lime-white and damp, soft at the throat and the insides of the arms, but already going faintly mineral at the shoulders, the elbows, the tops of the knees, where the sun hits her when she stands still. Fine white dust comes off on your palm if she brushes past, which she won't. Underwater those places look turquoise and smooth as tile-glaze. Here and there a real hardness has begun, a scale no bigger than a thumbnail, warm to the touch, with a builder's accidental thumbprint still softening in its middle. In a year or two, if the tank stays flooded, those may join. For now she must stay wet or she will do what new plaster does: tick, craze, split along invisible lines. You can see one old threat of it, a hairline pale scar down her left shin from some early morning when the level dropped too low before the sluice was opened.

Her hair is black, and not quite hair. It stays plastered in fine, divided strands even when dry, each strand splitting again at the end, round and delicate as the maidenhairs forcing themselves out of the wall where the inflow pipe sweats. She tends those ferns without knowing she is doing it. She breathes there. All day the corner steams gently around her mouth, and the ferns have spread a coin's width for every week she has lived.

The roar has made her. Or unmade certain ordinary things in her.

She has ears, but they are kept full of water by preference, two bright beads trembling in the hollows, because through water the room is not loud at all. Through water the pour from the bronze spout, the fat drops falling back off the vault, the gurgle in the overflow, the sigh of air in the drain — all of it separates and becomes distinct, a sort of slow, continuous architecture she can navigate by. Above the surface, if she ever tried to speak to you on the ledge beside her, she would shout without meaning to. Up there her voice is hoarse and badly judged, worn down to compete with stone. Down here she makes a smaller sound, clicking and humming in her chest, and the vault takes it and returns it from all sides at once so it seems to come from the walls.

At noon she is most herself and least visible.

Then she stands directly under the shaft of light on the submerged steps, where the heat comes up strangely through the soles of her feet, and lets the column hit her head and shoulders and break. The glare takes her edges. Her eyes, which are pale — not blind, but polished, reflective as the hammered tin up the shaft, throwing the light back before it can hurt her — go to slits, then close. Algae has taken in a faint tracery across her back where the light cannot quite reach her directly, between the shoulder blades and down the small, shaded channel of her spine: a green threading so fine it looks like veins, and she grazes it, sometimes, thoughtlessly, with her fingernails, and licks the mineral salt from them. Mostly she feeds on what the water brings her and what it leaches. She will press her mouth to a new mortar joint, milky in the first hour after filling, and drink there. She will catch what washes down from the filter beds outside — a seed husk, a drowned ant that wasn't fast enough, once a whole reed.

The reed on the ledge is hers. That is why it has never been pulled.

She lifted it out of the current, planted its roots in the crack at the ledge's edge, and leans it a little further toward the light shaft every few days. It is the only straight, patient thing in the room besides the column of sun, and she measures herself against it. Not her height — she is not tall, compressed somehow by the low vault and by a life mostly spent chest- or neck-deep, strong in the legs from standing against inflow, narrow above that as a swimmer is — but her progress. Leaf by leaf. When it gets its third good leaf she has looked, more than once, up the nine steep steps at the underside of the hatch.

She is frightened of the hatch, and proprietary about it. She knows its warped corner better than the people in the wash-house above do. She knows it must be kicked, not lifted, at the lower left, and on humid afternoons when it sticks she will climb just high enough for her shoulder to reach it, dripping onto the steps, and strike it once with a sound like a hand on a drum. Then she sinks again before the light from the floor above can fall flat across her face. The morning scrubbers with their stiff brush and wooden paddle have never seen her, though they have felt her move. At scrubbing time she makes herself small in the algae-corner, down where the water stays just cooler, fingers hooked lightly into the mouth of the overflow pipe to hold herself against the swirling, breath held, eyes open and shining under the disturbed surface while boots shift on the ledge and the paddle knocks the tiles. They scour, and she endures it, because she understands better than they do that she and the room are doing the same thing and require the same handling: keep it flooded, keep it warm, keep the spout running.

Shut the sluice-gate above. Let the level sigh down. Let the tiles cool and tick as they contract and the surface go dull and film over, and by morning she would be changed in a way she could not undo — stiffened, filmed herself, the green tracery blackened, the fern-corner of her breathing gone still. She knows this in her body the way you know a stair in the dark. That is why, in the night hours, when the black cistern on the roof has given up the last of the day's heat and the inflow runs merely warm, she does not sleep so much as drift, hung upright in the middle of the tank with her chin just clear, turning a quarter-turn an hour with the slow travel of some memory of the sun, listening through water-filled ears for any change in the pour.

For now the system holds. Pour, heat, overflow, condense, drip. Drops gather on the vault and fall back through steam onto her raised face. The square of sun travels its inch an hour across the sharp new tiles, climbs the far wall, and goes, and she goes with it as far as she can, following the last bright band up onto the ledge on wet feet, crouching there among the ewer and the wooden stool until she is almost dry enough to hurt.

Then the room dims. Then she slides back in, without a splash worth naming, and the water closes white and turquoise over the hardening, unfinished shape of her.
