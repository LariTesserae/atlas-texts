From above, no one lives here.

That is the first, clean impression the place gives you, and it holds for a long while after you have come down the thirty-one steps. Doorways open onto empty rooms. Beds are dust. Looms are threaded with nothing, market counters bear only the grooves where hands and knives rested for centuries, wells are capped with a crossed-out circle. There are no bones in the houses, no ash in the hearths that anyone has stirred recently, no footprints but the ones you make and which the hot draft immediately begins to blur at the edges.

And yet the city is not uninhabited. It is only unpeopled.

You feel it first in the sound. A place with no one in it should go quiet when you stop moving. This one does not. The pipes built into the terrace walls go on taking the rising air and turning it into low, shifting chords. Shutters lift and fall. A seed-drum booms somewhere high up, irregularly, against a wall. Under all of it, faint but constant in the heat of the cycle, leaves are striking leaves — not the soft hiss of foliage in rain country, but a dry, ceramic clatter, as if the whole hollow were hung with green-black tiles loosening in an oven.

The inhabitants keep to the walls.

They have to. They were made — or they made themselves — for the seams, not for the open ground. Look where they gather: not around the capped wells, not toward the thin amber light falling crookedly from the slit above, but crowded against the rock, flattened to it, root and trunk and vine all pressed toward the places where the earth exhales. Their roots do not grope blindly for water that stopped coming generations ago. They travel with terrible patience along mortar lines, around chimney vents, down the backs of houses, following buried gradients of heat the way a hand follows a warm pipe in winter. Where a fissure breathes fastest, they are thickest. Where the stone cools, they thin and stop.

If they are trees, they are trees that have forgotten the sky. Above the third tier they come out of the wall almost horizontally, trunks planed flat by their own weight against the rock, bark splitting into long, resinous plates. Their crowns do not reach up. They spread sideways and slightly down, umbrella after umbrella in the permanent twilight, positioned not to catch light but to stand in the path of rising air. Their leaves open flat when the thermal column over the plaza strengthens, darkening as they take the heat, and the whole tier ticks and rustles as they adjust, leaf overlapping leaf, seeking the exact angle of the draft.

Below them the ferns have taken the planters and the window boxes and then, finding those too small, the windows themselves. They spill in fans three meters across, fronds stiff with oil, edges curled from the dryness, unbitten by anything. Nothing has ever chewed them. No insect has stippled them, no grub has signed its name along a stem. They are perfect in the faintly obscene way of things that have outlived every mouth that could harm them. The vines are their more restless kin — or their hands. Vines loop through roof beams and hold sagging roofs up even as they pull them apart, a millimeter a year. Vines curtain the lanes, sway on the bridge ropes, find the seed-drum and worry it until it booms, find a door on its hinge and keep it moving just enough that the clay pipes beside it change note. On the lower stairs, where their oils have polished the stone to a shine, you can see the traffic pattern of centuries: not feet going down to fetch water anymore, but roots going down toward the floor, toward the vents, toward the source.

Cut a leaf — if you bring yourself to do it, in that close green smell — and it does not bleed anything like sap. The inside is hot. Not warm from the ambient air, but hot in itself, a fibrous pulp that gives off the scent of cooked nuts and pepper, as if the plant were slowly roasting its own interior and living on the process. That is the meal here. Mineral vapor from the fissures, crusting yellow with sulfur at the edges, white with salt where it cools. Heat banked all day in the empty plaza and given back slowly through the night. Light, for these citizens, is a rumor that arrives brown and indirect far overhead, and matters less than the weather underground.

The plaza is empty because they need it empty.

It is their hearth and their lung and, when the pods begin to fire, their mating ground. Nothing roots in that cracked clay — it is too hot at the center even for them, concentrated by the bell of the hollow until mirages break and reform above the stone — but nothing is allowed to encroach on it, either. The low curb of polished stones is kept clear. Houses turn their backs to it. That swept vacancy is what makes the column rise, and the column is how they travel, how they speak, how they reproduce. Through the long hot hours the resinous pods swell on vine and branch, tightening in the ascending air, until pressure splits them with reports sharp enough to feel in your teeth. A hundred can go in a minute at the height of the cycle, a ragged, echoing volley around the tiers, and then the air fills with seeds light as ash, edged with dry membranes, thrown up over the plaza and drifting sideways at last to lodge in a planter, a crack, the corner of an abandoned bedroom where a root has already flowered once, indoors, in the dark.

They do not need pollinators. They do not need rain. They need the city exactly as it was built and then abandoned to them: terraces like ribs to climb, pipes like throats to sound through, an empty center to breathe over. Whether the pipes still voice the wind by accident, or whether vine and root and warping door have learned over centuries to cover and uncover the smaller vents at the right hours — that is harder to say from inside a single visit. The chords shift as if answering something. The bridges sway themselves into creaking. At darkening, the inhabitants fold.

That is when they are most legible as a population rather than a growth. As the slit above goes from dull gold back to brown and the plaza begins to give its heat back slower than the walls, the pipes fall silent register by register, longest notes last, and across every tier the leaves that opened to the draft draw in. They fold into tight, overlapping scales with a sound like distant rain that will never fall here. The pods cease firing. Movement does not stop — root-tips go on nosing along warm seams in the walls, a new leaf goes on levering two bricks apart somewhere, very slowly, with all the force of its life — but it becomes private. Interior. You can hear dust sift from a ceiling. A seed come to rest on a market counter. For a short interval the city holds its citizens the way a body holds its breath before sleep.

And then there is the seed room.

Behind the capped wells on the lowest tier, in the one long room the vines have not entered, the bins are still sorted by size. The floor is still cleared. The pruning hooks are still bundled by the cold kiln, the stone blades worn to crescents, the clay tablets still filed from old weather to new: rain, jar, sprout — vent, sprout — sun crossed out — a leaf turned downward, toward the earth. Whoever carved the last of those had already understood what was going to be required. They were not recording the end of the city. They were recording a change of diet, of direction, of allegiance — away from the sky and its water, down toward the hot seams in the stone.

After that the tablets stop. Not because everyone died, exactly. Because carving stopped being the way memory was kept.

Memory is kept now in the bins, in seed sorted from seed, in the room that generation after generation of root and vine has refrained from entering — a restraint no mere plant observes unless restraint itself has been inherited. Memory is kept in the tiers: the flattened elder trees above, the fern children spilling from the planters below, the vines going everywhere like a nervous system, the whole green-black population exchanging places with the masonry so slowly that house and garden can no longer be told apart at the joints. No bones were left behind because there was never a clean moment of leaving. Bodies thinned. Habits migrated. The people who had learned to read heat instead of rain, who had crossed out the well and then the sun, who had turned the leaf downward on the tablet — they did not vanish into the desert they could no longer live in. They went into the walls they could.

So what lives here is one thing that is also many: a city-full of heat-eaters wearing the city as skeleton and trellis and instrument, descended from gardeners who became the garden. In the bright part of the cycle they fire their seeds into their own rising breath and call, chord by chord, through the pipes their ancestors built into the terraces — not melody, not quite, but the sound of a large, slow citizenry at work in its one hollow room. At night they fold their leaves like hands, settle their bridges, and wait in the unspent heat under the roof for the column to begin, almost imperceptibly, to turn again.
