They are not visitors to the dome. They are what happens when the dome lasts long enough.

From a distance — if there were distance in a bathtub of water under three meters of ice — they look like weather. A slow snow that sometimes falls up. Hundreds of them, thousands when the copepods are awake, each body no larger than a thumbnail, most smaller than a grain of rice, all of them pale in the way that things become pale when color stops being useful. White, glass, faint blue, faint pink where the gut shows through. No one is bright. Brightness would be a way of being eaten.

They live layered, because the water itself is layered.

Highest, pressed against the ice ceiling with their backs to the sky, are the amphipods. Curved like commas, like clenched fists, they walk upside down as naturally as flies on a roof. *Apherusa* and *Gammarus* and a dozen cousins whose names change by basin, all of them masons by appetite. They have seven pairs of legs doing seven different jobs — two for tasting, two for grabbing, the rest for holding on while the current tries to peel them off — and they are never not working. They graze the brown-green chains of ice algae that bead down from the brine channels, they scrape diatoms off the crystal, they eat, they void pellets still soft with mucus, and they press those pellets into the sponge and bryozoan lace that grows opportunistically wherever a surface holds still long enough. Then they live inside what they have excreted. A molted shell becomes a roof. A dead neighbor becomes a load-bearing wall. When an amphipod backs out of its old exoskeleton, pale and soft and suddenly too large for its house, something smaller is already waiting to move in.

They carry their young in pouches under their bellies, a cluster of orange eggs held against the cold, fanned constantly by pleopods so they do not freeze or suffocate. They cannot afford to lay eggs and leave. Nothing here can be left. Everything unattached drifts to the boundary between fresh and salt, bounces there a while in that invisible skin halfway down the dome, and is found.

Below them, in the flickering middle water, the copepods keep a different time. Translucent darts with single red eyes and antennae longer than their bodies, they hang motionless for minutes — heartbeat slowed to almost nothing, antifreeze proteins keeping ice crystals from seeding in their blood — and then flick, vanish, reappear an inch away. They are the plankton the tide brings and the city keeps. They feed the shrimp. They feed the young sculpins. Mostly they feed everyone by dying in enormous, unremarkable numbers and becoming detritus, the sideways snow that clogs worm tubes and sponge chimneys at the turn of the tide.

The shrimp are ghosts among them, finger-jointed and nearly invisible except for their black eyes and the dark line of their gut when they have just eaten. They walk rather than swim when they can, picking. The isopods, flat as coins, armor-plated and slow, bulldoze through the pellet-brick districts, eating the mortar as fast as it is laid. This is not vandalism. This is zoning. A wall eaten in the brackish middle streets keeps a passage open that would otherwise seal, and the amphipods coming behind with mouthfuls of diatom glass will rebuild it a hand's width over, where the current is kinder.

Down where the water turns properly salty and a fraction warmer — still below freezing by any human standard, still capable of killing warm blood in minutes — the tube worms keep the foundations in a state of permanent, deliberate collapse. They build paper chimneys out of mucus and silt, extend feathery crowns to catch what falls, retract at a shadow. Their chimneys dissolve at the tip and grow at the base in the same hour. Around them the snails graze, tiny coiled grazers whose shells knock softly against ice as they rasp algae from the lip of the dome, leaving clean tracks that other things follow by scent.

And stepping over all of it, impossibly, the sea spiders.

They do not look like they belong to the same physics. Legs like stilts, body reduced to a knot where the legs meet, gut and gonads pushed out into the limbs because there is no room anywhere else. They move in exaggerated slow motion, probing sponge with a proboscis, carrying — the males — great pom-poms of eggs glued to special legs, tending them in the dark. They eat anemones and bryozoans and things too sessile to have a plan. They are eaten, in turn, very rarely, because there is almost nothing to them but leg.

Patrolling the avenues between platelet stalagmites are the sculpins, and they are the only citizens with real size and therefore real authority. Finger-long, ice-colored, big-eyed in the winter dimness, they hover without apparent effort, pectoral fins fanning, mouths opening and closing to taste the water. They are camouflaged to the point of theology: you see one when it moves across a darker patch of algae and becomes, briefly, a silhouette. They eat amphipods who build across a thoroughfare. They eat shrimp who linger too long at the boundary layer. They eat each other's young. Without them the masons would seal the dome shut from inside and suffocate in their own beautiful architecture; with them, districts empty overnight and are founded again nearer food. Predation here is civic planning.

Nobody speaks. Nobody ever really touches. Antennae do that work — long, whipping, constantly sampling — reading iron from the algae gardens, salt from a new brine weep, the alarm chemicals leaking from a crushed copepod three streets away. When the tide squeezes through the crack in the back of the dome and the whole lens of meltwater tilts, every antenna in the city swings into the current at once, a forest bending, and every body braces or lets go on cue. The sculpins slide out. The worms retract, then bloom. It is the hour when the city feeds and thins itself in the same gesture.

They share a single metabolism stretched across species. The algae make sugar out of dim light. The amphipods make algae into pellets into walls into shelter for copepod eggs. The isopods eat the walls. The worms trap what the isopods loosen. The sponges — animals themselves, patient and porous — filter what the worms miss, and grow rooms the amphipods will later subdivide. Someone dies and is not wasted, because waste, in water this clear and food this scarce, is a form of information. A body says: building material here. Calcium here. A doorway, already curved.

Their lives are slow because cold is slow. Hearts beat a few times a minute. A generation can take a year. Growth happens in sudden jumps at a molt, then long weeks of hardening, grazing, carrying. If you watched for a month you might think they were contemplative. They are not. They are running a race at the speed chemistry allows, against ice that grows down, melts up, crawls with salt channels that open new pockets behind curtains of crystal — annexes, suburbs, kettle-sized rooms connected by passages one fish wide — into which families carry glass bricks, mouthful by mouthful, to start again.

By late winter many of them will be caught exactly as they are: an amphipod mid-carry, a sea spider mid-step, a sculpin halted with a mason in its mouth, all sealed crystal by crystal into clear ice when the dome freezes through. Others will feel the lens spill as the keel rolls, find themselves suddenly wall-less in the open sea under the floe, and swim up — weak swimmers, ceiling-walkers, bottom-grazers, all of them — toward another flaw, another hollow still deciding its shape, to press the first soft pellet against fresh platelet ice and begin the negotiation again, in silence, upside down, in the blue-green dark.
