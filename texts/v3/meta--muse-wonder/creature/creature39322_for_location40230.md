At first, arriving, you would think no one lives here at all.

You see the towers exhaling, the cranes turning behind glass, sled-loaders gliding on their rails, track being laid and relaid, steam folding itself into low cloud — and all of it nearly silent. Then a door unseals somewhere and a hundred of them cross a yard at once, shoulder to shoulder in padded suits, and not a voice carries. Only the squeak of snow under boots. Only breath, coming out white in front of every face and hanging there a second too long, as if each person is being followed by a small, loyal ghost.

They are bundled past recognition. That is their public shape.

Suits stitched in overlapping panels so a tear does not become a wound to the whole garment. Suits with loose hoods and looser doors in mind — everything on them is made to give a little. Zips are large enough for mittened hands. Seams are brightened with thread so you can see at a glance where the strain is going. Their boots have soft, wide soles; not for speed, but for listening through. A good walker here can feel a seam begin under a street the way you or I might feel a loose tooth with a tongue. They pause, mid-step, one foot still raised. They set it down a hand's breadth to the left. Everyone behind them adjusts without question.

Under the hoods they keep their hair cut short or braided flat, because damp gets into everything and long hair freezes at the tips by the end of a shift and snaps. Faces get weathered in a particular patchwork: cheeks polished red by cold, foreheads pale from hoods, chins chapped where breath has been condensing and freezing and thawing all day against cloth. Indoors their eyes look too large. They have grown up reading each other at a distance through fogged visors and across silent yards, so they watch more than most people do.

And they talk with their whole hands.

Outside, where voices do not carry and shouting is against the law, they have built a language of gesture that never quite stops. It is there in the canteens too, spilling over, even when the doors are sealed and they can finally be loud — and indoors, behind glass, they *are* loud, frantic with it, laughing, arguing, banging mugs, singing over one another in the heat. But the hands keep going anyway. A shrug travels down an entire work crew like a ripple. Warnings are flicked from wrist, not shouted: *ground's up. Pipe's hot. Mind the fox. Tea's ready.* Children learn it before they learn to read a map, which is just as well, because the maps change on Mondays.

They are not a solemn people. They are a sealed-in people. From outside, a night-shift canteen looks like an aquarium in a riot. From inside, it is just supper.

They live on top of one another because there is nowhere else safe to live. A family may have a room and a half dug into warm silt, a bunk niche behind a greenhouse stack, a sleeping loft whose ceiling is someone else's floor and whose floor rises, some months, very gently toward the window. Privacy is not measured in distance here. It is measured in curtains, in averted eyes, in knowing — instantly, without being told — when the people pressed against you on a walkway are present and when they have politely ceased to be. They jostle constantly and take offense rarely. Density is the weather indoors, too.

Everything they own can be moved by two people in one trip. That is not poverty. It is doctrine.

Beds on runners. Stoves on sled bases. Crates instead of cupboards, good gloves passed hand to hand until the stitching is more original than the leather, seedlings in tins, dried peas, fish, machine parts, a kettle blackened on one side from sitting always against the same warm pipe. Addresses are leased by the month and written in pencil because the building may heave and its number, which follows elevation, may be legally different by spring. Old photographs are not hung with nails; the wall cannot be trusted with a fixed point. They are tucked into flexible frames and re-hung after a shift, a fraction higher, a fraction crooked, and left crooked. Straightness is a temporary opinion.

Their days are made of re-work, and they do not resent it the way an outsider expects.

There are screw-crews out at dawn, turning the great foundation screws before the frost hardens. There are mallet-crews tapping ice clear of pipes — *knock, knock*, soft, wooden, patient — and fern-crews behind them cutting back the green that has already found the new warmth, and behind *them* the greenhouse tenders gathering the cuttings because nothing edible or useful is ever simply thrown away. A pipeline is not finished when it is laid. A street is not finished when it is paved. Nothing is finished. The young think in shifts; the old think in returns. To do a job once and expect it to stay done is considered, gently, a foreigner's superstition.

Warmth is their real currency, and they are fluent in it.

They can read a building the way a farmer reads soil: this wall will weep enough at noon to wash your face, that vent-way grows the best watercress, this stairwell is generous in January and mean in March. They harvest drinking water with brushes and sheets of felt, with cold glass angled into alleys where it is always privately raining. A rich household is not the one with more rooms. It is the one whose heat leaks in useful places. A poor one wraps its pipes too well and goes thirsty in a city covered in damp.

So they cultivate their leaks. Ferns beside the boiler housing. Tomatoes climbing the fire escape into snow. Beans feeling for the warm side of a stack. Their sleeves are often green at the cuff. Their food tastes faintly of iron and greenhouse: fish from the brackish edge where the sea has not quite decided to leave, peas dried and revived, tomato soup in winter, horsetail shoots, eggs when the coops are warm enough to persuade the hens. They eat communally more often than not because one hot room shared by thirty bodies stays hot, and because moving a crate of fish a few feet uphill when a seep opens under the market is simply what everyone does — without complaint, without noise, as if helping a tide move furniture.

They share the city, with an exasperated legality, with everything else that has learned to live in it.

Ravens on the cooling towers are not shooed; they are unofficial inspectors, and a crew will wait if a raven is worrying at a seam. Foxes come through the foundation tunnels that no human is allowed to block, hay-lined, waist-high, running straight through laundry levels and under canteens, and people step over the warm draught of a fox-passage the way they step over a sleeping relative's feet. Sparrows nest in junction boxes. Mice make nations in the insulation. Moths big as ash-leaves circle the grow-lamps all winter and dust the workers' shoulders with themselves. It is useless to call any of this *invasion*. There is no border left to invade across. The inhabitants simply assume a corridor, a ledge, a pipe, a life, will be shared.

Their superstitions grow in the cracks left by geology.

Do not leave metal tools out overnight on soft ground. Do not sleep on the ground floor with your shoes pointing at the door. Do not copy a pattern too exactly where the silt can see it — the ground here is impressionable, and has extruded stony lumps in the shape of dropped wrenches, and grown moss in green circuits along buried heating lines, and once, famously, produced a row of basalt columns so architectural that a warehouse crew tried to roof them before the surveyors stopped them. People laugh about these things in the canteen. They do not laugh about them after a heave, when a floor has risen two inches in the night and every threshold has to be stretched to meet it. Then the tools come in. Then the shoes are turned sideways by the bed, toes to the wall, like something that is staying.

Their elders are the true instruments of the place. An old woman can set a cup of tea down and tell you, from whether the surface stays level, how restless the week will be. Old men sit with their boots unlaced in the evenings so they can feel the building reconsider itself through the soles of their feet. They remember when this was sea-bottom and that was a moraine and the greenhouse fig was no taller than a thumb. Having no permanent ground, they have become permanent in a different way: in memory, in routes, in knowing which address used to be uphill from which.

The children, born here, find none of this strange. They are raised by a moving city the way other children are raised by weather. They learn to sleep through the deep subterranean crack that arrives through the teeth. They learn that a new hill in a rail siding is not an emergency but a discussion — cut through it, lay track over it, let the gradient join the route. They learn to move their market stalls a few feet uphill, to turn screws at dawn, to read a fox's intentions, to keep doors loose in their frames and affections looser in their tenancies. They put out a first leaf, sometimes, toward a warmth that has only just appeared.

That is who lives here: not conquerors of a new land, and not quite its tenants, either. Accommodators. Re-workers. People who have made a virtue, and then a culture, and then a body, out of yielding intelligently. Cold in their seams, damp on their sleeves, silent in the open and riotous behind glass, brushing water off cold glass to drink, growing food out of leaked heat, writing home in pencil in case home moves.

The plain is still rising under them, still trying out shapes, still half sea, half stone, half decided. And they rise with it — an inch, a hand's breadth, a changed address — arguing with it densely, electrically, greenly, in hand-signals and steam, day after day.
