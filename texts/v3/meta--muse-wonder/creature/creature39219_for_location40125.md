No one lives in the Upper Sea, if you go looking for living the way you would below the clouds.

There is no village. No harbors in the root-rafts, no lights arranged in streets in the blue-black deeps, no nets, no boats turned keel-up in the meadows for repair. Where the ruins stand — the fifty-mile causeway, the staircase down into a deeper basin, the round houses patiently unbuilding themselves in the warm currents — nobody repairs them. Where the stone takes a handprint in the clay-soft floor and holds it for an hour, nobody comes to see whose hand it was.

And yet the sea is not empty. It is only that its inhabitants have been worn smooth along with everything else, until the difference between *someone* and *somewhere* is mostly a matter of patience.

You notice them first as an absence of erasure. A footprint that heals too quickly, even for here. A broken frond whose edges have already been sealed with a clear, sweet-smelling sap. In the dry bell-chamber at the top of the drowned tower, where the dust hangs and considers, sometimes your written name does not soften evenly. Sometimes it softens letter by letter, in order, as if being read.

Then, if you stay long enough — and no one measures long enough correctly up there, the tides count time better than clocks — you see one walking.

On the floor. Not swimming. Walking.

Small at first, perhaps no taller than your waist, perhaps taller than you; the light lies about size and so does memory afterward. It goes upright on the bottom among the round ruins with its head bowed against a current that isn't blowing, placing narrow, splay-toed feet with terrible care in the ghost of a riverbed. Sediment falls sideways around its ankles. Bubbles sink past it and pool silver under the ledges. It does not look at you. Its eyes, if they are eyes, are covered over with a pale membrane, translucent as the inside of a leaf, and veined green.

Its skin is the color of things that have lived a long time out of direct sun. Not white. The color of wet bone, of roots, of fish bellies, shading at the shoulders and along the long, flexible neck into a living, photosynthetic green. Down its back and from the backs of its arms grow fans — not fins, not wings, but frilled, fretted fans of tissue so thin the late-afternoon light goes through them olive and amber. It opens them as it walks. It closes them when a cold front of clearer water comes through and the plants nearby close, too.

It breathes in the wrong layer for walking. That's how you learn it is young.

The young ones belong to the floor. They are born down there — though *born* does as much lying as *size* — in the pockets beneath the second surface, beneath the ceiling of gathered rain that hangs miles under the true sky of the sea. A child of the Upper Sea pushes its head up through that still, clear boundary into the trapped, airy water beyond it and takes its first breath there, warm as bathwater, tasting of minerals. Then it sinks again into the heavier water of its childhood and goes down to walk among the houses its kind no longer needs.

They walk for years. Decades, perhaps. Keeping to the old roads because the roads still remember being roads and sediment settles along them more reliably underfoot. They eat what rains inside the sea: pollen kept from sinking for years, pale seeds ticking down in silver lines, the soft fruit of the rootless meadows that flower as they travel. They press their broad palms to the vent-cracks and drink heat and minerals directly through the skin, and the fans on their backs flush dark with it. They sleep standing in the corridors of the stilt-forests, one hand curled around a trunk, swaying in the tidal inhalation, and while they sleep fine white rootlets grow from their heels into the floor and from their fingertips into the bark, so that waking is a slow, deliberate tearing, and they leave a little of themselves behind every morning.

This is not damage. This is how they learn the forest, and how the forest learns them back.

As they age, they get taller in the neck and stranger in the proportions, and the walking gets harder — not because the water resists them, but because it increasingly refuses to let them stay down. Air gathers in the hollow fans. Air gathers in chambers under the ribs, in the spongy tissue of the long throat. One morning — in a morning that is only a lessening of dusk — a walker lets go of the bottom and does not sink again.

That is the second life.

You have maybe seen it, once in a summer, once in a childhood, at the edge of visibility: a pale shape, long-necked, blind, moving through the slanting columns of sun with its fans spread like a peacock's tail in all directions at once. It is not fleeing you because flight belongs to animals who live in a world of edges, and it has very few edges left. Plants part for it as it passes, not out of fear but out of a deeper recognition. In the warmth of its wake, seeds burst open all at once.

The drifters are the great age of the species — if it is a species, if *age* is not another lie. They may drift for longer than the tower has been breaking the surface. They follow the sideways kelp-fields along the warm currents, province after province of horizontal meadows, and they sing down there where sound is eaten within feet. Singing, for them, is shaping the warm cloud of a breath and letting it go: a pressure, a tremor in the sternum, a vibration that the hanging bells under the meadows pick up and pass on, bell to bell, meadow to meadow, until whole fields pulse in sequence in the dark. At night the glow travels with the song. If you rest half-submerged on a high root-raft and watch the vegetation light up in slow sequence far below you, you are not watching weather. You are listening, badly, with your eyes, to a conversation whose sentences may take a season to finish.

They do not remember being walkers the way you remember being a child. The sea intervenes. Memory, like footprints in the clay floor, like names written in tower dust, softens at the edges, widens, heals. A drifter carries its whole youth in its body — the mineral rings in its bones from the vents, the green in its fans from ten thousand afternoons — but it could not tell you a single day of it. The locals below the clouds, who have never climbed through the vapor, dream of these creatures sometimes and wake with a word on their tongues. It erodes as they try to say it. That is the correct pronunciation.

In the end, which takes a very long time to arrive, the fans thicken. The chambers that held air begin to hold water instead, sweet and still, the way the huge leaves of the stilt-trees do. The drifter rises on an inhalation into the shallows at the rim and does not entirely exhale back. Rootlets, which it has been shedding and regrowing all its life, find a mat of other roots and take hold with a seriousness they never had before. The long neck stiffens, then forgets it was ever a neck and becomes a reasonable shape for a trunk. The fans become fronds. The green that was only on its back spreads.

Some of the trees in the forests you swim through grow down from the rafts and up from the floor until they meet in the middle and confuse themselves. Some of them do that because they are meeting *themselves* — the rooted elder above, reaching down, and the walker it once was, still rising in the wood's memory, reaching up. Their bark goes soft and spongy. Their leaves fill with held water. Corridors open between them that are exactly the width of a drifting body, and close behind you when you pass, gently, like a throat swallowing.

They stay there, rooted, for the longest life of all. They are the ones who smooth the footprints. Their roots go questing through the clay floor at night and find every sharp mark the day has made — your handprint, a fallen stone's scar, the snapped stem of a frond — and press it patiently back toward unmarkedness. They are the ones who read the names in the tower dust, not with eyes, which they no longer keep, but with the fine hairs along their submerged roots that can taste a disturbance in the water ten miles off and send, very slowly, a root's tip up through a crack in the stone to touch the letters back into dust. It is not that they object to being remembered. It is that they have been trees long enough to know what remembering costs, and they are kind.

And when a rooted elder finally dies — truly dies, which mostly means deciding, over the course of fifty years, to stop — its trunk goes hollow and fills with the airy upper water, and small, pale, splay-toed things that have been gestating in the dark of it like seeds in a fruit push their heads up through its still surface and breathe, and sink, and walk away along the old roads toward the ruins, opening fans they do not yet know how to use.

No one lives in the Upper Sea. There are no houses, no harbors, no graves.

There is only a single, slow people distributed across a whole warm ocean — walking its floor, drifting its currents, rooted in its forests, dreaming its paintings of a sun with too many rays — being gradually, tenderly, over centuries, turned from animal into place.

If one passes you in the dusk, open your hands and let the tide take you a little. Do not try to hold on to its shape afterward. It will soften at the edges, widen, heal. That is not you forgetting it.

That is it, still traveling through you, on its way to becoming a tree.
