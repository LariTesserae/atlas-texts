If you count only what can be seen at noon, the hollow is crowded and the question answers itself.

The swifts live there, after a fashion, and the snowfinches, and the choughs in their hundreds with their red lacquered beaks. The marmots live in the boulder-field where the wall has shed its teeth. Voles live under the sedges. Midges and pale mayflies live for an afternoon above the pool. A raven visits. An eagle visits more terribly. In the wet cracks the saxifrage lives, and the moss campion in its tight pink cushions, and the ferns that never quite finish uncurling before the season ends.

But none of them belong to the place the way it belongs to itself. The birds are commuters. They pour out at dawn and are gone over the rim, leaving the stone cylinder empty. The marmots are tenants of the warm hours. The flowers are a brief opinion the summer holds. Even the shepherds who built the little stone fold in the sheltered corner, who hammered the iron ring into the wall and cut notches in the post on the rim — even they only ever borrowed it. Their marks all say the same thing: *we stayed a little while, we did not stay.*

The true inhabitant is the one that never leaves, because it learned long ago how not to be seen in a place with nowhere to hide.

It keeps low.

That is the first law of the hollow, and everything that endures there obeys it: no tree, no tall flower, no standing stone that the winter wind cannot worry at. So the inhabitant does not rise. It does not rear up, or perch, or cast a shadow worth noticing. For most of the day, for most of the summer, you could look directly at it and see only the floor doing what floors do — moss darkening after rain, sedges stirring, a patch of ground near the wall giving off the faintest steam where the sun has warmed it.

Its back is broad and very slightly domed, grey-green, upholstered in its own slow garden. Things root in it the way they root everywhere in the hollow, shallowly, holding on: campion finds the warmer seams along its ribs and blooms there first. Buttercups open above its shoulders a day before they open anywhere else, fooled by the stored heat of a body. Where its hide has thickened with age it looks like nothing so much as weathered granite, striped orange and white with lichen, pocked with old scars that fill with rainwater and are mistaken, by finches, for tiny pools. Put your palm down to test the ground, as everyone eventually does, and the ground gives like a sponge and comes up wet — and if you left your palm there long enough, longer than anyone thinks to, you would feel the give come back. Once. Then again, much later. A pressure so slow it could be the earth settling. It isn't the earth settling.

It is breathing at the speed of stone.

Its head rests for most of the day at the edge of the black pool, in the one place visitors are instinctively careful not to camp. This is not politeness. The center of the hollow is where the cold gathers at night, sinking off the walls, but it is also where the inhabitant is least protected: there the hide thins to a dark, smooth membrane over the pool's rim, and the water trembles not only where the waterfall lands but along its whole still edge, in sympathy. Its face is built entirely for the acoustics of its home, and a face built for the Ear does not need to be beautiful in the way of eagles. It is low and wide and blunt, the color of wet slate. The eyes are small, grey-lidded, kept nearly shut against the thin-air glare; it lives in a bowl open only to the sky, and it has long since given up looking at the sky. There is nothing up there it cannot hear arriving.

What it has instead of eyes is hearing, and the hearing is extravagant.

Folded flat along either side of its skull, damp and fern-dark, lie the ears — not the neat cups of a marmot but great soft fans, veined like leaves, that uncurl a fraction as the day warms. One turns, almost imperceptibly, toward the cleft when a climber is still half an hour below it on the ridge, boots loosening scree. The other stays angled at the pool. It hears the thin fall quicken as the sun reaches the snowfield above the rim. It hears voles negotiating the root-ways beneath its own belly. It hears a sheep that has not existed for two hundred years shift in its sleep inside the ruined fold — no. It hears the *place where that sound used to be*, which is a different thing, and one this creature is uniquely equipped to explain if it could speak.

It cannot speak. Or rather, it has very carefully unlearned how.

Anything with a voice learns caution in the hollow, because the walls give every sound back enlarged, and then give it back again. A creature this size, with lungs this slow and deep, could kill the colony in the nesting holes with one startled cry. It could deafen itself. So over the centuries it has traded voice for another instrument: the hollow slab above the pool, and the resonant chamber beneath it, which is not a chamber in the rock at all but the cupped, empty architecture of its own breastbone, held just under the stone. When it wants — when it must — it knocks from below with the heel of a forefoot, short and padded and wide as a snowshoe, and the fall of meltwater does the rest. Boom. Boom. A note you feel in your sternum.

Shepherds thought it was the mountain drumming. It is the mountain's tenant, keeping time.

Mostly it keeps time for itself: slowing its heart in the cold morning when the melt is a trickle, letting the tattoo quicken at midday when the runnels rise and the whole floor is threaded with new water. But it is hard, listening to it across a long still afternoon, not to feel that some of the rhythm is attention. A pause after a raven crosses the rim. A softening, late in the day, as the birds come home and settle into the wall with a noise like a crowd finding its seats. Single, separate booms in the evening, spaced like a heart deciding whether to sleep. That is exactly what it is deciding.

It eats what the hollow amplifies toward it and what the hollow lets fall. Midges, in their thousands, drowned and undrowned, skimmed off the pool by a tongue the color of peat-water, unhurried and astonishingly long. Mayflies in their brief column. The occasional vole that mistakes a warm flank for safe ground. Fallen nestlings, which it does not hunt for and does not waste. Mostly, though, it grazes the way the place itself feeds: slowly, on light and melt and green things, lichen rasped from stone in the shadowed corner where nothing else bothers, moss cropped close along the runnels in the hour before dawn when the frost is just loosening. It is not a predator in any way that matters. It is something older and more patient than predation: a creature that has arranged to be fed by a season only eight weeks long, and to want nothing the rest of the year.

And it eats the sound.

This is its real work, and the reason the hollow is bearable. Left alone, a stone ear at the top of the world would fill up. Every shout would go on thinning and bouncing for days; every swift's scream would layer over the last until the air itself was unusable and the birds fled their own echoes. But at night, when the colony has gone quiet in the wall and the marmots have withdrawn and the fall has slowed, the inhabitant lifts its head from the pool's edge — an inch, then another, the garden on its back tilting minutely, a runnel somewhere finding a new path downhill across a shoulder — and it opens a mouth that has no teeth worth mentioning and begins, gently, to take the day back in.

It draws the echoes down off the walls the way you might draw smoke out of a room. Yesterday's boot-fall on the resonant slab. The three foolish shouts of a visitor, still circulating, going threadbare. The alarm-whistles of marmots, the mobbing of choughs, a whispered conversation by the pool that the speakers believed was private, because no place on earth is less private than this one. It swallows them in order, oldest and faintest first, and as it swallows, the booming under the slab changes — soft repetitions, distorted, diminished: a whistle returned at the pitch of a sigh, a shout come home as a murmur, its own name spoken in a dead shepherd's dialect and pronounced, now, only approximately right. It is not mockery. It is digestion. Sounds it has eaten return no more. Sounds it is saving — and it does save some, the tally-keeper's counting song, a dog's two-note call, rain on a bothy roof that no longer exists — it stores down in the long dark of its body, where they keep for centuries, slowly losing their meanings and keeping their shapes, like shells.

This is why you leave through the cleft quietly. It isn't superstition, exactly. Whatever noise you make on your way out will be the last thing it eats that night, alone, after you are gone, tasting of boot-nails and breath and the particular loneliness of the person who made it.

In winter it does the only thing left to do. When the spindrift begins to pour over the rim and the hollow starts its long erasure — pool, fold, floor, all smoothed away under white fill — the inhabitant walks, perhaps for the only time in the year, the short distance to the center. It settles. It folds its great ears down over its face like hands over eyes. It lets the snow take it the way the snow takes everything, and the drumming under the slab slows, and slows, until the beats are minutes apart, then an hour, then something nearer to weather than to a heart, and then it stops being heard at all, though whether it stops is a question no one has ever wintered there to answer.

Under the fill, in the black, it dreams the archive. Sheep bells in a lost tuning. Wind through a door that was burned for firewood before your language had a word for this mountain. The first swift ever to come screaming through the cleft, and the sound the hollow made discovering what it was for. It dreams them one at a time, at true volume, in a skull built to hold them, and the snow keeps the secret, and the silence gathers its strength for the thaw.

Then, for a short part of the year, the melt returns to the lip high on the shadowed wall. A hand's breadth of water finds the resonant stone. Somewhere under the wet green floor, an ear uncurls toward a sound it has been waiting eight months to hear again, played, as always, on its own heart.

Boom.

It is awake. It is listening. It was never, even once, an empty room.
