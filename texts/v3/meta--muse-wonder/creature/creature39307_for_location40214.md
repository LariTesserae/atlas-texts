If you stay long enough — crouched, so your knees ache, so your breathing slows to theirs — you stop seeing them as separate kinds. You see them as one population wearing many shapes, all of them translated into the same grammar: hunger, moisture, and not being eaten before you have mated.

The crickets are the country itself. There are too many to count and that is the point. Cave crickets, camel-backed, the color of ash that was once wet and has dried in the sun, though no sun has ever touched them. Their hind legs are folded catapults, their antennae are longer than their bodies and never still. They test the air, the stone, each other, your boot, the void beyond the web-sheet, with a fine, blind braille. Alone a cricket is a ridiculous, tender thing, all leverage and appetite. Together they are upholstery. They crowd the upper walls in a living fringe where the stone is warmest from the machines above, and when you exhale too hard the whole fringe ripples, not fleeing so much as passing the news along a body made of bodies.

They eat everything that can be persuaded to be food: paint flakes, cardboard gone soft with age, the dead moths that spiral down from the bulb, each other's shed skins, each other's legs when a leg is slow. A cricket molts pale and private in a seam and comes out ravenous for its own former self. Nothing shames them. They are both the herd and the pasture.

Below them, at exactly the height where a blundering cricket will eventually fall, the cellar spiders have made a city that became a continent. You cannot find where one web ends. The silk has merged, repaired, been eaten and respun so many times that the whole middle band of the hollow trembles as a single instrument. In each dimple of it a spider hangs or crouches, long legs folded like a closed umbrella, body no bigger than a lentil. They are pholcids, cellar spiders, trembling spiders, and trembling is their language. A touch on the far side of the sheet arrives as information. A cricket thrashing near arrives as weather, then as dinner. They do not chase. The world delivers itself, vibrating. When they kill, they wrap first and bite later, carefully, at leisure, drinking without tearing, leaving a husk so neat the carpet beetle larvae can move into it like tenants. When their webs tear, they eat the silk. Protein is too precious to leave hanging in the air.

The cockroaches own the floor-line, the jurisdiction where wall gives up and becomes ground. They are not the frantic scatterers of kitchens. Here they are pastoral. They move in loose, following herds along roads they have laid down themselves, pheromone trails polished faintly bright through the grey dust, generation after generation reinforcing the same routes between the firebricks, the lunch pail, the seam under the east junction box where it is always a few degrees warmer. They pause to groom antennae with fastidious, almost mammalian care. They cluster to mate, to feed shoulder to shoulder on a fallen spider, on a split label, on a dead roach not yet cool. In them cannibalism looks almost tender, a cleaning up of the family by the family. If the bulb snaps brighter or a forklift thunders overhead they all freeze at once, flat as coins, and for that half-second you can see how many there are: the floor itself, edged in amber and black.

Between their roads the smaller nations commute. Silverfish, quicksilver without the poison, go flashing from flake to flake of fallen insulation, eating glue, paper, dandruff, the sizing off a shred of cardboard, living on the idea of starch. Earwigs hold the dampless cracks that would be damp in any other cellar, pincers folded, mothers — improbably, devotedly — guarding white eggs in a place with no soil to guard them in, turning them, cleaning them, eating the ones that fail so the failure will feed the rest. Carpet beetle larvae work in slow, banded battalions through whatever fiber history has dropped: the abandoned wool glove that is to them a forest floor and a winter larder at once, felt hatched into tunnels, the felted lining of the old toolbox where beetle larvae of another, larger kind are warm in the rust, chewing the wooden handle down to lace.

And then, authority. The centipedes do not belong to the herd or the sheet or the roads. They patrol. Red-headed, hand-long and longer, each segment armored and precise, they move with a ripple that is entirely unlike the crickets' ripple — theirs is indecision made visible; this is decision, arriving many-legged. Where a centipede passes the crickets open a lane without being touched. The cockroaches flatten thinner. Even the spiders draw in. A centipede will take a cricket mid-stridulation, a roach mid-groom, a spider if it can reach it before the trembling becomes a blur too fast to bite. It coils by day in the overturned paint can, a sleeping king in a tin fortress, and by night it makes its rounds of the same walls, the same seams, eating the hollow back into balance. Its venom is the local law against overpopulation.

Opposing it, in the far corner under the firebricks, is the shrew. You do not, at first, understand that the twitching is an animal. A pointed snout, beaded with whiskers, testing, testing, withdrawing. A blur too fast for the eye to parse as fur. A squeak pitched so high it seems to come from inside your own teeth. The shrew lives at a speed the insects can only approximate. Its heart is a drum it can never stop hearing. It must eat its own weight, then again, then again, or die in its sleep, so its sleep is a series of famished naps between murders. Beetles vanish into it. Crickets vanish into it. Once, horribly, a young centipede misjudged the threshold of the bricks and there was a shrieking, thrashing knot for three seconds and then silence and the shrew grooming blood from its whiskers with insane rapidity. It is the smallest mammal in the building and it rules by metabolism.

The mice are larger, and therefore more cautious, and therefore longer-lived. They have worn actual highways, pale polished runways in the dust along the north wall, between the split conduit where they nest in shredded cable insulation and the lunch pail that still, after all these years, yields a seam of rancid fat when gnawed at the right angle. They press their bellies to the warm junction boxes in the cold months upstairs, they lick condensation that never quite forms here by traveling — impossibly, through gaps you would swear were sealed — up pipes to levels where water still sweats, and they bring that water back down inside themselves, to be stolen in turn. They are reservoirs on legs. They are also historians: their gnawing has opened the paint can to the earwigs, their droppings feed the larvae, their dead — an old mouse curled in the conduit, mummified dry as a leaf — are being unmade so slowly and so thoroughly that it will nourish the hollow for a season.

Above them all, wedged where a dead pipe enters the ceiling, roost the giants. One feral pigeon, sometimes a pair, blue-grey gone sooty, heads tucked into shoulders in the posture of gargoyles at rest. They do not belong to the floor's economy except as gods belong to it: distantly, by droppings and by accident. Their white excrement dries to a mineral crust that the larvae mine grain by grain, extracting some last ghost of the outside world — seed, grit, green stuff eaten over a parking lot half a mile away. When a wrench drops upstairs the pigeon bursts awake, wings clapping stone, and its panic becomes a catastrophe and a feast below: dust vortex, feathers, a rain of displaced crickets falling straight through the spider sheet, spiders trembling into frenzy, roaches surging for cover that is also food. Then the pigeon resettles, sighs through its nostrils, tucks again. It dreams, presumably, of ledges. It has chosen this ledge because nothing with hands ever reaches for it here.

New citizens arrive the way weather arrives. A moth, fat from some other summer, blunders down the vent shaft toward the caged bulb and spends its last night beating itself to scales, bringing in its body all the water and fat of a life lived among plants none of the natives will ever see. Lost flies ride the air handler in. A worker's crust falls through a grate. They are manna not because they are large but because they are foreign — their moisture has not been through the hollow ten times already. Everything turns toward them. Everything is already turning.

For that is how the inhabitants drink: each other, in sequence. The soft go into the armored, the armored into the fast, the fast into the furred, and all of them, without exception, back into the larvae and the roaches and the crickets who eat the dead before the dead have finished dying. A molt becomes a meal becomes a spider becomes a husk becomes a nursery becomes a centipede. Light is ordinary, stone is smooth to finality, and there is no green thing to start the chain, so the chain has learned to be a circle.

They court in the still air when the handler stops, antennae everywhere lifted into the close, animal humidity of their own massed breathing. They freeze as one animal when the forklift passes. They gorge in the fatal glory when a new bulb is screwed in and burns too bright for an hour. They go on, under your palm, under the factory, thousands of feet negotiating thousands of other feet, chewing audibly in the pauses between machines — a population so dense, so complete, and so cheerfully unsentimental that closing the hatch does not feel like sealing a pit.

It feels like closing a lid on something that never needed opening.
