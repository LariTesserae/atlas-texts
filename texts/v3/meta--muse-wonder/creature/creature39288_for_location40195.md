At first you don't see them at all.

The city overwhelms its people the way it was meant to. You see the avenues, the numbered market stalls, the four-story houses, the theatre swept clean for an audience that does not exist, and your eye keeps expecting crowds to account for it all. No crowds come. Only, now and then, a figure crosses very far away — too small for that width of street — carrying a mallet, a basket, a child on a pack-frame, and disappearing into a doorway that leads, more often than not, to another empty room.

Then, slowly, the figures stop looking like interruptions and start looking like the point.

There are forty of them, perhaps fifty in summer, when a few climb up from the villages below to help with cutting and carrying and go down again before the cold hardens. Those who stay the year round live close together, whatever their grand street addresses say. They say them, too. A girl of nine will tell you with complete seriousness that she lives on the Street of Rain, although the Street of Rain is a timber scaffold, a chalk line, and three walls rising out of raw tuff. Her family sleeps, like most families, down near the lower gate, in the oldest finished houses, where three kitchens can share their smoke and the sound of the channel, though still loud, has learned some manners.

They are a people built by altitude and carrying.

Nothing arrives in Brinnel except on a back. Grain, salt, copper, timber, cloth, lamp oil, a new bell rope, a marriage chest — all of it comes up the stair-path piece by piece, and you can read that fact in their bodies by the second day. Even the children walk with a particular economy, leaning slightly into a weight that isn't there. The women and men alike have deep chests and slow, deliberate breath; they can work all morning in air that makes a newcomer sit down suddenly on a paving slab with his head ringing. Their shoulders are strong in a way that makes their hands, by contrast, look oddly fine. Those hands are always nicked — knuckles scarred white, nails split from stone dust, fingertips polished smooth by wet tuff — but they are precise. They have to be. A wall here is not built; it is revealed, cut away season by season, and a mistake made by a grandfather may not show itself until his grandson cuts the drainage runnel wrong and invites a spring into the pantry.

Their faces are burned and wind-chapped at once. Sun at noon, winter in every shadow. Most of them squint from years of pale stone glare, and wear broad woven hats and wool that smells faintly, always, of woodsmoke and damp. Their hands go in and out of the channel water all day — numb to the wrist in seconds, red and tingling afterward — so that by forty nearly everyone has the same stiff, careful way of flexing their fingers over a fire at night.

But what you notice most, after the strength, is the silence they move in.

Not theirs. The city's. The channel takes speech and tears it up as efficiently as it tears down the mountain. So the Brinnelians talk with their whole bodies. Their gesture language is not a substitute for words; among themselves it is often preferred to them — broad, unselfconscious, beautiful in the way work-signals become beautiful when children grow up inventing flourishes. *Stop. Look here. Water rising in the lower tank. Bring the long rope, not that one. Your cut is drifting. Eat. Come home. I saw the swifts.* A conversation can cross the entire market square without a sound that rises above the roar: a mason on a scaffold answers a woman in a courtyard answers a boy on the bell-tower steps, all of them signing through drifting cloud that makes each figure appear and vanish like something semaphore-deep underwater.

The old ones sign more slowly. Some of them are functionally deaf, not from age alone but from sixty years of sleeping and waking inside that engineered thunder. They rest their palms on cistern walls to feel announcements. They watch lips less than hands. At evening tables, they are the ones who start the talking.

Because every night, when the sluice-keepers haul the great wooden gates part-closed and the main channel falls from a roar to a heavy, bearable rushing, Brinnel discovers its voices all at once.

It is almost shocking the first time: kitchens and courtyards filling with talk, not important talk necessarily, just *talk* — stories retold, prices argued, songs, long descriptions of a cut of stone or a dream of a condor, children shouting simply to hear themselves shout. People linger. They interrupt. They laugh too loud. Adolescents fall in love, noticeably, in that loosened hour. Marriages and quarrels alike seem to wait for it. A visitor accustomed to thinking of them as a grave, patient, monumental people may be startled by how funny they are after dark, and how unwilling anyone is to be the first to go to bed and let the water have the city back.

By day, patience reasserts itself, but it is not the patience of waiting for a thing to happen. It is the patience of people who have organized their whole moral life around work they will not finish.

Nobody in Brinnel owns a house in the way people below do. You inhabit the room your mother cut, you cut the room your daughter will inhabit, you spend your twenties roughing out a chamber you then leave empty and doorless for a year so the water can write its faint chalk-line confessions across the walls. If a spring chooses your future bedroom, you do not curse it; you become, perhaps, the keeper of a washing place, or the family with the famous pantry stream, cold and clear and inconvenient and secretly prized. Old Mereth, who at seventy-one is responsible for trimming doors as the bowl rises, keeps a notched stick for every doorway she tends and can tell you which houses are lifting fastest, which thresholds she has cut three times, which one she expects — smiling, without morbidity — her niece to cut after her.

Children are apprenticed accordingly. Not, at first, to masonry or weaving or the keeping of the sluices, though all those trades exist and matter. They are apprenticed to continuation. A boy learns to mix plaster by patching the great painted plan in the arcade: concentric streets, a second market, gardens on the rim, a completion date four centuries off by design. A girl carries water to the cloud-nets before she is tall enough to haul their ropes, learns which mosses can be eaten by the garden soil, which should be scraped from the library steps, which are left because her great-grandmother liked the color against the stone. At ten, most of them have already placed one course of fitted slabs that they will walk on as grandparents.

It makes them strangely companionable with absence. Empty rooms are not haunted in Brinnel; they are *expected*. A family will sweep the house across the street that no one has ever lived in as naturally as its own courtyard, lay a sprig of marigolds in a niche intended for a lamp in someone else's future, check the fern pit in the abandoned chamber where the spring won. They argue sometimes, fiercely, about the unborn — whether the Street of the Weavers should really be given to weavers, when there are currently two weavers total and both are over sixty; whether the second market is vanity; whether the plan itself may be changed. Then someone goes out and chalks the name board straighter anyway. The argument, like the rehearsal markets on the first clear day of each season, is part of maintaining a civic muscle they refuse to let atrophy just because history hasn't arrived yet.

So they ring the bell for twenty buyers. They open magistrates' stalls. They lay out beans and salt and cloth in beautiful, orderly rows and call prices to the water, and for an hour they practice being a city as one practices a language one hopes one's descendants will speak natively. A child stands on tiptoe to be a magistrate. The sluice-master, a widower named Toll who can judge the upper lake by the pitch of the third cistern, solemnly settles a pretend dispute about a pretend wall. Everyone understands it is pretend. No one treats it as foolish.

Their diet is narrow and their gardens are not. What little soil exists was carried up in baskets or made, season by season, from moss, ash, bean husks, kitchen waste, and stone dust composted in the sunken, sheltered courtyards. Broad beans, purple potatoes, bitter herbs, peas climbing the scaffolding of houses that will not be roofed for fifty years. They eat communally more often than not, because fuel carried up a mountain does not forgive private fires. There are no dogs to feed, no plow beasts, no cavalry in the future painted on the arcade wall. Only people, ropes, terraces, nets, and time. Perhaps because of that, guests are fed in a way that borders on the ceremonial. A stranger is a rare kind of plenty.

And they are attuned, as city people rarely are, to arrivals even rarer than strangers. When the swifts return to the bell tower, work pauses and the news crosses the bowl hand over hand. When the white moths thicken around the cistern lamps, children are called to see. When a condor passes — once in a few years, immense and unimpressed — the whole population spills out into avenues built for ten thousand and stands in scattered silence, fifty upturned faces, watching a shadow slide alone over roofs and stalls and scaffold and garden. It may be the only moment when Brinnel's scale feels right to them: something large enough, finally, to fill it.

They bury their dead small. They must; soil is labor here. But they carve their names deep into the gate wall, below the survey marks that no longer align, name after name in a hand that changes generation by generation, so that the road out is also the road through everyone who stayed. Below that, children have added, over the years, smaller carvings of unremarkable things. A bean. A mallet. A swift. A cup permanently filling.

By late afternoon the cloud usually returns, and the people draw in toward their few warm kitchens as their city disappears around them street by street. Somewhere out in the white, a chisel finds its rhythm again. Someone calls — actually calls, since the fog somehow softens the water for a while — that the nets are full. Hands rise in answer from doorways that may not exist tomorrow, answering out of houses half-cut, out of scaffolding, out of a future they tend like a garden.

They are not waiting passively for Brinnel to be finished. That is the misunderstanding a visitor takes down the mountain. They *are* Brinnel, happening at the speed rock allows: loud-voiced at night, fluent in silence by day, raising children for rooms they will never sleep in, in a city gradually rising under their feet, rehearsing — market by market, door-trimming by door-trimming, generation by generation — for the centuries when they themselves will have become the ancestors carved, just legibly, near the gate.
