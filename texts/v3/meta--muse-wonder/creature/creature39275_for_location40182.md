You would not see them first. On a survey they scarcely register — a few windbreaks, a sleeping platform lashed to a root-knee, smoke so thin the heat takes it sideways. From the open Floor a camp looks like something the vegetation has briefly tried and will reabsorb by next season.

You hear them last, which is also strange, because they are loud by necessity and silent by profession.

The plateau peoples are built, visibly, from the waist down first. A lifetime on the Floor gives them broad, splayed feet with soles so calloused and deeply patterned they look almost barked, grey-green at the heel from sap and ash and the fine dust of dried petals. They walk warm ground all day without hurry and without shoes, testing it constantly with the ball of the foot — not for pain, but for information. A hollow place sounds different under a step. A full place sounds different again. Old water-finders can still, in their seventies, stamp once beside a sealed well and tell you by the note that comes back up through their shinbones whether the Floor beneath is holding last month's rain or only dreaming about it.

Above the feet they are lean in the limbs and unexpectedly ample around the middle. Not fat, exactly. Stored. Everyone here stores — the Pillars in their trunks, the grazers in their humps and great ears, the low succulents in fists of flesh — and the people do it in the body and in the behavior at once. In the morning hours, when the mist is still in rivers between the Pillars and dew runs bright down every thorn, whole camps are busy with a concentrated, unembarrassed industry of gathering: leaf-cups emptied into skins, fiber cloths wrung out, children's hair combed wet and the combings licked, flat stones that were set out cold at night now lifted and drained mouth to mouth. Nobody wastes the white hours. By ten the world has dried and shut its mouth, and a person who has not drunk has chosen thirst until evening.

Their clothing is shed matter, by law and by preference: woven Floor-fiber, strips of fallen barkcloth beaten soft, grazer hide in narrow panels, ornaments of seed-head and polished bone and beetle wing that click when they move. Pockets are rare. What a person owns they tend to wear, carry on a cord, or know how to find again. A thorn needle, a twist of line, a fire-stick wrapped against sweat, a gourd. The one foreign thing you will sometimes see — a short blade of hammered metal gone green as a leaf, passed hand to hand down six generations, never resharpened on stone for fear of wasting it — is less a tool than a piece of ancestry that happens to cut.

They shout the way other people talk.

This is not temper. Shouting is their indoor voice, arrived at honestly: under the full roar of a Pillar in mating season, six feet is a distance and sixty is a country. Their public speech is hoarse, high-carrying, pitched deliberately over the frog register and under the canopy birds, with consonants thrown like stones. Children sound like small, furious adults by the age of four. Lovers bellow endearments across a camp with no sense of exposure; the roar gives everyone a kind of privacy. What would be intimate, in a quieter world, is not a lower voice. It is hands.

Because alongside the shouting there is the signing — and the signing is the real language, the one that has kept growing.

Among hunters, among intimates, among people working close together up in the Pillars where a sudden cry would startle everything for a hundred yards, they speak in two-handed sign of extraordinary speed and articulation. It uses fingers, wrists, elbows, the tilt of the head, the direction of the eyes. It has tenses made by where a sign is held in relation to the body: things near the sternum are present and felt, things at arm's length are witnessed, things described behind the shoulder are reported, ancestral, or doubted. It has a whole grammar for sound, necessarily. There are distinct signs for a call heard, a call felt through the Floor, a call expected from a regular animal at its regular hour, and a call that has failed to arrive.

Children learn it before they learn to shout properly, which means a plateau childhood looks, to a lowlander, oddly grave: toddlers perched on a root-knee conducting fluent, silent conversations with their grandmothers while, directly above them, several thousand frogs are doing their best to dismantle the sky.

Those grandmothers are hearing the world in layers as it leaves them. The loss is so regular it has become a social calendar. First, in middle age, the frog register thins — the struck-bell notes in the bromeliad cups — and a person is judged ready for certain kinds of work in the open, where that distraction matters less. Then the middle birds go, then the insects, until at last the very old retain almost only the lowest band: the deep hum of a Pillar through its resonant chambers, the infrasonic tread of herds coming through the mat, thunder deciding to exist. They are not treated as deaf. They are treated as finally filtered. An elder will sit with her back against a buttress fin for an afternoon, eyes open, apparently doing nothing, and then sign to the camp, without emphasis: *move east tomorrow. This one is finishing.* And because she is hearing the tree rather than its tenants, she is usually right. The flowering wave, when it turns, turns first in the wood.

Their year is the wave's year. It crests in the east after the dry-bright season, when the first crowns flush crimson at thirty miles and the grazers all turn as one animal, and then it walks west at the pace a herd can walk, week by week, Pillar by Pillar, orchard by orchard. The people move inside that pace like a knot inside a current. Ahead of the herds they harvest — canopy fruit taken from limb-gardens by climbers who have lived half their lives at altitude and move through the suspended thickets with a monkey's unthinking route-memory; erupting Floor-blooms gathered in the one hour they are protein-rich and open; succulent pith sliced and set to dry. Behind the herds they hunt, with sound as much as weapon: a herd announces itself, its predators announce themselves announcing it, and a good hunter is mainly a person who can subtract every call in a district and notice which absence is shaped like a cat.

Between Pillars, though, they do what the monkey troops do. They cross at dawn, in single file, in silence.

This is the most striking sight the plateau offers, if you are lucky enough to be up a root-knee and watching: forty people, perhaps, with skins and bundles and infants tied flat and silent against their backs, emerging out of the mist river at the foot of one green tower and setting off across miles of open Floor toward a tower that will not visibly grow for a day and a half. No singing. No shouted encouragement. The predators out there hunt by ear, and so for the length of the crossing the whole nation of them goes into its hands — a ripple of small signs passed up and down the line, lookouts fore and aft half-upright, scanning. They look diminished by the space and entirely native to it at once, like a sentence crossing a blank page.

When they reach the next Pillar they get loud again all at once, almost giddy with permitted noise, and the new district has to be negotiated with the way a household might be negotiated with. Each Pillar is a jurisdiction: its waters in leaf-cups and hollow crotches, its fruit falls, its sleeping limbs, the dangerous pits in the Floor near its base, the stretches where the mat is thin-skinned and quick to bruise. Nothing about this is owned the way lowlanders mean it, but everything is *known*, by family and by prior arrangement, in a dense unwritten cartography of who mended this well-ring, whose great-aunt frescoed the inside of that fallen trunk, who has the right to auger here and the duty to leave the scar clean. The etiquette is older than any current language and admits no cleverness: take what is shed, given, dead-and-dry, or grown for taking. Do not wound the Floor. Do not axe a Pillar. The one metal blade in a camp will be used on meat and cord and dead heartwood a thousand times before anyone jokes about living wood, and nobody jokes about it twice.

Even their dead observe it. A body is not cut into the ground. It is carried, usually at dusk when the day chorus is handing over to the night one, to one of the Floor's great stomatal pits, and laid at the lip on a litter of woven fiber with a skin of water and a handful of seed-heads, while the camp signs rather than shouts the relevant parts of a life. By morning the pit will have done what it does. No one describes this as burial. In the sign language, the gesture for it is the same gesture used for returning a borrowed cup: flattened hand, offered, withdrawn empty.

They have stories, of course, told in the one reliably lowered register — at night, inside the hollow of a fallen Pillar, where beetle-tended fungus gardens burn green in the dark and a hundred feet of mineral heartwood filters the roar down to something like distant surf. In that filtered quiet the handprint frescoes layer wall over wall, generation over generation, red resin over pollen-paste, a child's hand set carefully inside the print of an ancestor so the fingers touch. And the stories themselves keep circling the same events: the foreign axemen, and the mile-wide black spiral near the southern rim where nothing has rooted since; the season a district went silent; the year the wave failed to turn and the herds stood in the mist making a sound nobody had a sign for yet. Their cosmology, if an outsider insists on the word, is mostly a theory of attention. The world is alive, but that is the least of it. The world is *listening back*, through root and resonant chamber and the soles of your feet, and can decide, locally and without malice, to stop.

Which is why, when the great cessation comes — in midday, at midnight, everything within ten miles holding its breath at once, birds resuming nowhere, frogs nowhere, the Pillar-hum cut off like a string — the people do not panic and do not fill it. Camp by camp, line by line, they go still in the middle of whatever their hands were doing. A climber freezes on bark. A child is gently signed into quiet against a breast. For a minute, two minutes, you can hear the sap ticking in the ground and the blood in your own ears, and on their faces there is something more specific than reverence. It is the expression of tenants in a house where the owner has just, audibly, shifted in his sleep.

Then one frog tests the air, and another, and the birds resume mid-phrase, and the people resume too — shouting, signing, laughing at full volume, wholly themselves again — as if nothing had happened, and as if nothing ever happens any other way.
