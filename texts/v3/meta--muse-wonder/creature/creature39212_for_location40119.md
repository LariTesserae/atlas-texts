If you stay long enough, you stop thinking of it as a cave with animals in it.

It is an animal system with a cave around it. The stone is only the container — cold at eleven degrees, almost lightless, almost waterless — and everything that truly lives here lives either on the ceiling, in the dung under the ceiling, or on what falls from the ceiling.

The ceiling belongs to the bats.

Not to one kind. A dozen kinds share the unseen roof of the main vault, and they sort themselves without ever being seen whole. The smallest insect-eaters pack highest and deepest in the black, flank to flank, belly to belly, in mats so dense they stop looking like separate bodies and become a living surface that ripples when it dreams. Their wings fold tight as umbrellas; their heads tuck down; a constant fine rain sifts from them — urine, shed hair, discarded insect wings, mites, dead young, dung. The larger bats hang looser. Fruit bats, with dog faces and wings a forearm long, keep nearer the sinkholes where a memory of light reaches, in companies that quarrel and resettle all day. Between and among them are solitary hangers, pregnant females apart from the press, old males scarred at the ear, pups clinging blind to their mothers' chests with milk-teeth locked in fur.

No hour empties them. That is the first strangeness to get into the blood. Outside, bats would be a night event. Here they are weather without a forecast. Some are always leaving — sliding off the rock, dropping ten metres to catch air, streaming toward the skylights in a column thick enough to dim noon. Some are always returning, bellies tight with moths taken over the cold grassland, with midges from rivers you will never see from the cave mouth, with fruit pulp from valleys a whole night's flight away. They bring the entire country above ground back inside them, and let it fall. While one shift feeds, another sleeps, another gives birth in the dark to a single pup that is licked clean in the roar and learns, from its first breath, that the world is warm bodies on every side, ammonia in the nostrils, and sound that never stops.

And it never does. Under the roost you do not hear individual bats so much as layers: the needling pressure of echolocation at the top of hearing, the rolling squeaks and squabbles that travel across the roof in waves, the soft percussion of ten thousand wings shuffling for purchase, which becomes — when something startles a mat and they lift together — a waterfall going upward. The swiftlets stitch through it. They nest on ledges in the grey country between sinkholes, little soot-coloured birds who live half their lives on the wing in total darkness, navigating by dry, clicking tongues, leaving at dawn and returning at dusk to a cave that has no dawn or dusk except the one they carry in their bodies.

Everything else arranges itself underneath, in order of distance from the fall.

On the floor directly below the mats, there is no longer any stone to speak of. There is guano, built hill upon hill over centuries, crusted pale and dry on top, soft and hot within from its own rotting — the only ground in the cave that is warm to touch. It seethes. Dig a hand's depth and the spadeful comes apart into life in every direction: dung beetles rolling and burying, cockroaches flat and amber and unafraid of light because they have never needed to fear it, earwigs, millipedes slow as trains, maggots in densities that make the surface faintly move, mites too small to count making the dust itself alive. Flies breed here in clouds so thick above a fresh fall of dead pups that the air darkens. This is not filth to its inhabitants. It is soil, forest, and sea at once — the whole pasture of the dark world, renewed every night from the sky of the cave.

At the edges of the guano hills, where the crust thins over limestone rubble, the hunters take up their stations. Spiders hang in irregular webs strung between blocks, catching not flies from any meadow but flies fattened on dung and fallen moth-wings from bat mouths. Blind crickets, long-antennaed, pale as peeled roots, tick and probe across the stone, feeding on dung and fungal thread and each other. Centipedes, banded and swift, hunt them through the cracks; their venom works as well in the permanent night as anywhere. Scorpions of a sort, small and colourless, wait under flakes of stone. Everything here is a little paler than its surface cousin, a little longer in the leg and feeler, a little less invested in eyes. Eyes are expensive. Vibration is cheap, and smell is cheaper, and both travel well in still, cold air.

The rats understand this best of the vertebrates. They are not cave-pale — they commute too much for that, sleek, dark, full-eyed, fearless — but they have learned the cave's timetable to the minute. They run established paths between rubble and dung hill, timing their forays to the falls: a pup dropped still warm, a bat knocked stunned in a mid-air collision, a swiftlet fledged too early. They snatch and are gone before the next thing lands. And commuting after the rats, belly-scales whispering over guano crust and sharp new limestone alike, come the snakes. They too take fallen bats, sometimes still fluttering, and they take frogs at the pools, and young rats in the rubble. They are the ceiling's energy made long and patient. Nothing with warm blood stays down here permanently except the bats themselves; the rats and snakes are borderers, citizens of both worlds, thicker for the cave's subsidy.

The pools have their own, smaller citizenry, and it is the strangest because it looks the least like life at all. In water lens-clear and cold enough to ache the hand, rimmed with white calcite crust, live shrimp that are translucent as glass, their guts a dark thread visible through them, their eyes reduced to unpigmented dots that register nothing. They turn toward vibration, grazing bacterial film and whatever organic dust settles from above. With them, sometimes, are fish no longer than a finger, colourless, their scales catching torchlight like wet paper, drifting in the shallows and snapping at shrimp and drowned crickets. Frogs call from the pool edges in the twilight zones — real frogs, green-backed under the skylights where insects blunder down the light cones, paler frogs deeper in — and lay their strings of eggs in water that will neither warm nor dry nor flood, in a season that never changes.

Beyond the reach of dung and pool, life thins to threads. On the bodies of the dead, wherever a bat has fallen beyond the rats' patrol and been left, a white fungal fuzz spreads, digesting what beetles missed. It needs no light. It is the cave's only plant-like growth in the true dark, and even it is a kind of animal in its habits, eating flesh and dung rather than sun. Under the sinkholes alone does the ordinary green world get a foothold: ferns in the cracks, moss in velvet cushions, sour grass, algae filming the wet stone a little way out from each light cone — and once, memorably, a fig sapling that sent root-ropes down the sinkhole wall and turned every leaf one way, up its column of sun, like a crowd all looking at the same window. A few paces out, the green gives up. No seed sprouts in the black vaults. Nothing photosynthetic can pay its way there.

And then there are the people, who are not inhabitants at all, and know it.

They come down the smallest sinkhole on twisted-fibre ropes and ladders they remove when they leave, carrying stone knives, bone scrapers, baskets, gourds of water, smoking torches of resinous wood and fat. They bind cloth over mouth and nose against the falling dust, keep their mouths shut under the roost, shout into each other's ears, and work fast: gathering fallen young bats before the rats reach them, digging dung with wooden spades for the fields above, now and then taking a snake or a sack of fat crickets. They drink only what they brought, or what drips clean from a stalactite point — never from the white-rimmed pools. They walk stone where they can, knowing the guano hills will take a leg to the knee and fill a foot-covering with biting things. They build nothing. No shelter, no shrine, no path, because the cave would swallow ash and cairn alike under the next night's fall and they will not be there to maintain it. A week after a visit, the only trace is a softened place in the dung crust, already closing.

They are, in the cave's terms, just another edge-hunter like the rat and the snake — larger, rarer, better with fire — come to skim the subsidy and leave before their torches fail. Above ground their camps are rings of stones the wind worries apart. Below, they have learned the laws without ever writing them: navigate by ammonia and chatter toward life, by clean wet-limestone smell and a pool's regular drip-note toward open floor; never trust silence, which means only that the loaded ceiling above you happens to be asleep.

That is the whole population, and it closes into a circle with no loose ends. The bats eat the plains. The cave eats the bats. Beetle and maggot and cricket eat the droppings; spider and centipede and rat eat them; snake eats the rat; fungus eats whatever is left unclaimed, and the pools' blind shrimp graze the dust of it all out of the water. Even the dust you breathe under the roost is powdered wing and dung and shed insect skin. A bat born in that dark hears the same roar its grandmother heard, at the same volume, in the same eleven-degree air, above hills of guano rising a fraction higher each year under a ceiling still sharp, still young, still not worn comfortable.

Nothing here waits for spring. Nothing migrates except the wings overhead, and they only ever come back. Whoever lives here lives in a loud, damp, crowded equilibrium that renews itself nightly from elsewhere — and continues, without building up or running down, long after the brief, cloth-masked figures have climbed back toward the sound of wind in grass, with their ears still ringing.
