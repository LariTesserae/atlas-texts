There is one animal in the pocket. It is also the reason you would swear there are none.

You have probably looked at it. In a place where every trunk carries moss to the last twig and every fallen branch is caught a metre above the ground and slowly turned green, another long shape suspended in the weave does not separate itself from the rest. It hangs where the bamboo grass thins between three close trunks, belly up under a branch or breast down along one, and waits for the weather to move it.

It is about the length of a child, though much heavier than a child looks, and built low in every direction. Its back is bowed. Its head is set so deep between its shoulders that from above there may seem to be no head at all, only a continuation of the back, a darker mound where the eyes are. Its limbs are disproportionately long and end not so much in hands and feet as in arrangements for holding: four broad palms, the fingers long, webbed with loose skin at the base, each tipped with a blunt, black claw that closes around bark the way a crampon closes on ice. Standing, which it almost never does, it would reach your waist. It has no reason to stand. It progresses through the pocket on all fours, upside down as often as right side up, always with at least three holds taken before the fourth lets go.

Its skin is the colour the light makes of everything else there — black-green, grey in the flat glare when the cloud tears, almost luminous where the water stands on it. It is leathery and deeply creased, plated at the throat and belly with smaller, overlapping scales of skin no larger than the leathery leaves on the shrubs. But you would have to touch it, or come upon it in the three seconds after a squall when the wind has stripped it, to be sure any of that surface belonged to it.

For most of its body is not, in a simple sense, its own.

Moss grows on it as it grows on the trees. It coats the back, the forearms, the long outer surfaces of the thighs. It hangs in beards from its elbows and from the ridge of its spine. Liverworts plate its underside in pale, overlapping discs. A fern has established itself behind one shoulder, two fronds, no more, that unfurl a fraction in the warmer part of the year and close again. In the fork where one thigh meets its hip an orchid sits in a mat of its shed skin and old moss, and holds, like the bromeliads in the tree-forks, its own small cup of still water against the general shaking of the place. The creature carries perhaps three times its flesh weight in living growth. The growth is not camouflage, or not only camouflage. It is insulation in a cold that works slowly into living tissue and never quite leaves. It is a means of drinking. And it is weight — ballast, in a place where a body that cannot anchor itself usefully will be taken over the eastern rib and out onto the bare rock, where within an hour the wind would strip its coat and the coat, not the cold, is what it cannot survive losing.

Condensation forms on it as it forms on a leaf. Fog collects in the moss on its back until the moss reaches saturation, tips under its own loading, and releases the water in a slow thread that runs along a channel in the spine, is guided by the set of its head, and arrives at the corner of its mouth. It drinks by inclination, not by seeking water. After heavy rain, when the whole bowl ticks and drips at different pitches, it will move — ponderously, from hold to hold — to one of the black hollows in the rock, and lap from it before the hollow empties, and sometimes simply put its whole face under the thin temporary thread where it falls off a leaf-cluster, and swallow, and swallow, with its eyes closed. Its eyes are large under their hooded lids, a filmed grey-green, and mostly they are closed. The darkness at midday is not an inconvenience to it; it is the ordinary condition its sight is made for, and the brief directionless glare through a rent in the cloud flattens its vision as completely as it flattens yours, so that at those moments it presses all four palms flat and waits, blind, for the dark to be given back.

Its body temperature is close to the air's. Seven, eight degrees, rising a little after it has fed, falling at night to a state from which it takes the first hour of the morning's gusts to rouse it. Between gusts it is almost perfectly still — breath slow enough that the faint showing of it in the cold is lost among the drifting rags of mist caught between the trunks, heartbeat slower still. This is the other reason for the missing animal layer. It is not that the pocket is empty. It is that its single occupant moves on a timescale you do not stay still long enough to notice, and moves *with* the only other motion there. When the gust comes over the lip like water over a weir and every branch in the bowl thrashes and throws off its stored water in a hissing sheet, it lets go with two of its four holds at once and allows itself to be swung, sometimes a full metre, sometimes between one tree and the next, moss streaming, fern flat against its shoulder. In that general violence one more moving thing is invisible. In the sudden complete calm that follows, it has already taken its new holds and frozen. You arrive at the memory that something at the edge of your sight was in a different place a moment ago, and there is nothing to attach the memory to.

It does not come to the ground. The ground — root, rot, stone, and moss holding itself to stone — is not forbidden to it; its claws are made for the sharp young rock as much as for bark, and its palms are so thickly callused that the black blades that would cut your hand open do not mark it. But the fallen branches never reach the ground in the pocket, and neither does it, because everything it eats is in the canopy's slow traffic of death. It eats rot. It works its blunt muzzle into a caught branch that has lain a season in the weave, softened, gone green at the surface and black and fibrous inside, and it eats the wood as a paste, unhurriedly, hours at a bite, taking in the fungi that have taken the wood in first. It eats the pallid, wind-borne flowers where they lodge in its own moss, and it licks, with a long and very rough tongue, the mineral dust that sifts down through the canopy on still mornings — pale dust, grey-red, with a taste of iron to it, that has been shaken grain by grain out of the swinging buckets overhead in the gusts. Iron, and other things the pocket's soil-less floor cannot provide, arrive by cable, on schedule, whether it needs them that day or not. It keeps to the eastern rib in the hour after a prolonged storm, when the tramway has been running hard through the cloud, and licks the rust-scaled leg of the pylon where the dust has drifted and stuck. Standing close, it puts its cheek against the buried cable's line and rests, and if the wet-weather ticking of the current is something you feel in your teeth, for it the sensation is closer to a voice it has arranged its whole hearing around.

For its life is timed, not by light — there is not enough light to time anything by — but by the cable. Every half minute or so: the rising hum, the rattle as the hanger takes the strain, the bucket swinging out of the fog with the ore shifting and grinding inside it. It knows the interval the way you know your own pulse; it can be roused out of its cold morning torpor by a delay of a few seconds in the hum, head lifting, filmed eyes opening on nothing. It never crosses under the cable line except in the silence between buckets, moving then with a deliberation that is almost haste, three holds, then four, then three, and it is through and frozen before the next hum rises in the wire. Whether it learned this from a near thing in its youth, or whether the knowledge arrived with it in the world, there is no way of knowing, but the scar that runs under the moss from its left shoulder to the middle of its back is long, and straight, and the moss has never grown as thickly along it as elsewhere.

It makes almost no sound of its own. Once or twice in a season, usually in the worst weather, when the wind in the notch between the rock ribs rises close to a scream and the buckets come through at their steadiest, it answers — not the wind, but the cable, a low electrical ticking at the back of its throat, felt as much as heard, that sets the fine bones of the skull humming in sympathy. There is no one to answer it. It is not a call. It may be nothing more than the overflow of a nervous system tuned for decades to a single frequency. It has the place and the pylon in the same part of its mind, if that distinction would even hold: the one regular thing, arriving loaded out of the darkness, swaying over the treetops, vanishing into the darkness, has been the fixed point of its world for longer than the moss on its back has been growing.

Because it is old. The fern behind its shoulder is not the first fern. There are rings in the thickest of its moss-cushions where growth stopped for a bad year and resumed, stopped and resumed, farther back than the flagged, stunted trees immediately around it have been alive. And it is not the first of itself. Among the shapes caught in the weave a metre up — recognisably wood for a season, then only a long green shape — two or three are not wood, in the particular slump of their length, in the way the moss has taken them not as a surface but as a continuation. Its kind does not fall when it dies. It takes its final holds, in the fork of something or along a branch, and stiffens there, and is absorbed on the same schedule as everything else in the pocket, still recognisable as an animal for a season, then only as a long green shape. From one of those, in a wet year, in the cup of water an orchid holds at a dead hip, a new one may have begun — small, blind, already furred with the first spores of its coat, already holding on. Or it may come from elsewhere in the bowl by a means no observer has been present for. The pocket is eighty metres across. It can be crossed, on foot, in an hour. It has never been crossed slowly enough.

Outside the eastern rib, one step past the last flagged tree, the vegetation thins to a crust of lichen and then nothing. It has been to that edge. There is a place where the moss of the last trunk bears the compressed print of a broad palm, returned to over many years, springing back a little less each time. It goes there in calms and hangs facing outward, out of the dark saturated room of green and into the weather arriving at full force over wet black rock, rain coming up the mountain from below, cloud tearing past on its way to fall into the bowl behind it. What it can perceive out there, with eyes made for drowned light, is hard to say. It does not cross. Its coat would not survive the crossing, and its food would not follow it, and the cable hum fades quickly on that side of the rib, lost under the roar. It stays at the palm-print tree until the next gust arrives and shakes all the stored water off the leaves at once, and then it lets go with two holds, and is swung back in among the trunks, and is gone into the general green before the calm has quite settled — the pocket's one animal, wearing the pocket, eating its dead, drinking its fog, listening for the bucket that is due, and due, and due again out of the cloud.
