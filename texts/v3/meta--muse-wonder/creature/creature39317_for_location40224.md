They are not separate from the animals, here. That is the first thing you have to understand, because from below — from where the cable comes up out of the cloud — you would expect a crew. Engineers. A garrison of specialists enduring the altitude for a wage.

Inside the roof it is more like a roost that happens to include people.

The people are the largest animals in the fissure, and not always the most numerous. They live the way the swifts and the choughs do: in the warm junctions, in tiers, close together, taking advantage of every vertical inch that isn't needed for work. Their sleeping cages climb the east wall three and four high, curtained with patched blankets, and they sway almost imperceptibly when the roof is breathing. They learn to sleep in that sway. They learn to sleep in shifts, in lamplight, with the forge breathing orange through the grate below them and the great spooling drum turning somewhere in the dark, winding and unwinding the line that keeps them attached to the world.

Newcomers arrive pale and loud on the gondola and are immediately, gently, corrected.

Don't shout. Don't waste it. The air inside has been thickened only just enough to live on; it will not carry profligacy. Speech here costs breath and water, and so it has become short, low, and physical. People speak close, foreheads nearly touching, a hand sometimes resting on a forearm to keep the other person there for the whole sentence. It looks intimate from a distance. In a way it is. There is no private conversation that does not require proximity, and no order given across a room. You walk to someone. You clip yourself to the rail beside them. You say what is needed on the exhale.

Their language, over time, has thinned to match. Fewer filler words. A great deal said with a tap on a tether, a glove held up against the light, a glance at the birds.

Because they watch the birds the way other towns watch the weather.

A person who has lived here a season knows the chalk nest-map the chief engineer keeps as well as the wiring diagram. Knows which truss the yellow-billed choughs favor, which vent cowl not to clear, which crane is idle until the fledglings leave the sheave housing. A person who has lived here a year stops ducking when a swift stitches past his face in the Yard, wing ticking the air where his head was a second before. He knows where the sparrows — fat, dust-brown, generations removed from any tree — wait under the mess benches for crumbs from the food bricks. He steps around the mice in the duct access ways. They are solemn, unafraid, well-fed on stores and warmth, and they look at him with the calm proprietary air of older residents.

In some sense they *are* older residents. The swifts nested in this cliff before it was a city. The choughs moved into the roof trusses the winter the roof was finished, finding steel warmer than stone. The pigeons came up as messengers and stayed as citizens. Spiders hold every warm corner. Silverfish run the seams of the stores. Moths make a slow, thriving weather around the amber lamps, and the swifts harvest them at lamp-out. Nobody nets a bird. Nobody calls them pests. Where the birds won't fly, the fans have failed in some pocket of the air, and people do not go in to breathe there until it's cleared. The ventilation is not trusted until it is visible.

The people have become visible in the same way — adapted, in clothing and habit, to be read at a glance in the permanent evening.

Everyone wears wool under everything, even in the forge pit, because the cold is not a weather outside but a property of the place itself. Breath shows while they work. Fingers go numb on bare metal, so tools are wrapped in tape and cloth, benches are edged in felt, motor housings are lagged not only for heat but for touch. Their gloves have the fingertips cut away for the fine work — rigging, splicing cable thick as a wrist, setting the teeth of ice-engines, mending the roof membrane — and so a long-term resident can be known by his hands: oil worked permanently into the knuckles, nails short and split, fingertips callused and faintly grey from pumice and hot dust, astonishingly dexterous and always a little cold.

They wash in dust. That changes a person. Skin gets dry and fine-grained, polished at cheekbone and brow where masks seal for outside work. Hair is kept short or bound flat under wool caps, never loose near a drum or a catwalk edge, and it smells of ammonia vapor, lamp oil, forge heat, and other people. There is no scent of soap or green things here. There is no green thing to remember it by.

They move muffled. Felt soles on ladders. Felt padding on the conveyors. Shoulders brushing at shift change on catwalks barely wide enough for one, and yet no jostling — a hand at an elbow, a pause, a body turning sideways in its harness. Everything is tethered, including them. Even indoors, even crossing a floor they've crossed ten thousand times, the clip goes on when the roof sucks in hard, because a gust can swing a lamp string, swing a crate on the overhead track, swing *you*, and anything dropped goes down through the grates to the sorting pit and anything dropped outside goes down four thousand meters and does not stop being gone. Children are not really raised here, but the habit is childlike in its absoluteness: check the tether, check your neighbor's tether, sign for your water by weight.

Signed, weighed, sipped — never poured. Their throats are always a little sore. Long residents develop a particular economy of swallowing, of wetting the mouth before a necessary longer sentence. The rime-crews, who go out before dawn on lines to scrape last night's white fur from the windward rock before the wind can strip it, are accorded a quiet precedence at the flasks. They are the ones who have seen the sun come up over a visibly curved horizon and a sea of cloud below, squinting hard after the dim interior, eyes watering in the thin star-dry air. They come back in through the Lips with frost in their eyebrows and the insulated boxes heavy on their backs, and for a moment at the airlocks they are simply stared at, because they have been *outside*, where the ibex sometimes stand at night licking spilled salt and looking in at the lamplight with their long, patient faces.

Everyone here has a work that serves the line. Forge-hands down in the pit, casting anchor bolts and pitons and rib sections, timing their movements to the soft, furious *whump* of oil quenching. Spoolers tending the cathedral drums. Rig-runners and roof-menders who know the membrane by sound, who can tell from a creak in a rib whether a panel wants patching with aluminum or just re-tensioning. Weighers, sorters, cooks who reconstitute nothing — there is no water to reconstitute with — and break the bricks that came up the cable into portions that can be eaten slowly. Electricians moving through a city that hums more through rock than air, because there is no combustion here; the thin air will not spare its oxygen for fire, except the forge's managed hunger.

They are not hardy in the romantic way. Altitude takes them in small, accumulating debits: headaches that settle behind the eyes, sleep broken by the body's alarm at thin air, noses that bleed when a gate cracks and the cold knifes in, a thought that arrives a second later than it should. They work in bursts because of it — as the gondolas dictate, as the gusts dictate — and rest without apology in the swaying tiers when the gates seal and the fans labor through the walls and the whole city draws inward to mend and sort and re-rig.

And some of them stay. A rotation becomes a year. A year becomes the refusal of the descent, the too-thick air below, the blinding, profligate water of the lowlands. Like the sparrows, there are people here who have not seen a tree in so long that the idea has become theoretical. Their world is two hundred meters long and thirty across: a roofed mouth in black stone, lit amber, crowded shoulder to shoulder, alive at every level.

If you stood among them at lamp-change, you would see it all move together: a lamp string swinging in its slow arc, a man on a ladder leaning instinctively with it, clipped at the waist; a chough shifting its red feet on the warm truss above him, unconcerned; sparrows bursting from under a conveyor as a crate passes; a mouse withdrawing, unhurried, into shadow; the forge sighing orange through the floor; and every human voice, when it came, low and brief and close, as if the whole dense, tethered, waterless city were speaking — as it lives — into each other's ears.
