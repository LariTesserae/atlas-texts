At first you would say nothing lives here for long.

The hare is gone before it is a hare — a flattening of dry grass, ears laid back so hard against its skull it seems earless. The birds do not fly so much as surrender and recover, thrown sideways over their own nests. The fat bees only work the inch of still air behind a boulder. Even the one great scavenger, broad as a door, is only ever visiting the wind. It hangs a thousand feet up without moving and then is not there.

The shepherds, you are told down below, only come up seasonally. That is true, and it is not true. 

Some families never quite go down again.

You could walk half a day between huts and not know you had passed one. You could be standing on a roof. Their houses are not built *on* the plateau, they are excused from it — a round wall of smooth, egg-shaped stones sunk three feet into peat, another two feet above it, a dome of sod and felt and turf held down by a net of braided grass rope and boulders too heavy for a man to lift alone. From any distance it is another hump of old, eroded ground, leaking a ribbon of smoke that the west wind snatches flat the instant it leaves the vent.

The people who live in them have taken the same shape.

They are short, not in the way mountain peoples are often short — compacted — but *lowered*. Wide through the hips and shoulders, short-legged, with a permanent bend that is not a stoop of age but a way of standing learned in childhood. When they walk into the wind they lean at an angle that would make a lowlander fall. When they walk with it they lean back against it, trusting it, letting it hold them up for a step, then catching themselves. Even indoors, in the sudden muffling quiet of felt and stone, they do not quite straighten. Their spines remember.

Their children learn the tether before they learn to walk properly. A light cord of sheep-wool and grass, one end at the child's waist, the other at a mother's wrist or a stone weight by the door. Not cruelty — affection made practical. A toddler can be gusted off his feet on a clear day. Older children are tied to each other in the bad passes between basins, three or four in a giggling, stumbling line, hats knotted under their chins, hems of their felt coats sewn all around with flat river pebbles so the cloth clinks softly when they move. They sound a little like the cairns when the wind finds them.

Everyone up here clinks, rattles, is weighted. Nothing is unsecured, including people. Tools on wrist loops. Shears, knives, cups, loom shuttles — if it is not tied, it will be a valley away by evening, in pieces. A man's hat has stones in the brim. A woman's apron has a pocket of polished discs she will set, without thinking, on any paper, any light bowl, any infant left sleeping. Their hair is braided tight to the scalp and wound with cord and small bells — not for beauty, though it is beautiful, but so kin can hear each other move in the roar without shouting. In a gale the whole family becomes audible: bells, pebble hems, breath.

Breath is the other architecture. At five thousand meters the air is a rumor, and they have chests for it — deep, barrelled, slow. They do not pant when they climb the soft loaves of hills; they time their steps to long inhalations that seem to go on too long, and speak, when they must speak, on the out-breath, close. Forehead to forehead. Mouth to ear, hand cupped not against the wind but around the other person's jaw, so vibration carries through bone. At any distance conversation fails, and so they have given it up in favor of something richer: a sign language made with whole arms, forearms flashing pale felt against the grass, readable across a basin. A flap, a circle, a sudden crouch means sheep scattered, means squall in ten minutes, means the lake-basin has dried to salt and the flock must not be taken there. At night, or when cloud drags its belly across the plateau and the world narrows to white, they drum. Not into the air. Into the ground. A low thump through peat and stone that you feel in your molars and in the soles of your feet, from a hut you will not reach until tomorrow.

Their faces are made by sun and grit. The light up here falls straight and honest through thin air and burns even while the wind freezes you, so skin goes dark mahogany and thick as leather across cheekbones, nose, the backs of hands, and stays unexpectedly pale where chin-straps and headcloths sit. Eyes are narrowed not from suspicion but from a lifetime of squinting against blown sand — deep lines radiate, eyelashes grow thick and pale with dust, and many of the older ones have a milky film at the outer corners that makes their gaze seem distant even when they are looking right at you. They blink slowly. They can spot a single sheep — shaggy, low-bellied, the color of wet stone — at a distance a visitor would swear was empty grass. They spot weather earlier, reading it in the singing stones.

For they listen to the plateau the way lowlanders listen to a river. Certain boulders, hollowed by ten thousand years of grit-wind into flutes and bowls, hold a note. Each one different. On a strong day a field of them makes a broken chord you feel in your teeth. The hut families know their local stones by pitch the way you know voices. A rising hum means the wind is veering and will drop into the hollow after dark. A stone that stops singing has clogged with wet moss; a storm is making the air too heavy. Children are sent out at dawn — weighted, tethered to a line — to press an ear to the cold rock and run back and sign what they heard.

What they eat is what can keep low and keep still: hard sheep that graze bent like everyone else, their wool dragging near the ground, matted with seeds sorted by dust devils. Cheese salted and wind-dried on stone slabs under nets. Peat-root bread baked in dung fires that burn hot and quick and smell sweetly acrid. Tiny bitter herbs that flower a centimeter above the soil, gathered by the fistful after a squall when the wind has combed them upright again. Water squeezed from moss. In the morning they harvest dew off felt, off grass, off metal left purposefully out overnight — wringing cloths into wooden bowls while the racing cloud still drags through the tussocks. They waste nothing that the wind has done for them. Where the pale dust devils wander the basins, depositing what they pick up in inexplicable sorted drifts — seed here, spray-dried insects there, shining beetle wings in a glittering line — old women follow in the afternoon calm and collect, unhurried, as if shopping in a market only they can see.

They are not many. A hut holds an extended family — grandparents whose knuckles have swollen into knots as smooth as the loose stones outside, parents, three children, perhaps an aunt who never married because her hut-partner was lost, decades ago, not to a fall but to a gust on an exposed ridge that took him clean off the path and set him down, broken-legged, in the next valley, where he could hear his own flock bells for two days and could not crawl upwind to them. That story is told forehead-to-forehead, without embellishment. The wind is not a god to them, and not an enemy. It is a condition, like having a roof.

So they do not curse it, and they do not stand upright to admire the view. Standing upright is for cairns. The dead are helped to do it. When someone dies, the family carries the body — it is astonishing how light the old ones are, hollow-boned with age and altitude — to the field of smooth stones and builds them up inside a man-high stack, each stone chosen round and egglike, fitted without mortar so the perpetual west wind can find the gaps and hum through. A good cairn sings in the dead person's approximate pitch. Families passing weeks later will pause, lean, listen, and know who is still sounding, who has settled silent as the stones shifted.

To a visitor, sweating hot-faced and cold-eared, shouting himself hoarse and losing half of every sentence, they can seem slow, almost indifferent. They wave you in with a whole-arm signal, duck you through a waist-high door into heat and peat-smoke and sudden quiet, set a stone on your cup so it does not skitter, and feed you without asking your errand. They will touch foreheads to hear you. They will answer briefly, economically, hands still busy — shearing, braiding rope, mending a felt panel with sinew — because up here stillness is not rest, it is just being briefly unblown. If you ask how they bear the noise, the oldest ones tend to look puzzled. They have a ringing silence in their heads even indoors that they were born inside of. Quiet, true lowland quiet in a sheltered valley on a windless night down below, frightens their children when they are taken down to trade. It makes their ears ache with expectation. It makes them feel unheld.

They fear very little up here except enclosure. Trees unsettle them — things that insist on standing straight and catching the wind until they break. Deep valleys feel like pits where the sky narrows to a strip. They prefer a world that is three-quarters sky, where everything living is small and far from the next living thing and has miles of grass and singing stone in between, because that distance is not loneliness to them. It is room to be blown clean.

At dusk the squalls blow off the edge of the world, the lakes go mirror-thin and furious with spray at one end, the tussocks spring up again minutes after being flattened, shaking off rain, and hut by scattered hut the pebble hems settle, the bells go still, the felt roofs keep buffeting softly like something breathing in its sleep. Somewhere a cairn holds its note. Somewhere a child, tied lightly to the bedpost against no danger at all indoors except habit, sleeps through the roar that will shape his spine, his chest, his language, his whole low, anchored, efficient life on a mountaintop that finished rising so long ago it has forgotten sharpness — and has taught its people to forget it too.
