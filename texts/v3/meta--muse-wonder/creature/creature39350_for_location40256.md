The people of Ternlow look ordinary at first, and they work very hard at it.

That is, in fact, most of the work.

From a distance — insofar as anyone in Ternlow is ever at a distance — they are just northern people in a cold place: pale from the flat light, wrapped in too many layers, moving slowly between low white buildings with their shoulders up against a damp that never quite becomes rain. Up close you notice the layers are not all clothing. There is the wool layer, and the waxed layer, and the patched layer that used to belong to someone else and may again, and then a personal layer of habits worn as visibly as coats: counting under the breath, touching doorframes twice, saying your destination aloud before you set out for it, glancing — casually, constantly — at fence posts, corners, stair rails, the edges of other people, to help them continue.

A Ternlower is never not attending. It is bred into them now, four generations in. The children of the original engineers learned it at the table along with how to hold a hot cup with both hands. *Look at the room while you leave it. Thank the stair. If you meet someone you know who does not know you yet, be polite and remember what they say.* Attention is a civic utility here, like heat. Like heat, it is hoarded, shared, and never wasted on the assumption that things will simply stay.

So they live close. Absurdly close.

Nine hundred meters of reliable world has to hold a university, a prison, three religions, several industries, a market, archives, slums, avenues, cisterns, and everyone who has ever been born there — plus, on any given day, a margin of people who have not quite been born there yet, and a few who are mostly finished being alive but have persisted, like a demolished doorway, as a chill outline in a familiar chair. There is no room for solitude in the architectural sense. Households accrete rather than form: grandparents, tenants, cousins, an apprentice from the undercity, a younger version of the woman from two floors down who needed somewhere to sleep before she met anyone, all sharing a stack of rooms that may have an extra kitchen this winter and lose it, politely, by spring. Privacy is not a door. Privacy is a convention, elaborate and tender — averted eyes, a curtain of steam, the agreement not to listen to the version of an argument happening on the other side of a wall that has not been built in this decade.

They are warm-blooded in the way furnaces are warm-blooded: by constant, collective effort. The cold sits at one or two degrees and never leaves, and their bodies have made a long negotiation with it. Ternlowers run low and steady. Their hands are usually warmer than their faces. Their breath shows even indoors, in small, economical puffs, and they talk close enough for that breath to be felt, because voices don't carry and because closeness is how you know the conversation is still occurring in the same room. They touch constantly — a hand on a forearm while passing in an alley barely wide enough for two, a shoulder leaned briefly against another shoulder in the market queue, children sleeping in braided heaps in the cistern courts where the black algae breathes out its iron smell once a day and everyone inhales, at the same moment, without noticing they have timed it together. The inertial sinks in the floors eat their footsteps and give back heat, so there is a moral quality to restlessness here. Old people pace. Toddlers are encouraged. A still house is a cold house, and a cold house drifts.

You can tell a native by how they walk on ground that has not agreed to be ground yet. They place their whole foot down at once. They don't trust heels. They pause, almost imperceptibly, at thresholds, at the bottom of stairs, at street corners where the basalt goes pale and granular, giving the world a moment to decide to be crossed. And they count. Everyone counts. Stairs aloud, always, in a friendly, carrying murmur — *eleven, twelve, thirteen* — not for themselves but for the stair, which might otherwise get lonely and improvise. Shopkeepers count their shelves in the morning. Lovers count each other's buttons. The boundary watchers, who sit in heated shelters at the edge of the rendered perimeter with thermoses and knitting and survey remotes, count fence posts for twelve-hour shifts, and when the flowers appear in the boundary moss — small, wrong, intent — they count those too, out loud, in committee, while someone fetches the burner.

Their appearance has begun, gently, to keep up with their circumstances. It is not dramatic. No one glows. But four generations of being cooled, condensed on, and only loosely edited by causality have left marks. Their hair goes white early and stays thick, beading with moisture that doesn't freeze. Their eyes are adapted to that unchanging mid-morning grey: they can read the difference between a wall, its reflection running three seconds late, and the cooler outline of the wall that used to be there, and they know which one to lean on. Lichen grows on the north faces of towers and anchors the world chemically; a little of it also grows, in bone-white and rust-orange rosettes, in the seams of outer coats left hanging too long by a door, and very occasionally — on the very old, on loom-tenders who sleep down in the undercity — along a forearm or the back of a neck, where it is not scrubbed off but tended. Teased gently. Thanked.

The loom-tenders are a caste unto themselves, though Ternlow would never use the word. They spend their working lives in the silent pressure of the causal looms, persuading rooms to have already existed, and they come up blinking with frost in spiral patterns on their sleeves even in summer, complaining cheerfully about their molars. They can hear, they claim, when a new street is about to become inevitable: not a sound, a loosening behind the teeth. They drink everything scalding and sugared. They are disproportionately likely to be met, at the market, at ages that don't line up — a loom-tender of sixty buying plums beside herself at twenty-two, in training, neither of them making a fuss beyond a certain dry nod, as if at a colleague. This happens to everyone sometimes. It happens to them *professionally*.

Because time in Ternlow is not broken, exactly. It is unfinished, like everything else. It has not yet learned to keep to a single path through the city any more than water has.

So people keep notebooks. Waterproof, pocket-sized, written in soft pencil that survives damp. In them: what a not-yet-neighbor said over tea in the year she didn't know your name, and whether it turned out to be advice or warning. Which stair had fifteen steps on Thursday. The name of a child who appears in a family photograph held by parents who, at the time, were nine years old. They print calendars with two blank days a month and fill them in afterward, in different inks, without embarrassment. School begins with arithmetic and is mostly, for the first few years, an education in tense — how to conjugate a verb for something that is happening, has happened, is being rehearsed by the city, or has been retroactively true since last week. History at the university is a contact sport. The prison has had one famous inmate for decades, held at five different ages in five adjoining cells; the guards bring five trays, play cards with the middle one, and do not discuss which of them will commit the crime. The religions — all three founded by accident, one of them by a misdelivered sermon to a room that was sometimes a chapel — mostly amount, in practice, to this: *stay, watch, be kind to whoever arrives, write it down.*

Mirrors are covered with cloth between uses. Toiletries happen in brief, brave unveilings. Old people are shaved by other old people, because a reflection lagging by whole seconds will smile after you've stopped, and at a certain age you do not need to see your face rehearsing expressions without you. For the same reason, Ternlowers are unusually good at faces in person. They study each other while talking, with an frank, animal attentiveness that visitors find unnerving for the first week and then, after the first week, find themselves doing on the stairs, aloud: *thirteen, fourteen.*

There is not much fauna and what there is has joined the arrangement. The winter gnats keep to the vent-steam. The eyeless moths keep to the heat of the looms. And there is the fox — one fox, at several ages, red entering an alley that white leaves — for whom saucers are left out as a matter of municipal policy disguised as private kindness. Ternlowers do not discuss the fox because discussion feels, somehow, like counting a stair that has asked not to be counted. They feed it the way they feed everyone: hot, regularly, and without requiring it to be only one thing in exchange.

They eat printed food and salvaged delicacies and whatever can be coaxed from the warm places: pale grass that never seeds, cut fine into broth; algae skimmed after the daily breath; moss-fed snails from the pipe gardens; tea, endless tea, tea as a medium of exchange and verification. To offer tea is to say *I accept the version of you currently in my kitchen.* Funerals are held with similar pragmatism, sometimes more than once for the same person, each one a little better attended, the eulogies improving as the deceased settles. Nobody finds this morbid. They find it honest. A demolished wall can persist as a chill you walk through on humid days; why shouldn't a grandmother persist as a tendency, in a particular chair, to comment on the weather that hasn't been invented yet?

What they are, finally, is dense in the same way their city is dense. Not crowded — *concentrated*. An entire people's worth of watching, remembering, warming, counting, and agreeing, folded down into a hamlet's worth of streets because the alternative is to spread out and stop being real at the edges. Each of them carries more life than one lifetime strictly permits: instructions from their older selves, childhoods revised by new wells, rooms they will need next year arriving early and standing smelling of cold ceramic until they grow into them. Each of them is, in a small and workmanlike way, unfinished — holding several states at once, politely, without choosing, until somebody who loves them looks steadily for long enough that they settle, embarrassed, a few centimeters to the left of where they were, into being a person again by breakfast.

And they know how it ends. The builders say it at christenings, at graduations, at the burning of the moss-flowers: in a few hundred years the drones will inch out for the last time, the basalt will click into permanence, rivers will commit to their beds, mirrors will keep up, and a child will be born who is only ever one age at a time. They say it with real hope. They say it the way you might describe a long illness finally lifting.

Then someone refills the kettle, someone else recounts the stairs on the way to the door out of habit, and outside the window the unfinished dark goes on holding its stone and water and nothing in patient suspension — and Ternlow, warmed hand to hand and watched street by street by the people who are its true perimeter, declines, for one more evening, to be merely a place.
