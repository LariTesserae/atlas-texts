At a distance — and distance is never great here, sixty or eighty paces from terrace wall to terrace wall — they look exactly like people who keep a garden.

They are the right shape. They stoop, they carry baskets against their hips, they sit cross-legged to sort seeds. A woman cards grey fiber beside a bed of scarlet lilies. A man holds bark-strip in his teeth while both hands are busy on a trellis. Children run errands at the slow, absolute seriousness children bring to errands everywhere. If you are introduced, hands are offered. The hands are warm.

It is only after you have shaken a few that you notice the warmth is not the warmth of blood moving under skin. It is stored warmth. It is the heat of the tiles at noon, kept and given back evenly. A keeper who has been working in the shade of a planted wall will be cool on the shaded side and warm on the other, and will stay that way for an hour, the way brick does.

Their skin takes this wonderfully. It is fine, matte, unglazed-looking, the color of whatever part of the garden they have spent the season in — terracotta along the forearms of the pot-movers, ash-pale on the women who work the lime beds, a deep greenish brown on the old man who does nothing now but sit in the moss-felt with his back to a wall, combing it. Fine brick-dust settles on all of them, but not the way dust settles on a visitor. On a visitor it lies in the creases. On a keeper it *patterns*: along the knuckles in tile-joint lines, in the whorl of an ear, down the center parting of the hair, as if the body underneath were inviting it to become grout.

Their hair is the grey fiber the woman is carding. It comes off them in season, in soft drifts at the nape, and is carded, and spun, and woven into the reed roofs and the sleeping mats and the strips they tie trellises with. Nobody finds this remarkable. Their fingernails grow the way reed grows, in rings, with a slightly harder band for each dry season that has not happened yet. They trim them by rubbing them on a warm tile. Their teeth — they use them constantly, for cord, for bark, for holding a bone wedge while the mallet comes down — do not wear. If anything they sharpen with use, like the carvings on the gate pillars, fresh-edged no matter how many hands have passed.

Cut one, which nobody does deliberately, but gardening is gardening and a slipped wedge happens: a keeper does not bleed. A fine perfumed dust puffs out and settles. Or a clear oil beads, and hardens in the light into a pale varnish across the cut, and over slow weeks the place fills level again, a shade lighter than the skin around it. The eldest are mapped all over with these pale repairs. It does not look like scarring. It looks like mending. It looks like the place on a pot where the crack unfurled itself shut.

They do not drink. This is not a discipline; there is simply nothing to drink with. They eat what the beds give up: the small black warm seeds that the dry well brings up an afternoon at a time, roasted until they split; leaves picked tissue-dry off the stem and crisped further over the kiln that is never lit; a paste of ash-white tubers grown in chimneys that tastes of smoke and pepper and, faintly, of the chimney. They eat slowly, from bowls that are still slightly soft around the rim if last year's meal in them was a large one. They chew thoroughly. Swallowing, in the quiet, is a private sound that arrives later in your own jaw.

They speak the same way everything else here makes sound: badly, at the wrong distance. So they have learned to speak close, or not with their mouths at all. A keeper wanting your attention across the bowl will tap a tile, once, and wait for the tap to arrive inside your teeth, and then say the thing to whoever is standing nearest, who says it to the next, task to task, until it reaches you word-perfect and entirely without having crossed the air. In conversation they stand nearer than you are used to. Breath here smells of heated straw and pepper. Their voices are soft and unhurried and completely uninflected by urgency, because urgency has nowhere to go: the seedlings in the next bed are already up, three days before the seeds for them will be planted, and everyone is planting faithfully, without surprise, to give those seedlings a past they can rely on.

Ask one where the kiln is and you will not get a direction. You will get an order of work. *After the re-potting. Before the vines are cut. Two tasks past the woman carding. When the blue pot has been moved the width of a hand.* Ask how old a child is and the child's mother will consider, honestly, and answer with what the child is currently for: *She is at the pot-moving age. She was at the seed-sorting age last winter, or she will be.* This is not evasion. It is the only chronology that holds in a place where the gate you came in by is now further along the path than it was, planted over behind you, expecting you to arrive. The keepers are not confused about time. They have declined to be oriented by it in only one direction.

Their children arrive the way everything arrives here: somewhat before they are caused. A keeper who intends to be pregnant — intention is the usual word, said in the weather-discussing tone — takes to standing in a particular bed in the afternoons, in the good stored heat, with her feet in the manufactured soil of crushed brick and ash and shredded reed. She does this for a season. Her shadow, the other keepers say, gets better at standing there even when she has gone in to the loom. And then there is a child in the hamlet who has, as far as anyone can establish, been helping move the pots a hand's breadth sunward for several weeks already, following the chalk marks, and who now requires a name and a sleeping mat and feeding at meals, retroactively, so that the helping has somewhere to have come from.

They name children for tasks rather than sounds: little *After-Re-Potting*, little *Before-The-Vines-Are-Cut*, names that they will move out of as they move into other work, so that a very old keeper may answer, if pressed, to a name that is really a whole afternoon's sequence — *When-The-Rope-Is-Wound-And-The-Beds-Are-Combed-And-It-Is-Almost-But-Not-Yet-Dusk*. The eldest keeper by the dry well, winding the rope that lengthens as she winds it, has a name like that. Everyone agrees she is eldest. She also has the sharp, fresh face of someone recently carved, all her edges unsmoothed, and a century of pale mendings across her hands, and if both of these are true — and in the garden both of these are kept, because each does different work — then eldest may be a task too. Someone must sit by the well and wind. She is at the well-winding age. She has been at it, perhaps, since before the well was dug, which was after the garden was built around it.

Work is not what they do. Work is the medium they move through, the way fish move through a medium they do not comment on. The pots must be moved a hand's breadth sunward each week or the plants in them — which have already flowered, some of them, in the position the pot will occupy next month — would be stranded without a history. The moss-felt must be combed and scraped and re-laid like carpet or it will begin, untended, to arrange itself into patterns no one taught it, which is considered untidy. The rope must be wound. The vines must be cut so that the cuttings, laid on tiles, can spend the next month manufacturing their own pots — grit first, then a wall, then a rim, fired without fire — and it is understood, gently and without doctrine, that this is not something the plants learned from the keepers. It is something the keepers and the plants both caught, the way the brooms caught it. Left leaning in a doorway at dusk, a broom will be a few inches into its sweeping by dawn, the dust in a small perfect arc. A keeper finding it so does not startle. She picks it up and continues from where it had got to, mid-gesture, as you would take over a sentence from someone who had paused for breath.

This is, if you stay long enough, the thing you will have to understand about who lives here: the boundary you arrived with, between the keepers and the kept, is a built thing, and it has been built away.

The small brown birds that open their beaks on the perches and produce a visible trembling effort and no note — those are palms that finished. The children will show you, if you are patient, the dry fronds sighing down onto the tiles, and in the morning the bird, new and already knowing where the beetles carry the brick-dust. The bird will spend its years being stepped around on the paths, tame as furniture. When its feathers go pale it will take to standing in a bed for longer and longer portions of the day until its feet are difficult to distinguish from stems, and then it is a plant again, and expected to flower, and does — magenta, copper-red, the humming blue — tissue-dry and months at a time. The lizards patterned exactly like the tiles they are not currently on are on their way to being tiles, one undisturbed summer at a time, an arrangement of glazed scales among the leaves, breathing after they have become ornament. The beetles carry brick-dust from bed to bed on their backs, deliberate as porters, as porters. The snails comb the moss with their passing.

The keepers are simply the slowest form this circulation takes. The most deliberate. The one that can hold a wedge in its teeth and answer a question.

So an old keeper does not die the way you are braced for. There is less and less of the person-direction in him, over years. He takes to standing in one place in the good heat. He is brought his seeds there. His skin patterns more deeply, tile-joint fine, then tile sized. His name shortens, task by task, as other keepers quietly absorb the ends of his afternoon into theirs — someone else is combing now, someone else has always been combing — until the name that is left is only *Where-He-Stands*. The copper knife, much honored, the only metal thing in the hamlet, is fetched then, and used for the only thing it is ever used for. It cuts rope for a funeral. Not his rope, exactly. The rope: the long wound one, the one the eldest is always lengthening at the well, which runs, if you follow it with your eye and are willing to be patient about corners that take twenty minutes to turn, through every house and around every bed and once, loosely, around the place where he stands. They do not hurry. The cutting is done in the tone of weather.

Afterward there may be a new gate in the garden, where no gate was needed: two short pillars of unglazed brick, a lintel of charred wood bound with rope, made for stooping, sharp-edged as if built last spring. Or a stretch of planted wall that holds the evening heat a little better than the wall beside it. Or a keeper-shaped freshness in the carvings on the old gate — tally marks, birds, a spiral of thumbprints no one remembers cutting, darkened underneath by a century of hands. The hamlet does not hold a space open for him. That would be a gap, and nothing here admits a gap wide enough for dirt. Someone is already standing in the bed. The pots are already being moved toward the place he stood, a hand's breadth a week. He has not gone, in the sense that the bird has not gone when it becomes difficult to distinguish from stems. He has proceeded, at the pace of a crack unforming in clay, into the part of the garden that does the holding-up instead of the part that does the tending. Or the other way round. Both statements are kept.

And the newer keepers — the ones with fewer pale mendings, the ones whose names are still short enough to say in one breath — they card the grey fiber that comes off their own heads in season, and they re-tie the trellises with their teeth, and they plant on Tuesdays what came up the previous Friday, faithfully and without surprise. They are not maintaining the garden. They are not trapped in it, either, though the gate will be further along the path than it was when you look for it, and your own dust has begun, by then, to pattern along your knuckles. They are the garden, in the form it takes when it needs hands. The garden is them, in the form it takes when it needs somewhere warm to stand and flower.

Stay long enough and they will begin, very courteously, to bring you your seeds where you have taken to standing in the afternoons.
