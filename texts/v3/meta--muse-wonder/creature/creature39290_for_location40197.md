The people who live there are spare in the same way the town is spare — not because they are frail, but because nothing about them is wasted.

There are perhaps two hundred and fifty of them, under sixty or seventy roofs. You could learn every face in a week, and every voice in less than that, because voices are part of the town's economy too. No one shouts across the square. No one needs to. A normal speaking voice carries cleanly over flagstones, dies at the next corner, and is enough. So they speak low, in short, finished sentences, with long comfortable gaps between them — not taciturn, exactly, but unhurried, as if speech were another thing to be measured out and not spilled. Children learn this early. Even at play they are quieter than valley children, running in the empty paved spaces between houses, their footsteps louder than their calling.

They look weathered rather than old. The high light and cool wind do that. Faces are narrow, browned only lightly — the sun here is sharp but people have the habit of working in shade when they can and keeping covered — with lines deepened around the eyes from years of looking across bright stone and down long drops. Eyes are often narrowed, not suspiciously, just against glare. Hands are the most telling part of them: strong, dry, knuckles thickened from stone and wood and loom work, nails kept short, skin polished smooth at the fingertips the way thresholds are polished. Everyone's hands know how to mend. It is not a specialty. It is literacy.

The smith two lanes from the square works perhaps more often than anyone, and even he works in bursts — a morning of clear, spaced ringing while hinges and chisels accumulate, then days of quiet fitting, filing, sharpening by hand. The weavers work behind shutters. The masons split stone with wedges in the old way, listening for the fault in the rock. Most people do a little of everything by season: tend a walled garden of onions and bitter greens and a row of beans in the most sheltered corner, carry wood, clear the drainage channels before the wet weather, lime a cistern, adze a beam to match the beam it replaces. There are no idle trades here and no purely ornamental ones. If a skill does not help keep water in, stone up, food stored, or cloth on backs, it does not survive more than a generation.

Their clothes come from their own looms for the most part: undyed wool in greys and browns, woven tight against the wind, smelling faintly of damp wool and the herbs they rub into stored cloth. A wool layer from morning until noon, off for the warmest hour if the season allows it, on again before supper — visitors find the rhythm fussy until their own skin learns it. Shade at this height is genuinely cold. The townspeople move in and out of sun and shadow the way other people move between rooms, without thinking about it. They stand talking in a patch of late light. They set a bench where the wall will hold the day's last warmth. Lizards do the same on the walls at noon; the people understand lizards.

And water governs them more completely than any council in the meeting hall ever could.

Every child can do catchment arithmetic before they can write a neat hand: so much roof, so much slope, so much dew expected off the tilted slabs in a dry month, so many people under that roof, so much held in barrel and cistern and trough. They learn it by listening at dawn. The whole town wakes, in a sense, to dripping — into barrels, into stone troughs, into the covered cisterns whose cold damp air is the true center of life here. A person will lie still a moment and judge the night's yield by sound alone: a thin irregular ticking means a lean week; a steadier patter means washing can be done, beans can be soaked without anxiety. They rise and check levels the way farmers elsewhere look at the sky.

No one wastes water and no one performs thrift about it either. It is simply manners. You wash in a basin and the basin goes to the garden. You never let a channel run blocked. You know what your house holds, and — this matters — you roughly know what your neighbor's house holds too, because in a bad stretch the communal cistern behind its cold house is shared by rules older than anyone's grandparents. A guest is welcome, but a guest is also, unavoidably, a number of cups a day. Hospitality here is real and it is counted, and both things are understood without embarrassment. To invite someone to stay is to say: we have done the sum, and you fit inside it.

That same sum sets the size of every life. No new house can be raised unless its roof can feed its people, and the number of roofs has not changed in living memory. So people do not marry merely when they wish to, in the valley sense. They marry, often, when a house empties — when an old couple dies within a season of each other, when a widow moves in with her sister, when a family line simply ends and the meeting hall reassigns stone walls and catchment rights to another pair who have been waiting, sometimes for years, with a patience outsiders mistake for indifference. Some young people leave for the valley and do not come back. This is expected and not treated as tragedy. The town replaces itself; it does not multiply. Those who stay accept the shape of that bargain so young that it stops feeling like a bargain at all. It feels like proportion.

Their families are therefore small and their households elastic. A house might hold grandparents, a married son, and one child. Or three unmarried sisters who weave. Or a widower and an adopted nephew from the house two lanes over that had one child too many for its cistern. Kinship matters, but the roof matters more. People are often known by their house as much as by their name — the family at the upper lane with the good north-facing slabs, the woman whose cistern never sours, the man in the house above the bakehouse whose roof-slates bead dew better than anyone's because his grandfather set them at exactly the right tilt. Lineage here is partly architectural. Ancestors are remembered in lintels they replaced and walls they rebuilt on the same foundation line.

Food follows the same narrow logic. The granary and bakehouse are communal because grain must come up from below on pack animals — the gardens cannot grow it — and what arrives at such cost is not divided carelessly. Bread is dense and rationed by habit rather than by decree. Onions, bitter greens, beans, herbs with small fierce leaves. Goat cheese and occasionally meat from the pens downslope, where the bells sound small and distant when the wind turns. Meals are plain, hot, and eaten indoors out of the draft. No one is hungry in a normal year. No one is ever far from knowing exactly how many weeks stand between the town and hunger if the rains fail and the dew thins. That knowledge keeps them calm rather than anxious. Anxiety, like shouting, wastes something.

They are long-lived, on the whole, and age slowly in the visible ways — bent a little, as the two pines by the lower gate are bent, all in the same direction, from a lifetime of the prevailing wind and of stooping over low garden walls and loom frames. The old are the town's archive. Because repairs are made to be invisible, someone must remember that this slate was replaced forty years ago and that one was not, that this beam is oak and that one only looks like oak, that the cistern under the third house on the east lane cracked in a hard frost two generations back and was sealed in a particular way that should be checked first if the level drops. Old people carry the true map of the town, which is not the lanes but the history of maintenance. They are consulted the way other towns consult records.

Children are raised into sameness without much solemnity about it. They learn to split kindling to a standard length, to sweep an empty plot that will stay empty, to leave alone the wiry grass between flags unless it blocks a drain. That last lesson is characteristic: not everything growing must be used, not everything empty must be filled. The gaps between houses are not failures of building. They are how the town breathes. Children absorb the idea early that a complete life does not mean a crowded one.

They are not solemn people, for all that. There is laughter in the bakehouse queue in the morning, dry and brief. There is gossip, carefully dosed, in the cold doorway of the cistern-house. There are festivals of a sort — the return of the swifts to the eaves, the first full cisterns after the damp season, a wedding when a house changes hands — but celebration here looks like a slightly better meal, an extra lamp lit, people standing in the square a little later than usual in the cooling light, talking in that low spaced arrangement of voices over stone. Nothing overflows. Even joy is kept within the walls of what the place can hold.

The other inhabitants belong in the census too. A few patient dogs asleep in doorways, out of the through-draft, who rouse for the pack animals and nothing else. An occasional cat, lean as its owners, employed against mice in the granary. Goats downslope, heard more than seen. Swifts screaming briefly in their season and then gone. One raven, most days, crossing the square alone. Lizards at noon, moss in the damp north corners, lichen spreading its grey and dull orange rosettes across roof and wall at a pace the people find entirely reasonable. Nothing here multiplies beyond its allowance. Everything is kept, and keeps itself, at the scale the water permits.

If you stood among them in the square in late afternoon — light normal and level, air already cooling, thin smoke rising, someone shaking out cloth two lanes away with a sound like a small flag — you would not think them diminished by their limits. You would think them finished. A people made to the measure of a high saddle of grey rock: quiet-voiced, deft-handed, patient as repairs, content to polish the same thresholds their grandparents polished, and to gather, night after night, the slow dew that lets a complete little urban world go on being itself, exactly one more day.
