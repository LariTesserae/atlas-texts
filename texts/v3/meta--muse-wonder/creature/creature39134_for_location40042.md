It is not empty because it is inhabited the way a body is inhabited — not by one thing sitting in the middle of it, but by everything at every edge, keeping still until stillness itself starts to move.

The first citizens you would miss are the ones doing the living on purpose.

They are voles, for the most part. Low, blunt, compact animals made for a world with a ceiling, their backs flattened by habit if not by bone, bellies dragging close enough to the mud to read its temperature. Short tails. Short legs. Faces that end abruptly, as if designed to be pushed into narrow places without ceremony. Their fur is the exact compromise the room requires: dense enough to shed peat water in beads, rough enough to carry the smell of sedge and earth back into the tunnels, brown above and paler below from a life spent mostly in the dark. What color remains is not for display. Nothing down here is looked at for long.

Their eyes are small, damp, and nearly decorative. They do not navigate by sight so much as confirm, at the last moment, what their whiskers have already decided. A vole reads a lane the way you read a page with your fingertips in the dark — whiskers forward, nose working, ears flattened, the whole skull turned into a listening device pressed from time to time against a root to hear the huge, dangerous weather of the surface: ice ticking, fox feet, an owl settling somewhere above the mat where no shadow can fall down here.

What they are, anatomically, is teeth with an animal attached for transport.

Their incisors never stop growing, and that is not a detail, it is the government. Left alone, their own mouths would kill them; left to work, their mouths keep the city from being strangled by its own ceiling. So they gnaw in shifts, not hungrily but civically — root tips trimmed back from doorways, root hairs cropped from sleeping nests, supports chewed to a safe thickness, runways reopened where the mat has grown a pale curtain overnight. You could, if you stayed quiet, hear the whole constitution being renewed at the edges of the room: scrape, pause, scrape. A sound so small and constant it becomes a kind of silence with teeth in it.

They carry outsize things. A seed twice the weight of the carrier, held high so it will not drag in the rill. A curl of leaf used as a sled. A dead beetle dragged backward toward the fungus beds. A mouthful of dry grass for the communal hollow, where the nesting material — shredded leaf, seed-down, fur, a stolen feather worked soft — steams faintly with the accumulated heat of sleeping bodies. They are tidy in the manner of creatures who cannot afford rot near water: latrines at the cold end, capped with peat; husks sorted to the midden; drains kept open; lanes kept ostentatiously clear, not because they love openness but because everybody needs a margin of empty mud around their own scent.

They live spaced apart in a crowd. At every crossing there is a scent post, a deliberate smear on a root: *occupied, occupied, occupied*. That is how the city can hold more life in one low room than the marsh holds in acres and still feel, to any one animal moving through it, almost private. You own as much world as your whiskers can reach. The rest is tactfully someone else’s.

Interleaved with the voles, thinner and faster and much less civic, are the shrews.

If the voles are the builders, the shrews are the appetite that patrols them. Needle-nosed, velvet-dark, vibrating at a pitch the voles never reach, they cannot afford the voles' patience. Their hearts run too hot, their hunger renews too quickly; a shrew that stops eating begins, in a real sense, to disappear. So they are always inserting themselves into the economy at its tender points — into the fungus chamber where pale sheets of growth spread like curd across warm reed and dung, into the beetle stacks, into the side tunnels where a brief, muffled commotion resolves into a shrew emerging alone, licking its teeth. They are tolerated because they keep the insect population from becoming the population, and because their hunger makes them exquisitely alert along the entrance, where the false tunnels wait for a weasel, rich-smelling and ending in cold water.

Then there are the commuters and the seasonal guests.

A harvest mouse, smaller and more golden than the others, with a tail that can think for itself, has claimed the rusted bottle-cap cistern for a lookout and woven her nest into its crimped crown. From there she can feel the whole plaza through her feet. Field mice descend the exit tunnels with their cheeks packed tight as luggage, trade seed by leaving it, and are gone again above the mat before the root doors have finished closing. They belong to the city the way weather belongs to a house: regularly, and without staying.

And under, over, through, and literally *upon* all of them is the rest of the census, which cannot be separated into livestock, clothing, vermin, and citizenry because the categories keep eating each other.

Moths sleep in their hundreds across the largest storehouse roof, wings folded and overlapped like shingles, becoming insulation, becoming a roof that occasionally rearranges itself. Beetles stack in layers beneath loose bark, legs tucked, shells beading with condensation, slow-breathing stores of protein tended by no one and harvested by everyone. Woodlice commute beneath the floors. Centipedes hunt the roof beams with a patience like a held breath. In one cold corner, white springtails cover the peat so thickly that the ground appears frosted — until the frost pours, all at once, around a stone.

In a side chamber, kept warm by banked reed and droppings, the fungus grows. That is the true farm and the true stomach of the place. Vole droppings feed it; it feeds the beetles; the beetles feed the shrews; the rill takes whatever is excess and returns, with every thaw-pulse from above, drowned seeds, stunned flies, minute crustaceans flipping in the black shallows. Foraging follows that rhythm the way tides set a harbor's hours. Even predation has been made municipal. Nothing is wasted because waste, in a room this low and wet, is just poison that hasn't spread yet.

Below them all, sealed into the floor itself, are the winter sleepers — frogs in individual mud cells lined with their own glassy slime, throats pulsing once and then, a long time later, again; salamanders laid in the wettest runways like discarded ribbons, shining and barely metabolizing. They are not citizens so much as furniture that will, in spring, get up and leave. The snails have glued themselves across the mouths of empty houses and become the doors. Even the plants conspire in the housing market: root hairs grow fastest toward animal warmth, so a sleeping cluster of voles may wake gently netted in white filaments and have to chew itself free, leaving the cut hairs behind as fresh felt for the nest.

There is no ruler. There is a rota.

At any given moment someone is awake trimming a doorway, someone is crossing the plaza with a seed, someone is dragging food to the fungus, someone is lying absolutely motionless with her skull against a root listening for the world that wants to eat them, and someone else is asleep being slowly, tenderly overgrown. They communicate by taps — two strikes on a root, felt rather than heard — because a voice travels up through the mat to things with better ears. Their whole society is pitched below the threshold of notice. It is metropolitan in everything but volume: zoning by scent, public works, storehouses on stilts that are also built to float, false districts for predators, drains lined with shell, three exit tunnels everybody knows by heart. And it is all, consciously or not, temporary. The stores are bundled. The houses are repairable, not permanent. When the ice loosens and the mat drops they will not defend it. They will leave correctly, upward, toward the hummocks, and let the room drown black to the ceiling.

Which may be the truest thing about who lives there: they do not mistake the place for the world. They are a season's worth of small, warm, gnawing, listening animals who found a pocket under the skin of a marsh and turned it, for one winter, into a city — not by conquering the roots and the water and the dark, but by agreeing, shift after shift, body after body, to keep negotiating with them at exactly the speed at which they are being grown over.
