Nothing lives here, at first.

That is the first true thing, and it stays true for a long while. The grass at the mouth lives, after its fashion — three or four tussocks holding on in the light, growing so slowly that growth and decay have reached an agreement beside them. The shrub lives, grey-green and resinous, unchewed. Their roots live a second, blind life below, coming down through the ceiling cracks in pale threads to feel for a world that is not there.

And then, far past where any of that can reach, the dust in the back alcove is deepest.

It lies in overlapping drifts where the ceiling lowers, soft, undisturbed. If you put your palm down there — if you had come all that way on your hands in the dark, feeling for the back wall — you would find it warm like everything else. Warm an inch down. Warm all the way through. You might not notice, the first time, that one part of it is warmer still, not by much. A degree. The degree a body gives to stone when it has been lying against it for a very long time.

It is lying there.

It has been lying there so long that the distinction between it and the drift is mostly a matter of patience. From above it is dust. From the side, if there were light to see it by, it would be a low, broad flattening in the dust, the size of a large hand spread open, then a little larger — a dinner plate, a serving dish turned upside down, limestone-pale, its edges thinning out into the floor until you cannot say where floor ends and it begins. Its back is not smooth. It is cracked into loose, irregular polygons, like the floor near the entrance, and each crack is filled with finer dust, so that it wears the cave on itself. Fallen flour settles on it. It does not shake it off. Over years the settling becomes a kind of shell, not grown but accumulated, softened at the edges, drifted up its sides exactly as dust has drifted up the sides of the fallen blocks near the walls.

It does not move while you are there. It does not move, in any ordinary sense, while anything is there. Movement is not how it spends itself.

Underneath, where it meets the ground, it is different. Pressed flat to the packed earth is a broad, soft belly, leathery and unsegmented to the touch, faintly damp in the one place in the hollow where damp is hoarded and never spent. Not wet. Nothing here is wet. But its underside keeps a private humidity sealed between skin and stone, the moisture of its own slow breathing recycled for a lifetime, and in that thin, warm dark it digests.

It eats what arrives, which is almost nothing, at the rate at which it arrives, which is geological.

Wind does the provisioning. A grass blade that bleached for a decade at the threshold eventually becomes light enough to travel. A fragment of leaf, a husk, a grain of pollen from the sparse country above, a root hair finer than thread that finally grew long enough to break under its own weight and fall — these things enter on no draft you could feel, settle, and in the course of years are worked inward across the floor by the minute settling of the cave itself, grain by grain, until they reach the alcove. When one reaches the edge of it, it waits there too, sometimes for a season, sometimes longer, until the edge advances.

For the belly does advance. Not by crawling, not with limbs — it has none that you would recognize, no legs jointed for the floor, no claws for the walls. It expands on one side by the width of a hair and contracts on the other, a peristaltic shifting so slow that the dust above it never cracks. It can cross the chamber. It has crossed the chamber. The journey from the back alcove to the salt bloom on the wall at hand-height takes it most of a decade, travelling at night and day alike because there is no difference, travelling through a silence its own movement does not disturb. It leaves no track because its track is this: dust resettled behind it, grain by grain, until the floor is smooth again. The cave takes the sound of it and returns nothing, as it takes everything.

The salt is why it goes. That thin white crust, a few crystals wide, is its pasture and its well. Once, in some wetter decade, moisture passed along that seam and left the minerals behind, and it goes there to press its front end — its head, if head is the word for the end that tastes first — against the stone and take the crystals in. Salt, and with the salt, limestone, trace elements leached from the caprock a century ago. Its mouth is not a mouth but a rasping, muscular patch on the underside of the leading edge, which it works against surfaces the way a snail works, except that a snail is hurried by comparison, and wet. What it cannot use it gives back dry: fine white pellets, uric and mineral, indistinguishable from the limestone flour of the floor. It makes no droppings anyone would read as droppings. It makes floor.

That, and the roots, is the whole of its economy.

When a root thread comes down far enough — and they do, one here, two far apart there, feeling down the walls in the dark — it finds them before they find the floor. It can taste a root in the air. Not smell, exactly; it has no apparatus for the open air, its whole sensing surface is its underside and the rim around it, a fringe of short, pale, exquisitely sensitive filaments that read the world the way you read the chamber, by contact. Warmth gradients. Vibration through stone. The faint chemical signature of living tissue against dead dust. It will station itself under a descending root for a year, two years, rising imperceptibly as the root descends, until the tip touches the rasping patch and is taken, a fraction of a millimetre at a time, trimmed back to a blind end. This is why the roots end blind in the dust-filled air, sparse, going nowhere. They are grazed by something slower than their own growth, which keeps them exactly in balance. From a root it gets the only real water of its life: sap drawn down from the grass in the light, a few cells' worth at a time, sufficient because it spends almost none. Its skin does not sweat. Its breath does not mist. It loses water the way the cave loses water, which is to say, in principle, over centuries.

It has no eyes. It has never had the use of eyes, and the lineage it comes from — for it is a relict, the last expression of something that lived here when the back wall still sweated and a pool stood in the low corner — traded them away so long ago that there are not even vestiges, no pits, no light-sensitive spots. The darkness is not a condition it endures. It is simply not a category. The entrance, twenty paces off and around the bend, exists for it only as the faintest periodic thinning of the warmth in one direction, the stone marginally less steady where the day breathes against the slope. It does not go toward it. There is nothing for it at the threshold that is not delivered, eventually, to the alcove.

It has no ears, and makes no sound. No call, no mating song, no warning. It is alone and has always, in its own lifetime, been alone — born, if born is the word, from a leathery egg laid in the damp that no longer exists, or budded from a predecessor, or arrived as a juvenile small as a fingernail in an era when the chamber was merely dim and moist and full of ordinary cave life. How it came to be the only one is a question with a slow answer: everything else needed more. The insects needed the plants. The spiders needed the insects. The bats needed all of it, nightly, in quantity. One by one, as the country dried and the pool shrank to a damp patch and the damp patch to a stain and the stain to this, they thinned and left or thinned and stopped. It stayed because it could reduce itself to meet the place. It reduced its size, its speed, its temperature of activity, its wants, until what it wanted and what the hollow provided were the same list: warmth, salt, dust, a root now and then, and time.

It is old in a way that has no ring or scar to count. Its back has incorporated limestone flakes that fell before anyone's memory, grown around them the way dust grows around everything here. In wet years — the rare years when rain finds some other crack, when the roots thicken and the salt bloom spreads a crystal wider — it feeds a little more and adds a little tissue around its margin, and the polygons of its back split and re-form to cover the new ground. In dry decades it consumes its own edge, almost imperceptibly, and the polygons close up. It has been larger. It may have been, at its greatest, almost twice its present spread, when the chamber floor was still being trodden by other feet and dead things fell uneaten in quantities. It does not remember. Memory is not one of the things it kept. It keeps the shape of the alcove in the shape of its resting body, and the route to the salt in the differential wear of its rim, and nothing else needs keeping.

Stay long enough and you would understand that the silence is not empty. It is occupied, at a tempo below hearing. Once in perhaps a month, the whole broad underside contracts in a single coordinated pulse, a breath taken through the skin and let out into the sealed humidity beneath it, and the dust on its back shifts by less than a grain's width. Once in a year it commits to a repositioning — a rotation of a few degrees, following the deepest softness as the drifts rearrange themselves. Once in a decade it crosses for salt. These are its events. Between them it does the thing the hollow does, which is hold its warmth evenly, change almost not at all, and let what falls on it become part of it.

It is not waiting for the rain to return, or for a mate, or for the roots to reach the floor at last. Waiting implies the future is different from this. For it, the future is this, extended. The grass at the mouth will grow a fraction of an inch. The fallen blade beside it will take another year to become dust, and the dust will travel, and eventually it will arrive at the edge of a low, warm flattening in the deepest part of the dark, and be taken in so slowly that taking and resting are the same act.

Then the chamber will be, once again, exactly as it was. Empty at the center. Everything set wide apart. The floor smooth, trodden by no regular traffic, the back wall curving round in its blind alcove, the dust lying deepest and softest there — breathing, about once a month, too softly for the stone to give back.
