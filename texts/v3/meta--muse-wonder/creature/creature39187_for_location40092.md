No one swims to meet you.

That is the first way you know who lives here. You come down through the ice-skin expecting the place to react — a scatter in the kelp, a shadow going to ground in a canal, something startled from a roofless house — and nothing does. The fronds close behind you at the speed of fronds. The meadows brush you and resume their sway. A month could pass in the clearing of that water and still no eye would have found you, because there are no eyes.

The inhabitants are rooted.

They were not always. Or rather, they arrived the way everything here arrived: carried in, set out in rows, spaced by a hand and a line. Pondweed along the canal banks for a hedge. Kelp along the field walls. Eelgrass and stonewort in the paddies, coppiced darkness in the plots, red crusts left to take hold where the blue dusk comes down a little stronger. A planting, not a wilderness.

It has been a wilderness of its own kind for so long that the distinction no longer holds. The builders drowned the continent they had paved, layer over layer over layer, and stocked it the way you stock a garden — with nothing that has a mouth. And the garden, left to itself under fifty meters of near-freezing water, sorted itself into a population, and then into something nearer a single resident.

It lives at the temperature where water is heaviest. It lives slow enough that slow stops being a useful word. A push of current comes through a kelp stand at a walking pace and the twenty-meter fronds answer it minutes later, purple-black blades still finishing the last weather while the next one begins. Growth is measured in seasons. Collapse is measured in seasons too. Rot, in the oxygen-thin bottom water, is measured in generations. Nothing hurries because nothing is hunted, nothing is hungry, and nothing is waiting for a gap to dart into. There is only light failing to arrive, cold failing to lift, and silt falling in white flecks from the green bloom far above — the only rain it has ever known, a snow of dead summer settling onto leaves that will die, in their turn, and snow downward again.

This is how it eats. It has no mouths in it, so it eats itself by falling down.

A kelp forest shades its own lower stems until they weaken. A meadow thickens until the center of it starves in its own turf. Mats accumulate ungrazed, a meter thick in the hollows between terraces, half-rotted, cold-preserved, and the next planting roots directly into its ancestors. Every paddy is a compost heap with stone walls. Every building is a planter now — silt in the doorways, eelgrass up through the floor, moss swallowing lintel and wall-top until the house is more cushion than stone — and what lives in the houses is the same thing that lives in the canals and up the three useless stairs and along the fractures of the causeways: green in grey, rooted in made ground, growing out of the last version of itself.

If it has castes, they are places.

The tall ones stand on the walls. Kelp is the forest and the architecture both, its holdfasts gripping unmortared stone, its stems rising toward a noon that, ten meters down, already surrendered its light. From below it is a population standing shoulder to shoulder in the dusk, heads lost up in the darker blue, swaying with that long underwater delay, as if thinking and moving were the same gesture, poorly synchronized. It makes lanes of the old roads. It narrows canals to corridors. You navigate by its alleys the way the builders must have navigated by their hedges, except the hedges have gotten twenty meters high and moved into the sky.

The low ones crowd the fields. Eelgrass, stonewort, dense as turf inside the little stone enclosures, exactly where soil once sat and waited for a crop. This is the great common body of the place, league after league of meadow divided by knee-high walls, stippled with liverwort, furred at the edges, flattening under a passing current and lifting again over the course of an hour. It keeps the shape of agriculture long after agriculture has stopped meaning labor. The rows persist because the walls persist, and because everything here inherits the plot it falls in.

The small ones do the upholstering and the remembering. Moss and liverwort and red algae crust every stair, every gate-post without its gate, every peck-mark left by a dressing hammer thousands of patient blows ago. They soften inscriptions first into suggestions and then into bare texture. They round the world. They are the part of the inhabitant that lives pressed flat to the builders, a skin over their work, taking the shape of carved characters it has long finished erasing. Where stone was once the distinction — building against field, road against ditch — the moss has made it all one continuous surface, one body sitting on another body that sat on still another pavement below the rubble fill.

And in the middle of it all, it keeps one place bare.

Kelp does not grow on the great circle at the lowest point of the basin. Only a short, even turf of stonewort covers the enormous stones there, in the clearest and coldest water in the world, under the slow convergence of currents that comb it and comb it. Whether the inhabitant maintains that, or merely tolerates it, is unreadable. It may be reverence learned in the root. It may be chemistry. But the effect is of a room kept swept in a house otherwise entirely overgrown — a plaza where the population thins to a lawn and stops, and the white snow falls, and nothing rises to interrupt the dusk.

It has no calls. Its sounds are accidents: a waterlogged beam shifting in a collapsed roof and knocking once, very far through the water; silt ticking down; in winter, the surface ice groaning fifty meters overhead and coming through as a pressure in the teeth. The resident does not answer any of it. It senses, if sensing is the word, with its whole extent — current against frond, new sediment against leaf, the weight of its own dead settling a finger's thickness in a single submarine fog and erasing what little record a visitor leaves. It tastes mud. It tastes itself. The water it lives in smells, when lifted into air, only of it: green things and stone and cold.

It does not know it was planted for drowning. It does not know it is lush past reason in a place with no sun to speak of, because it has reduced itself, species by patient species, to exactly those of its members that can want this much light and no more — this much cold, this much dark, this much unending stillness under pressure. Anything that needed an animal has gone. Anything that needed a season has compressed its life into a permanent late afternoon under water. What remains is viable and complete and entirely without witness: a continent that feeds on its own fall, divides itself into fields it did not build, and settles year by year, a fraction deeper, into the shape its makers left scored into the earth for it.

You are the first animal it has touched in a very long time.

It closes behind you at the speed of plants.

And stays.
