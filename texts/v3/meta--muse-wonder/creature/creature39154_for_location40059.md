From a distance, they look like no one at all.

A tower at dusk, forty terraces lit, gardens spilling down its flanks, markets burning in a bright coil around its middle — and almost no movement visible. No crowds on the open ground, because there is no open ground, not really. No roads to be crowded. No one shouting across a street. If figures appear on a terrace they are still, or moving in what looks like slow semaphore, arms wide against the green murk, bright sleeves snapping — not waving for help, just talking.

Come closer, through a doorway that leaves your ears ringing and your shirt damp with some other tower's morning, and the stillness resolves into a density of gesture so constant it becomes its own weather. They are speaking everywhere, all the time. They speak with their hands in the big outdoor sign that carries across a terrace, elbows out, fingers splayed to be read through mist. They speak smaller in elevators and market galleries, one hand fluttering at the hip. They speak most intimately without hands at all: two fingers walking a sentence down the inside of a lover's forearm, a thumb pressed once for *yes*, twice for *stay*, a whole argument conducted on the skin of a shoulder while both parties stare politely out at the jungle. Text blooms and fades across the inside of their eyelids, delivered by implants that shave the roar of the basin down to a hiss they can live inside. You learn to recognize the look — a sudden half-blink, eyes unfocused, mouth soft — of someone reading a message from a tower twelve miles away while their body stands in line for water.

Their ears are beautiful, and unmistakably not quite natural. Within days of birth a child is fitted with shunts grown from its own cartilage: pale, spiraled structures that bloom at the side of the head like small listening shells, channeling the pressure away from the delicate inner architecture, dulling the cicada-saw and the frog-drum and the subsonic pulse that lives in everyone's sternum. They grow with the skull. They scar in family patterns; jewelers' cousins, flesh-smiths, carve fine ridges into them as they form, so that by adolescence you can read a person's tower, lineage, sometimes trade, from the sculpture at their ears. Children touch each other's shunts the way children elsewhere pull sleeves. Elders' shunts calcify, yellow slightly, become less efficient and more sensitive at once — old people hear *downward*, into the infrasound, into the towers and the settling rock, and are consulted the way sailors once consulted barometers. When a very old person goes still in the middle of a market gallery, hand flat against a living wall, nobody interrupts. They may be listening to the building decide something.

They are built, visibly, around intermittence. Their clothes are layered for a fog-burst: outer wraps that drink water and give it back slowly, hoods that unfold like the cupped leaves of the local trees, sandals that seal. Everyone of working age carries a soft flask at the hip, not full — full is a temporary condition, celebrated and slightly embarrassing, like wealth. They time their lives to the towers' exhalations with an instinct visitors mistake for piety. Laundry appears on terraces in the grey hour before dawn. Baptisms wait, sometimes for days, the infant signed over and over with its new name while the family watches the tower's flanks for the first warm roll of mist. Lovers, too; conception is a calendrical art. So is pouring anything that needs to cure, burying anything that needs to soften, opening the ground-floor chapels where the meadow-flowers from the last burst are pressed, still faintly humming, into paper.

Indoors, they are different animals. The double-skinned walls of a tower cancel the basin's frequency, and behind them lies a silence so total that newcomers — doorway-born, mostly, tower children visiting cousins six valleys away — sometimes sit down hard on the floor and cry. The natives seek it the way they seek water: deliberately, ritually, by the paid minute in quiet-rooms where an attendant times the door with a sand-glass and no one, ever, signs. Speech lives in those rooms. That is where the spoken language is kept, the way seeds are kept. People's voices are soft from disuse, oddly formal, startlingly young-sounding even in the old. A grandmother may take a grandchild into a quiet-room on the child's name-day and say the name aloud, once, so it exists in air somewhere. Marriages are whispered. Confessions are whispered. There are professional speakers — cantors, judges, midwives — who train their throats like athletes and can talk for an hour indoors without coughing, and hearing one is a civic event. To be spoken to, unmediated, at close range, in silence, is so intimate that doing it casually is a scandal and doing it well is a form of seduction more potent than touch.

Work, here, is mostly a matter of attention to things other people cannot afford to notice. Doorway-tenders stand at the humming frames in the cores with their teeth lightly clenched, feeling for the wrong note, the sourness that means the lattice is straining against too much freight, or wet weather coming the wrong way through. They fine people who arrive before they leave with the weary air of clerks fining late taxes. Reed-keepers go down — actually down, out, onto the ground, which most tower-born do only a handful of times in a life — and walk the jungle in shunt-muffling hoods, tending the twenty-foot hollow reeds that drink the city's noise and pump the water cycle along. They come back after a season with a particular glazed serenity and a tendency to turn their faces, unconsciously, toward the nearest doorway-frame, like plants. Gardeners ride the fog. Sky-watchers read the sails by their shadows and call the weeping before it falls, signing *cover, cover, cover* across whole terraces of people reaching for leaves the size of doors. And everywhere, unobtrusive, the listeners: people paid by the tower councils to keep a palm against a wall for hours, monitoring the pulse. When one of them straightens up and signs *hole* — meaning the pressure has dropped out of their chest, meaning a tower somewhere has gone silent — an evacuation begins without a single alarm needing to sound.

Because the towers are, in the local understanding, also inhabitants. Not metaphorically, quite. The pale ceramic and living timber, the translucent sweating stuff that seals its own cracks overnight and dreams in faint traveling patterns of light when the weather turns warm and wet: it is tended, fed, argued with, mourned. Each tower has a name that is also the name of everyone born in it, a second surname worn in the ridges of the ear-shunts. Each has habits. This one exhales early and generously and its people are famously clean, famously early, famously smug. That one runs warm, dreams in spirals, keeps producing excellent cantors and unreliable doorways. Funerals are held against an interior wall, and afterwards no one says where the ashes go, exactly, only that the tower keeps its own. When a tower falls silent — felt first, as a hand withdraws from the inside of the ribs — its people file out through the emergency frames while they still hold, or walk, if they must, walk out across the open ground between ridges, in a long silent line, not signing, not looking back, leaves turning to watch them go. They are rehoused by cousins and rivals and strangers in towers they have visited all their lives and never lived in, and they are, for a year or so, unmistakably displaced: reading the wrong tower's pulse for the time of day, turning toward doorways that open onto the wrong weather, waking at the wrong exhalation. Homesickness, here, is a rhythm disorder.

Their children grow up vertical, unafraid of heights and faintly baffled by horizons. A tower child knows forty kinds of leaf by silhouette, can read text with their eyes closed, can hold a whole conversation while hanging laundry, and may reach adulthood having stood on actual earth fewer times than they have fingers. They play at the edges of terraces in the permanent amber late-afternoon, daring each other to take the shunt-mufflers off outdoors for a count of three — and come reeling back, weeping and grinning, flooded, having heard the world raw for the first time: the billions of cicada-analogues, the four-winged hunters stitching screams between towers, the reeds pumping, the frogs, the freight pods arriving with their soft concussive *whump*, and under it all the great felt pulse, the note the whole city is carefully not playing. They describe it afterward the way mystics describe anything: badly, happily, for years.

And at night the sign-talk slows but does not stop; it goes close and tactile in the dimming galleries, hands to forearms, foreheads nearly touching, while the membranes above glow with stored light and the basin changes key around them. They emerge onto the terraces in their night wraps and stand looking out — over jungle, void, a distant tower lit like a slow lighthouse, more jungle — feeling the roar through the soles of their feet and the elegant shells at their ears, three million of them spread across sixty miles of young black rock, most of whom will never meet, all of whom are, at every moment, almost touching. They are not a quiet people. They are a people who moved their noise inward, into bone and skin and eyelid and wall, and learned to live inside a sound too large to hear — tended, at the last, as much as tending, by the tall, sparse, dreaming things they live in.
