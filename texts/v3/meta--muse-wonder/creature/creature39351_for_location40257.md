You hear its inhabitants before you understand that they are inhabitants.

From the road down, it looks like a town that happens to have birds on it. Stand in it for an hour and the proportion reverses. The people, the plant, the forty buildings — those are the furniture. What lives here, in the dense, continuous, occupying sense, is a population that has filled the place to a number and then stopped changing.

Every perch is claimed.

That is not a figure of speech for crowded. Every roof ridge has its row, shoulder to shoulder, facing into the draft from the cooling towers with beaks slightly open to cool themselves. Every pipe-run, handrail, antenna, windowsill, bollard, and wire. A gull on the clinic sign. Jackdaws down the canteen gutter in order of size. Starlings packed so tight on the laundry vents that the vents appear to be feathered. Pigeons on the one bench in the one acre plaza, rotating in strict, unspoken shifts so the wood is never visible.

And above them, all day, the rest are waiting.

A slow, flattened spiral turns over the quarry — gulls, starlings, jackdaws, pigeons — circling not in excitement but in patience, like people taking a number. When a bird below dies, which happens constantly, mid-perch, mid-call, tipped off a rail by age or a fight or a fan blade, the body has barely begun to fall before another drops out of the queue, lands in the exact vacancy, turns into the wind, settles its feet, and opens its beak. The roofline does not change. Photographs ten years apart have the same silhouette, tail for tail. The birds will tell you, if anything can, that a shape here is not made of individuals. It is made of places to stand, and the standing is continuous.

Below the roofs it is the same density, only warmer and closer.

Rats use the pipe-bridges in the open. Glossy, well-fed, unhurried rats, commuting along the warm runs between the rendering plant and the grain spill by the loading yard, pausing for forklifts the way pedestrians pause for cars. They do not hide because nothing here has ever successfully made hiding necessary. The official cats — collared, numbered, rostered for particular buildings — lie on transformer housings in the steam and let sparrow chicks walk across their paws. A cat who chased anything seriously would be chasing forever, and would starve in the middle of abundance. They have learned economy.

A pair of ravens lives in the housing of the decommissioned siren, and has lived there so long the town has quietly struck that siren from every emergency plan. Bats do not so much emerge at dusk as leak continuously from a ventilation shaft, a ribbon of them, because inside the ducts it is always roosting-warm and always almost dark. In the lagging under the cold store, where waste heat keeps the pipes at blood temperature, sparrows are raising young in December and in July with the same frantic, cheeping regularity. Down in the sumps and culverts, under the drain covers that tick faintly against their frames, thousands of frogs are doing what frogs do on the first evening of spring — calling, mating, laying — because for thirty-one years it has *only* been the first evening of spring, nine degrees and damp and insect-thick, and nothing has ever given them the signal to stop.

It is an animal town built exactly to the size of its waste. The plant makes food at a fixed rate, heat at a fixed rate, standing water at a fixed rate, midges at a fixed rate. The population simply solved for those numbers. That is why nobody clears nests anymore. Maintenance crews work around a swift colony in the pipe lagging the way they would work around a load-bearing beam; there are pipes that will never be replaced because replacing them would, in the site manager's words, change the sound. In the first decade a contractor brought a falcon to clear the cooling towers. The falcon assessed the endless, replenishing supply of pigeons, took up residence in Tower Two, and stayed. By seniority it is now essentially a foreman. It hunts on its own schedule. The others make room.

They have all been here long enough to learn the machinery by heart, and then to speak it. The starlings and jackdaws do the reversing beep of the yard trucks perfectly. They do the two-tone warning before a pressure release, in sequence, from different roofs, like a relay. They do the ammonia leak alarm flawlessly enough that the plant had to abandon sound for anything serious. Real emergencies now arrive as a puff of wintergreen through the ventilation, a chemical sweetness on the back of the tongue — the one channel the birds haven't colonized. Yet. Human beings, outcompeted in their own signaling system by the tenants on their roofs, have adapted by going quieter and closer: conversation here is either a full shout, beak-open into the roar, or a low, flat, lip-readable murmur developed by people who lean in under the noise and speak almost without breath. Children learn it early. Visitors shout until their throats give out, get headaches by evening, and wake at 3 a.m. in the ringing.

And the people *are* inhabitants, fully — just a rarer species in the census, adapted as specifically as the rest.

They live in the three-storey hostels with their laundry strung indoors across the hallways, because laundry hung outside comes back speckled and filmed and smelling of the yards. They wear the same orange in shift after identical shift, take the same posts, shout the same greetings into the same wall of starlings and engines, and dampen at the shoulders within minutes of going outside, coats beading, sleeves whitening with the fine dust that sifts off every roof. They buy earplugs and boot covers and netting and tea from the one open shop. They step around nests in doorways without looking down. They know which handrails are warm, which ledges are claimed, which cat belongs to the clinic. They can sleep through a pressure release and wake instantly if the level *changes* — and it never changes, which means they sleep very well here, and almost nowhere else. Out in the world they lie awake in the intolerable quiet, ears ringing with an absence, and come home down the three switchbacks with relief: to the compressor roar coming up through the floor of the car, the frogs in the drains, the queue turning patiently overhead.

Nothing here is young or old in the usual way, because nothing here is seasonal. There are always chicks. There are always eggs in the warm lagging, always tadpoles in the green-black puddles that have never dried, always a new bird dropping into a dead bird's outline, always a new orange figure crossing the yard to take a post just vacated. Funerals and replacements are not events. They are the mechanism.

Stand still among them at midday and you stop being able to say where the town's population ends and its architecture begins. The feathered roofs. The rat runs. The cats on the transformers, breathing steam. The ravens in the silenced siren. The frogs making the pavement hum at a frequency you feel in your molars. The people murmuring lip-to-lip under a noise their own tenants can imitate better than they can. All of them facing, one way or another, into the same draft. All of them holding the same note at the same volume, not building up, not running down.

They are not a community that lives in an equilibrium.

They *are* the equilibrium — feathered, gloved, whiskered, web-footed, numbered and collared and orange-coated — a single population wearing many shapes, that found the one sustainable number for a place where spring never ends, and has held it, loudly, for thirty-one years.
