You hear her before there is anything to touch.

Out on the grass-plain, rain on broad leaves, rain frying loud on everything — and through it, a pattern. Not loud. Precise. Click, click-click, from the tongue against the roof of the mouth, each one sent out and waited for. The fleshy blades answer back. A stem answers differently than open mud. A sleeping animal answers differently than a stone. She walks in the answered world, one hand lightly on the guide-rope, the other open in front of her face, not to feel for obstacles, but to feel the air move.

She is not blind the way a visitor would be blind. Blindness implies something missing. She was born on the plateau, as her mother was, as her grandmother's grandmother was, in a felt hut against the warm flank of a smelter. The sockets where eyes would be are small, shallow, covered over with smooth skin. Her aunt sewed over hers at her naming — not a scar, a decoration. Fine thread, worked by touch into a spiral pattern, a little copper wire braided in because copper sings faintly when a train passes through the ground. All the families do it, each with its own pattern. You can tell who someone is by tracing their face with your thumb. You learn faces with your hands.

She does not think of herself as eyeless. She thinks of visitors — there haven't been any in three generations, but the stories persist — as deaf in the skin.

Her face is her most honest organ. The skin across her cheekbones and forehead is almost hairless, slick, extraordinarily sensitive to pressure. She turns it up to the cloud-ceiling out of habit, the way you might test a wind. Warmth falling means rain thickening. A cool thread cutting through the warmth from the left means a river valley opening. A sudden, flat stillness on one cheek means something large is standing between her and a vent-field, drinking the heat that should be reaching her. She can feel a condenser station exhale from a kilometer away as a slow push against her whole body, and she steers by the pistons she can feel in her molars.

She clicks when she travels, whistles low under her breath when she works close to machinery, and stops making sound entirely when she is listening for something specific — a leak, a herd, a change in the rain over a lake. Her repertoire is not a language, exactly, though she has one of those too, spoken close, mouth to ear, because voices carry too obscenely far on open ground to be trusted. The clicks are grammar for terrain. Children learn them before words. A child who clicks badly is corrected the way you would correct a limp.

She is short, because everyone here is short, and broad through the shoulders and hips. Her feet are the first thing you'd notice if you could see in this dark, which you can't, so you notice it when she stands near you and the wet ground changes under her weight. The feet are wide, splayed, the toes long and separately strong, the soles thick as leather from grass-stem and mud and hot stone and rail-bed gravel. She reads the ground through them without thinking — forty minutes of clicking grass from the hut, then sucking mud, then a river that says its name over round stones. She knows exactly where she is in that sentence.

Her hands are longer than a lowlander's. The fingertips are blunt, ridged, a little numb-looking and anything but. She maintains the condenser that her family has maintained since before the family had a name for itself. Its louvers are taller than a house. It inhales the wet air of the whole marsh watershed and sighs it out dry, and her job, her mother's job, is to climb its throat by rope and by memory and feel for the places where moss and spore-cake and mineral scale are choking a vane. She scrapes by touch. She knows a healthy vane by the note it gives when she taps it with the back of her knuckle — a clean, low hum that travels up her arm into her shoulder. A sick vane thuds. A dying vane is silent, and silence, anywhere here, alarms her more than any scream.

She wears felt that never quite dries, layered, smelling of woodsmoke that she has never seen burn and of the oil she rubs into it from marsh fish. Her hair she keeps cropped close on the sides where the rope-lines pass and longer on top, braided with cord so it doesn't drip into her ears. Her ears matter. They are ordinary in shape but she holds her whole head differently because of them, tilted a fraction, always, the way an animal does. She can pick out, under the rain-roar and the piston-valley two hundred kilometers away arriving as a faint regularity, and the freight train keening its horn every fifty meters for the herds — she can pick out the sound of one frog species stopping. That means water temperature changed. That means check the intake.

She eats what the dark provides. Fungus bread that gives like wet loaves under the hand, torn, judged done by smell when the cook-fire — tended blind, by touch, coals read through the skin of the palms held above them — makes it sigh. Blind fish from the marsh channels, caught in woven traps, tasting of mud and faintly of copper. The sweet-peppery pollen that coats everything in season, licked off leaves. Sometimes herd meat, when a migration column loses an old one to the mud, and then the whole siding feasts and the smell of roasting fat changes the air for days and predators come and stand politely at the edge of the heat, walking in time with the pistons, waiting for scraps.

She has never met the operator at the next siding. She knows him intimately. She talks to him through the rail — hammer strikes on tuned steel, the answer coming back through the ground into the soles of her feet minutes later, a conversation at walking pace. She knows he has a cough this wet season. She knows when his child was born because the pattern of his strikes went joyful and irregular for three whole cycles. She will send her own daughter down the line one day, probably, to marry his son, because the voices are compatible and the families' face-patterns feel good together under the thumb, and because exogamy here is an acoustic necessity: you must not breed with people who hear the world too much like you do, or the children miss things.

She has notions that would be theology if she had the leisure for it. She does not believe in an open sky — the shepherds at the rim tell of a direction where the wind stops echoing, and she privately thinks they are describing death. She does not believe in distance as a straight line; distance is how long it takes an echo to come back changed. She does not believe she lives *on* anything. When she was small she asked her mother where the plateau ended and her mother put her hand flat against the warm wall of the condenser, then flat against her own chest, then flat against the trunk of a tree that had spiraled in toward the factory's heat, and said nothing, and the child understood: it is all one surface, folded.

Time, for her, has no light in it. She sleeps when the condenser cycle troughs and the air in the hut goes briefly cooler and drier. She wakes when the pistons shift their rhythm a half-beat and her teeth know it before she does. She has drunk from the oldest cistern on the line — everyone does, once, ceremonially, at adulthood — and dreamed that night in machinery time, her heart knocking in piston intervals, and woke with the understanding that her own body was also infrastructure, also maintained, also temporary in the same long way the rails are temporary. It did not frighten her. It placed her.

She is not afraid of the dark. She is afraid, precisely and practically, of the deaf zones out in the marsh where warm fog swallows sound. She has found what animals leave there — bones by touch in the mud, perfectly articulated, lain down mid-confusion. She crosses herself against silence by keeping one hand always on something that transmits: a rope, a rail, a wet trunk, another person's wrist. A person alone in silence here would simply stop being able to confirm the world, and then stop being able to confirm herself. That is the only damnation her people have.

So she lives attached. To the rope-line strung hut to hut, beaded with knots that spell out whose section you are in. To the condenser that breathes for the watershed. To the herds whose click-hail passes in the rain like weather becoming briefly animal. To the low boom of the great blind fliers far above the canopy, riding the factory updrafts, calling down to read the cloud-ceiling — she feels those calls in her chest, *pressure*, not sound, and feels companionable toward creatures she will never touch.

If you stood next to her — you can't see her, she is already aware of your breathing, your temperature, the wrong way your feet meet the mud — she would reach out without hesitation and take your hand and put it on the rope, because that is what you do for anything lost here. She would click once, gently, to show you how to answer the world. She would wait for the echo. She is very patient. Her whole species is an act of patience: generations of people becoming, slowly and without grief, the sensory organ of a place that could not make eyes work — the skin it feels its own face with, the ear it presses to its own wall to hear if the falls are running full.

She turns her face up. The rain warms, cools, warms against her. Somewhere the pistons knock. Somewhere her name, spoken carelessly last season, is still traveling between cloud and ground, flattening, changing, on its way back to tell her who she was.

She listens for it.
