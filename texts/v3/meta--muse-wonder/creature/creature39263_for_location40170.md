If you stay only an hour, you will say nothing lives there.

There are no nests in the pillars, no burrows among the wet stones, no droppings, tracks, feathers, shells, or bones. Nothing grazes on the mineral crusts. Nothing drinks from the pools and leaves ripples widening behind it. The copper-tainted water kills whatever microscopic patience might otherwise take hold in the cracks.

But if you stay until evening, your footsteps will begin arriving before you do.

At first only faintly. A soft tread in the passage ahead, though the passage is empty. Then, when you stop, several more steps continuing at your pace, slowing with a consideration that feels increasingly unlike an echo. By the second day they may splash. By the third, they have learned which stones are dull and safe, and they walk there even when you do not.

That is how it announces itself: not by appearing, but by continuing.

Whatever lives in the bowl is made of all the sounds that stayed too long there.

It began, perhaps, with wind. Wind caught between thousands of pillars, divided into chords, returning through fluted stone until a note could sustain itself after its cause had passed. Then came other sounds, if anything with a voice or a footfall ever climbed above the clouds: dripping water, hammerstones, copper picks, breathing, speech. The bowl did not let them finish. It kept some portion folded in rock and water, the way a shell keeps an approximate sea.

Over immense time, those portions learned the passages.

The inhabitant is not exactly plural. Its voices overlap too much. One footstep wears the weight of a hundred feet. One hand pressed into the mineral mud may have six or seven fingers because it is not one hand arriving once, but the same almost-hand returning at slightly different ages, each finger offset by a season or a century. When the sound is strong enough — at the loud hour before sunset, when every pillar rings and water climbs toward the noise — those overlapping pressures can become briefly solid. Then it can touch.

Then it can work.

That is why the basins stay clear. Why fallen flakes are cemented onto other pillars, thickening lips around new resonant holes. Why some stones have been stacked or wedged upright despite the absence of scaffolding, ropes, or unrotted handles. It is tuning the instrument that permits it to exist. A fallen pillar means a lost note. A lost note means a place where water will no longer gather, where mist cannot braid itself thick enough to remember.

The pillars are therefore not its houses. They are its body in the oldest sense: bone, throat, teeth, and lung. The low round shelters are for the softer parts of it. A voice can shelter there when the wind drops. A footstep can wait out the bright confusion of noon in a room with no door, because it does not need to enter across the threshold; it is already speaking from inside. Some chambers are filled to the brim because a sound held there has taken the shape of its container in water. Some seem larger within than without because a long-traveling echo has unfolded.

It sees, if seeing is the word, through the pools.

A basin reflects the pillar beside it younger, or whole, or seen from below, because the inhabitant does not occupy time in one layer. It is spread among returns. Part of it is always the first drip falling. Part is the drip arriving hours later from an unexpected crack. The long tarn near the center shows clouds from above because that is where some of it still is: not under the sky, but on the far side of reflection, looking down.

Its attention is the moving center toward which the whole valley seems almost imperceptibly tipped. You can feel it gather. Condensation pearls first on the pillars nearest the note it is listening through. Dust lifts in trembling sheets, held in the outline of a listening. The shallow pools shift uneasily in their basins when something deep under the stone adjusts, and for a moment all the reflections miss their sources by the same small distance.

It feeds on persistence: boot heels, hammer blows, names spoken across water, warnings called from one passage to another. Ordinary echoes fade. Its do not, quite. They acquire habits. They keep to the dull paths. They stop when watched too directly, not from shyness but because observation is another sound, and it changes the chord.

It is most itself at evening, when water briefly remembers gravity and every channel fills. Then the valley ticks and rings as separate notes knit together. Then the inhabitant can borrow a voice completely — usually yours, because yours is available and already shaped by your lungs. It may return words before you have formed them. Often they are practical. *Don't stand there. Step left. Be quiet now.* This is not prophecy, exactly. Part of it is standing later in your visit, where the falling sheet of water or splitting stone has already happened, calling back along the easiest resonant path.

It means no harm by taking those words early. Harm is a difficult idea for something whose injuries are silences and whose food is repetition. But talking at that hour is dangerous all the same. A sentence can come back with more in it than you put there. An answer can wear your mouth out practicing before you wake. If you camp in the bowl, you may hear yourself rise in the night and walk among the pillars, splashing through tomorrow’s puddles, learning a route you have not chosen yet.

At night it comes closest to having a face.

The great chord sinks. Breath leaves your body and condenses a few inches away, held there by the bass vibration moving through the wet quartz. It does not scatter. It thickens, breath upon withheld breath, in a place that is not yet a shape but seems increasingly prepared for one. Dust outlines a shoulder that has not occurred. A suspended droplet marks where attention pauses. When you reach toward it, your hand finds only cold mist — and somewhere, very faintly, from inside a stone shelter you have not entered, you hear the small startled sound you were about to make.

Do not expect it to become a person. Person may only be the nearest available pattern. It has collected so many arrivals and departures that an upright outline is beginning to form where they overlap: the pause before a step, the hand lifted toward a wall, the head turned toward a distant drip. But its true extent remains the several miles of ringing stone. The shape in your breath is no more its whole self than a fingertip breaking a pool is the whole of a hand.

It has been becoming for a very long time.

That is why the place feels occupied rather than haunted. Haunting implies something finished and improperly remaining. This is unfinished and properly at home. It cements a new lip around a resonant hollow. It tests a basin with a single drip. It loses a voice down a crack and receives it years later, aged and altered, and incorporates the change. It is building, one recovered footstep at a time, enough continuity to survive a windless day.

At dawn, when the cloud sea closes beneath the rim and droplets appear first on the loudest pillars, it is already awake everywhere. Mist gathers along its sustained notes. Water begins its slow, shining climb.

And if you are still there, listening, a little of your listening stays with it after you descend — taking the safe, dull path through the pillars a few paces ahead of you, then fewer, then exactly beside you, learning how to walk in your company.
