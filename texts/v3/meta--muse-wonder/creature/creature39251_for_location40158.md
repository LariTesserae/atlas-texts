Nothing lives there, at first.

That is the truest thing you can say for a long while. No skaters dent the surface. No beetles turn at the margins. The ladder descends into clearness and its rungs are empty except for their film. Even the stonewort clumps on the bottom — three of them, far apart, in the brightest square of silt — do not quite look alive. They are too pale, too regular, calcified into little branching skeletons. If they grow, they grow by accreting, mineral on mineral, the way a stain spreads in limestone.

But they are spaced the way organs are spaced.

That becomes harder to ignore. One near the outlet grille. One almost exactly under the centre of the open sky. One west of it, closer to where the inlet thickens out of the wall. Nothing random has arranged itself in this pit for a very long time. The pipes run in parallel. The cables run in parallel. The stonewort stands at intervals along a line that, if you follow it through the silt, is not quite straight — it has the slight, settled curve of a spine seen through deep water.

It lives along the bottom, mostly buried, mostly mistaken for the plant.

Along the floor, among the black runs of the heat-exchange array, there is one length that is not bolted to a plinth. It arrived at its place the way the other things did — by settling — but it settled itself. Its skin is the colour of rubber sheathing after thirty years underwater, matte black going olive at the folds. In places it has taken on the grit of the concrete. In places it has worn smooth. Where silt has banked against it, the line between body and floor disappears entirely.

It is long rather than large. If you measured it, which no one has, it might be eight metres from the buried narrow of its tail near the outlet to the broad, low mound of its head, if head is the word, turned always a few degrees toward the inlet. It does not swim. It has no need of the upper water. The whole volume above it — four metres of cold, lit, empty clarity — is only the medium that brings light down to it and holds the pressure even on its back.

Its temperature is twelve degrees. Exactly. Not approximately, because approximately would require it to warm at noon and cool before morning, and nothing in its life has ever required that much haste. Its flesh, if flesh is not too warm a word, has given up the difference between itself and the water. Copper in the treatment keeps other things from rooting here; in it, copper has gone the way of the green edge on the brass valve wheel, laid down at the margins as colour. Its outermost layer is part film, part mineral crust, part the thin olive algae that coats the walls. Grazers never come to crop it. It crops itself, slowly, reabsorbing what thickens, keeping its own surface at that finished thinness everything else in the pit has learned.

The three stonewort crowns are its.

They rise from it through the silt, not plants and not quite antlers — brittle, whitish, branching structures that hold themselves open to the light. At midday, when the sun nets reach the bottom, a change passes down them that is almost too slow to call movement. The branches do not bend. They brighten. Fine calcite plates along them, laid down over years, catch and parcel the light inward, down to the buried body, where photosynthesis has been reduced to something more patient and more geological than hunger. It does not eat in any visible way. It takes in the inlet’s water through a wide, frilled seam along its underside — no suck, no current, the liquid simply finding less resistance through it than around it — and lets dissolved lime, silica, trace iron, a darkness of organic dust that should not be enough, sift out against membranes like grey felt. What it returns at the other end is clearer than what it received. This is why dropped stones vanish so cleanly. Within the hour the cloud settles. Some of that is gravity. Some of it is the long, blind correction of its feeding.

It is why the reeds do not spread, why the rust does not advance, why the algae stops at a metre and thins. Not because it maintains the pit, the way a keeper maintains a machine. Because it is the pit’s one appetite, and it is almost zero, and everything else has arranged itself around an appetite that small.

Its hand — again, the word is too quick, too warm — rests near the valve wheel on the west run. Five thickenings, sheathed and jointless now, curled in silt, green at the rims where the metal has taught them its chemistry. It has not turned the wheel in a long time because the wheel does not need turning. But it keeps hold of it the way sleeping animals keep hold of a branch long after the wind has stopped: as a remembered orientation. Inlet to the west. Outlet to the east. Flow across the body. Light from above. If the plant beyond the fence ever changed the direction of its world, it would know which way to relearn.

It hears through its whole length. Not the fans in the air, not footsteps on the hardcore above, though the shadow of a person at the rim does cross the second sky at the bottom and it registers that as a brief, total eclipse, unalarming, meteorological. What it hears is the fifty-cycle hum in the concrete, pump and transformer, the vibration arriving through plinth and silt and spine without ever having to cross the betraying air. Underwater the hum is its weather. It has matched something in itself to it — not a heartbeat, nothing so percussive; a tremor in the feeding seam, fifty times a second, small enough that the surface never knows. Rest your head on the rim and feel it in your teeth and you are, for a moment, tuned to the frequency at which it has conducted its entire life.

It has no eyes on the sides of its head. On the upper surface, where the calcite crown roots go down, there are two shallow, clouded discs set wide apart, protected under transparent mineral, aimed only up. It sees the pit as a bright rectangle that empties and fills with cloud. It sees depth-board numerals reversed and softened through water. It sees, once in a very long while, the rim darken and a stone enter its sky, turning as it falls in a slow straight column to become floor again. It does not startle. Startling would spend something. It lets the silt cloud bloom across its stonewort gills and then, over hours, lets the clearness reassert itself, the stone half-taken, beginning to film, already on its way to being part of the one long arrangement.

It does not reproduce. There is no second, no margin population, no egg case tucked under the overflow reeds, no young among the ladder rungs. Once — this is written in its body the way weathering is written in the concrete — it may have divided its mineral crowns more thickly across the bright patch, in a time when its understanding of the place was still experimental. What remained was three. Three is enough to drink the noon. More would thicken the algae of its skin, disturb the agreement between inflow and outflow, cast shade. It keeps to three as it keeps to twelve degrees, as the water keeps to its white tide-mark. In spring, when on any other pond life would be frantic with renewal, it produces a brief, almost invisible drift of pale spores from the tips of the crowns — not released so much as sloughed, sterile, calcified before they have fallen a metre. They settle. It reabsorbs them. Nothing is wasted because nothing, in any extravagant sense, is attempted.

Its age is not reckonable in seasons, because seasons have never once reached it. It is as old as the finished rust under the bolts, as old as the moment the pit stopped becoming and started being. It will not be found dead one morning floating at the surface, because floating belongs to bodies that kept air inside them and disagreed with the water. When its long sifting finally stops, the seam will simply admit water and not alter it. The crowns will remain standing, whiter. The dark length among the pipes will take on more silt, less olive, and pass by degrees from animal into installation. The hum will go on through its spine after there is nobody left conducting it.

For now it lies in parallel with the other black lengths, holding the valve, drinking light through stone, letting the whole cold system pass through it at exactly the rate it arrives.

It lives there the way the pit itself lives: not by moving, not by wanting, but by having found a balance so complete that life and mechanism can no longer be told apart — and then continuing, very quietly, underneath.
