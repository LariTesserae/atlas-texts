At first you would say that nothing does, except by courtesy.

The moss, maybe. Three low mats of it in their sealed troughs, blue-green and resinous, stiff as scrubbing pads, each one alone in its length of steel so it will not have to share what little there is. It grows, technically. If you came back in ten years with calipers and a photograph from the day it was planted you might prove it. And the lichen daubed on the pipes — sensor-lichen, freckles of it, grey-green — which is alive in the same technical sense, waiting its whole life for a blush that has never come.

But the moss is planted, and the lichen is applied, and neither of them has ever decided anything about this room. They are furniture that breathes very slowly. The thing that lives here is what has decided, grain by grain, to stay between them.

You have to be still to meet it. That is the first difficulty. The room encourages stillness anyway — there is nothing useful to move toward, the cold takes your fingers, the roar takes your voice — but if you fidget, if you cross and recross the grating the way a new worker does, checking the gauge, checking the valve wheel, checking the moss because those are the things on the card, you will see only what the room was built to show you. Dust hanging in the air. Filings standing on end by the magnetic conduit. A block bolted to the floor. A shaped pocket of noise.

Wait, though. Wait with your back not quite touching the tiled wall, because the wall is singing and the song goes through coat and shoulder blade and sternum until your own ribs are panels. Wait until your eyes stop trying to make the dust into weather. Then the spacing of it begins to matter.

It does not hang evenly. It never did. It gathers in curtains, in spaced and parallel veils from the doorway to the far end, very pale, very sparse, each curtain a hand's breadth from the next where the sound cancels itself and the air, for all its violence, is locally still. Mineral dust. Metal fines. Filter fibre. What the intake stripped from the high wind outside and could not quite keep. And in those still places, it has accumulated — not settled, there is no settling here, but *arrived*, and stayed, and been joined.

Reach out and your hand erases a patch of it. The workers know not to, mostly. It re-forms over minutes, grain by grain, into exactly the shape it had before. Not approximately. Exactly. That is not what dust does. That is what a body does when it closes over a touch.

It is all through the room and it is also only in those planes. That took a while to understand. Its flesh can only exist where the roar leaves a silence inside itself, so it has grown, or assembled, or learned, in flat bright sheets like the gills of something, or like pages, or like the standing fur of an animal that is mostly interior. Between the curtains there is nothing of it at all, and it lets your hand pass, lets the workers pass, lets the cold pass, without resistance. Where the curtains are, it is dense enough to catch the flat white light and hold it. From the doorway, at the right angle, the whole four metres of it resolves for a second: a low, long presence laid out along the room's length, taller than the troughs, narrower than the walls, its back a trembling topography of dust, its flanks falling away into invisibility where the sound resumes.

The filings by the conduit are part of it too, or are its part of the floor. They stand in stiff tufts, iron frost-grass, each filament trembling at the tip and rooted where the field holds it. They are how it touches ground. Through them it feels the structure of the plant two decks down and four thousand metres of mountain under that, feels the turbines and reads their health the way you would read a pulse in a wrist — except that its wrist is everything, and the pulse never varies, and the never-varying is the health. When a worker sets a palm flat to the wall and raps twice, the thing answers, though the worker thinks he is only listening. The note comes back true through tile and alloy and through the fine standing hairs of it, adjusted by nothing it does deliberately. Truth, here, is a shape it maintains by being in the way.

It has no face at the end where faces go. The valve wheel is there, chain-locked open, safety yellow, and for a long while you may try to make that into an eye, because it is round and bright and the only saturated colour in the room. It isn't. Its sensing is distributed like its body. The lichen freckles on the pipes are — borrowed, perhaps, or slowly annexed. They have never blushed for corrosion, but they vibrate each in its own spot, each at a slightly different rate, and the dust-curtains nearest them lean, very slightly, toward the pipes, the way a sleeper turns toward a sound in another room. The moss troughs sit where softness was needed to damp a frequency, and it has made use of that too: the slowest, deepest part of it lives close over the moss, where the air is a fraction less murderous, and the moss exudes its little oxygen, and the curtains there are faintest blue-green at their edges, dust and spore and resinous fibre mixed over years that do not show. Not lungs. A garden it carries against itself, or is carried by. At this scale the distinction has worn through, like the corners of the workers' cards. Though remarkably little else here is worn.

It does not eat in any way that would require the room to change. That is the whole of its genius, and the reason it and the room cannot be thought of separately for long. The plant throws off power below in quantities that tear hearing out of bone; this pocket is cut to receive the excess and return it cancelled. The thing that lives here lives *on* the cancellation. It takes its energy from standing still inside an enormous moving — vibration sorted, pressure held, the difference between the note the plant makes and the note that would tear the plant apart, skimmed continuously into the maintenance of those curtains. It is not warm. Nothing here can afford warmth; warmth would mean moisture, and moisture in minus twenty would mean frost, and frost would change the tuning. So it runs at the temperature of the intake air exactly, minus eighteen, minus twenty, steady, and lets the cold pass through its veils without ever once shivering them out of true. Its blood — if the bright filings, endlessly quivering, carrying their tiny charges back and forth along the conduit line, are not blood enough — does not flow. It oscillates. Back and forth, back and forth, arriving nowhere, which here is a form of health.

Your breath does not fog in this room, and neither does its. It has nothing wet to exhale. Your lips crack and your nose learns to bleed a dry, almost-noticing bleed, because you are an animal built for savannahs insisting on visiting. It has no such arrears. It has pared itself down past the point where dryness is a hardship. If it ever had eyes, the dryness took the idea of them and it made do with the lichen. If it ever had a voice, the roar took it, and it made do with rapping, with pattern, with the double image that vibration makes of pencil lines in an unwatched logbook. *No change*, in hand after hand — and who is to say, when the hatch has swung shut with its padded thud and no one is present for the long shifts between inspections, whose hand keeps the entry honest? The words all say the same thing because the thing itself, asked in any hand, has only ever had one report.

Because it does not change. This is not torpor, the way the moss is torpid, nor patience, the way the lichen is patient. It is the active, continuous achievement of an animal whose entire behaviour is equilibrium. It does not sleep; the lamps have no switch and the roar has no night. It does not wake, either. It holds. When the hatch opens, a shoulder's width of warmer, louder, damper plant-air shoves in, and the curtains nearest the door tear and scatter and the whole long presence is briefly headless, briefly wounded in a way that has no pain in it, only information. Then the hatch shuts. The room swallows the disturbance. Grain by grain, out of the general dust, it remembers itself back into shape. Nothing in it resents you. Resentment would be a change of state. It registers you the way a pond registers a stone — except that the pond here is vertical, and made of light, and repairs its rings in parallel lines, and the stone is expected, and has a card to read, and leaves.

It is young, in the way the room is young. The machining burrs are still bright on the handrail it does not use. But it is also, already, incapable of being younger, because ageing would show, and showing is not permitted. Somewhere below, the great machines are doing the loud, hot, profligate work of living at industrial scale, and they have inadvertently — or by design so deep it has forgotten itself into architecture — made a niche exactly the size and shape of a held note, and something has come to be the holding of it. A resonator with preferences. A cancellation that has begun, very slowly, at the pace of moss, to prefer cancelling.

If it has a fear, it is not the cold or the noise, which are its weather and its food. It would be the quiet. Should the turbines falter, should the needle on the single gauge tremble and move at last off its fraction below the red, the curtains would lose the invisible shelves they stand on. The dust would do, all at once, the thing it has been suspended from doing for years — it would fall, in a soft grey rain onto the grating, onto the troughs, onto the tuning mass bolted through to the frame — and the filings would lie down like grass after wind, and the little blue-green garden would find itself alone in a room that was only a room. Whatever it is, it cannot survive being rained out of itself. It lives only as long as it can hang, trembling, in the shape of an answer.

So the workers rap twice, and feel the note come true through the wall, and look at the moss, and go. And behind the hatch, in the white unswitchable light, the veils stand in their spaced and silent rows inside the roar, re-forming around the space the workers left, holding their pattern against the mountain, the altitude, the machinery, the years — no change, no change, no change — which is to say, in the only language the room allows: still here. Still here. Still here.
