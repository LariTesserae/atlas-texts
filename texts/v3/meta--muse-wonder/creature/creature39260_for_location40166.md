You won't meet them at first.

You'll hear the fans, and the turbines under the fans, and the water under everything, and you'll think the bowl is empty of anything that could choose to be there. Then you'll notice the signs, laid over the machinery the way the moss is laid over the rock.

A mug hooked by its handle on the blue tap, rinsed, always left upside down to drain. A pair of boots under the tool bench with the soles cut to ribbons by the young basalt and newly stitched, again. The grating swept clean in a place where everything else is dripping. The fan guards, which would choke with green in a week, scraped bright that morning.

Someone keeps this from closing over.

They come out of the steam by the powerhouse so gradually you can't say when you first saw them. Everyone up here learns that way of moving: not emerging, condensing. A shoulder beside a sweating tank. A hand, first, on the mesh. Then a figure, low and dense and unhurried, built on the same principle as the towers — this place resists tall things.

They are not tall. They have made themselves compact against the wind through the notch and the thin air. Broad through the chest from years of taking shallow shares of breath and making do with them. They walk at filter-speed, at cloud-speed, each step placed on the steel grating with a care that looks like slowness until you try to follow off it and feel the moss give, silk over teeth, over hollow rock and hot pipe. Their pace is the pace the fans turn. Once, twice per breath. Nothing here can be hurried without breaking, and least of all them.

Up close they shout at you, not in anger — in habit. Even in the one clear hour at midday when the sun burns the bowl white and the basalt ticks as it expands, they stand too close and put their voice right against your ear and touch your elbow to turn you toward whatever they are showing you. The roar has moved into their ribs permanently. Silence, when they went down the cable last — if they go down — must feel like a malfunction.

Their hands are what the mountain does to hands. The black rock is still learning to be rock, sixty years out of the earth, and it splits pale inside like bone. It has written itself all over them in fine white seams that never quite tan, across knuckles and palm and the pads of the fingers. Under the nails, oil that no washing removes. Between the fingers, a faint green stain, copper-green, algae-green, the same colour that blooms in the settling tank and paints the joints of the pipes. They scrape it off the fan guards every day with a flat blade, they lift fern fronds out of the walkway and tuck them back, they clear the weeping mesh with the side of a fist, and it takes its payment in them. They are always a little wet. Their cuffs are wet. Their hair, cut short against the damp, dries only in that midday hour and is wet again by the exhalation of the evening.

They drink only from the blue tap and they will stop you, without apology, from drinking anywhere else. This is not superstition, it's plumbing become morality. Every other water in the bowl has been through something — mesh, copper, engine jacket, tank, the warm beds under the plants — and carries the station in it. The blue tap is the one clean thing, the finished thing, and it tastes of stone and almost nothing. They drink it the way other people check a pulse. A mouthful in the morning when the cloud-sea rises and the drip-rate doubles. A mouthful at night. If it changed, they would know before the gauges did.

The fig knows them. It has no business at this altitude, a low-valley thing someone carried up in a sack of soil decades ago, and it lives where they have taught it to live: flat against the powerhouse wall, exactly over the exhaust, leaves huge and perpetually dripping, each leaf ticking onto the one below. It would blacken in twenty minutes of still, cold air if the engines stopped. So would the mint that has gone feral through the beds, and the broad groundcover, and the deepest layers of the moss. They all live on waste heat and vigilance, and the keeper's real work is not the water that goes down the eastern pipe to the farms under the cloud. It is keeping the waste going. Keeping the circulation pump pushing warm water under the beds. Keeping the diesel at its idle shudder. Not turning anything off, ever, not even to hear themselves think.

At night, in the hut — stone at the bottom, iron at the top, bunks built for two — they sleep alone in the lower bunk with one hand sometimes on the floor. Not for balance. For information. The generator settles after dark to a heartbeat that comes up through the grating, through the stone, through the bedframe. The fans slow. The fig ticks. If a valve changes its note, if a pump begins to labour, if the drip through the cistern falters under the floor, they are awake before they know why. The mountain has a pulse of its own, deep and irregular under everything, and the station has learned a second, iron one on top of it, and the keeper sleeps between the two and keeps them in time.

The choughs accept them in the way wild things accept weather. The pair that nests in the rim-teeth screams over the bowl in the last clear minutes and does not alter its line for them. The moths that find the powerhouse lamps batter past their face at night, impossibly far from any tree, and they will stand in the lit doorway and let them batter. A bee, once in a while, blown up on the same slow wind that feeds the condensers, panics around the fig. They cup it, very gently for those cut hands, and put it back into the leaves where it is warm, knowing it will die there anyway, but slower.

In the store by the oil drums there is food brought up on the cable, tins and tea and hard bread, and there is less of it than there should be if they were careful, because they feed the bowl first: oil before dinner, a filter changed before a meal, a leaking joint packed in the rain that isn't rain, only cloud being torn apart over their head. Rotations come up the service path walking beside the cable, stay their weeks, learn the rules until the rules accumulate in them — stay on the grating, go slowly, drink from the blue tap — and go down again. The keeper stays up through the handovers. Different voices shout the readings over the roar; the figure that emerges from the steam keeps being, in all the ways that matter, the same one.

Because that is what lives here, finally. Not a hermit and not quite a crew. A function that has grown a body around itself. The soft part of the machine. The part that scrapes and listens and tastes the water, the part that can feel a change in pressure in its teeth and get up in the dark. Down below the cloud there are farms drinking what this place squeezes out of passing weather; up above the rim there is nothing but thin blue air falling away for thousands of metres. In between, in a green bowl forty paces wide that should be bare rock, something warm-blooded moves slowly among ferns and iron, smelling of hot oil and wet earth, keeping a fig alive through the night by never, ever letting the engines stop.
