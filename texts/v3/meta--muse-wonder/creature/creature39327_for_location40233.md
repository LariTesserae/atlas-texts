Nothing that lives there lives the way a town lives. There is no one who stays put, no one who owns a room, no one who can rest in the open. The inhabitants are all tenants of the current, and they survive by knowing, to the second, where the water is about to go.

The first you notice are the char.

Hundreds of them, then, at the center, thousands. Silver-flanked, pale-spotted, cold-water fish, long as a forearm, moving as if they were one animal poured down a boulevard. They do not fight the cross-currents that stagger a swimmer. They tilt into them. A flick of the school and a wall-current that would have taken you left takes them, faster, around a corner, down a flume-street, into a basin where they hold for a while in a loose, revolving cloud. Another flick and they are gone entirely — not scattered, gone — vanished into the walls.

The walls are bored for them. Rows and rows of holes, deliberately sized, at different heights: low holes for when a district is draining, high holes for when it is filling, holes just large enough for a char's body and too small for the main force of a flush to follow. Inside, in the absolute stillness the builders left behind the stone, the fish hang head-to-current and wait. You can see their eyes in there, in the dark of the refuges, a line of faint, patient glints. The youngest live deepest in the holes. They never come out except in the twenty seconds between one siphon letting go and the next catching, when the whole valley holds its breath, and then they dart out only far enough to snatch a midge, a shred of sponge, a grain of something that has fallen from the sky, before the pull resumes.

That falling is their whole food-web. Nothing green grows here — no weed, no algae slick, nothing rooted. Everything that lives eats weather. Pollen dusting the lake in a spring gust. Carrion flies and drowned moths. A feather turning slowly down through grey-green water. Snowmelt cloudy with grit and crushed insects. It rains through the surface and gets sorted, terrace by terrace, by mouths.

The mussels do the first sorting. They have cemented themselves blackly in the one place a mussel can live in this: the exact inner angle of every step, every sill, every stair-tread where the rush, for perhaps an inch, falters. Half-open, filtering, thousands of them making the water incrementally clearer than the mountain left it. Their shells are eroded like everything else, thinned at the hinge, river-rounded, grown into the stone so that shell and step are hard to tell apart. When a flush comes they shut in unison — not a sound, exactly, but a soft, distributed cessation, as if a district had closed its eyes.

In the gutters and the shallow runnels between flags are the loaches, mottled the precise colour of wet grit, flattening themselves into seams that do not look wide enough for a body. They live where the water is fastest and thinnest, lashing from one seam to the next, grazing the invisible film off the stone. With them, invisible unless the sediment lifts, are smaller things still — larvae, shrimps like glass, worms in the silt that collects for an hour in a corner and is then torn away. They are the reason the stone is not slimed shut. They are eaten almost as fast as they grow.

The crayfish come out at what passes, down there, for midday. They back slowly across the open plazas, absurdly exposed on the polished stone, claws raised not in threat but into the flow, to taste it, to feel the first tremor of a change in level. Their shells are pale, almost translucent at the joints, from the cold and the dark and the lime in the water. They are scavengers and janitors: a dead fish in a tank-corner, a sodden bird washed in over the spillway, the soft tissues nothing else can reach. When the basin they are crossing begins to empty, they know before the current shows it. They stop. They lower themselves. They spread their legs and make themselves into part of the floor, and the water, when it comes hard and suddenly sideways, breaks over their backs like over any other cobble.

Deeper down live the ones you see least and need most. Eels occupy the drains.

They fill the U-bends, the throat-holes, the low mouths under the domes where the system exhales into darkness and to somewhere below the mountain. Thick as wrists, dark, unhurried, braided together in groups in the very places that would silt and choke first if they were empty. When a siphon changes somewhere above them they come out — not fleeing, patrolling — moving against the new current with a slow, muscular ease nothing else in the lake can match, nosing into grilles of stone, worrying out compacted leaves-that-are-not-leaves, sponge, grit, a dead char wedged in a bend. Then, one by one, they slide back into the drains. Without them, in a generation, the machine would strangle. With them, whether they intend it or not, it does not.

And in the lee of blocks, in sheltered corners where even a flush arrives as a mere persuasion, live the things that are barely animals at all. Freshwater sponges the size of fists, mustard-grey, porous, attached like soft stone to stone, pulsing almost imperceptibly — drawing the water in, letting it out cleaner. White bacterial mats spread in the lee of a column or a statue's broken shoulder, a mineral felt that thickens on carrion and rust and the faint iron seep where a fitting vanished centuries ago. These are the true permanent residents. A char lives years. An eel, decades. A mat simply continues, eaten back by loaches, regrowing in the calm after a flush, white on white stone, the only colour that can afford to stay.

Above them all, cutting down through their world and refusing to belong to it, are the otters.

They come from the bright surface, from the real air above the rim, in a burst of bubbles and speed. They turn once, insolently graceful, in a doorway full of churn, and disappear into a roof-space, a cistern dome, a valve-house where air has been trapped since the lake first filled — old, cold air smelling of wet lime, renewed a sip at a time with every change of level. That is where they rest, where the fish cannot follow and the current cannot reach, and where you will find them watching you with their heads low and their claim absolute. They eat char. They crack mussels on the steps the mussels are cemented to. They leave bones and shells in the air pockets in neat, accumulated middens. They are visitors and predators and, in the end, the closest thing the place has to people: warm, breathing, coming and going on a schedule of their own, using rooms they did not build for purposes the builders almost — almost — foresaw.

For nothing here was left to accident, not even them. The refuge holes were made for fish before there were fish in them. The fish-ladders' treads are pools a crayfish can brace in. The drains are eel-sized. The domes hold air whether or not any otter ever finds them. The builders stocked their waterworks with its own keepers and then withdrew, and the keepers took over the maintenance without ever understanding it: mussels straining, sponges sipping, loaches grazing, eels clearing the throats, char stirring oxygen down from basin to basin with their own bodies, crayfish removing the dead before the dead can rot and foul a sill.

At the spiral basin at the center they all, in their separate ways, pay tribute. The char circle it by the thousand at a precise distance, in the churn where the oxygen is richest, neither swept into the throat below nor willing to leave. The eels wait in the rim-drains for what the spiral concentrates. The otters dive through the ring of fish, scatter it for a silver instant, and take one. Even the sponges downstream of the spiral are larger, fed by the finest, most thoroughly ground rain of food in the lake.

They have no names for the districts. They have timing instead, written into behaviour the way the bands on the statues were once written in stone: flatten now, hide now, feed now, move — now, before the street reverses. Eleven minutes, perhaps. Perhaps nine. A sill spills somewhere out of sight, a bend catches, the vortex in the plaza doubles and sheds children down the side alleys, and every living thing in the valley adjusts at once without consultation.

Then, for twenty seconds, it stops. Grit settles. Mussels open. Char hang motionless in doorways, gills barely moving. An otter's whiskered face appears in a bubble of trapped air and looks out, upside down, at an upside-down world gone suddenly legible and still.

Then the water remembers what it was built to do, and so do they.
