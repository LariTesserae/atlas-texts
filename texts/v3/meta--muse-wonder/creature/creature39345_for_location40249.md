If you stand on the rim long enough, you stop seeing them as separate from the work.

From above, at midday, they are movement between roofs: a back bent under a basket of pale rubble, moving up the terraces by the long zigzag ramp; a head in a felt cap ducking under a lintel; two children crossing a lane sideways, pressed flat to a wall while a hand-cart feels its way past; a woman shaking out a blanket that will never properly dry and hanging it again across the air between two houses. None of it hurries. All of it is continuous.

Close down in the streets, among them, they are unmistakably a pit people — not by birth, not yet, but already by habit of body and mind.

They are the builders of the place and its inhabitants at once, and there is no clean hour when one stops and the other begins. A man spends his morning at the new face at the south end, swinging a pick in the short, careful *tok, tok* that will not shatter the bronze, and in the afternoon he is re-hanging his own door because its green frame has twisted again in the damp. A woman who has trodden mortar barefoot in a pit at dawn — feet grey-white with clay, skirt kilted up, calves strong as rope — will spend the evening sweeping her allotted stretch of drain channel with a twig broom, stooping not because the task asks it but because all their doorways have taught them to stoop. Children are not idle ornaments to this. They are the town's quick circulation. They run cups of water to the mortar mixers, run word of a blocked gutter three terraces down, run barefoot up to the rim to watch for the party coming in across the plain with thatch or salt or timber, and come pelting down again before the load itself is lowered. They thread between adults without being told to move. They know, by the time they can carry a half-basket, which roof drips where, who is cooking at which hour, and never to leave a stone in a water channel.

Physically they are being shaped by coolness and carrying.

They are paler than the people of the plain above — not sickly, but shade-kept. The sun reaches them late and leaves them early, and the cold air that pools in the bowl keeps their hands cold long into a working day. Their fingers are quick despite it, accustomed to tying lashings, picking grit from clay, sorting beans, re-wrapping tools in oiled cloth so the damp will not get at an edge. Nearly everyone over the age of twelve has thickened wrists and a particular callus at the base of the thumb from pick and mallet, and broader, harder shoulders from baskets. They carry loads up, always up, on their backs, foreheads sometimes strapped, knees taking the ramp in a short, patient rhythm they can keep for hours. Their feet are tough and often bare indoors and in the mortar pits, the nails cracked, the skin between the toes softened by perpetual wet. In winter there are chilblains. In summer there are still chilblains, in the lowest houses by the cistern.

They dress for damp rather than for cold in any simple way: felted wool that sheds a shower and can be worn clammy without misery, undyed cloaks that smell permanently of woodsmoke and stone, felt caps pulled low, woven shirts under. Nothing crisp, nothing bright that will hold its colour long in that air. Cloth comes off the loom grey, brown, the off-white of sheep, and stays in that register, darkened at hems by lane-mud. Bone needles mend it. They mend it a great deal. Almost nothing they own came easily down the ramp, and it shows in the careful, unembarrassed patching of everything — baskets, roofs, sleeves, the wooden buckets of the water-wheel.

What strikes a stranger most, after the stoop, is the quiet.

It is not timidity. It is acoustics learned in the body. A raised voice in those narrow cuts comes straight off the opposite wall and into a neighbour's window, and there is no living here that is not within a few feet of another family's sleeping, cooking, quarrelling, child-bed. So they speak low and close, face turned a little aside, and they have become masters of the whole vocabulary that replaces shouting: a tap on a wall that means *your gutter is spilling*, two taps that mean *cart coming*, a wooden clapper at the rim at nightfall that settles over the bowl like a hand. They argue quietly, which does not mean gently — a dispute about digging sideways under a shared wall can run for days in murmurs at doorways, relentless as seep-water — but they settle such things, because they must. The first laws are not written anywhere: *do not block water. Do not cut under your neighbour without telling him. Burn when it is your street's turn.* A man who let his drain silt up would not just flood his own storage pit; he would flood the house below him, and the house below that, all the way to the cistern. In a place dug out of one piece of rock, selfishness has a literal downhill.

So they know each other with an intimacy that would be oppressive anywhere less interdependent and is here simply the texture of life. They know whose baby wakes before dawn, whose roof leaks onto whose bean pole, whose grandfather's wrists have given out at the face and must be given the lighter work of sorting flint or minding the water-wheel, squeaking and pouring at the lowest point. There are few old people, and none yet who were born here — this is still a first generation. The elders are founders in their fifties and sixties, people who remember living up in the wind on the plain and chose, or were driven, to go down into shelter and stone. They are the keepers of the unwritten rota: which streets fire in the morning and which at evening so the smoke does not pool and choke the bowl, who takes which stretch of channel after a sudden shower, how far a new terrace may cut before the bedding plane must be tested for frost-splitting. Their authority is not loud either. It rests in being consulted at a wall, in a doorway, while work goes on around the conversation.

Their houses are small because every beam was lowered on ropes and every basket of spoil was carried up by someone, and their lives conform to that scale. Families crowd multi-generationally into rooms that lean their backs into the rock — grandparents nearest the hearth cut deep into the back wall, children sleeping above the storage pit under the floorboards where the onions and grain keep cool and must be bailed dry after heavy condensation. There is no room to store anything sideways, so they store downward and upward: baskets hung from roof beams, hens in wicker pens on the roofs, herbs in table-sized courtyard plots behind houses, a single bean pole tied to an eave. Meals are what can be carried in and kept — porridge, leeks, onions, bitter greens that tolerate shade, dried things from the plain softened in cistern water. They eat staggered, by street and by fire, and the smell of the town tells the hour better than a bell: wet clay in the early morning, onion and porridge as the upper streets cook, green wood at dusk when the lower streets get their turn.

For all the density there is surprisingly little display, little surplus, little privacy in which to cultivate either. What wealth exists is kept wrapped: a bronze pick, inherited and guarded and lent reluctantly, its edge treated like a member of the family; a good rope; dry timber seasoning slowly under a lean-to; a store pit that does not leak. These things are known about, too, of course. Everyone knows who has what. Envy, like noise, has to be kept low to be livable.

And underneath the routine there is a strange, shared forward tilt to them, as if the whole population were leaning slightly toward a future they are literally excavating. No one here lives in a finished house on a finished street. The corners near your home are still sharp; the cut bank behind your neighbour's roof still shows last month's pick marks; the town ends at the south end not in a wall but in a work face that advances inches a day. They measure time that way — by terraces opened, by how far the cistern had to be deepened last year, by a child's height against a doorpost that itself has been re-hung twice. They expect to revise everything. Doors warp, walls sweat, a promising bed of rock splits in the first hard frost and a plan changes. Patience is not a virtue they preach. It is the pace the stone allows.

At night, after the clapper, the bowl goes very dark and very close. Fires sink to embers in the deep hearths. Water goes on dripping from the seep line in the western cut, ticking into channels, finding the cistern. Somewhere a baby is hushed against a shoulder; somewhere a late basket creaks as it is set down for the morning. The swallows are still in the overhang above, the pale lizard still by the cistern stones, and below the rim, packed wall to wall in the cool, the people who dug their town downward sleep in it — stooped even in their doorways by day, soft-voiced even in their disputes, hands cold, shoulders strong, bound to one another by shared walls and shared water and the daily, basket-by-basket agreement that this raw white hollow in the plain will, in time, become a place that was always there.

They are not there yet. They know it. They keep digging.
