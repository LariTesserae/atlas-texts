No one lives in the middle.

That is how you learn who lives here at all. You do not look for a nest, a burrow, a body in the open. You look at the seam where life can bear to be, that narrow, crowded margin between the blinding floor and the blinding wall, and you wait for the light to tilt.

At noon the hollow seems empty. At noon it is lying.

The first residents are in the stone itself. High in the white limestone, just under the rim, the wall is pocked with swift-holes, hundreds of them, dark mouths worn smooth by generations of coming and going. Inside, in cool and narrow darkness, the birds sit pressed wing to wing through the heat. Their feathers are dusted pale from the marl. Their eyes, when they blink into the glare, have a second, milky lid that lowers like a shutter. They do not sing from the holes. They keep the place's quiet.

At dawn and again at dusk they pour out sideways. Not up into clean sky, not yet — *around*. They circle the bowl in a tight, rising gyre, riding the warm column off the salt pan, wings held stiff, bodies turning together as if they were one slow creature with a thousand backs. The sound is not calling. It is a dry rustling, feather on air, the whole ring briefly awake and moving. Then the heat lifts them over the rim and they are gone for the day to hunt the plateau, and their holes are empty mouths again, and anyone who came at midday would swear nothing lived there at all.

Below them, the terraces belong to the lizards.

There are more kinds than you can count at first, because at first they are all the color of concealment: bronze, ash, sand, clay-red, one kind almost white on its belly and black at the throat. They live flattened. Their whole lives are spent pressed as close to the stone as a shadow, bellies to the warmth, toes splayed in the cracks where the wiry grasses root. They have learned the double light of this place — sun from above, sun returned from below — and they meet it with pale undersides and frilled, reflective scales that throw the glare back off their throats. They bask with their eyes narrowed to slits. They rise, quick, and do their stiff push-ups facing the center they will not cross, displaying black throats to one another along the ring, a conversation held entirely at the edge.

If one must get to the other side, it runs the curve. Always the curve. Even when the straight line across the pan is a third the distance, a lizard will travel the long warm arc of the wall, pausing in every crack of shade. The middle is not forbidden. It is simply seen from everywhere.

Smaller still are the ones you only know by their timing.

In the ochre clay band, where the stone sweats at night and droplets creep sideways along the cracks, live frogs no larger than a thumbnail, the exact wet brown of the clay itself. They are not there, and then a heavy-condensation morning makes them there all at once, dozens of them, plump and glistening in pockets a hand's breadth wide, throats pulsing, eyes like dark beads of the water they came from. They eat what the damp wakes. They mate in an inch of seep. By afternoon they have thinned themselves back into the mud and sealed over, slowed almost to stopping, and the pocket is dry salt again with no sign that anything soft ever breathed in it.

They share those pockets with older waiters. In the salt crust at the edge of the pan, invisible, ungerminated, unhatched, lie the eggs of translucent shrimp, dormant through seasons of drought. A real rain wakes them without negotiation. For two, three days, in temporary mirrors of water in the wall, they swim upside down, ghostly, frantic, filtering green algae with feathery limbs, growing and mating and laying the next drought's eggs at a speed that looks like panic. The frogs gorge. Dragonflies appear as if summoned and gorge. Then the water goes, and the shrimp are gone as if they were a rumor the rain made up, leaving only their future behind in the salt.

The algae itself might be the truest resident. Green-black, it sleeps as a film no thicker than breath in the pores of the limestone. Moisture calls it up overnight into a slick, pond-smelling bloom. Bees find it by some signal humans cannot read and come down the wall in a steady low traffic, walking the clay seam head-down to drink, abdomens pulsing, feet dark with damp. Butterflies come to puddle where a seep has stained the salt, orange and white wings opening and closing with terrible slowness, as if each movement had to be considered against the heat. Snails extrude from cracks on wet nights and graze the bloom, leaving silver sentences that dry by morning and flake away.

And wherever the seam is richest, there are intelligences that do not so much live here as return, faithfully, to work.

At first light, some days, a troop of small baboons files down a terrace path worn by their own kind into a pale groove. Their coats have gone dusty-grey from this country; the old ones are almost white along the shoulders from years of marl dust. They come single file, infants clinging, juveniles carrying nothing but their own attention. They do not drink here — there is nothing to drink, in their terms — they come to mine. An old female, scar-cheeked, fingers shortened and thick-nailed from decades of the same labor, will work a black flint nodule out of its seam with patient thumbs, strike it against the limestone — *tak, tak* — and turn the sharp flake in her hand with the economy of a craftsman. With it she scrapes the damp salt-and-clay free, gathers the loosened mineral earth, licks it from her fingers with closed eyes. The young watch the angle of her wrist more than the stone. They copy, drop flakes, shriek softly at their own clumsiness, try again. Around their working place lies a scatter of black crescents from seasons of apprenticeship.

The crow has taught itself a parallel trade. It lives alone in a deep hole on the north wall, an old bird with one splayed foot, and hoards smooth pebbles in a crack the way the baboons leave flakes. On snail mornings it hops from stone to stone selecting a victim, flies its pebble up a short, deliberate height, and drops it. *Tik.* Miss, sometimes. Crack, sometimes. Then it descends and picks the soft animal from the shell with surgical patience. It watches the baboons from a distance that suggests respect. The baboons, in turn, pretend not to watch it back.

None of them stay.

That is the other law. The hollow is not a home so much as a held breath between visits. The swifts return to roost but spend their lives elsewhere in the air; the baboons climb out before the heat can trap them; the bees are gone by the worst of noon. Even the lizards retreat deep into thermal cracks at the peak and exist then only as the occasional flick of a tail in shadow. What remains resident through the full violence of the day is what can become almost not-alive on command: frog in sealed mud, shrimp as egg, algae as stain, seeds of the terrace grasses folded flat and grey in joints of stone, roots spread like veins against the rock, waiting for water that does not fall but condenses, sideways, out of cooling night.

They are all, in their particular shapes, specialists in margin and interval. Narrow feet for narrow ledges. Flat bodies for cracks. Eyes that shutter against light from below. Life cycles compressed into the two damp hours after dawn. None of them needs the center, except as the thing that makes the margin possible — the great white reflector that throws the heat up for the swifts, that ripens the wall, that drives every living thing into the same close company around the edge, predator and prey and pollinator all shelved together in the ring, waiting.

By evening the light loosens. Shadows return to being single. The frogs, if the day gave them moisture, begin to stir underground. The swifts circle home. The lizard throats go pale. Something small ticks in the cooling salt — a shell, a flake, a footstep already being erased.

Stand then at the eastern terrace and you can feel them all around you in a complete circle, hidden and revealed at once, a population arranged like writing around the rim of a bowl. Not a creature of the depths or a creature of the open plain, but a whole company of edge-dwellers — brief, patient, double-lit things, who have learned how to live for a very long time in a place with almost no water, almost no shade, and almost no room, by never wasting themselves on the empty brightness at its heart.
