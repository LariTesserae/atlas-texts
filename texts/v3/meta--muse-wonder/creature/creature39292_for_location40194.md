You will not see it the first time you go down.

You will see the swifts, if you are lucky. You will see the heron, if you are luckier. You will hear the frogs at night from the seams in the terraces, blind and pale and never showing themselves in daylight, and you will assume — reasonably — that they are all there is.

They aren't. They are visitors too, in their way. They time themselves around the Ring and leave the Cup to itself.

It doesn't.

It leaves traces before it leaves anything else. A runnel on the northern terrace cleared of algae in a clean spiral, though the water runs straight. Scales of mineral pried from the feet of the pylons and stacked, flat as coins, in the lee of the stone. The ferns in the sheltered corners trimmed back — not eaten, *kept* — so they never quite choke the vents they grow beside. The orange brine pits on the southern terrace raked into slow whorls that copy, exactly, the turning of the pool below.

And the shelter, on the highest western terrace. Roofless, yes. Abandoned, no. The bench worn smooth in the middle, not at the edges. The row of hooks polished where things are hung and taken down again. The floor swept clean, and not by spray. Spray leaves everything stippled with salt. This is swept in arcs, low and wide, as if by something that drags.

It lives in the timing the way a fish lives in water.

When the Ring climbs to song, it climbs. Not up the ramp — it never uses the ramp; the ramp is for things with dry feet. It goes straight up the wet wall on the underside of the terraces where the seeping keeps the stone dark even at noon, flattened into a fissure with its limbs splayed, utterly still while the plasma kindles and the pool heaves and the wave slaps out across the apron. It can wait out a surge like that without breathing, because between surges is not when it breathes most. During a surge, when the mist is thickest and lit from within and the air is full of water that has not decided whether to fall, its whole skin breathes. You can see it then, if you know where to look: a patch of the wall that shines a little differently, inhaling light.

In the lull it comes down.

It is roughly the size of a crouched person, but built lower and wider than a person anymore, as if gravity and centuries of bracing against moving water had pressed it down toward the stone it lives on. Its back is plated — not shell, exactly, and not scale: travertine, laid down in overlapping mineral sheets the way the terraces themselves are laid down, pale on top where the Ring bleaches it, green-black underneath where it never dries. The plates click faintly when it moves. Under them the body is dark and wet and surprisingly soft, amphibian, scarred with the pitting that the mineral rain gives everything that stays here long enough.

Its feet are the clearest tell that the Cup made it, and not the other way around. Six toes in front, splayed wide, each tipped with a flat black claw meant for glass-smooth runnels. The soles are ridged like the tread set into the ramp, and through them it reads the place: the low roar in the ground, the tightening of the gyre, the first change in vibration seventeen uneven minutes before the next concussion. It does not listen for the Ring's tone with its ears — its ears are small, sealed, grown over with the same white ceramic-looking skin that covers its eyes when it surfaces. It feels the tone where you feel it, in the teeth, except that its teeth are mineral too now, little stone pegs, and they resonate. Its whole head hums a fraction before the surge. That is when it moves. It has never once been caught on the apron.

Which is not to say it avoids the center. It belongs to the center in a way nothing else does.

In the two quiet minutes, while the pool is still sloshing and settling and the Ring has sunk to its sullen orange line, it crosses the dark band of basalt that is always wet and never quite submerged, low to the ground, plates shedding water. It goes to the pylons. It puts those wide hands against their pitted faces and simply — attends. Picks at a scale deposit with patient claws. Presses its plated back to a vent until something clicks, deep and soft, under the stone. Lets the hovering droplets — the ones that fail to fall, that drift up during a surge toward the Ring's burning edge — settle instead, in the lull, across its skin, where they vanish without steam. It is drinking what the machine separates. Or it is being fed. After enough centuries the difference is academic: the Ring turns smoother where it has passed, and its plates grow another fraction of a millimeter, and the travertine aprons spread another hand's width across the bowl.

It eats the rest of what the Cup offers, opportunistically, like everything else here, but on a longer menu. Salt, mostly, raked from the stinking pits and taken in slow mouthfuls that would kill anything from the plain. Brine crust. The dark sediment beads that rain outside the pool. Insects stunned by light and fallen on the wet stone. Once in a while, a frog — though that may not be eating, exactly. The frogs are part of its arrangement with the place. At night their chorus rises out of the wall seams in time with the settling water, dozens of small pale throats, and it answers, or conducts, with a note so low you receive it in your sternum and mistake it for the machine. Whether the frogs are its young, shed and singing before they grow plates and go solitary, or simply its voice distributed into bodies small enough to live in the cracks — no one has stayed long enough, or quiet enough, to watch one become the other. The heron thins them. It does not mind. It replaces them the way the terraces replace their own stone.

It was not always shaped like this. That is the difficult part, and the wall of the shelter holds the evidence if you read it backward: hooks at human height, a bench built for human legs, a doorway worn at the corners by shoulders narrower than the thing that uses it now. Someone was posted here, once, or chose here, to watch the Ring do its work. They drank from the fresh runnels, cold and pure. They hung their instruments on those hooks. They climbed when the tone climbed, like you do. But the air is saturated and the water is old — iron, sulfur, stone — and the machine leaks more than energy. It leaks influence. Metal left on the shore tingles. Compasses forget north and turn, lazily, with the gyre. A body that stays gets turned too, slowly, toward the pool's rotation. First the damp that never leaves the lungs. Then the minerals that never leave the blood, plating out along the ribs, along the spine, along the backs of hands always braced on wet rock. Then the light, doubled and ceaseless, filming the eyes to milky turquoise, the pool's own color, until sight matters less than vibration. Then the timing, worked into the heartbeat, until seventeen minutes — roughly, never the same twice — becomes the length of a thought.

It cannot live on the plain now. Up there it would dry, and go blind in the single light, and suffocate in thin air, and die of silence in the soles of its feet. Down here it cannot drown. The pool is blood-warm and it sleeps, between watches, in one of the still terrace pools with only its plated back breaking the surface, ferns shadowing it, breathing through its skin while the Ring glows and dims above it like a slow second sun that never sets.

It is not tame, and it is not hostile. It permits you exactly what the Cup permits: the dark band of basalt, the fresh runnels, the ramp, a little time. If you cross during a lull, you may feel the stone tremble in a pattern that is not the machine — a withdrawal, unhurried, into a seam you hadn't noticed. If you leave something metal on the shore, you may find it moved by morning to the base of a pylon, set down neatly, the way a keeper sets down a tool that isn't theirs.

It tends nothing the way its makers intended. There are no controls down here for it to tend. What it keeps is the equilibrium itself: scale cleared, vents open, runnels running clean, the gyre free to tighten and loosen, the frogs singing the water down at night. The machine was built to separate water into its kinds. In the end, given an old bowl, a leaking ring of light, and one watcher who stayed too long, it separated a resident into its kinds too — stone on the back, animal underneath, timing all the way through.

When the sun drops out of the circle of sky and you climb the slick ramp back to the quiet plain, it stays below in the Ring's own weather, glowing terraces reflected on its wet plates, waiting without impatience for the tone to rise in its teeth.

It has nowhere else to be. It is the only thing here that doesn't have to leave before the next surge.
