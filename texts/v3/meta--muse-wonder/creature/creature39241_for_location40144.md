The people here are not tall. Nothing here is wasted on reaching.

They are broad through the chest and narrow everywhere else, with long ribs that keep working long after the rest of the body has stopped. Their breathing is the most noticeable thing about them when they are still — slow, deliberate, audible in the thin air at a distance of two or three meters and then not at all. A whole roomful will fall into the same rhythm without thinking about it, as people below fall into step. Children are taught not to run. Not as discipline. As physiology. Running makes the blood sing painfully behind the eyes, makes the voice go thin to nothing, and there is nowhere so urgent, in a city that has produced the same tonnage for centuries, that it needs to be reached at a run.

They move the way the machines move: unhurried, well-balanced, with the minimum of lift. An old press-operator crossing the plain between districts walks with his feet barely clearing the dust, each step placed inside the polished lane, so that after sixty years his personal track is not a scatter of prints but a single shallow groove, deepening. You can tell a person's age, roughly, by how resolved their path has become. The young wander. Their prints meander around the habitat court, double back, trample each other by the vat-sheds and the schoolroom doors. The old have been worn down to lines.

Their skin takes the color of wherever they work and then keeps it, because nothing washes off. It dries, it oils, it accumulates. Smelter crews are black to the elbow and reddish-black at the throat where the heat opens the pores and iron dust goes in permanently, stippling them. Coolant workers have pale, almost translucent hands and a chemical whiteness around the mouth. The vat tenders are yellowish, warm-smelling, their hair — what hair anyone keeps, cut close against the dust — smelling yeasty for years after they have transferred to other work. Everyone else is the grey-brown of the plain itself. They anoint themselves in the morning shift-change with a thin machine oil cut with solvent, worked into elbows, lips, the webs of fingers, anywhere that would crack. It is not vanity. Dry skin splits in air that has never held moisture, and a split does not weep here so much as it powders at the edges and stays open, filling with ash-fine dust, until it is cleaned out by hand.

They have very little sweat to lose. What water the body can spare goes back through loops more efficient than any in the factories, and they are proud of this in the quiet way they are proud of the old machines. A man who sweats visibly outdoors is either ill or newly come up from below, and the newly come up do not last long enough to become a type. The pressure below crushes them pleasantly, apparently; up here, the few who are sent down on freight business come back describing the lower air as drowning, as soup, as a hand over the mouth, and they sleep for a day in the thin familiar warmth while their inner ears stop aching. Almost no one is born below. Almost no one, having been born up here, can be anything but a visitor there.

Their eyes are large, and they use them differently than a person would expect. Pupils that open to their full extent in the amber pool of a court lamp do not fully close again for a long while. They are excellent at seeing that something is there — a wall where the stars stop, a lizard where the warm stone has a different dullness, a figure occluding the quartz gleam in a road-lane three hundred meters off. They are poor at detail at a distance, and they do not trust it. In districts built windowless, lit always downward and always shielded, sight is a short-range sense, a courtesy sense. Faces are for close up.

At any real distance they rely on the other senses the plateau has sharpened. Smell first. A child who cannot yet read can navigate home through unlit streets by the layered pockets: the hot-iron exhalation of the generating hall, the scorched-dust smell around the cable trenches, the specific sourness of their own block's waste reclaimer, the faintly sweet oil their mother uses on her hands. They learn people that way too, before they learn them by face — everyone carries a district on them, and under it a trade, and under that a private smell of skin and diet and the particular loop their water has been through that week. In a place where smells do not disperse, lying about where you have been is almost impossible.

Hearing is for what is underfoot. Voices do not carry in the thin air, and these are soft-voiced people by necessity and then by custom; conversation is a close activity, heads inclined, sometimes a hand resting lightly on the other person's sternum to feel the words as much as hear them in a noisy shed. But sound through bedrock travels faithfully. They know the press by the tremor in a cup, a rail car by a faint ticking in the floor plates an hour before it arrives, a cracked flywheel by a change in the molar-deep hum of the power station that no outsider would swear was there at all. Their most common gesture in a group is a sudden shared stillness, palms down, listening through stone. Their language has a dozen ordinary words for kinds of vibration and only one, borrowed and seldom used, for echo.

They live turned inward, the way their buildings are. A habitat block presents basalt to the plain and life to the court: cooking smells thickening around the communal vats, children being taught their letters on slates of polished obsidian, washing done with grit and oil over basins that are ceremonially never filled, old people sitting with their backs to the warm wall where the lizards sit too, in the same motionlessness, for the same heat. The lizards are not pets, exactly. They are tolerated, fed flakes of the pale crust from the waste channels, occasionally eaten in lean years when the freight lifts are late. Nobody remembers deciding this. Like everything else here, the arrangement has simply persisted long enough to become structure.

Food is paste, mostly, and they make no apology for it and perform no disgust. Protein from the synthesis vats in its several standard textures, mineral salts refined from well-gas and scrapings, fat rendered from vat cultures that gives everything a faint petroleum sweetness no one down below has ever been able to eat without gagging. Meals are measured not for flavor but for water content. To offer a guest the first draw from a freshly charged loop — water that tastes only of clean pipe, not yet of people — is the highest hospitality the plateau affords, and it is drunk slowly, communally, in sips too small to be called drinking, while everyone politely notices nothing about the smell of the person who offered it. Thirst is not a drama here. It is an accounting condition, chronic and perfectly managed, like the altitude.

Because nothing is washed away, their history is under their feet and they grow up literate in it in the most literal sense. School begins with tracks. A child learns the block's past by following it: this dense trample outside the reclaimer door was the winter — there is no winter, they use the word anyway, from old records — when the loop failed and people queued with sealed vessels. This line, doubling and redoubling on itself, is where a woman paced for three nights while the presses were silent for repair, the only silence anyone living remembers. This set goes straight out past the last lamp and does not return and does not stop, and children follow those the farthest, in packs, daring each other, until the dust begins to look untouched between prints and the animal fear of being too far from a smell they know turns them back.

They do not bury their dead where anyone walks. A funeral is one of the few processions the city makes outdoors in company: the body dried first in the hot rooms above the furnaces, reduced to a weight even a child could carry, then taken by rail to the capped junction and on foot, slowly, the last kilometer to the lip. There the one natural wind in the world takes the dust of them off the rounded edge in a brief glitter and drops it three thousand meters into a darkness that sends nothing back. Then the mourners turn around and walk home in their own footprints. To an outsider it would look unbearably bleak. To them it is the only form of disappearance available in a place that forgets nothing else, and they value it precisely for that. The dead stop accumulating. Everything else does not.

Their culture has the plateau's conviction in its bones: that the final form has largely been reached, and that goodness lies in maintenance rather than invention. A good worker is not one who improves a machine but one who returns it to its tolerances so exactly that the next century will find it unchanged. Marriages, apprenticeships, the naming of children — names are reused in strict district rotation, so that a good track in the dust may belong to four generations of the same woman — are all arranged with an eye to replacement without alteration. Novelty is not forbidden. It is simply regarded as a kind of erosion, interesting in rocks that finished it eons ago, embarrassing in people. Their art, such as it is, is the polishing of lanes by use until the quartz veins show through in long dull lines, and the arrangement, in interior courts, of unpainted black modules in proportions so old that no one experiences them as choices anymore.

And yet the plain makes its exceptions, as it makes the lizards and the beetles and the mineral crust that is almost life. Once or twice a year the reports come in from the far districts, always the same: someone has seen the long low animal loping hairless and dust-colored from one pool of light to the next, unhurried, uninterested. What the track-readers say only among themselves is that its prints, where they cross a lane, are not prints. Not quite. The heel is too long. The weight is distributed wrong for four legs and right, terribly right, for someone who has been walking on all fours for a very long time, someone whose groove wore so deep and so straight that at some point they went down into it and did not get up.

No body has ever been found. Bodies, up here, do not oblige by vanishing.

Only by being carried, at the end of a life spent walking the same warm black ground under the same fixed stars, to the one edge where the dust at last is allowed to fall.
