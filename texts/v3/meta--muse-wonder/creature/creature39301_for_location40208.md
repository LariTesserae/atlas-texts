They are white with work.

Not pale the way a face is pale after winter, but dusted through — hair, lashes, the creases of knuckles, the whorls of ears — with stone flour that never entirely washes out because there is never water enough to waste on washing. It settles into the skin. It lightens their footprints on dark planks and darkens again where sweat cuts through it on a neck, a forearm, the small of a back bent over a saw. A newborn is the darkest thing in the Low Making for a few hours, slick and startlingly pink, born in a hammock beside a wall still smelling of the cut. By the second day the dust has found it.

They have lived under the Plate for generations enough that the rim country has become a kind of weather report to them: a place men claim to have seen earth, where lamps are unnecessary at noon, where sound goes away and does not come back. They do not believe most of it. Their eyes have widened to the gloom — large, patient, slow-blinking eyes that can find the chalk line on a lintel by a hooded oil flame, the hairline flaw blooming across a slab, the particular grey-green smear of slime on a cistern lip. Visitors come down the ramps weeping and half-blind. The basin-born merely turn their faces toward a voice and wait for the rest of the person to become visible.

But sight is not how they know the world. Thirty paces is the end of seeing. Everything beyond that arrives.

So they are listeners first, and builders second, though they would say the two are the same trade. A child learns the acoustic map before she learns the streets: this vault throws a voice twice, that pier eats the high notes, here the treadwheel's groan means you are under the north kilns, here the dripping is fast because the forge-yards are firing above. They know who is coming by the strike of a heel on flagstone, brick, clay tile, plank — for there is no ground, and never has been, only floor laid over floor over the flat crown of some older vault that no living hand cut into. They know a good stone from a flawed one the way you know a voice in another room. Clear. Or *tock*, like a bad tooth.

They shout. Everyone shouts, even mothers to infants, even lovers in hammocks lashed six feet apart under a stair, because the working noise does not stop and because a hand signal is invisible in the dark. Their private speech has therefore gone partly into touch and tapping. Three taps on a pillar: *hold*. A knuckle run along a scaffold rail to clear a lane. A bell for a falling load, a horn for a rope going, and underneath all of it the intimate percussion of fingers on a wrist, on a beam, on the taut canvas of a bunk — a small, close language for the things that are not work, carried in vibration the way other people carry whispers. Their throats are ruined early and beautifully. Old women's voices come out like rope over stone. Men who have sung the saw-stroke for thirty years can set forty strangers breathing together with one note.

Their bodies are made by the tasks the way the tasks were made by bodies before them. Sawyers go lopsided, one shoulder dropped and corded. Rope-men have palms like horn and forearms banded with old burns from hemp. Mortar-mixers, who work sparing because water is owned, disputed, and inherited by the barrelful, stir with their whole backs. Stone-carriers move in a permanent, listening crouch, head cocked away from the block, feeling the singing of the rope through the soles of their feet. Almost nobody is tall here. The stacked street-levels and low vaults have been selecting, for a long time, for people who can duck without thinking and work a full shift under a ceiling that takes the heat of their own breath and returns it as rain.

Because the Plate sweats. Their sky drips in remembered lines, off beam-ends and cracks, warm on the back of the neck while both hands are on a rope. So among the most respected figures in the basin are the gutter-men, who patrol the underside with scrapers and wooden spouts and can read a drip the way a farmer elsewhere reads a cloud — whose cistern it belongs to, whether it will strengthen, whether the kilns above are being pushed too hard. A family's wealth is not stone; stone is everywhere and belongs to the Making. Wealth is a clay cistern that does not leak, a barrel of relatively clear drip-water, a lamp niche with oil measured by the spoonful and a wick that does not smoke.

The scraper-boys, paid in that oil, hunt the cistern lips for slime and mash it into paste. The lamp-tenders move through the niches like a priesthood of small flames. The bell-men are half deaf and entirely trusted. And the testers — old men whose hands have begun to tremble too much for the chisel, and young girls whose ears the saws have not yet dulled — go even through the finished districts, morning and evening, tapping every pier and lintel and slab with wooden mallets and chalking the note it gives back.

They are the only ones who regularly walk the empty city.

For by the custom that is harder than law, no one sleeps in a finished room. The completed quarters stand row upon row, doors pegged open, hearths never lit, smelling only of stone — and the real town clings to their outsides like swallows' nests: scaffold platforms, rope bridges, hammocks, lean-tos of plank and sacking, cook-boxes slung beneath stairways, bunks stacked three high in the lee of a new wall. A family lives for months against one façade, close enough to touch the wall it is not permitted to live behind. Children do their lessons in chalk on the backs of stones waiting to be laid. A grandmother dies in a bunk within arm's reach of a perfect, empty bedchamber. When the street is declared complete, everything unpegs and unlashes and moves one street onward, and the whole improvised town rebuilds itself against the next raw front, a little more worn, a little more expert, leaving the finished rooms behind to cool into a quiet that nobody trusts.

Quiet is the alarm here. Quiet may mean a cracked pillar no longer carrying its share of the song. Quiet may mean a fall has buried the bell. So even tenderness in the Low Making tends toward noise: they hang more bells on a street they are worried about, light more lamps though the oil is dear, set more gangs to work within earshot. To reassure a child at night — night and day being mostly a matter of shift and habit under the Plate — you do not hush her. You tap the beam above her hammock, steady and clear, and she listens for the true note and sleeps.

What they eat arrived dead, and they know it, and it makes the rare living intrusions into events. Grain, salt fish, hard beans, rendered fat, hauled down the ramps from a world they mostly do not picture. A moth around a lamp can stop a whole lane — hammers lowering involuntarily, forty dusted faces turning to watch it blunder in the light. The pale rats are tolerated and named, because they find fallen grain and abandon a gallery before the air goes bad. The blind, hairless dogs sleep against kiln walls and are stepped over with great care. Once a year a bat finds a vent-shaft and is followed by ear through the beams all night, bell-men calling its progress street to street, until it escapes upward or is caught exhausted and wrapped in cloth and carried up like an ambassador from a country no one else has visited.

They do not know what the Making is for. This is not ignorance they experience as a wound; it is simply the shape of inheritance. The plans come down as sequences — this wall to this chalk line, this pier to that height, this stair built correctly into a ceiling, this doorway opening correctly onto solid stone — recopied onto lime-washed boards by men who cannot read the old marks as language. To leave a prescribed stone unlaid is the only crime the basin fully agrees on. It is worse than theft of water. It is worse than sleeping, secretly, one night, in a finished room, though a few in every generation do that too, and come back to the scaffolds before dawn describing a silence so complete they could hear their own blood, and are not envied.

Ask them who they are building for and they will look — politely, shouting — for the practical meaning of the question. For the next course. For the street after this one. For whoever is meant to stand one day on the upper surface of the Plate, that bare, unfinished plain of fresh slabs above their dripping sky, acre upon acre of clean cold stone where no soil has been brought and no seed sown. They climb up a vent-stair sometimes with a shuttered lantern, stand a moment in the cooler moving air, look across the new-made ground of a world they have built but do not occupy, and go back down to the noise and the wet and the work, because below is where people are.

And mostly that is true. Mostly, the inhabitants of the Low Making are the builders: chalked, loud, half-deafened and exquisitely hearing, born on scaffolding and buried — since there is no earth to receive them, and no finished room may hold them — by being carried up the long ramps toward a rim they have never seen, while below them the wave of their town moves one street onward and rebuilds itself against another wall.

Mostly.

The testers know one other thing, and speak of it less. Now and then, tapping a pier, or a floor that no living builder cut a way into, the answer comes a fraction too late. Not an echo — every tester learns the echoes first, the way you learn your family's voices. Later than that. Fainter. In the wrong place in the sequence, as if something very deep in the stone, or beneath the lowest floor anyone remembers laying, had listened to the mallet, considered it, and tapped back.

When it happens, they chalk the note. They hang another bell on that street. They light another lamp.

And then the whole lane, without discussing it, works a little louder — gangs answering gangs, saws singing, the treadwheels groaning a block up inch by inch through the lamplight — as if to make very clear, to whatever else lives in the Low Making, in the dark under the floors under the floors, that the place is occupied.
