There is, properly, only one resident.

It is tempting at first to count many. From the rim you can pick them out: the cushion plants, the ankle-high willow, the black sedges, the moss campion with its white stars, lichens in copper and sulfur. A flora. A community occupying a valley.

That lasts until you watch for an hour. Then a patch of sedge flowers, is stripped by the felt growing up around it, collapses, and is gone under a sliding sheet of something moss-like that is itself already rooting into the trunks of the pumps below. A willow is not growing in soil. It is growing out of sedge peat, out of a dead pump, out of another willow folded upside-down under the mat and still alive. Its roots do not end in the ground. They braid into root-cables. Its leaves exhale water that condenses over a hot node and rains back down and is taken up again, half a meter away, by the sedge.

Nothing here lives *in* the valley in the way an animal lives in a meadow. The meadow is the animal.

It has filled the basin rim to rim, eleven kilometers of it, not by spreading over the land but by replacing the land as the thing that has shape. Under it the rock has been smoothed and digested to soft contours. It has been this size and this shape for thousands of years — an old, settled, eroded equilibrium — and not one stem of it will be in the same place tomorrow.

That is how it lives: by staying entirely still at the scale of a landscape and never stopping at the scale of a body.

It has no center. No head, no heart, no seed from which the rest unfolded and to which signals report. Control is everywhere and therefore nowhere. A hot node warms, condensation forms above it, the extra water weights the felt, the felt thickens, the node is insulated and cools, the felt above it thins and tears — regulation without a regulator, the way a liver manages chemistry without ever knowing it is doing so. If there is a self here, it is not a point. It is the closed loop.

Its skin is the part you recognize. Half a meter of photosynthetic crust, cold-adapted, dense, low to survive the wind and its own shifting. It takes the hard high light and the nightly frost and puts out spores into a green haze that never rises above ankle height because there is no need for it to rise — everything it could land on is already it. Pollen germinates on the leaves of its neighbors. A seed here does not look for soil. It looks for another plant, and finds one.

Under the skin it stops pretending to be a plant at all and becomes the rest of a body.

There are tissues for transport: braided root-cables, black as wet lignite, copper fibers running through them, pulsing under pressure. There are tissues for work: barrel-shaped pump-stems that swell and contract inside fluid-filled chambers, forever, muffled to silence. There are tissues for heat: fermentation organs buried deep enough that winter never reaches them, holding a steady internal warmth against air that is freezing at noon, leaking fog up into the sun. There are tissues for digestion pointed the wrong way — not inward to a stomach but downward into the world. Crusher roots secrete acid in pulses, wedge into bedrock with slow hydraulic force, grind stone between self-renewing plates, and pass the flour up through the system to be sorted. What cannot be used is excreted at the surface as heavy metallic scales, warm, smelling of ozone: aluminum here, manganese there, copper salts along a different seam, each tissue refining a different element out of the mountain it is eating.

There are even tissues for lightning. Tall dry stalks on the ridges accumulate charge in the thin cold air and discharge it root to root through the conductive net, so that on gray days a faint blue tracery moves silently under the green, contained, spent inside itself.

And all of these parts are disposable. That is the crucial difference between it and a machine, and between it and an animal. A pump grows too fast, bursts, and is compost by evening. A new strain of felt overgrows a node and cooks itself to death and leaves a black steaming pit that becomes a pond that becomes a sedge bed. Crusher plates wear down and are shed and grow again. Rot is not its winter. Rot is one of its organs, sitting a meter from new growth, trading places with it daily. Cut it open — it cuts itself open often enough, in its silent slides — and the layers do not read like geology. They read like a body that has folded over its own history: green skin, brown felt, black cable, a red seam of compost, pale root, stone flour, and then green skin again, deep in the dark, upside down, still living.

So it is one thing, and it is also a crowd in permanent competition with itself. Strains spread, insulate, starve, burst, outgrow. Fibers compete for the minerals in a dropped glove and pull it apart along the seams. Evolution is not something that happened to it long ago in order to produce it. Evolution is happening inside it, at visible speed, as a routine metabolic process. It does not maintain a perfect form. It maintains a form by constantly losing control of it, slightly, everywhere, and reabsorbing the failure as food.

It eats sunlight and stone and its own dead. It drinks the same water forever — cup-leaf to fog to ice to melt to cistern to pump to cup-leaf — never a river, never dry. It excretes heat and sorted metal and oxygen. It makes oils to lubricate its own moving joints, resins and antifreeze sugars that keep its tissues working at temperatures that would kill most life, and which incidentally make it inedible. Nothing grazes it twice.

The few animals you could name are less its neighbors than its inclusions. The pale moths that work its flowering stalks are airborne for their whole adult lives because to land for long is to be grown over; they are, functionally, a mobile part of its reproductive system. The translucent, eyeless beetle in the pump chambers has never been found anywhere else, feeds on shed chamber lining, and likely could not survive an hour outside one; it is less a tenant than a gut microbe with legs. Everything else is transient. A finch crosses without stopping. A carcass, if one falls, is not scavenged — it is processed, fur and bone subsiding into the felt in a week, sorted like rock flour, like a glove, like anything that holds still long enough to be classified as material.

Which is to say it does not notice you, and that is not indifference in any mental sense. It has no apparatus for noticing things that move. Its senses are pressure in the cables, heat in the nodes, light on the skin, charge in the stalks, acid on stone. A footstep registers, if at all, as a brief compression to be healed. Standing still beside it registers as substrate. Moving through it, you are permitted the way a river permits a swimmer: not welcomed, simply not yet incorporated.

Old as a valley, young as this afternoon's shoots — knee-high, flowering, and collapsed between morning and evening — it lives in the contradiction without resolving it. At the largest scale it is the most stable thing in the high country. At the smallest it is turbulence that never repeats. A standing shape made of matter that is never the same matter twice, like a rapids, except the rapids has grown roots into the mountain and a skin against the frost and has been standing there, manufacturing itself out of light and rock and its own body, for longer than there has been anyone to come over the pass and fail to find the ground.
