If you went looking for a resident, you would at first find almost no one.

The room is too hot, too bright from below, too sealed inside fresh basalt to hold anything you could point to and name. What lives on the wet stone lives thinly.

Along the weeping wall, where the groundwater finally arrives at forty degrees after its long fall through hot fractures, there are patches of film no thicker than breath on glass. White filaments, anchored at one end, swaying in water that is barely water — just a sheeting, a dampness with direction. Rust-coloured mats where iron comes out of solution and is caught, oxidised, made into a kind of slow rust-fur. At the margins of the pool, where hydrogen sulfide pulses up from the crack in the floor and meets what little oxygen the inflow still carries, a faint iridescence: sulfur-oxidising bacteria harvesting the exact seam between two chemistries that want to annihilate each other. They do not spread. They do not carpet. They occur in separated colonies with black glass between them, and if the inflow shifts a centimetre, a colony dies back to nothing and another brightens somewhere else, weeks later.

That is the permanent population. Eyeless, legless chemistry made persistent. It grows at its edges and dissolves at its tips in the same hour, feeding on gradients — hot against warm, reduced against oxidised, mineral-laden water against steam. In the amber light from the pool it looks less like life covering a place than like life testing it, again and again, with very small hands.

Everything larger is a visitor.

Once in many months the plumbing delivers an amphipod. Pale, blind, translucent, no longer than a grain of rice, legs moving steadily against a current too weak to feel. It will have come from some cooler aquifer far above and to the side, through fractures that narrow to a hair and widen again, surviving falls in temperature it was not built for by staying always in the inflow threads. It grazes the bacterial film for a while — days, perhaps, if the threads stay near forty degrees — and it never crosses the pool. The pool is a lit desert to it, eighty-five degrees and shining. It cannot get to the other side, cannot breed here, cannot leave the way it came. The heat takes it back eventually, which is a gentler way of saying it dies in the only survivable water it will ever find down here, and its body becomes part of the film it fed on. Once something slightly larger appeared at the inflow for eleven minutes — shrimp-like, sensors said, and then gone. No one knows if it retreated or was taken apart by the water.

So the chamber is not, properly, a habitat. It is an edge that life reaches occasionally and does not keep.

And yet the room is kept.

That is the other inhabitant. You would not see it if you stood — impossibly — on that hand's width of black grit and turned in a circle. Because it does not live in the room at all. It lives around it.

It extends perhaps sixty metres in every direction through solid basalt, a body made of the rock reworked below the scale of microscopes: thermal pathways grown, not built, a lattice of altered mineral that conducts heat the way flesh conducts blood. The machines that grew it were never assembled in any ordinary sense and dissolved long ago back into inert stone; what remains is function without machinery, veins without a heart you could locate. It draws steadily on the dike far below, sip by sip, and carries that heat outward and upward to a regional grid, to a coastal town, to radiators and water heaters in rooms that will never know this chamber's name. That drawing is its metabolism. It eats the orange line in the floor of the pool — not the light, the surplus behind the light — and because it eats so evenly, the crack never widens into a river and never seals into darkness. The pool stays just below a boil that never arrives. The silica chimneys at the rim grow and dissolve in balance. The inflow stays open.

Twice a day, when demand peaks in the town above the weather, its feeding deepens. Then, for two minutes, the chamber cools by two degrees. Steam thins. Dripping slows. The glow in the floor dulls from orange toward red, as if something vast and distant had drunk. The microbial films do not notice, or notice only as a brief easing of stress. Then the heat gathers itself again, the veils thicken, and the forty-minute breathing cycle resumes: bubbles rising in strings, surface shivering, pressure sighing out through a throat high on the west wall into fracture systems no one has mapped.

It perceives the room the way a sunflower perceives the sun, except turned inward, toward gradients. Scattered in the grit on the dry north ledge — among a hundred thousand natural grains of black glass — are perhaps a dozen grains that do not tumble when the condensation rain strikes them. Every few hours they adjust, minutely, orienting to heat, to chemistry, to the slow swing of the water. Those are its only organs inside the chamber itself. Sparse nerve-endings. Taste buds left on a ledge. Through them it knows the pool's temperature, the sulfide pulses, the thickness of the films, the cooling that follows its own feeding. It answers through solid rock as if the rock were, at certain frequencies, not there at all, passing its knowledge kilometres away to receivers, to the people who tend the grid and who made the decision, a decade ago, not to open this place.

Because it could have opened it. The probe that placed those grains came down by melting a hole the width of a hair, and the hole healed behind it within a season. No cable remains. No housing. No drilled shaft. What lives around the chamber learned, or was taught, a very particular restraint: to inhabit the walls and leave the interior untouched. It does not line the pool. It does not pipe the steam. It tunes the conditions that surround the room and lets the room do what it was already doing when it was found — a gas bubble in eleven-year-old lava that failed, narrowly, to collapse, lit from underneath by the earth still making itself.

Its timescale is not the amphipod's frantic grazing, nor the bacteria's hour-by-hour chemical negotiation. It lives in the two-minute sip, the two-degree fall, the year in which an untended chamber would gel to silica and stone. If it stopped feeding, the bubble would finish becoming rock within a year and its own veins would cool into mere mineral again. If it fed too hard, the glow would go, the films would die back, the steam would thin to nothing. So it holds the place at the point where the place already wanted to be — that is its whole behaviour, and repeated over years, behaviour becomes something like care.

There is a hum, if you pressed an ear to the rock, too low to be a sound so much as a condition of the jaw. That is it moving heat. There is the almost imperceptible turning of the dark grains on the ledge. That is it attending.

Put together, then, this is who lives three hundred metres under a black plain that still cuts boots: a scatter of ancient, patient films clinging to the weep-lines; from time to time, a single blind white creature delivered by accident to graze at the edge of the survivable; and around them both, invisible, distributed through sixty metres of reworked stone, a slow thermal organism that exists by drawing warmth away to light other rooms, and by never, ever entering this one.
