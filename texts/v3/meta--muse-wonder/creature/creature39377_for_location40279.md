It is not the shrimp.

The shrimp comes and goes across the thirty-centimeter glass, translucent, eyeless, picking with too many careful legs against the current until the vibration shakes it loose again. It does not live in the bell. Nothing that can let go lives there.

Nor the eel, when it comes. Nor the orange-black mats of iron bacteria furred on the seams outside, precipitating rusticle by rusticle. They grow there, but they grow the way calcite grows. They have no more tenancy than the salt teeth under the hatch.

The tenancy is in the stool.

Not at first glance. At first glance the bell seems edited, as everything in it has been edited, down to stool and gauge and glass. But the riveted seat is worn in a way water cannot wear iron. Its center is polished. The grate beneath it is polished in two places, heel-sized, where boots have braced for years against the eleven-second hammer blow. The trembling gauge has a smear across its face, at exactly hand height, where a thumb wipes the condensation away without looking. And the loose screw on the floor — the one that jumps with the silt and resettles in concentric ripples, never washed through the grate — is not lost.

It is hers. She puts it back.

She is the listener.

She was maintenance once, before maintenance became a rumor the upper districts told about why their basements stayed dry. Third generation, perhaps. Perhaps she was the apprentice to the man who hand-lettered DO NOT SPEAK HERE, and he was apprentice to the woman who understood, the first time she sat down, that the sign was not an instruction but an epitaph for conversation. The evacuation never quite finished and neither did she. When the crews went up the tram tracks for the last time, following the handrails out of the brown-green water, one set of wet footprints came back down.

She does not live in the bell. No one could. The bell is only the ear, and you cannot sleep inside your own ear. She lives in the throat behind it, in the maintenance level that is not on the maps, where the six district pipes converge and the heat of the pumps keeps a maze of false bulkheads and walk-through mains at blood temperature, day and night. Her dwelling is whatever the vibration has not yet shaken apart: a hammock slung inside a decommissioned pipe, lined with canvas cut from a drowned shop awning and dried stiff with mineral; a niche where eel meat, caught stunned off the intake screens, cures black in the hot air; three changes of clothes gone the color of rust water; a tin cup chained by its handle so it cannot dance off and be lost. Everything she owns is tied down, wedged, or bolted. Everything loose long ago migrated, as she did, toward the grate.

And she herself has been edited.

She keeps her head low, even out in the turbine hall where she could stand. Not from age — though she is old now in the way iron is old down here, furred and rounded and still hard at the core — but from habit learned at the node. Standing in the bell puts your skull where the pressure doubles and the world greys. So she has learned a permanent, patient stoop, her spine curved like a pipe elbow. Her hair she crops to the scalp with a sharpened fitting, because hair in the resonance tickles unbearably and then begins, faintly, to sing. Her ears are the least remarkable thing about her from the outside. Inside, the drums are thick white scars, healed and rehealed. Ordinary sound reaches her muffled, as if through thirty centimeters of scratched glass. A voice in the dry districts — on the rare occasions she goes up for salt, for batteries, for news no one believes she needs — arrives too small to bother with.

What she hears with is the rest of her.

Her molars, several of which she has allowed to go loose in their sockets because a loose tooth reads grit in a pipe better than a tight one. Her sternum, broadened and flattened by decades of bracing. The soles of her feet, horn-thick, bare whenever she can risk it, because boot leather lies. Most of all her diaphragm, that involuntary muscle, which flutters once per breath when a leak opens three streets away, long before any instrument in the upper city knows to register a loss. The engineers built the bell to make the flooded infrastructure legible to a body. They did not reckon on the body staying long enough to become fluent.

She takes her shift the way the pumps take theirs: continuously, with variations. When the beaded curtain outside the porthole is thick — the compressed-air leak feeding bubbles up past the glass, a portion drawn in with each drop in pressure — she settles onto the stool and closes her eyes. The porthole is not for her. It is a courtesy the builders left for men who still trusted sight. She wipes it anyway, from time to time, for the shrimp.

Then the city begins to report.

Clear pipe: even. A pipe furring up with scale: grit in the back teeth, a taste like sucking a coin. Water-hammer every eleven seconds up through heels and shins, setting the loose teeth aching in a rhythm she no longer counts because counting would imply waiting, and she is never waiting — she is simply in it, the way her heart, slowed over the years into the system's own tempo, now gives one heavier beat as the valve slams somewhere upstream, as if answering. Every forty seconds a pump changes load and the pitch drags upward, an organ leaning on its keys, and she leans with it, minutely, from the hips, reading the drag for the whine of a bearing going dry two districts off under the riverbed.

She keeps the record where vibration can't erase it. Not on paper. Paper pulps, ink swims, and anything flat eventually walks itself across the floor. She keeps it in notches cut into the warm side of a cable as thick as her thigh, a braille of the drowned streets, and in a code of taps she sends up the pipes on the hour with the screw — the lost screw, her stylus — three streets reporting, a leak, a fur, a clear run of song. Whether anyone up there still knows the code she does not ask. The tapping is not a message. It is a valve opening for a pressure no one else is alive down here to need.

She never speaks in the bell. That was the first thing taken. Not the hearing — the voice. Speech came back into her chest amplified and wrong, a hand closing around her own heart, so she unlearned it the way you unlearn touching a hot pipe. Her jaw is strong from clenching against the hammer blows. In the turbine hall she can manage a whisper, gravelly, used mostly on herself, on the pumps, names of streets that no longer exist above the waterline spoken the way other people say prayers. But mostly her language is posture now. A tilt of the head toward one pipe of the six. A hand laid flat on the bell wall, fingers spread, going still.

Between shifts, when the bubbles thin and the pitch turns meaner and closer, she leaves — not because she wants to, but because the room dismisses her, a clock made of air. She backs out through the wardrobe-sized hatch, seals it by feel, and sleeps in her pipe in a chin-strap of her own making, because once, years ago, she hummed in a dream and woke with her ribs bruised from the inside and blood in her mouth where a tooth had worried itself free. The city had tried to sing back through her. She does not permit it anymore. She is the ear, not the mouth.

Her skin has taken the district's colors. Black manganese in the creases of her knuckles like the skin on the brass fittings. Pale calcite feathers at her elbows and the backs of her heels where she brushes hot leaks. Rust in every scar. When the sodium lamps pulse outside and the silt thins, and the traffic light far above blooms green through the murk for no one, she is already watching for the change in note that the green cannot tell her. If an inspection drone comes tumbling past, white flare, trying to right itself, she watches it without pity or wonder. It is a thing that trusts its eyes, in a place that punishes eyes.

When the surge comes — storm gate opening, rain on dry streets far above — she does not leave the stool. This is what she is for. The water outside the glass boils sideways, debris rises, the bell groans in its mountings and the note climbs until she is not hearing it but being briefly, repeatedly erased by it, air compressing warm against her skin, compressing again. Her hands find their polished places. Her heels find theirs. The gauge needle blurs past red and she reads it, calmly, in her teeth, in her sternum, in the fine grey silt jumping on the floor around her bare feet and resettling, ripple by impossible ripple, into the shape of the flood.

Then it passes. The pitch falls. She breathes.

The pumps never stopped; that is why it is so warm. But the pumps are not who lives here. The pumps are only what the city does. She is what the city, in the long dark after the evacuation, slowly grew in order to know itself — one human body, bolted like the bell to the throat of six rivers, kept in a closet of trapped air behind a sign that forbids her to answer, listening to a drowned street go on forever deciding whether to fail.

And when she dies, as she will, mid-shift, between one hammer blow and the next, the place is already prepared for it. It will shake her few loose things down to the grate and wash them through. The stool will stay polished a long while. The screw will go on jumping in the silt, resettling, waiting for a hand to pick it up and put it back — for the next body the silence down there teaches, eleven seconds at a time, how to be an ear.
