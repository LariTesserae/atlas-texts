If you stay long enough in the Crown Marsh, you stop being able to tell where the list of animals ends and where the list of neighbours begins.

It starts small, because most of the life here starts small and stays inside something else. Every bromeliad in every tree-fork is a pond, and every pond has its tenants: a frog no bigger than a thumbnail, translucent green, that never in its whole life touches the ground; a clutch of blind white fish, fingernail-long, circling in water that has never been rain and never been lake, only condensed air; beetles that walk upside down on the surface tension with iridescent feet. When the pressure rises on the forty-day breath and all the little ponds brim and link, for one night every tree is an archipelago joined by shining bridges. The fish travel. The frogs change key. In the morning they are home again, in their own leaf, as if nothing has happened.

Above them the lakes have their own permanent population. Waterbirds with absurd, splayed toes walk out across the floating leaves arguing in flat, duck-like voices, stabbing for snails. Things move under the tea-dark water that are not quite fish — long, eelish, blind in a different way, feeling the minute change in pressure through their whole bodies and rising to spawn only when the channels run a finger's width high. In the vent fields the algae mats are grazed by snails with hot shells, and the mats themselves, emerald and sulfur-yellow, bloom and die and bloom again on a yearly turning so regular the tenders can set their calendar by the smell: pepper first, then rotten fruit, then hot copper.

At dusk it all gets loud. Frogs in the lakes, frogs in the bromeliads, frogs in the puddle held inside a single curled leaf. The cicada-things in the fern crowns begin that glassy, sustained ringing that finds the hum of the pylons and locks to it, horribly and beautifully, until you cannot tell whether the sound is coming from the insects or from the air itself. Bats pour out of the warm caves in the rim-rock. And then the gliders launch.

They are the first inhabitants you learn as individuals. Membrane-winged mammals the size of large dogs, heavy in the chest, soft-muzzled, with eyes that reflect the lakes. They live in colonies in the rim trees and the cave mouths, in lineages that the tenders can recite. All evening they ride the thermal up off the warm water, out over the abyss, hit the air-wall at speed — *kissing the sky*, the children call it — and let that soft resistance decelerate and push them back in a long, banking turn over the marsh, mouths open in clouds of midges. Each one turns with a different creak and flutter. A child who has grown up on the eastern terraces can stand on a stilt-hut porch with her eyes closed and name them as they pass: this one favouring a torn left wing, this one young and showing off, this one old enough that her membrane is patched pearl-white where it healed.

There are agreements with them. Not metaphorical agreements. Fruit laid out on flat stones at the rim, renewed when the trees fruit on the pressure cycle. No lights flown after dark over certain lakes in the breeding weeks. Gliders, in return, do not nest in the lift-head house, and they keep the big hunting bats off the terraces, and their droppings keep the rim trees alive. A tender spends as much time maintaining that relationship as she does clearing fern-root out of the water channels. Both jobs are done the same way: very little, very carefully, repeated for years.

The people are the strangest specimens on the mountain because they do not belong to either timescale at first.

There are never many. A rotating crew in the stilt-huts above the eastern lakes, built of fern-wood and the same dull pearl substance as the pylons, on five-year postings. A few families in hollows on the southern terraces where the paths are less slick. Supply crews who stay only the turning of one breath and leave with their sinuses aching and their clothes already rotting off them. Nothing made for the dry world lasts long up here. Leather goes green. Paper swells shut. Iron sweats and pits. Lungs, at first, feel drowned.

Newcomers wilt, exactly as a plant brought up from the plain wilts. Not from lack of water — there has never been a lack of water in the Crown Marsh — but from the wrong kind of abundance. The air is so saturated your sweat never leaves you; it sheets, it shines, it runs into your eyes salt while the air tries to give you fresh water back through your skin. You have to learn to drink without swallowing. To breathe slowly enough that the thick air does not panic you. To sleep through your ears popping as the lentil inflates in the night. For the first month tenders move as if underwater, because they are.

By the second year they have changed gait. Everyone up here walks barefoot where they can, even on stone, placing the whole foot down on the warm, slick paths to feel the tiny blue flowers close before they are crushed. They move unhurriedly not out of philosophy — though it becomes philosophy — but because haste up here accomplishes nothing and breaks things that will take ten years to heal. A fern uncurls on its own schedule. A channel clears and silts on its own schedule. A pylon crack narrows, visibly, in a decade.

Their bodies accommodate. Hair stays wet permanently and learns to host a fine, harmless green film that keeps midges off. Skin shines always, darkened and softened, rarely cut and quick to close when it is, as if the humidity remembers the shape of health. They cool their fevers with their hands in the uphill channel in the central bowl, where the water creeps thirty meters up a polished basalt chute to the upper lake, and they time conceptions and harvests and journeys down to the sighing-out of the air through the pylons, when the warm wind smells of stone. Their voices go low and unprojected; in air this thick, and in a bowl that throws every glider call back as a soft echo, there is no need to shout. A marsh-born child assumes the world is larger on the inside than the outside, and much larger than it sounds.

The children are few, and they grow up bilingual in these minor languages: the change in frog-song that means the lakes will connect in three nights; the difference between a pylon in normal hum and a pylon working on a repair; the names and tempers of gliders; which bromeliad ponds hold fish that bite and which hold fish that can be taught to rise to a fingertip. They are excellent swimmers before they are good walkers. They leave, at dusk, those brief trails of darkness in the carpets of blue path-flowers, the blossoms closing at their approach and opening minutes later behind them.

Some tenders leave at the end of five years. The descent has to be done like decompression, in stages through the three layers of cloud, days spent in damp intermediate camps re-learning how to be dry. Some of them never manage the plain again; the specimens brought down shrivel, confused, and so do they. They come back up in the lift-car with their skin already sighing with relief at the first fogging of the windows.

And some few stay past any posting. You can tell them not by age — the marsh preserves things oddly, softening rather than sharpening them — but by slowness of attention. An old tender can put a palm flat on a piece of fallen technology in the western forest and tell you, without looking, that the tree-fern growing through it has just put out a new frond, because the signal lights embedded in the wreck now pulse green in time with sap, and sensors long forgotten open like seed pods in the rain. She will not describe this as miraculous. She will describe it as what happens if you leave two living systems alone together long enough: they stop trying to convert each other.

Which is, finally, the largest inhabitant.

Late at night, from the southern rim, you can sometimes see the air-wall not at its edge but across its whole span — a faint hexagonal shimmer moving over the stars at the speed of a cloud shadow, cells rearranging, repairing, re-thinking a geometry that no single year can contain. The pylons around the rim, crooked rings of pearl three or four meters high, bud new nodules over decades to replace themselves. The nano-lattice in an abandoned shrine still keeps a room at perfect humidity for instruments nobody remembers, and so keeps a perfect garden instead. The water still, patiently, goes uphill where it was told to.

Nothing up here believes the pylons are merely machines keeping the weather in. After a while, the tenders talk about the kept air the way they talk about the glider colonies: as an elder neighbour with whom you have an agreement. It breathes in for three weeks and out for three weeks. It feeds the rock until the rock weeps. It turns a stooping bird back in a surprised, perfect arc, without harming it. It thinks, if it thinks, in repairs that take longer than a human posting, longer than a human childhood, and are made anyway.

So the Crown Marsh is lived in at every speed at once. By fish who live and die in a tablespoon of water in a tree. By frogs who sing one note for a month and another for the next. By dog-sized gliders who kiss an invisible sky for the pleasure of the turn, and know the stones where fruit will be left. By people who arrived in a humming insect of a lift-car, learned to drink from the air, and learned after that to do almost nothing, almost perfectly, for years. And underneath them all, holding the warm wet lentil of air against a cliff that falls kilometres to cloud, by the slow, humming, self-mending thing the pylons make together — less a wall than a resident, established, settled into itself, talking to itself very loudly in its sleep.
