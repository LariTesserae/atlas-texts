At first you would say that nothing lives there. Not really.

You would count what you can find by touch and call that the inventory. The transparent shrimp, thumbnail-long, ghosting from basin to basin under the warm water, visible only because it bends what little there is to bend — and here there is no light at all, so even that is not seeing, it is a movement against your ankle, a tickle of legs, gone. The white isopods on the walls, motionless for so long they seem like another kind of popcorn calcite, until one shifts a fraction and resettles. The opalescent film at the edges of the pools, which is alive in the way a skin on milk is alive, breaking at a fingertip and slowly closing again behind it.

You would sit on the flat stone in the center, because the stone asks you to sit. Cross-legged, feet in the basins, as the arrangement of the cups and the resting place suggests. And after a while — an hour, in a place that has no hours — you would begin to understand that the stone was not worn smooth by water.

Water wears travertine up, not down. It lays it on in lips and scales and knobs. What wears a hollow into young stone, in the absolute dark, is a body returning to the same posture, over and over, for longer than a posture should be kept.

Something lives here that sits.

You will not see it. That sentence has to be done differently here. *No one* has ever seen it. Seeing is not the sense this place allows. You will know it the way the chamber knows everything: by nearness, by displacement, by warmth arriving where warmth already is and yet is somehow warmer.

First, a pool beside your foot will empty by a thread and not refill in quite its usual rhythm, because something has slipped into the runnel beneath the floor and is moving, slow as groundwater, through the pores in the young stone from one basin to the next. Then the dripping over your left shoulder will change. Not stop. Veer. One prized drip, nudged aside by a hand that is not yours, landing instead in a cupped palm with a softened *plink* that is flesh, not stone.

Then it is beside the resting stone. Not approaching, exactly. It was never entirely away. It divides its time between the pools and the stone and the niches in the wall, and most of its movement is below the threshold of movement — an inch relinquished, an inch regained, a joint freed with a sound so small your teeth feel it as a click.

It is smaller than you expect a person to be, and at first you do think *person*, because of the way it breathes. Breathing through cloth. Breathing as if the saturated air had to be chewed. The breath comes perhaps six times in what, outside, would be a minute, and between breaths there is a patience that the shrimp also have, and the isopods, as if everything down here has agreed to spend life at the speed at which stone accumulates.

It keeps its feet in the water, too. That is how you find its shape, if shape is the word: two feet that have learned the basins by heart and step only where the rim will hold, a spine bowed into the permanent bow the ceiling demands, shoulders perpetually ducked under the stalactite teeth. Its hair is gone, or else it has gone the way the wooden beads went — stiffened, paled, thinned to mineral threads that break if brushed. Its skin is not white the way the isopods are white. It is white the way the walls are white. In patches. Moonmilk soft where the water runs continually, giving under pressure like wet cheese; popcorn crust where it dries, which is nowhere, which is never, and yet crust forms anyway, in the creases of the neck, along the collarbones, over the knuckles — little knobs beginning, dense as teeth, that it works loose while it sits.

That is the work. You can hear it once you know to listen for it, underneath the dripping. A scraping, intimate and unhurried. The chipped blade is not outside in the ash; that one was left by somebody who only waited out weather. This one is older, or the same one carried in, and it lives in a niche with the cups, and its edge is kept usable against dawnless time. The dweller uses it on itself.

Not to wound. To remain unfused.

Chip, at the ankle, where a film has begun to sheet over foot and basin both. Chip, at the hip, where sitting has offered the stone a long, still surface to claim. Chip, between the fingers, which would otherwise web into paddles like a salamander's, and perhaps, generations of sitting ago, were allowed to. It scrapes itself free the way you would clear ice from a step if winter were not a season but a substance your own body secreted. When a flake comes away, it does not throw it aside. It presses the flake into a seep, wedging it just so, a little stone diverter to nudge a drip toward a drinking basin. Everything it sheds becomes a tool. Everything it makes is already on its way to becoming geology.

It eats what the chamber can offer, which is to say it eats the chamber at its earliest, softest stage, before the chamber hardens. A thumb skimmed along submerged stone gathers the opalescent film, and that is food. A mouthful of moonmilk before it sets, tasting of limestone and clay and faint iron — which is also the taste of its own mouth, and of yours now, and of the water, so that eating and drinking and breathing are not well separated here. Now and then an isopod, taken with a patience of hours, a hand closing at geological speed so as not to startle what has almost forgotten startling exists. Now and then, rarely, a shrimp, transparent all the way through, so that for a moment, passing down, it is a brief shape in a throat, and then it is gone.

It drinks from the least-stoned cup. The third cup is not a cup anymore; everyone who has left an account of this place assumes some visitor abandoned it too long. It is more likely that it was *its* cup, once, the way the other two were, and that its life here can be measured out in cups — one fused entirely to its niche, one filmed white and going, one still clay in the hand. When that one sets, it will not go looking for clay above the treeline. It will simply cup its hands. And later, when the hands go, it will drink the rain off the ceiling directly, face upturned under the drips, eyeless as the salamander that was found once in the lowest pool and never again.

Perhaps that salamander was not an error in the accounts. Perhaps it was only an earlier stage of accurate description.

Its eyes are not missing. That is the thing your fingers would find, if it let your fingers reach its face, and it might, because touch here is not intrusion; touch is the only courtesy available. The eyes are present under lids that have been filmed over, not sealed by injury but eased shut by uselessness, the way the blackened fern at the entrance persists more as memory than plant. There has been nothing to look at for so long that looking has been honorably retired. It does not need eyes to find you. Your heartbeat arrived before you did, squeezed through the crack ahead of your shoulders. In this muffled, hide-lined dark your heart is a loud, fast, almost panicked animal sound, and it sits listening to it the way you sit listening to the drips — and yes, after a while, the drips will seem to synchronize with it, or it with the drips, and you will not be entirely wrong. It slows its own heart deliberately, a discipline, an offering, so that yours will slow to meet it and stop hammering to get out.

It is old. Do not ask how old in years; years are a measurement from outside, from a world where snow melts and summer dries the mountain to dust and the drip rate inside, scandalously, does not alter. Ask instead how thick. The soles of its feet are thick with travertine that it permits, because a certain amount of incorporation is protection against the floors it wades through. The resting stone has a hollow, and its body has a corresponding stone, and they fit. Around the edges of the chamber, low in the lowest pools where the water leaves through pores no finger can find, there are curled shapes in the rimstone mosaic that you took, learning the room, for natural basins. Lip overlapping lip like scales. Some of them have the length of a forearm if you follow the curve. Some of them have, at one end, a clustered density very like toes beginning to be teeth.

It tends those, too. It clears the pores beneath them so the pools never rise. It keeps the agreement: inflow, condensation, outflow, mineral laid silently over mineral. If a pore clogs, the chamber would drown in its own warmth by inches, and everything soft would set at once. So it moves through the basins and feels underneath the water for blockages with the attention of a gardener, and breaks the film, and frees the flow, and in doing so tends what it was, and what it will be, without any apparent grief about the arrangement.

Because this is the final correction to your first inventory: it does not live here *in spite of* the fact that the place coats everything left too long and incorporates it, basin by basin, into its floor.

It lives here *by means of* that fact.

Above the treeline, among the cushion plants growing sideways out of the wind, life has to hold on in cracks and wait out weather in a ruined windbreak. Down here, life is held, and warmed, and fed mineral by mineral, and given all the time there has ever been. The price is only that you must keep moving, minutely, forever — freeing an ankle, turning a cup, shifting your weight on the stone by the width of a hair before the stone notices and accepts you. A whole existence reduced to the exact size of not quite setting. It has found that size. It fits through the pores under the floor. It fits under the ceiling's bow. It ghosts between basins the size of a thumbnail and the size of a body without troubling the distinction.

It will know when you have stayed long enough, probably before you do. You will be sitting very still, heartbeat married to the dripping, room dissolved into womb or lung or the inside of a single tooth, a first white dust settling on your sleeve where it trails in a seep, a film beginning to sweeten the back of your hand. Then something will take your wrist — not gripping; *encrusting* would be too fast a word for so gentle a contact — and with the blade, with a care you will feel in your teeth, it will scrape the new skin of stone off you and turn your hand, palm up, toward the bend where the rootlets hang dead and blind and the black fern waits at the limit of the vanished light.

Not rejection. Husbandry.

It is already returning to the resting stone as you crawl, knees in the warm pools, toward the blade of cold thin light that hurts more than the darkness did. It settles. It puts its feet in the basins. It finds the drip with the good cup. Far under the floor, a pore clears, a pool sinks by a thread, another fills, and something transparent moves from basin to basin, and something white shifts a fraction on the wall and is still, and the chamber, pocket-sized and ongoing and tender to the palm, goes on being made around the one slow body keeping itself, just barely, unmade at its center.
