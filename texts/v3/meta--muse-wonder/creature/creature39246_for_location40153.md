She is the easiest thing in the hollow to miss, because she has stopped distinguishing herself from it.

At first she is only a shape in the shallowest edge of the pool — a pale, submerged rise where the black water seems to thicken. Limpets are fixed to her in their gray rosettes. Snails move across her, rasping algae from her skin as they would from stone, and she allows it in the way stone allows it. Moss has taken root along one shoulder and down the long curve of her spine. Liverwort spreads its wet tongues between her fingers.

If you stay long enough — and you must stay longer than feels reasonable — one of her fingers withdraws.

It takes perhaps a minute. Perhaps ten. The cold has made a philosophy of slowness here, and she is its oldest practitioner.

She is built low and long, though not quite as a salamander is built, and not quite as anything else now is. Her body is the length of a person curled against themselves, folded at the waist, folded again at the knees, settled into a basin in the peat that her own weight and centuries of dripping have made exactly her shape. Her skin is diluted milk over black water. In the dimness you can see through it in places: a slow architecture of vein and organ, a heart suspended in her throat like a snail's heart in a translucent shell, beating, pausing, forgetting, beating again. Eleven seconds. Eleven seconds. Eleven seconds. The drip from the ceiling has learned it from her, or she from the drip. By now it hardly matters which began.

She has no useful eyes. There are places where eyes were — two filmed hollows under the skin, turned permanently toward the pool — but sight would be an extravagance here, and she has given up extravagances one by one. She knows the hollow by pressure and by taste. She knows it by the minute changes in the seep-water running over her back, by iron and calcite on her tongue, by the vibrations of moths resettling themselves on the wall at a distance of twenty paces, felt through rock, through peat, through the rootlets of moss where they enter her skin. She knows a beetle crossing the ceiling before it falls. She knows you, though the knowing has only just begun in her and will not finish until long after you have left. A thought, with her, is not an event. It is a season starting.

Her breathing is almost indistinguishable from the pool's other exchanges. The living film of insects on the surface parts faintly when she inhales — once, perhaps, in the time it takes condensate to gather and fall from a fern — and closes again when she lets the air go back into the hollow's unmoving air. Her body temperature is the temperature. Seven degrees. Eight. The blood moves in her like meltwater through hairline cracks: everywhere, soundlessly, without apparent destination. Cold has slowed her rotting, but it has also slowed her healing, her hunger, her aging, until all of them have nearly met and cancelled out. She is not immortal. She is merely happening at a rate that makes the distinction pedantic.

She does not hunt.

Hunting would require an interruption. Instead she lies with her mouth half-open at the waterline, her lower lip a pale ledge below the film, and receives what the hollow delivers: a drowned moth, loosened from the wall by its own weight of beaded water; a fungus gnat taken in the hanging snares and cut down by rain that is not rain but condensation; a blind shrimp, white as a peeled root, blundering upside-down from the rocks into the slow drift of her breath. Swallowing takes the better part of an afternoon. Digestion takes a month. The white fish hang in the water beside her face while she feeds, motionless, unafraid in the ancestral sense, and pick at what escapes her.

Everything here has its parasite, and she is not exempt. Leeches no bigger than commas attach in the folds of her, milk-pale themselves, drinking so slowly she may be said to be sharing rather than losing. Mites move in the moss on her shoulder. Fly larvae live for a while in the shallow, self-renewing ulcer where a fallen stone cut her, before she was finished changing — the scar is older than the trees outside the mountain, and still, patiently, almost closed. When she sheds skin, which she does in soft transparent sheets once in a great while, beetles meet it before it can settle. What they leave, fungal thread laces white and converts, without smell, into peat, into moss, into her again.

She has hands, though she has not used them for anything you would call use in a very long time. Four long fingers to each, webbed to the second joint, the nails grown out into curved translucent plates like snail shell. They rest open on the bottom of the pool. Caddis larvae have incorporated shed fragments of those nails into their cases, along with sand and black grit and bits of shell, so that she is slowly distributed across the floor in other creatures' architecture. Once — very far back, in the part of her life that occurred at a warmer speed — those hands may have lifted stones, or built, or beckoned, or held something to keep it from falling. The hollow has no record of it. Whatever she arrived as, the place has edited her down to what can remain: mouth, skin, patience, a heart that keeps the interval.

The salamanders sleep in the moss around her head. In winter, when the entrance seals with its skin of ice, they press into the warmest places on her, which are not warm, only less cold — the hollow beneath her jaw, the seams where limb meets body — and she does not dislodge them. One might take an hour to climb onto her. She might take the rest of the winter to decide to let it stay. By spring the decision will have been made so thoroughly that neither of them will remember it as one.

You might wonder if she is lonely, but loneliness is a function of missing, and missing requires the belief that things could be arranged otherwise. She has the drip, arriving. She has the moths, adjusting by fractions in their thousands. She has the snails grazing her forearms, their rasping felt deep in her teeth, and the pool rising against her side by exactly nothing, forever, and the slow traffic of shrimp across the underside of stones. Once in a decade a fern spore from her own shoulder falls into the wet seam of her elbow and opens there, translucent as an insect wing. She is not alone. She is upholstered in attendance.

What she is dreaming, if the long pauses between heartbeats hold dreams, is not something that would survive translation into a warmer mind. Perhaps she dreams at the speed of calcite being laid down — drip accreting to drip, channel smoothing to channel — one image per century, brightening almost imperceptibly. Perhaps she is dreaming this exact moment, you crouched beside the pool with your soaked clothes and your offended pulse, and in her dream you have already been here for years and are only just beginning to be noticed. Perhaps she dreams nothing at all, having finally synchronized herself so perfectly with a finished place that experience and expectation are the same event, occurring, neither early nor late.

When you leave, squeezing back out through the slit into the bright alpine wind, she will not watch you go. The film of insects will close over the small warmth your body disturbed. The moss will take back the prints of your knees without springing, slowly, the way it takes back everything. Her finger will complete the withdrawal it began when you arrived.

Sometime after you have forgotten the exact quality of the quiet in there — sometime next year, or the year after — she will finish registering that something large and hot and brief was once beside her pool, and she will turn her head, a fraction, toward the place where you were.

And then, satisfied, she will begin, very slowly, to turn it back.
