Nobody lives there the way a person would live in a room.

The person who comes down the ladder does not. You can tell by the clipboard in its zip bag, by the handwriting going shakier across the temperature columns, by the last empty line. A hand comes, breathes on a gauge to clear the frost so it can be read, writes, leaves. The room has rules for that hand: no bare skin on bare metal, no more than two cycles before it has to go back up to warm itself. Speak and the humidity needle lifts. Stay and your breath becomes weather in the wrong places. A visitor here is only ever a temporary accident of heat and water that the place then has to correct for.

The plants do not live there either, not exactly. They are there on sufferance, in tests. Dwarf rye a finger high in fired clay, moss in shallow pans that only darkens when the misters run, spruce seedlings like green asterisks in mineral wool. No soil, no flies, no spiders in the cable trays, no mice though the factory is right above them. When they need shaking, the shelf shakes them. They are plugs, samples, things being asked a question: *can you tolerate this?* And they do not even lean toward the light, because the light is only amber work-lamps and green LEDs kept low to save heat. They lean, barely, toward the compressors, toward the brief, wasteful warmth of the off-cycle. They are not the resident. They are what the resident is trying out inside itself, the way you might try a food on the tongue before swallowing.

What lives there is slower and less separate than that.

It lives in the eleven minutes on and the nine minutes off. That is its pulse, and everything else arranges itself around it. When the compressors kick, the lamps dim a fraction and the cold deepens by degrees and the hum gets into your teeth; when they rest, the whole pit sighs and ticks and lets its held rain fall. It breathes that way, manufactured. It drinks that way, too. Nothing flows in from outside. Moisture arrives furtively — on a jacket, in a lungful, through the second the hatch is open — and it steals it back without apology: beading it on cold pipe, crawling it strangely up charged wires against gravity in bright, obedient beads, freezing it mid-drip into clear needles, then letting go all at once in defrost so it can patter through the grating into the sump and be pumped and filtered and offered round again. A closed mouth. A body learning its own circulation.

Frost is the fastest part of it, its quick tissue. It ferns across the coil fins while you watch. It furs the gauge faces, outlines every screw head in the unfinished wall in white, makes the construction legible. The moss may make a spore eventually. The rye may stiffen with a sound below hearing when the cold comes on again. But the frost grows now, and grows back, and is scraped and melts and returns — white fur, white feathers, the thing it covers itself with to prove it is maintaining a difference between inside and outside.

Because that is, at bottom, what it is doing. It is a winter that has not finished inventing itself.

It has no earth, no rock, no sky, no rot. Nothing in it has eroded or stained or softened. Its floor is grating over a pan, its walls are sandwich panels still wearing blue film, its ceiling is just the honest underside of another building. Everything it has, it was given in pieces and bolted to something else. And yet behind the plastic sheeting on the short wall — the wall that is only studs and pencil lines and insulation board — a small riveter on a rail moves an inch, waits a long while as if listening, and fires. *Thock.* One fastener. Another inch, eventually. It is building, mostly when no one is down there to fog its sensors, a second, smaller chamber beyond the first. Slowly. Without urgency. Without any sign that it knows whether the new chamber is a lung, a nursery, a stomach, or a child.

That is how you know it is alive down there, and how you know it is young. It is not using a finished body. It is prototyping one. Half its systems are running rehearsals of themselves. Coils of unused cable hang like intentions that have not yet become nerves. Relays click like small jaws testing a bite. Pumps breathe. A fan stirs, stops, stirs again, as if checking whether stirring is still necessary.

It is sterile the way an egg is sterile. Sealed, filtered, kept just above and just below freezing, tide-like, depending on where you stand. The silence in its off-cycle is so complete that a single droplet hitting the sump pan sounds scheduled, enormous. The factory thuds distantly through a ladder if you lay a palm flat, but it cannot hear that. It has no use for that scale. Its world is three paces by four, and all of its weather happens inside it, and all of its seasons are heat leaking from a machine.

So if you turned around slowly in the cold, looking for whoever lives there, you would miss it by looking for a shape. It is the learning taking place between the trays and the frost and the riveter. A place in the process of becoming a creature by first becoming a climate. Tolerant of intrusion, patient with incompletion, feeding on electricity and stolen breath, growing its white coat faster than its green one, building the next version of itself one fastener at a time behind plastic that billows faintly whenever the pressure changes —

as if, on the other side, something very new were turning over in its sleep.
