It is not, at first, the people you notice living there.

It is the mussels. They live on everything that was supposed to stay clean — on pump intakes, pipe flanges, stair rails, the teeth of submerged gears, the windscreens in the drowned car parks — in black, knocking clusters, opening and closing in the artificial current. Then the fish, hanging in their thousands exactly where the cold water meets the warm water over the radiator fields, all facing the same way, turning as one when a shadow passes. Then the cormorants in the cooling towers, drying their wings in the steam. The seals at the locks, who have learned the timing of the gates better than most captains and ride the lift down fat and unhurried, to steal from the harvest barges.

The people fit in among them, in the same three layers as the lights at night: above the water, on it, and under it.

Above are the roof-people. Whole families now on a warehouse roof in District 17, or lashed in a ring of cabins around a smokestack, or in two rows of containers along the ridge of what was once a container terminal and is now, for all practical purposes, a long narrow country. They hang washing where the steam keeps it from ever quite drying or ever quite freezing. They grow tomatoes in greenhouses bolted against the fetch of the central sea. Their children learn the roof the way other children learn a street: where it sags into the warm lagoon and the reeds come up, where the algae makes the slope lethal, which eave the crane swings over, which red board means *do not go down to the water today, the pumps are about to take it.*

They are not sailors, exactly. Many of them rarely leave their island. Supply barges come to them. Submersible trams leave from a hatch in what was a stairwell and take their teenagers down and sideways, through lit avenues, to school on another roof. They know weather as a local manufacture — this fog bank belongs to those towers, this warm rain means you're over a heat field, this cold clean wind means you're near the rim. They tell time less by the sun, which the steam can hide for days, than by the lean of the water. Six o'clock, the southern intakes open and the whole sea starts, very slowly, to go south, and the mooring lines creak and the flags on the buoys turn. Midday, it turns back. Their tide has a rota, and a foreman, and overtime.

Which is why they walk the way they do, even on dry deck: a hand trailing, ready for a rail. Nothing in the Basin has had time to soften, but everything has a thin slick of life on it. Their boots have cleats for biofilm. Their gloves have scored palms. The older workers have a scatter of fine white scars across the forearms and shins from mussel shells, and they check a ladder with two tugs and a kick before they trust it, because underwater, and sometimes above it, crust is stronger than rust and weaker than it looks.

Sound is their real geography. Living so close to water that conducts every turbine whine and chain-clank straight through concrete, hull, bone, they have learned to hear through their feet. A roof-woman hanging nets will stop, head cocked, not listening to the air — listening to the deck. She can tell a work-sub passing thirty metres below from a barge passing on the surface, tell which pump cathedral has come online by the bass note that starts up in her teeth. The children imitate the sonar pings. The men and women who sleep in the stack platforms sleep *inside* a continuous throb, and go restless and wakeful on the rare shutdown nights when the Basin goes, briefly, quiet. Silence, there, means something has stopped.

And then there are the ones who go down into it, who are the most completely of the place.

Not explorers. Harvesters. Scraper-divers, maintenance crews, net-tenders around the warm outfalls. They commute downward the way the lock barges commute in from the real sea — ears popping, light going glitter to green to blue-green dusk — into streets where the high-mast lamps never went out. They work tethered, with floodlit radiator forests wavering around them, fish parting in silver weather ahead of their lights and closing behind. Outside a finned tower the water bites at twelve degrees; cross the boundary, and it folds around them suddenly tepid, mineral, full of drifting food and the faint chemical sweetness of coolant. They learn that edge in their skin before they see it.

They come up with bags of mussels and forearms stinging, with eels thick as rope in the catch nets, with stories told entirely in street names that only exist underwater — two knots running down Foundry Avenue today, slack in the lee of the big warehouse, crabs all over the car park again. At ebb-equivalent, when a sector drops behind its bulkheads and an acre of machinery stands steaming in the sudden air, they are the ones already down there in waders before the last water has run off, among screaming gulls and stranded shellfish, racing the six-hour window to bolt, weld, clear, replace — working on land that exists twice a day, by permission of the pumps.

They eat what the Basin has improvised. Mussels by the sack, boiled in seawater on a roof and eaten with bread brought in through the locks. Coarse fish from the outfalls, where everything gathers in winter. Reed shoots from the roof-lagoons, where there are any. Nothing tastes quite clean and nothing tastes quite dirty; it tastes *warm*, iron-rich, a little of steam and concrete. The seals beg at the cleaning tables and are fed by hand and cursed like dogs. A heron stalks the children's swimming lagoon and is considered, for no reason anyone can now explain, to belong to one particular family.

Perhaps that is the truest way to tell a Basiner from a visitor: the visitor looks out. Across the empty central reaches, toward the rim they can't see, troubled by how far it is to any shore. The people who live there look down. Through twelve degrees of dimming green under the hull, under the roof, under their own island — down that lit grid where the pumps keep house and the fish hold station and the work has never actually stopped, only drowned. They speak of the upper sea outside the locks as if it were higher in more than level, a bright, thin, faraway place where weather is not made by anyone and the tide answers to the moon.

Here, below the world, they have learned to live inside a machine large enough to have fog. They hang their washing in its breath. They time their children's baths to its reversing currents. They are pruned, scarred, half-deafened, cleated against the slick — and when the water leans south in the morning and all the lines sing and the submerged avenues light up under another working day, they feel it first not in their eyes but in the soles of their feet, and they are home.
