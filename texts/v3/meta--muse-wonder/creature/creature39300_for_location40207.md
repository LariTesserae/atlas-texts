No one lives there, in the way people mean living in a place. The keepers do not. They come up the ladder with their wooden tongs and their woven nets, they hold their breath and go under the lintel, they work for a while in the warm dark tapping signals against the stone, and then they rise again into the thin, killing air of the summit. They belong to the wind and the scree. They are only visitors, however many generations they have visited.

The ones who live there have never left. The living are never lifted out at all.

At first, swimming, you would say it is the fish. They are what moves to meet you in the middle water. Hand-long, hand-pale, drifting at the level of your face with a slowness that looks at first like stupidity and then reveals itself as something else entirely — a complete absence of fear, of haste, of any reason to hurry that the world has ever been able to teach them. Their eyes are still there under the skin, you can find the faint seam of them, but the skin has grown over in a pearl-white blindness, smooth as the faces on the lower wall. They navigate by taste. Their whiskers go before them, fine and trembling, reading the water the way your fingers would read those shallow, braided inscriptions: pressure, mineral, warmth, the faint chemistry of another body having passed this way an hour ago.

They do not startle when you come. They turn aside at the last moment, unhurried, and resume. Some of them swim upside-down along the vaulted roof, mouths to the stone, grazing the crust there as if the ceiling were simply another floor that happened to be above them. In here direction has relaxed. There is no surface to rise to for most of the year, no bottom that was ever meant to be a bottom. There is only wall and wall and water between.

But the fish are only the most visible nation, and visibility means almost nothing in a place where the one shaft of light dies an arm's length down. Lower, along the walls, the Cistern is paved with mussels.

They overlap like shingles, like scales, thousands of them cemented into the lower rings where the current from the pipes is slowest, opening and closing on a timescale you have to learn to perceive. A mussel shutting is not an event. It is a decision the whole wall makes over the course of an afternoon. Between them, in the seams and the silt, are the snails — and if you stay long enough, if your breaths in the trapped air pockets get slow enough to match the place, you come to understand that the snails are the true population. Tens of thousands of them. A gravel of them on the stairs, a beading of them along the overflow channel, a slow snow of small, amber, translucent shells over every flagstone.

Each grazes a territory no wider than your palm and does not leave it. Each lays down, over a life that may outlast the keeper who tends it, a polished meander in the mineral film — a path you can feel with your fingertips as a smoothness within smoothness, a record of appetite written, and simultaneously erased, at the speed of a mouth. This is how the names went. Not by flood or by vandalism. Face by face, letter by letter, the snails ate the makers into anonymity.

In the niches, where a statue's lap or a feeding trough makes a pocket of stiller water, live the shrimp. You do not see them at first. You see a density, a faint thickening of the shadow, a salt-like grain against the stone. Then your hand passes too close and the niche empties itself backward in a single coordinated flick — hundreds of them, glass-pink, their organs showing through their shells like thoughts — and they are gone into cracks too narrow for any fish to follow. They reassemble when you have been still long enough to be reclassified as furniture.

Along the tops of the partition walls, where the baffles break the Cistern into its slow cells, the eels work. Glass eels, nearly transparent, laboring along the stone edge with a muscular patience, heads lifted slightly, tasting. You can see the small dark thread of their gut, the bead of their heart. They have not been taught hurry either. Nothing here has. A crab picking through the silt beds that have gathered on the drowned stairs will take a full minute to consider a grain before it takes it. Even predation is dilated. Things eat each other here, mostly — that is the economy, that and the insects that blunder in through the roof vents in summer and drown, and the mineral sweat leaching through the mortar, and twice a year the sunken tray of pounded grain and dried meat that disappears over days, not minutes — but nothing feeds in a frenzy. A mouth closes. Over hours, something is gone.

Around the mouths of the clay pipes, where the seep comes in at a finger's width a season and the water moves just enough to be a rumor of movement, the sponges have built. Everything in you wants to call them plants, or fungi, or mineral growths, because they do not do anything you can catch them doing. Soft, fingered colonies, white and ochre, fanning out around the pipe mouths like patient hands held into a stream. They filter a current too slow to feel against your cheek. The snow that falls when you brush the roof colonies — that slow white descending of particles, settling, being eaten — that is them too, and the fish, and the crust, all shedding into each other. The Cistern feeds on its own slow weather.

And then there is the deep cell.

Even the keepers do not go there. A long scrape on the wall: turn back, you have gone far enough. It is for the breeding fish, and no hand enters there, ever. So no one now living above the water has seen them, if seeing would even be the word. What is known is known by other means: a displacement felt through a partition wall when something larger than a hand-long fish turns in the dark on the other side; a seasonal thickening of young fish in the middle water, arriving as if issued; whiskers, sometimes, longer and heavier than any whisker in the outer cells, tasting through the gap under the baffle and withdrawing again.

Old fish, perhaps. Or a fish. Or a few who have been growing down there on snails and drowned insects and each other for longer than the keepers' custom can account for, in water that fell as rain before their tenders were born, growing at the speed everything grows here, which is to say past the point where growth looks like change. Their young come out. They do not. It may be the oldest agreement in the Cistern: the outer rings for the tended and the harvested dead, the center kept.

Outside, on the bare summit, the drum gives no sign of any of this. It squats in the wind, windowless, rimed in winter, drinking sun through its black roof all summer and giving the heat back into the dark all night. It breathes a faint mineral steam from its vents. But under the lintel the cold stops, and the blood-warm water closes over you, and the nations resume around you at their own pace — fish turning aside, mussels considering, shrimp reassembling in the niches, snails continuing their minute, polished work of forgetting everyone's names — indifferent, not hostile, simply complete without you, in a sea they have made out of a reservoir, above the clouds, in the dark.
