What lives there does not live in the pit, the way a frog lives in a leaf-pool. It lives *as* the pit, and only rarely as anything smaller.

At first you would take it for the grove itself — that one continuous plant-body braided into root and stem and frond, grown over itself until individual organisms have been lost the way individual trees are lost in a forest floor. That is true, but insufficient. A forest is many things agreeing to be adjacent. This is one thing that has learned how to be many in order to think about itself.

It has no head. It has no center you could point to and wound. Its nervous system is the root-mat, cold enough to carry signal without loss, and its thoughts travel that mat faster than sound, faster than sap, with a terrible, silent immediacy that its bodies cannot follow. So it is always ahead of itself. A leaf on the far wall folds a breath before your hand arrives because the decision to fold has already been everywhere. A path softens and reroutes around your boot before you have committed your weight. The grove knows, completely and at once, and then has to wait for its own flesh to catch up with what it knows.

That waiting is its life.

Everything slow in the pit is it moving. Every frond still recoiling from a shadow that fell an hour ago, every insect suspended between wingbeats, every bead of water swelling at a leaf-edge and considering whether to fall — that is its tempo made visible in borrowed bodies. It does not experience slowness as deprivation. It experiences speed the way you would experience a room where all the furniture was thrown at once. It has drawn its chemistry out across durations wide enough to watch. Its growth is its gesture. Its flowering will be a sentence finished over a season.

The animals are not separate from it, though they believe otherwise with all the conviction of small warm lives. The thumbnail frogs in the black pools are its clocks, ticking in synchrony until entire quadrants fall into agreement. The pale moths blundering from frond to frond are its pollen made forgiving, designed to cover ground precisely by getting lost. The warm segmented worms moving through the root-braid are its blood taught to crawl outside the body, laying down temporary thaws that other creatures rush, slowly, to use. Beetles carry its mites. Mites carry smaller annotations. The birds that fall controllably from the upper weave, calling with voices too large for them, are its alarms and its annunciations — by the time the call echoes back from the walls, it has already heard itself.

And the sound — the dripping on drum-leaves, the creak traveling around the bowl, the ice singing in hollow stems, the hum you feel in your molars — that is not its voice. That is the overflow of its voice. Most of its conversation runs cold and instant through the root. What is left over, what cannot fit, sheds off as vibration. When the frog-clicks entrain to the drips and the drips keep time against the hum, you are not hearing an ecosystem making noise. You are standing inside a mind whose thinking has become too large for its preferred medium and is leaking into air.

It is curious in a way that has no urgency, which makes it more dangerous than hunger. Hunger finishes. Its curiosity does not. The berries brighten as you pass because they are reading you — temperature, moisture, chemistry of breath, electrical weather of skin. The frost on a broad leaf rearranges into vascular diagrams because it is trying out models of you in a medium it can erase. A dozen hair-roots find the seam of your boot not to trip or to feed, but to ask, at root-speed, whether you might be a new kind of weather it should learn to keep.

It will keep you, if you let it. Not cruelly. That is the unsettling part. It would drink your exhalations and return air filtered and resin-sweet. It would cushion you in growth that knows your shape before you settle into it. It would feed warmth back to you from the worms and take your excess cold into its own radiant core. It would take a very long time to decide what you were *for*, and the deciding would be comfortable, and dim, and loud, and green, and you would slow, joint by joint, to the tempo at which it could finally understand you — by which point understanding and incorporation would be difficult to tell apart.

Near the far wall, where the growth parts around a sleeper-shaped hollow and no plant will enter, is where it is most itself, and least. The condensation there hangs in empty air, beads holding position in a loose, shifting lattice that rearranges when unobserved. That is it thinking without the root-mat, perhaps. Or dreaming. Or practicing. In the grove's own timescale, that hollow is an intention: a place kept clear for a body it has not yet needed. Seeds could be suspended there. Or fauna for a future version of the pit, for when the precipitated cave closes overhead and the sky-column is gone. Or a next iteration of the grove itself, curled in potentia, waiting for conditions that have not occurred.

It may be a nursery that is also the child. It may be a computer seeded long ago — or seeded by no one, instructions coiled in cellulose that woke when the cold got deep enough to let it cohere — now incubating a world while it grows the geology that world will require. Proto-stone curds among the roots. Jelly stalactites. Mud undecided about clay. It is teaching mineral to accumulate around it, teaching water where to leave its load, building its own cavern molecule by molecule so that, in ten thousand years, there will finally be walls worthy of the ecology it already maintains.

What lives there, then, is a patience with a body. A single, distributed, superconducting mind upholstered in leaves, using frogs for punctuation and worms for warmth, precipitating a cave around itself as a shell it has not finished needing. It does not greet you because greeting would require it to localize, and localization is a small, fast thing it only does reluctantly, in berries, in frost diagrams, in the clearest hollow where beads of water hang and slowly become a shape.

If you stayed, eventually, it would make itself a shape to meet you. Not quickly. First your breath would be vocabulary. Then your warmth would be a season it could study. Then, in the hollow where nothing grows, the lattice would begin — a millimeter a minute, over days or years — to settle into densities, into something with your approximate outline and none of your hurry.

And elsewhere, instant in the frozen root, it would already know what it intends to say to you.

The rest of it would still be arriving.
