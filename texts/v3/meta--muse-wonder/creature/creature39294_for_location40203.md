At first, no one lives there.

That is not a trick of emptiness, the way a desert is empty or a new house is empty. A desert has lizards under the stones. A new house has dust already gathering in the corners, deciding to become permanent. Here the dust is scraped up before it can settle. The pools have no insects. The courtyards have animals, but they are fired clay on plinths, low and smooth and speciesless, with a blank rectangle left on each flank where a name will go later. Their eyes have not been cut. They do not watch, exactly. They practice being watched.

You can walk for three days across the steaming tile and meet nothing with a pulse. And still you will never, in all that walking, feel unwitnessed.

The tools see to that.

A mallet left on a wall at dusk will be warm in a new place at dawn, its face powdered with fresh lime. A mixing trough will be turning, slowly, the paddle shouldering through slurry with the patience of something chewing — and if you stare at it, it stops, not guiltily, not suddenly, but with the air of a thing that was never moving at all and is surprised you thought otherwise. Look away to light a lamp — though the lamps light themselves, sweating their stone-oil up through their own clay throats — and when you look back the paddle has advanced half a turn. Rope creaks on a hoist beside a scaffold that encloses nothing. The nothing, by morning, has a tower in it.

So the first resident you invent is a builder. Shy. Peripheral. Very large, or very many. You begin, as all travelers here begin, by leaving courtesies for it: tools laid out in a row instead of dropped, a trough refilled, a chisel set edge-up so it can be found in the dark. In the morning the row will be gone and a wall that did not exist will be standing a league off, still wet, and somewhere in it, if you search long enough, your chisel. Sealed in. Not lost. Filed.

It does not want your help. It wants, if it wants anything you can name, not to be caught in the act of having hands.

Stay longer and the builder resolves less, not more. It is not a crew working when you sleep. Sleep here is shallow and steam-filled and you wake often, and the avenues have migrated anyway, in silence, around your sleeping body, carefully, the way water goes around a stone. It is not invisible men. It is not gods with copper gloves. It seems, increasingly, to be the place itself doing what a body does when no one is describing it: shifting weight. Healing over. Growing a stair where a stair is about to be needed. A tile lifts, and under it is not dirt, not a burrow, not a root, but another tile, newer, undecided — and under that scaffolding, and under that more scaffolding, wet mortar breathing in the dark. If there is a creature, it lives between the floors. It *is* the between. The world has no underside because the inhabitant is all underside, everywhere, pressing gently up.

Its habits are architectural rather than animal, but they are habits.

It numbers things. This is its way of holding them in mind. Placeholder trees are numbered at the base, where the bark would begin if bark had been invented. Boulders in the stockpiles — the rehearsal mountains — are each chiseled with the coordinates of the peak they will someday join. Bricks carry their future addresses. It is a census-taker with no population yet, writing down where everyone will have lived. Sometimes you find a stone numbered for a place you have just come from, a place that, when you left it, had no stone missing. Sometimes you find one numbered for you. Not your name. There is nowhere for a name to go yet. Just a low number, fresh-cut, still sharp, on a smooth clay shape in an empty courtyard, waiting for its plaque.

It is fastidious to the point of cruelty about life. Life is a finishing step and it has not been scheduled, and the resident enforces the schedule the way an immune system enforces a body. Let a basin sit undisturbed in the lamplight long enough for a green film to begin — a mere rumor of algae at the tile's edge — and by morning the basin has been repaved, sterilized with kiln-hot tile, its water rerouted through a settling tank carved with instructions no one living wrote. It does not hate the green. That would require it to have decided what green is for. It simply removes it, promptly and without malice, the way you would remove a word from a letter you are not ready to send.

And yet it is building baths.

This is the part that stops being merely strange and starts being lonely, if you stay. Bath complexes a day's walk from anything, basins fed by clay manifolds and no pump, steps worn by no feet — worn, rather, in anticipation, the tread hollowed to the shape a foot will make after ten thousand years of feet. A cistern-house alone in a lake of tile, brimming. An observatory whose copper slit studies the inside of the unfinished sky, whose mosaic floor shows constellations that the painted stars overhead have not agreed to yet. Houses for digestion, for worship, for weather that hasn't been ratified. Doors that open onto more floor — until, on a later visit, they don't, and there is a room behind the door that smells of wet lime and has your footprints already drying in it, leading in.

It is preparing a world for inhabitants it has not invented. Or — this is the thought that comes in the flooded districts, wading ankle-deep down a staircase where every step holds its own private, level flood — it has invented them, and they have already arrived, and they are not the builders.

They are the shadows.

The placeholder trees cast leafy shadows, though the trees are clay and bare and have never borne a leaf. Towers that are still chalk outlines and a smell of rope lie across whole boulevards in correct, angled shade, days before scaffolding gathers. In the late brightening that passes for morning, when the vault-lamps thin the steam, you can see them everywhere once you learn to look down instead of up: shade without casters, pools of cool in shapes too specific to be accidents. A colonnade's shadow, arch by arch, on an avenue where the columns are still numbered in a stockpile three days' walk away and somehow also on the horizon, never nearer. The fringe of a forest canopy over a field of fresh tile. Once, in a finished courtyard — lamps lit, cistern full, everything at that aching pitch of almost — a traveler's shadow fell across the paving accompanied, for the length of one held breath, by others. Not threatening. Domestic. A taller one. A smaller one running a few steps ahead, the way small things run in courtyards, in and out of the shade of trees that are not there.

Then the seams widened and the courtyard politely took itself apart, and the shadows went with the components to wherever such things are stored.

So perhaps the census is not empty. Perhaps the resident population is entirely preliminary: shadows first, then scaffolds in the shape of the thing, then the thing growing warm and wet inside the scaffold like something in an egg, then the brief finished moment, then disassembly, because a finished thing casts only its own shadow and the place cannot bear the reduction. Its true citizens may be the *about-to-be* of everything — the leaf before the tree, the star before the sky, the footprints ahead of you in a doorway that exists only where a ceiling-stalactite of lime grows down and kisses a pillar growing up, and on the other side the lamps burn on the reversed side of their niches and your wet prints lead away in front of you, already setting into tile, making a road that other travelers' maps will disagree with.

You will try to leave, of course. Everyone does, about the time they notice their own shadow has begun arriving in rooms slightly before they do. It isn't that the floor prevents you. Prevention is a finished law, and the laws here are still roped off with copper cord and little clay bells that ring only after you've crossed. It's that distance is unratified, and the stockpile on the horizon can stay on the horizon for three days, and the paving unspools fresh-laid behind you, taking your footprints into the permanent record, and one evening you will set down your mallet to rub your eyes — mallet? You don't remember picking up a mallet, but your hands are powdered with lime and it seems rude to drop it — and you will notice that you have been, for some time, laying the tools out in rows. Edge-up. So they can be found in the dark. By whom is a question the steam declines.

And the next traveler, crossing that district in the dripping quiet, will hear, hours later and miles away, the soft ceramic *clack* of two vast things meeting with unnatural precision, and will look around for builders and see none — only a trough turning when not watched, a hoist creaking beside an empty scaffold, a lone wall with a door and a lamp and nothing behind the door but floor.

Until a later visit. When there is.

No one lives there. That remains true. It is just that "no one" turns out to be a very large, very patient, very busy category — the handless builder between the floors, the leaf-shadows waiting for their trees, the numbered animals waiting for their names and eyes, the future tenants whose baths are already warm — and if you stay long enough, wading the ankle-deep stairs, passing the temporary doorways, breathing the hot copper air that smells faintly from inside your own chest, the category makes room. It numbers a plinth. It leaves a blank space where the plaque should go.

It has all the time in the world, because time, too, is still under construction, and it is in no hurry to schedule the finishing step that would make you — finally, dangerously — alive.
