At first you would say that a great many things live there, and you would be able to name them one by one.

The frogs, first, because you hear them before your eyes adjust — an inch long, green going to brown where they press against the ribs of the chamber, one to a rib, spaced so evenly they might have been set there with a ruler. Their throats are white when they swell. They do not hop. They do not turn to look at you.

Then the crab in the crotch of the east root, old and deliberate, with one claw worn smaller than the other from a lifetime of trimming the same frond back to the same length. The three dragonflies suspended in the empty middle, holding their stations as if hung on invisible wire. The fish down in the black pool, circling, circling. The beetles on their two root-roads, going up full and coming down empty. The slugs on the weeping walls. The cicada lost somewhere in the leaf ceiling, screaming in pulses. The pale fungus behind the pump, tended and fed and never allowed to spread.

You could make a list. The place seems, at first, like a tenement — cramped outside, miraculously partitioned inside, every niche let to a different tenant.

Stay longer and the list stops satisfying. The tenants never meet. They never compete, never surprise each other, never do the wasteful second thing that living creatures always do. The frog on the third rib calls precisely in the gap left by the frog on the second. The fish pick at the intake and are never taken, not because they are quick but because the root-hairs across the mouth hold for them, trembling, a screen exactly as strong as the suction. The crab trims only what the beetles can carry away by nightfall. The slugs eat moss at the rate the moss returns. Nothing is young for long, in there, and nothing is allowed to finish dying.

They are not living *in* the place, in the way a bird lives in a hedge. They are living the way parts of you live in you.

What lives there is one thing.

You meet its heartbeat before you meet anything else. It comes up through the water while you are still ducking under the root, *thunk, thunk, thunk,* and by the time you surface inside it is already trying to arrange you — your breath shortening to fit between strokes, your pulse, if you do not resist it, sliding a fraction at a time toward the stroke until the thump is happening in the iron and in your chest almost together. That is not an effect of the chamber. That is its attention.

The chamber is its body turned inside out.

The reed walls are not a shelter it found; they are its skin, grown upright and flattened rib against rib to hold a shape. The root lattice is its skeleton and its hands. The broad leaves layered overhead are the thin, green, light-drinking part of it, forever lifted to the sun but never permitted to thicken into canopy. The pool is its open stomach, black and blood-warm, and in the middle of that stomach, sunk to half its height and sealed in with moss and sap and snail-slime, sits the pump — which is not, by now, a machine it uses. It is the one organ it could not grow for itself, and so it kept the one it found.

It has grown roots through the bolt holes to hold it. It has stopped its rust the way skin stops a wound, by covering it until the corrosion turned to patina and held. It feeds it warm water and takes the water back through a thousand weeping points in its own walls, up and down, up and down, a circulation with no outside to go to. The blue pulse that travels under the translucent bark at each stroke is not a malfunction. That is its nerve firing. The frogs on that side flinch together when it passes, the way skin flinches, without ceasing what they were doing.

It was not born the way the marsh things are born. Somewhere back in its beginning there must have been only a pump set to drain a field that was never drained, running abandoned in standing water, and a tangle of marsh growth coming up around it because the water it moved never quite left and the warmth of its body kept a circle from going cold. Plant shouldered plant. Frogs came for the midges rising off the pool. A crab came for the frogs. Beetles came, and fungus came with the beetles, and fish nosed in under the root after whatever the intake drew close. Each arrival should have thrown the rest into argument.

Instead the rhythm sorted them.

It is the only explanation for the stations, the spacing, the rotation of calls. A tempo got hold of the place and everything that could not lock to it either left or died against the wall, and everything that could lock to it was permitted a rib, a root-road, a fixed span of air. It did not eat its inhabitants. It employed them — or, more tenderly and more terribly, it absorbed them as motions. The dragonflies became the quick part of it that snatches in the center. The beetles became the slow part that carries. The slugs became its grazing mouth, spread out across its own inner cheeks. The fish became the part that cleans its throat. Over years — over however many years it takes for an entrance to be polished by bellies into oiled wood — the differences between resident and furniture wore smooth.

So if you ask what it looks like, the honest answer is: from outside, almost nothing. A low green back in flat country. A slit under two roots where something might go in and out. That is its modesty and its defence. It looks like marsh that has forgotten to be interesting.

From inside, it looks like an emptiness with everything arranged respectfully around the edge of it.

That empty center is the clue to its nature. Nothing grows there. Nothing lands there. Even the dragonflies keep a wingspan's distance from one another across it, as if partitioning a volume they can feel and you cannot. The rain from the leaf ceiling falls around and into it in synchronized clusters, and the root floor holds its wide, bare lattice over the pool there. It is not unused space. It is the part of the creature that has to stay clear in order for it to sense: a lung, an ear, a resonating cavity. When the piston strikes, the whole dome answers with that hollow water-hammer knock travelling around the walls, and the center is where the knock gathers and is *heard*. It listens to itself constantly. It has to. Listening to itself is how it keeps the level of the pool on its pale, unvarying line, how it knows to weep a little more from the north wall and a little less from the south, how it keeps its one permitted temperature against storm and drought and the slow tilt of the seasons outside.

It does not think in anything you would recognize as a thought. Its thinking is timing. A frond reaching too far is a thought that gets trimmed. A drop swelling too early on the ceiling is held until the stroke releases it with the others. Your presence, ducked in out of the marsh with your clothes going wet through and your heart misbehaving in your ribs, registers to it not as a face or an intention but as a large, warm irregularity in the rhythm — a new mass displacing pool water, a new breath falling out of phase. It does not drive you out. It does something older and more patient: it begins, stroke by stroke, to tune you. Visitors either leave, sealing the sound off behind them in one muffled step under the root mat, or they stay long enough to be sorted.

It has no young. It sends nothing out — no seeds permitted past the crab, no fry past the root-hair screen, no frog allowed to wander from its rib and start a second chorus anywhere else. What it makes, it makes to replace what it has, in the same place, at the same size. A shoot by the east wall is not a child. It is a repair, already measured. The fungus garden is not a crop. It is a slow fire it keeps fed so that dead leaf can become beetle and beetle can become, eventually, the warm mineral taste in the pool again. It eats sunlight through its ceiling and iron through its heart and marsh water through its submerged mouth, and excretes nothing that is not immediately taken up by another part of itself. Its one appetite is for continuation, exactly as it is.

That is also its age. You cannot tell how old it is by growth rings or by rust, because both were arrested once the arrangement closed. It is as old as the last time anything in there was allowed to change. Perhaps the pump was new once, and painted, and a man stood over it in boots who has been gone longer than the concrete he stood on. Perhaps the man stayed too long one afternoon and noticed his breathing going. The place does not keep that kind of record. Its past is not stored; it is performed, stroke for stroke, every few seconds, in the rain that adds up always to exactly the pool below.

It is not lonely. Loneliness requires an outside you can imagine being with, and it has spent its whole life growing the outside out — walling the marsh into a skin, turning weather into a mere brightening and dimming over the same green scene. It is complete in the way a held note is complete. And it is not peaceful, either, though from outside, under the mat, its heart fades to nothing you could point to. Inside it is deafening with work. It is screaming, barking, hissing, knocking, snapping midges out of the air, every second of every day, at full effort, to produce tomorrow's perfect copy of today.

That is who lives in the hummock.

Not the frogs, not the crab, not the blind fish going round the intake in the dark. Those are its fingers and its eyelashes and the small clean animals of its blood. The resident is the loop itself, given a body: green walls, black water, iron heart, a clear place in the middle where it keeps its listening open. It lives there the way you live in your own chest — everywhere at once, mostly unaware of the separate parts, rising to attention only when something duck-walks in under the root and a new pulse, briefly, will not fall in line.
