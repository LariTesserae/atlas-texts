No one lives there in the way a house is lived in.

That becomes clear if you stay long enough to look for the usual signs. No nest tucked under the ledges, though there are ledges in plenty. No burrow in the moss, no droppings, no gnawed wood, no midden, no path worn by feet coming and going to water. Nothing hunts there. Nothing is hunted. A vole crosses a boot and continues, unhurried, as if it has wandered into a room where the ordinary rules of alarm no longer apply. A raven comes to the unfinished wall and sits with its beak closed.

The human shelters on the east side only make the absence clearer. They are built the way you would build if you were trying to be near something without presuming to share its space: backs to the wall, faces turned away from the centre, roofs low, everything made of stone and split wood and turf that can be left damp for a season and found damp again when you return. Someone slept there, poor and briefly, on hazel woven thin over cold air. Someone drank condensation out of a trough and hammered the trough a little deeper when the drip moved sidewise in its course down the rock. Someone wore smooth a patch of wall at hand height, pressing a palm flat and tapping with the fingers, saying whatever needed saying through stone.

They were not residents. They were attendants. Or visitors who had learned attendance was the only safe form of visitation.

Because the hollow is already occupied.

You feel the occupant before you see anything of it, which is fitting, because it has no surface to see. At the rim your ears pop not from descent but from meeting it — a pressure standing in the bowl like water stands in a well, except that this water is moving, always, upward and outward in a slow spiral, and it is made entirely of sound. It takes you first in the sternum. Then in the teeth. Then in the backs of your hands, where the skin is thin enough to admit that the roar is not approaching you. You are inside it. It is around you the way weather is around you, except weather passes.

This does not.

Far below the fissure in the floor, caves run through the limestone to the sea cliffs, and the tide works in them twice a day, pushing air ahead of the water as it enters, drawing it back as it leaves. The hill adds its own slower breath, cold air sinking and warming air rising as the temperature turns above ground. All of that vast, dispersed breathing is gathered, funnelled, and forced to speak through one black crack no wider than a man's shoulders. The bowl above the crack is the mouth that shapes it. Or the ear that holds it. After a few thousand years the distinction has worn away, the way the spiral grooves have worn into the walls.

It lives in that shape, and only in that shape. Two steps beyond the rim it does not follow you. It cannot. Outside the hollow it would come apart into ordinary wind and ordinary silence, the way a whirlpool cannot leave its river. Inside, it is continuous and coherent and almost improbably old: a single note sustained longer than any forest around it, with a hiss tearing along its upper edge and, deep underneath, the occasional knock of stones shifting in the dark passages below, which arrives through the soles of your boots like a second, irregular heartbeat.

It has habits, which is another way of knowing it is alive there and not merely happening there.

It keeps its floor clean. Nothing loose is allowed to remain in the bottom of the bowl — no leaf, no grit, no fallen twig, no dead insect. Whatever falls in is worried back and forth across the bare limestone, abraded, lifted at last in the turning air and carried up and out, to be laid down at the rim in sorted drifts. Pine needles here. White pebbles there. A crescent of beetle shells, hollow and shining, arranged with a precision no one would claim and no one can quite dismiss. It is housekeeping at a geological pace and also a kind of feeding: the occupant takes the small dry deadfall of the forest, grinds it against stone, and expels what it cannot use over its own threshold. The drifts at the lip are its leavings. The swept dish of the floor is its one clear piece of territory, defended without malice and without exception.

It grooms the walls too, though more slowly. The spiral grooving in the limestone is its work over centuries, each groove deepened by the width of a hair in a year, the whole bowl gradually assuming the shape of its own movement, like the inside of a shell assuming the shape of the animal. It does not build in any human season. Builders came and began a fourth shelter on the south side and got it knee-high and stopped, and the pile of stones they sorted by size is still there, in order, waiting with an indifference that exposes them: the pile belongs to an afternoon. The groove beside it belongs to a millennium. Whatever stopped those builders — deafness, nosebleed, a winter, a death in some village now gone from the flat woods — the occupant did not notice, and did not pause, and has not paused since.

Much of what grows in the bowl grows in relation to it, the way things grow around a large animal that does not eat them. The moss is thickest where the blast is broken. The liverworts flatten themselves into the lee of every bulge. The ferns have gone waxy and thick-fronded, built to tremble without tearing, and they tremble all day, all of them, so that from the rim the walls look faintly animal already, shivering in a green half-dark. Condensation beads on the cold stone because the breath rising from the caves is a fraction warmer than the air pooled above it; the moss catches that moisture and reaches downward in long filaments toward the floor, and the filaments never arrive. The moving air dries them back. Year after year they extend and retreat, extend and retreat, feeling for a ground they are not permitted to touch. It is hard not to read that as acquaintance. Things that live pressed close to the occupant learn its reach exactly.

It cannot be looked at directly, but it can be made visible against its will, which is why flame is forbidden down there. A lamp lies flat and gutters blue and goes out, and its smoke, before it dies, writes the whole body of the thing in the air for an instant: the spiral rising, the shear where the upward breath meets the trapped cold, the pocket behind the east ledge where the terror of the noise drops merely to terribleness. That pocket is where the attendants slept. It is the hollow's one fold of shelter, and they took it the way small fish take the slack behind a stone in a current — gratefully, and without imagining the river had made it for them.

Its moods, if they are moods, are tidal. Over hours the roar thickens and sinks as water far away, out of sight, in darkness under the headland, begins to push into the cave mouths and compress the air ahead of it. Then your molars ache with it, and conversation, which was already impossible, becomes a kind of joke your throat attempts and abandons. Over hours it thins again, at the turn, never to quiet, never anywhere near quiet, but to a state in which you can almost imagine the shape of a voice inside it — and that may be the most dangerous hour, because people must historically have leaned toward that almost-voice, cupped their ears, stayed a few minutes past prudence, pressed their palms to the worn patch in the shelter wall and tapped faster. It has no voice. That is worth insisting on. The attendants learned to speak with their hands precisely because they had given up being answered. What rises out of the fissure is breath, not utterance. It does not call the raven, and the raven, sensibly, never calls back. They keep company the way cliff and sea keep company: by proximity, over time, without exchange.

As for what it is like in itself — if a thing so entirely made of passage can be said to have an inside — consider that it is deaf by definition and blind by architecture, and that its whole knowledge of the world must arrive as pressure and grit and temperature. It knows the tide as a swelling in its own chest twice a day. It knows the seasons as changes in the density of the air it is fed, and as frost thickening on the undersides of its ledges, and as the summer forest dropping different litter onto its floor to be sorted. It knows a human being, when one descends the bark rope with a mallet and a basket, as a brief obstruction: a warmth standing in the spiral, a softness that alters the hiss where it passes, eyes watering, mouth held slightly open so the pressure has somewhere to go. Then the obstruction climbs out, the spiral closes behind it, and the bowl is again exactly as loud as it was — for nobody, for the black moss and the trembling ferns and the sorted shells at the rim.

Which returns a kind of dignity to those three low shelters, and to the rotted sleeping platform, and to the stack of dried moss bricks someone cut, perhaps to stop their ears or to stop a gap in a turf roof, and never came back to use. They suggest that for a long while — long enough for the thatch of living branches above the rim to be woven, and to grow wild, and for the rope handrail to go soft as cloth — people understood that this was a neighbour and not a resource. They maintained the troughs so the next visitor could drink. They left tools where the next mender would expect them. They turned their dwellings away from the centre, not in fear alone but in something like manners, the way you do not stare at a creature whose attention, turned fully toward you, would destroy the organ you'd receive it with.

And when they stopped coming — because villages thin, and roads move, and eventually the knowledge of how long you may safely stay becomes a story and then not even a story — the occupant did not diminish. It does not require worship or maintenance or witness. The troughs go green and hold their water anyway. The moss climbs a thumb's width in a decade anyway. A fern uncurls a fraction each week in the quivering dark.

So that is who lives in the hollow: not the builder, not the bird, not the deaf vole crossing through on an errand of its own. They were all, in their turn, guests in a room that was full before they entered it. The resident is the standing breath of the place itself, gathered up from tide and cave and cold hill and given a throat of limestone, old enough to have carved its chamber into a shell, fastidious enough to keep its floor bare and sort its dead at the door, and solitary in the complete sense — sounding, continuously, at the dark and the wet stone, whether there is anyone at the rim to feel it in their teeth or not.
