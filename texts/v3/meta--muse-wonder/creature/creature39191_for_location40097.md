The people who live in the bowl are never entirely dry, and they do not expect to be.

You notice feet first. Everyone goes barefoot indoors and mostly barefoot out, except on the stair and in the terraces, where they wear wooden pattens strapped on with bark cord — flat, clattering soles that lift them an inch above the wet. A lifetime of slick stone, roof turf, root-matted lanes and stairs dished by water has made them walk differently: weight low, knees soft, toes spread and gripping almost without thought. Children learn it before language. Old people keep it after their knees have gone, feeling for the mossy patch with a toe and going around it while still talking. Their soles are thick as leather. Their toenails are kept very short. Between the toes, if you look, the skin is pale and permanently wrinkled, as a washerwoman's hands are.

Above the feet they are a layered, patched, constantly steaming people.

Nothing they wear stays on a body all day. Wool tunics, bark-cloth aprons, woven reed overshirts oiled until they shine — everything exists in twos and threes, one on, one dripping under an eave, one spread over a balcony rail turning in the wind. Clothes are loose so air can move, tied so vines and horns cannot catch, and dyed in what the place gives easily: alder brown, moss green, madder gone rusty, undyed sheep grey. On a bright interval after rain the whole town changes color as people peel to their lighter layer, and then changes back, snap of awnings, rattle of clappers, when the next shower crosses the rim.

Their hair is worn cropped or braided tight to the head, threaded often with colored wool so you can tell a household across a noisy lane. Beards are rare and short — they hold water and mold. Everyone smells, pleasantly and unavoidably, of woodsmoke, wet wool, goat, green leaves, and the sour-sweet smell of dough kept in a warm kitchen. They comb each other in the evenings on porches, not from vanity but as inspection: for ticks, for lice, for the small green rash that comes in the folds of skin if you let damp sit too long. Babies are rubbed with oil and ash and passed hand to hand to be dried by fires that never quite go out.

They are not tall people, though that may be the bowl as much as blood — generations of carrying water, grain, timber and children up that stair, stooping under balcony and branch, climbing to roofs with a sickle in the teeth. They are broad through the shoulder and calf, wiry rather than heavy, because fat is hard to keep warm when it is always damp and because no one here ever sits for a whole day at one task. Even the weavers rise to clear a channel. Even the miller is up a ladder retying a vine. Rest, for them, is a change of work.

And they are loud, in the way river people are loud. Ordinary speech is at a volume a lowlander would use to call across a field; not angry, simply pitched over water, bells, frogs, geese, millstones, rain on thatch. Consequently their faces are expressive and their hands never stop. A conversation across the market is half shouted and half signed — flat palm for *wait, herd coming*, two fingers hooked for *open the upper sluice*, a circling hand for *after this shower*. Children sign before they speak clearly. Lovers sign across lanes at dusk. Arguments are enormous, theatrical, conducted window to window over the backs of sheep, and end just as suddenly when something needs lifting.

That interruptibility is the center of their character. No one in the bowl plans to finish anything in one go. A wall is woven until the ducks need moving; the weaving is resumed after the shower has been sluiced away; the mushrooms gathered on the way back are strung before the bread, which must be eaten before it softens, which means neighbors are called in, which means someone's roof goat is argued down from the mill path on the way. To a visitor it looks like distraction. It is, in fact, a highly trained attention to sequence. They know by sound which wheel has sped up, which channel is choking, whether that bell means second sluice or lower bridge. They can hear, under the general roar, a goat kid stuck in the hedge, a particular dog returning without its child, rain coming not by the sky but by the frogs changing key.

So most knowledge here lives in ears and hands, not in stores. Iron rusts, paper mildews, ink runs; therefore they keep very little written and remember in work-songs, in knot patterns, in the order you clear the runnels from the spring down. An old woman sitting in a niche by a channel junction — too stiff now for roofs — is not retired. She is infrastructure. She watches water color. She knows which household upstream has washed wool, which has a sick pig, when frogspawn means a clear week. She dips a finger, tastes, raps her clapper twice, and a grandchild is already moving with a rake. The very old become fixed points like that, placed where lanes and waters cross, wrapped in oiled wool even in summer, fed broth and gossip in equal measure, listening for the town for people whose arms are full.

Children are everywhere and belong, practically, to the lane more than to a house. They are born into noise and sleep through geese, bells, night frogs, babies answering babies across the alleys. They are carried first in slings against a warm, damp body, then set down to follow ducks, then given real errands absurdly early because a four-year-old who can run a basket along a balcony plank is genuinely useful. They learn the rules as footing: never step in the drinking runnel, never chase chickens into the mill race, goats may go on roofs but you may not follow onto the mill roof, if you find a channel blocked you clear it even if it is not yours and tell the house below. By eight they can rebind a living wall. By twelve most have spent a season on the stair with the donkeys and come back with calves like wood and a lifelong contempt for straight, dry roads.

They eat what thrives wet and quickly: barley cakes and closed-oven bread eaten still warm, cabbage in every form, beans, onions from the roofs, mushrooms strung in garlands over every kitchen, duck and goose eggs collected daily from improbable places, soft cheese made in the morning and gone by night, pig and old goat in stews that simmer in the perpetual fire. Nothing keeps, so feasting is not stored for festivals; it happens whenever too much is ripe at once, which is often. A glut of apples, a pig slaughtered, a dry hour long enough to bake twice — the lane eats together under the balconies while steam rises off their clothes.

Their houses are not so much owned as maintained by whoever is currently strong enough to keep them from becoming thicket. Marriage, inheritance, fostering — all follow maintenance. You marry into a lane as much as a person, because you are taking on its stretch of channel, its house-tree, its section of hedge. People move often between households for a season of work, and think nothing of it. What shames a person here is not poverty — almost nothing can be accumulated except skill, dry firewood and a good roof — but letting water silt. A dry-lander miser hoards coin. Here the unforgivable man lets leaves rot in his runnel and makes his neighbor's yard flood. The household below will come up with rakes, clear it through his courtyard in silence, and he will have to feed them and be thanked pointedly until he understands.

And they are tender, in a rough, constant, animal way. Because stock cannot be separated, neither can grief or affection. House toads are fed by name. A good duck-herding dog is mourned on a porch with as much ceremony as a grandfather, and may get the better songs. Goats are scolded like cousins. Swallows returning to the same eave-cup are greeted. This is not sentimentality about nature; they cut, cull, slaughter, scythe and trim all day. It is cohabitation learned at close quarters. You cannot pretend, in the bowl, that you live alone in your species. You wake with martins in your roof and mice in your thatch and someone else's baby crying back at yours, and you arrange your soul accordingly: permeable, noisy, amused, exasperated, never sealed.

That is why they do badly, the few who leave, in quiet dry places. They complain that rooms echo, that food keeps suspiciously long, that no one shouts, that their feet cannot feel the floor, that they wake in the night frightened by silence and have to go stand by a well or a stream to sleep again. Those who stay — and most stay, and the stair brings in just enough spouses, traders and foundlings to keep the blood fresh — acquire with age a look visitors mistake for wetness itself: eyes bright from constantly shifting shade and sun, skin weathered and very clean, hair steaming faintly by a fire, hands never idle, voice ready to lift.

In the morning, when the mist is in the lanes and the lamps are still amber in their niches, they are already moving: first up whoever the dogs woke, which is everyone, feeling with bare feet whether the night has moved a stone, lifting a fallen vine off a window, freeing a duck from a woodpile, tasting the channel, listening to the wheels. They step into the town as it rearranges itself around them, and rearrange a little of it back — goat here, stall there, sluice open, child redirected, bread in, awning out — not trying to still the place, only to stay in time with it, like dancers who have lived their whole lives inside one loud, green, falling music.
