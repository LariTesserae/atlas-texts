If you go by who is *always* there, the humans are not the inhabitants of the Sump. They are the most intermittent thing in it.

The permanent population is the one that has learned to live without light.

It begins, if you start at the bottom, with the white mats. They grow only on the hottest pipes, around the heat exchangers in the old power station, thick as wet felt, blind and patient. They do not need sun. They eat warmth and whatever minerals sigh out of the iron, and they pulse, very faintly, when the pumps change rhythm. Where the outflow cools, the mats fray into green algae — long, pale hairs streaming from gratings, from valve wheels, from the lettering on street signs. The algae wave without stopping. They are the only trees here, and they grow sideways, in the current.

Working the mats, endlessly, is a colony of translucent shrimp.

You see them only when a headlamp declares a volume of water and holds it still. Then they are suddenly there by the hundreds, glass-bodied, their organs showing as small dark commas, their legs making a constant, nervous stitching in the black. They pick at the bacterial felt, they carry fragments, they turn waste over and over until it becomes something else the mats can use. Nothing here is wasted because the shrimp do not permit it. A flake of rust, a shred of algae, a dead shrimp — all is gathered up in those transparent claws and redistributed. When a diver passes, they scatter not in panic but in pattern, parting along invisible lanes, and close behind him like water remembering a shape.

A cod sometimes comes in with the tide through the lock gates that no longer quite close. It is not a resident. It is an accident with gills. For days it will circle Market Square at head-height, mouth opening and closing on the cold, bumping softly into netted crates and turning, bumping into the tram. The shrimp ignore it. The crews guide it out again if they can, with lights shuttered on and off in a slow corridor. To kill it would be to introduce a feast and a rot the system would take weeks to absorb. It is easier to let the bewildered thing live and leave.

The sturgeon is another matter.

No one now living has seen her whole. She entered as a fingerling through a burst screen before most of the present rigging was lashed in place, and she has grown in the dark for decades, fed on whatever the tide pushes in and whatever the borough sloughs off. She is too large now for the gates, too large for the sluice. She knows other exits — culverts, cracked settling tanks — but she no longer fits inside her knowledge.

You know her by her effects. A groove kept perfectly clear in the silt down the middle of Tram Street No. 1, where her belly has passed, season after season, wearing the fine grey down to concrete. A crate of bolts nudged aside by exactly the width of her head. A rope, slack for months, found thrumming faintly, though no current should touch it there.

Once, perhaps twice in a season, she makes a full circuit. Doors swing ponderously and settle. The hanging stores in the square sway in layers, though no pump has changed its beat. The shrimp vanish into the mats. In the dry pods up under the ice, someone will put a hand flat to the wall, feel her going by in their sternum like the flywheels, and say nothing. Her passage is treated as an inspection. If she can still move, the currents still move. If she can still move, the Sump has not yet begun to freeze around them.

And then there are the crews.

At most — at most — there are nine. Three in the water, three in the pods, three up top on a surface rotation that is officially a job at an unmanned reservoir, with paperwork, with daylight, with explanations. They do not live here the way the shrimp live here. They trespass for a living, in heated suits patched so many times the original colour is a rumour, suits that plug into the return pipes and borrow the town's waste warmth the way a lamp borrows current. Unplugged, a person is a brief, bright expenditure of heat, cooling measurably minute by minute. Plugged in, he is part of the infrastructure.

In the water they are slow to the point of insulting gravity. They pull themselves hand over hand along ropes gone hairy with cold growth, each rope known by texture — this one braided and soft to Foundry Row, this one wire-cored to the cable works, this one beaded with knots outside the chapel. They keep their headlamps off more than on. Navigation is by touch, by silt under glove, by the faint difference in temperature on the exposed skin of the wrist where seal meets suit. Warm on your left cheek: the exchangers. Cold sinking along your spine: the outer wall. A tickle of rising bubbles against your mask: someone above you, working.

They have learned not to make noise. A dropped wrench would not clatter; it would descend in obscene silence, trailing a thread of disturbed silt, and join the slow traffic of lost objects that circles the square forever at ankle-height. So nothing is dropped. It is *released*, and everything released is tethered — tools to wrists, wrists to rails, ration tins to belts, a man's whole body to the borough by a line that pays out behind him like a memory. The older keepers move with a terrible, balletic economy, never stirring more water than the borough's own breathing. The new ones — there is a new one every few years, brought down through the ice with his eyes wide behind his mask — kick. They make clouds. They are forgiven for one rotation, and tapped at sharply through pipe-code until they learn.

Pipe-code is the language. Taps, pauses, a glove placed flat against another man's facemask so the words can be felt as pressure if not heard. Lamp shutters: *come, stop, cold here, line fouled, look*. In the pods it is different. You duck up from below through a hatch into air — real air, pocketed, improbable — and it hits you like a blow: warm, wet, smelling of skin and soup and hot wiring. There you unfold. Suits are peeled off and hung like emptied people. Hands crack. Hair steams. Voices, unused for hours, come out too loud and are hushed by habit. They eat bolted to the wall so crumbs don't drift when the hatch opens. They sleep in shifts in the lamplight, curled in pods nested like swallows' nests up under the ticking ice, while below them the town sorts its scrap again and the crane makes its blind circuit.

They are not, mostly, young. Cold at one to three degrees rewards a slower heart and a body that does not protest the long stillness. Their faces in lamplight are pale in a way that has nothing to do with lack of sun and everything to do with blood kept close to the core. Their fingers are permanently, lightly pruned inside their gloves. They keep their nails cut to nothing. They put their bare hands on a return pipe to think — the phrase *is* literal; you will see a keeper, mid-task, stop in the black water, take hold of a warm pipe, and hang there, head bowed inside his helmet, while a decision assembles itself. They stand downstream of one another for company as much as for heat.

They move the tram on Thursdays, by hand winch, from one end of its loop to the other. No one rides it. Its windows pass in the dark full of water and preserved timetables, lit for a moment by a status light, then gone. Reasons of balance, they say. Reasons of *not letting a thing seize*, which in the Sump is the same reason. They sort scrap that was sorted last month. They strike a chapel bell rarely, and then only once, and feel it loosen their teeth. They keep posters legible behind glass and boots paired by a doorway and call this, not preservation — maintenance. The distinction matters to them. Preservation is for dead things. They intend to keep this one working.

Whether there are keepers who never rotate up — whose names do not appear on the unmanned reservoir's paperwork, who are born to the pods and the lungful of soup-smelling air and only guess at weather — the surface crews do not discuss over an open channel. It would be a thing that could not be explained up there. Nothing is taken out through the ice that cannot be explained up there. That is the last rule, and human-shaped secrets fall under it.

Between visits, if there are visits, the Sump does not empty. The mats thicken and are cropped. The shrimp stitch. The kelp along Foundry Row reaches, thin and widely separated, for light that never arrives and does not die for lack of it. The sturgeon wears her groove a fraction deeper. The welders wake, make a furious silent star for ten seconds, and go out, as if someone had just been there.

Perhaps she is the true measure of who lives here: not the brief, borrowed-warm people clinging to the pipes, but anything that has accepted the terms — cold, dark, near-silence, everything tethered, nothing wasted, movement only as fast as the borough breathes — and on those terms, continues.
