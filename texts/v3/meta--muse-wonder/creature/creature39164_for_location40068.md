From a distance they look like another part of the building.

They are low, for one thing. Not stooped — built low, the way the houses are built low, broad through the shoulders and hips and short in the leg, with a center of weight that sits close to the ground. Coming up a wet causeway in pairs, pulling a cart of cut stone or roof tile on wooden rollers, they do not loom over their work. They lean into the harness of woven fiber together, heads down, and move at the speed the slick stone allows. It is the only speed anyone here has ever needed.

No one hurries. There is nowhere in their world for hurry to have come from. No animal to chase or be chased by, no herd to get in before dark, no season turning hard against them, no rain that must be beaten. The springs do not change by the hour and the gardens do not ripen all at once. A task takes as long as the water takes to clear a channel, as long as stone takes under a stone hammer. Their pace, even uphill, even under load, is a working pace that can be kept all day and again the next day, and their breathing keeps to it — slow, deep in the thin air, unembarrassed by the damp. The warm altitude has made big, quiet chests, nostrils that flare a little wider than they would in the low country, skin that has learned to live wet.

They are never entirely dry, and they do not try to be.

Their skin is brown in a range of browns, darkened by ordinary sun, but it holds a sheen even indoors, even first thing in the morning. Between the toes, at the creases of the neck and elbow, the damp settles and stays. Children are born with their fingers already a little pruned, as if they had been soaking. Adults anoint the places that would chafe or soften too much with a resin pressed from the same dark timber they use for lintels — it smells sharp and green, it darkens the skin where it is rubbed in, and it keeps rot from taking hold. Their hair, worn long by women and men alike and usually bound back with fiber cord, dries stiff and then dampens again by midday. Lime gets under their fingernails from the whitewash and stays there in a pale crescent. Copper stains the edges of their hands faintly green, from gutters and hinges and chisel hafts, so that an old mason's hands look a little verdigrised at the knuckles, like the buildings they maintain.

Their feet are their most honest feature. Broad, splayed, the toes separate and gripping, the soles thickened not by dry callus but by a dense, pale toughness that can read wet limestone through a sandal. The sandals themselves are plaited reed and fiber with shallow cross-grooves cut or woven into the sole, copying the grooves cut into the steep paths. Many go barefoot inside the house platforms and in the garden terraces, where the soil stands a hand's breadth over water and a bare foot can feel for root and stone. A lifetime of slick steps has made them place each foot whole and flat before trusting it. They rarely fall. They rarely run. A child who runs is not scolded for noise, exactly — there is so little noise to break — but corrected for footing, the way you would correct a hand carrying a full basin.

They eat what the terraces offer and nothing else has ever occurred to them. Broad leaves beaded with water, cut morning and evening; grain cut and regrown on the same plots, the stubble dug straight back in with wooden forks to rot in the warm ground; fruits set by hand and therefore set deliberately, only as many as a household or a district means to use. There is no meat in their cooking, no milk, no egg, no honey, no fat rendered from anything that moved. Their oils come from pressed seed and resin, their richness from pounded grain and leaf pastes, their salt, such as it is, from mineral scrapings where spring water leaves a crust on pale rock. They eat often and not heavily, in the unseasonal way the plants feed them, and their teeth are worn flat and even from grit and fiber. Their bellies and their larders are never very full and never empty. Hunger here is not a sharp animal thing. It is a slow lowering, like a cistern that can wait until tomorrow to be useful again.

Because there have never been animals, there are whole rooms missing from their minds, and the quiet fills those rooms without any sense of loss.

They have no word, except as a plant-word, for tame or wild in the animal sense. Nothing is kept. Nothing is herded, ridden, yoked, hunted, fished, trapped, or fed from the hand except another person. The carts are theirs to pull because pulling is simply what a pair of neighbors does. They have no leather, no wool, no horn, no bone tools, no feather except as a shape — the soft brushes the children carry through the crop rows, row by row in the still weeks, touching flower to flower, are made of fine plant down lashed to a reed stem, and called feather-brushes for their shape alone. Their cloth is beaten bark fiber and woven leaf fiber, their rope is fiber, their mats are reed, their hinges beaten copper. When they want to describe fidelity they do not reach for a dog. When they want to describe stubbornness they do not reach for a mule. Their comparisons come from water finding its level, from moss taking a shaded step, from a channel that has been widened rather than dammed. A person who takes more than their share is not greedy in the abstract; they are someone whose tank has no overflow, and everyone downstream knows exactly what that means.

That downstream knowledge is the real government of the town.

Families are bound less by blood, though blood is honored, than by the order of water. Your house platform stands where it stands because the culvert beneath it feeds a particular rill, which feeds a particular tank, which overflows to a particular garden. The people above you owe you the steadiness of their guttering. The people below you are owed yours. Marriages are discussed, in part, as water is discussed: whose cistern runs a little high, whose terrace could take another row if a channel were widened through a cousin's plot, whose aging parents will need a daughter or a son to move uphill and keep the spillway clear of reed. Children belong, in a loose and practical way, to the stretch of channel they grow up sweeping leaves from. They learn the sound of their own few hundred yards of water before they learn to read the notches cut in stone that record levels. In a soundscape with no birdsong, no insects, no frogs, no barking, a changed trickle carries like a voice raised in another room. Old people, sitting under the wide eaves mending mats, can hear a blockage two houses away and call out, mildly, whose turn it is to lift the stepping-stone and clear it.

Their voices fit that world. People speak low because low carries. Wet stone and moss drink echo, voices stay in the courtyard or the street where they were made, and no one has been shaped by the need to shout across a field to a beast or a work-gang. Even argument keeps its volume down and goes on longer instead — a slow adjustment of terms, a widening of the channel of the subject, until pressure equalizes. They are not meek. Stoneworkers settling the siting of a new platform can be immovable for days, returning each morning to stand on the same wet plot and restate the same objection about drainage. But conflict rarely sharpens. Like the cliffs, like a new wall, it keeps its first angle for a long while, and then, very slowly, weather does its work.

They grow as steadily as they live. There is no burst of births in spring, no lean winter. Children arrive one by one, in any month, spaced by years, into households that have arranged the water and the food and the extra hands for them. They grow slowly on the thin air and the unrich diet — small, then suddenly longer in the leg for a while, then filling out sideways into the adult breadth, full-grown late and then hardly changing for a long span. Hair greys. Corners soften. A woman's face at sixty has the same structure it had at thirty, darkened at the mortar-lines, so to speak, ferns in the joints. They live long by any low-country reckoning, and they die, most often, not of sudden injury — though slick stone and heavy tile can kill — but of a gradual failure to keep the damp from the lungs, a winterless, seasonless closing-in of breath, the chest's trickle narrowing at last. The dead are washed, wrapped in fiber, and carried by pairs on foot, at cart pace, to the terrace plots at the south end where the town gives out to marsh. They are dug in shallow, because deep digging finds water, and marked with a squared stone whose corners are sharp the first year. Grain grows over them in the same terraces. It is not a metaphor they make much of. Rot is quick and useful here, and the body, like stubble, is another thing the warm ground knows how to take back.

In the mornings they rise when the marsh mist burns off to its accustomed height, neither earlier nor later, because the light does the same thing every day. In the evenings they eat under the eaves and listen to the drip-lines ticking into the gutters, each house making its small, regular contribution to the channels. Lamps are plant-oil in clay dishes, low and smokeless enough. Loom-work, stone-scraping, brush-making, the mending of roller and harness — all of it is hand-work with a small, gritty, or wooden sound, quickly swallowed by wet banks. By full dark the town is quieter than most living places ever manage to be, and the quiet is not tense. Nothing is out there to call or answer. Nothing is penned and restless. The cisterns settle at their black lip. The fountain in the central plaza goes on neither rising nor falling.

If you stood among them on that plaza at noon, sandals darkening on the damp pale stone, you could be forgiven for thinking at first that they were an undemonstrative, unadorned people, slow even to dullness. Stay longer and the dullness resolves into something more exact. It is the completeness of a people who have never had to organize their lives around another species' hunger, fear, labor, milk, young, or death — only around water, stone, leaf, and each other. They are balanced the way their waterworks are balanced, not because they are virtuous in the abstract, but because any imbalance announces itself immediately, downstream, in a dry garden or a flooded floor. They keep the level. They widen; they do not dam. They pollinate by hand what the absent world would have pollinated for them, and do not experience the chore as a lack. It is simply the work of fruitfulness, theirs included.

They seem, in the end, less like visitors to that high, new, wet plateau than like the plateau's own way of becoming inhabited: squared and hand-dressed, low against the wind off the rim, green at the edges within a year of being made, sharp-cornered only where they are young. Still on the surface. Holding their shape. Not waiting for anything to arrive, because everything that lives here is already here — people, and plants, and the slow mineral life of wet rock, going on at exactly the rate it goes.
