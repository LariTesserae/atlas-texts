Nothing up here lives the way things live below.

Below, life holds territory. It makes a burrow, a nest, a clearing, and defends the space inside it. On the plateau, space is worthless — there is too much of it, all flat, all alike, all already covered in mat. What is scarce is a warm hour, a dry-enough night, a mouthful of water that will still be there tomorrow. So everything that truly belongs here lives in *time*, in narrow slices of the day, and passes the rest waiting, closed, or hidden inside something else that is alive.

Start with what you are standing on, because that is most of them.

### The Mat Itself

The mat is not a meadow. It is a crowd so old and so densely intermarried that individual plants have almost ceased to matter. Tussock grass sends roots down into cushion-moss; cushion-moss grows up through dead grass; rosettes root wherever a mole-rat has shouldered up fresh red earth; tiny flowering herbs live their whole brief lives in the inch of sunlit water trapped between other leaves.

It is eaten from below by mole-rats, from above by stock and grasshoppers, trampled flat every day and unflattened every night. It does not resist. It outgrows. Cut it and it closes. Burn a patch — if you can get it to burn at all, which mostly you cannot — and the edges creep in within weeks. A dead hut, a fallen giant, a herder's footprint: all of it is simply slower mat, waiting to be reabsorbed.

If you put your ear to it at noon you will hear it working. Not metaphorically. Roots creaking, insects chewing, water ticking down through a hundred layers of stem and sponge toward rock that may be a metre below and may never see the sun. The mat is the plateau's soil, river system, and forest floor stacked into a single wet mattress perhaps a forearm deep. Most of the animals here do not live *on* it. They live *in* it, at different depths, like tenants in different storeys of the same sodden building.

### The Giants

The great rosettes are the only things that live at human scale, and they do it by refusing to hurry.

A young one spends years — nobody knows how many, nobody has stayed up here long enough, generation after generation, to count properly — as nothing but a dinner-plate rosette flat in the mat, indistinguishable from its smaller cousins. Then, for reasons of age or patience, it begins to lift. The trunk is not wood. It is packed fibre and old leaf-bases, soft enough to cut with a stone knife, light enough that a fallen giant can be dragged by two people. Year by year it accretes, wearing its own dead leaves as a skirt because shedding them would mean losing insulation, losing stored water, losing the slow rot that feeds its own roots in a place where the soil is this thin.

At the top the leaves are enormous and deliberate. Felty, silver-backed, each one a solar collector and an umbrella and a blanket. Open flat at midday to drink the vertical sun. Tilted in the afternoon downpour so rain runs *inward*, not off — funneled to the well at the heart of the crown, and down the trunk in runnels to the roots. Folded up at night, not quite closed, when the thin air loses its heat all at once and the sky goes black and depthless. You can hear them do it if you camp near one: a faint, dry, papery shifting after sunset, leaf easing against leaf.

They flower once. Once, after decades, sometimes longer, the centre of the crown pushes up a spike taller than the plant itself, dense with small flowers, and for a few weeks that single giant becomes the busiest address on the plateau — bees in a furious halo, sunbirds stabbing in iridescent green and scarlet, beetles arriving on the wind from giants flowering kilometres away. Then it sets seed, and dies, and does not fall. It stands dead for years, skirt rattling, trunk hollowing softly, full of nesting insects and trapped rain, while its seedlings take hold in the enriched mat at its feet. The spacing you see — a giant every twenty, thirty, fifty paces — is not planting. It is the distance one dead giant can feed.

They are the plateau's trees, wells, apartment blocks, and graveyards, all in the same slow body.

### The Ones Who Never Touch the Ground

In the well at the heart of each living crown, in maybe a litre of dark, tannin-brown water, lives the frog.

It is thumbnail-sized, translucent gold, with eyes too large for its head and toes that end in soft discs for gripping wet leaf. Its skin shows the workings underneath — a darker smudge of gut, sometimes the pale gleam of eggs carried inside the female. It has, as far as anyone can tell, no reason to ever descend. Some of them are born, breed, and die in the same crown, three generations in a pond ten centimetres across, four metres above the ground they will never stand on.

Their world has weather of its own. Morning: the well is cold, skimmed with dew, the frog wedged down where leaf meets leaf, motionless, the colour of weak tea. Midday: the crown opens, sun strikes the water, midges and tiny beetles blunder in and drown or are taken with a tongue-snap so fast it is mostly a click. The frog climbs a centimetre into the light and becomes, briefly, jewel-bright. Afternoon: the leaves drum, the well brims and overflows, tadpoles — laid as a small clutch of eggs in the axil, hatched into water no deeper than a finger — swirl in the sudden current and wedge themselves hard against the leaf-bases not to be washed out into the void. Night: the great leaves fold inward above them, a green tent, and the well keeps the day's warmth an hour or two longer than the open air. The singing, when it comes, comes muffled through that tent. Each giant hums faintly to itself, in a different pitch depending on the depth of its water.

They are preyed on by almost nothing up there except each other, the occasional bold sunbird, and people with fine woven nets, for whom a crown's worth of frogs is a sour, crunchy, highly prized mouthful. Mostly they are preyed on by the plant's own lifespan. When a giant flowers and dies, its well drains, leaf by leaf. The frogs of that crown must do the one thing their parents never did: climb down the dead trunk in the wettest part of a wet night, cross the roaring dark of the mat — grasshoppers, mole-rat mounds, a universe of mouths — and climb another living trunk to another small sky. Almost everything about them says they are bad at this. That almost none of them make it hardly matters. A crown that reaches them receives, usually, exactly one gravid female. That is enough.

There are thousands of these vertical ponds across the plateau, each one genetically alone, each one sounding slightly different at dusk. The herders are not being poetic when they navigate by frog-song. They are walking from inhabited giant to inhabited giant, from water to water.

### The Day Shift and the Underneath

At ground level the census changes completely, and gets louder.

The grasshoppers come in sizes from a grain of rice to the length of your thumb, many of them flightless, their wings reduced to bright useless flakes — why fly, when there is nowhere different to fly *to*? The thumb-sized cicada-things stay down in the root zone and sing through the mat itself, so the sound arrives through your knees and boot-soles as much as your ears. Beetles work the cushion-flowers in polished blues and greens. Ants — small, red, patient — farm trails up the trunks of the giants and rob drowned insects from the edges of the crown-wells, hauling bodies many times their size down into the mat in staggering single file.

Everything that flies by day is either eating these or being chased by something that does. Sunbirds drop out of the empty sky as if invented by it, hang buzzing at a flowering cushion no bigger than a coin, and are gone. Swifts spend the noon thermals screaming through columns of rising insects above the hot stone pavements, never landing, barely even flapping, mouths open. In season the seed-eating flocks come, loose rivers of small brown birds that settle and rise and settle, harvesting the tussock heads in a rolling wave two hours wide, and behind them, always, a few patient storks turning in the same updrafts, silent, waiting for the mole-rats.

The mole-rats are the giants of the animal kingdom here, which tells you everything. Big as a forearm, blunt-headed, nearly blind, fur the exact red-brown of the earth they push up, teeth that grow their whole lives against the toughness of roots. They live in family tunnels just under the mat, in the dark between plant and rock, and you will likely never see one alive above ground. You will sleep above them and hear the gnawing through the floor of the world. You will find their mounds every morning, fresh, steaming faintly in the cold, already being knitted over with new green. You will find, occasionally, one taken by a stork at the instant it pushed too far into daylight, which suggests a whole unseen discipline down there about how close to the surface it is safe to come, and when.

They are engineers second only to the mat. Their digging aerates it, buries dead growth, brings mineral earth up where seedlings can use it. Their abandoned tunnels become dormitories for insects, hunting runs for small snakes — thin, brown, frog-specialists, nearly as shy as the mole-rats themselves — and, in the dry weeks between rains, the only reliably damp refuges on the open rises. Half the plateau lives underground for half the day and doesn't consider this hiding. It considers it the main residence, with the surface a dangerous, delicious roof-garden to be visited at the right hour.

### The People

And then, thinly, people.

They do not live up here the way the frogs do. Nobody does, above ground. The herders who bring their small shaggy stock up in the warm months — cattle no taller than your waist, broad-chested for the thin air, coats that shed water like the giants' skirts shed it — are visitors who have learned to behave like residents of the time-sliced world rather than owners of space.

They are not tall people, and on the plateau they seem even less tall, because they build nothing above shoulder height and rarely stand fully upright in the wind-driven mist if they can be doing something on their knees in the mat. Their lungs are made over generations the way the mole-rats' teeth are made in one life: deep chests, slow resting breaths, a capacity to work all day at an altitude where a newcomer's lips crack and their cooking water sulks below boiling. Their feet are broad and hard as leather, because boots rot up here in a season and bare feet, paradoxically, do not — living skin outgrows the damp the way the mat does.

They wear the plateau. Leaf-fibre rain capes that work by overlapping like thatch. Woven grass, giant-leaf thatch, bags of braided tussock. Their stock are grazed in slow rotation around the cairn-lines, never kept in one place long enough to eat the mat down to the sponge, because everyone understands — in the body, not as philosophy — that the mat *is* the water. Kill it in a patch and that patch becomes bare pavement, then dust, then a place the frogs go silent around.

They eat frogs and insects without embarrassment and mole-rat with ceremony, dig them with fire-hardened sticks, cook everything slowly over smouldering dead-leaf because that is the only fire the place will regularly give. They resharpen one iron blade for a decade. They restack every cairn they pass without being asked, the way you would close a gate. They leave polished basalt hand-axes by shelter fire-pits for whoever comes next, and take them again without ceremony, because up here a tool left in a hut does not belong to a person. It belongs to the shelter, which belongs, increasingly each year, to the mat.

Their children are born below, mostly. Their old people retire below, mostly. Up here they keep to the middle slice of life, the strong-lunged years, and even then only seasonally — which is why the plateau has their huts, their paths, their standing stones at the central pan, but not their graves. The dead go down. What stays up is just structures learning how to become mounds.

Ask them who lives here and they will not, first, say themselves. They will point — down, into the turf, with a toe; up, into a passing crown of leaves; out, across a view with nothing in it but giants standing in their spaced patience all the way to a horizon that is not, properly, a horizon at all, just the place where the sky-plain curves away.

They are right. They are the plateau's guests, tolerated because they have learned the house rules: live low, live briefly, live wet, keep moving, leave nothing that cannot be eaten by green. The residents are smaller, louder, slower, and far more numerous. They were converting this ancient flat sunlight into song for a very long time before the first cairn was stacked, and on the evidence of the mat already fur-furring over last season's hut, they intend to be doing it long after the last one slumps.
