At first you will think no one lives on the surface.

You can walk for days from condenser to condenser, following the faint green stain of a leaking seal, crossing ceramic shielding and solar skin and avenues that quietly convert light into power for the depths, and meet no one. No smoke. No voices. No footprints — the dust creeps away along its field-lines to the processing gullets almost as fast as it can be disturbed, and the ground heals the insult of a boot within the hour.

Then, at dusk, during the brief copper lowering of the reflectors, you will be standing still — because you have finally learned that stillness is the only way to see anything here — and the plain will exhale around you, and they will be there.

Not emerging, exactly. Becoming visible. Which is not the same thing.

They were in the path of the shadow.

Shade is scheduled here, and so are they. A cooling fin a kilometer away throws a blade of shade that travels its slow arc once a day, and along that moving darkness, things gather the way shore-life gathers along a tide-line. The surface people — the Roofers, the Skin-Tenders, the Bright-Kin, their own word for themselves translates closer to *Those Who Are Allowed Up* — arrange their entire waking life inside that travelling coolness. They walk within it, bent slightly, unhurried, their long shadows doubled and trebled by mirror-light into pale ghosts ahead of and behind them. When the shade thins, they thin with it. When it leaves a district, so do they.

They are tall, at first glance, and then realize much of the tallness is equipment. A broad, low crown of woven ceramic fiber rides above the head like a flat mushroom, throwing a personal rim of shadow over face and shoulders. Under it, a second brim of dull mirror-cloth hangs to the elbows, moving minutely on its own, tilting to the light, so the wearer seems always to be standing inside a small, attentive tent. Their suits are not cloth as you would know it. They are skin printed by the world itself: fine overlapping scales of bone-white ceramic, solar membrane, and something softer and faintly pink at the joints that flexes, seals, reclaims. Sweat never reaches the air. Breath goes down, not out. A tube at the throat catches every exhalation, cools it, strips it, returns the water to a bladder worn flat against the spine — their wealth, their inheritance, worn where they can feel its weight lessen.

Take the crown off one of them — you will not; no one takes the crown off in noon — and the person underneath is smaller than the silhouette promised. Compact. Dense-boned. Built low to the wind-sheet that slides along the surface carrying its glittering dust.

Their skin is the color of the plain, and that is not metaphor. White-gold on the shoulders, bone-grey along the shaved sides of the skull, faintly rose where the blood shows through at cheek and knuckle, patched in places with a grey-green roughness that is almost, but not quite, lichen. Some of that roughness is age. Some is sun-scar callused over generations. Some, in the very old, is deliberate: a living rind grafted into the skin that opens its microscopic pores only when the surface cools, drinking light through a waxy mineral crust, feeding a little sugar and a little oxygen directly into the blood beneath. The oldest tenders photosynthesize while they sleep, if sleep is still the word for the bright trance they take face-down at dusk, palms spread on the warm panels, listening through their teeth to whatever the depths are doing.

Their eyes are silvered slits.

Not blind — the opposite. Three lids: an outer lid against dust, a translucent inner lid that can be closed for hours against the glare while still reading brightness as terrain, and beneath, an eye that has learned to sort light the way your ear sorts sound. They can tell active solar skin from prayer-tile from living metal at a distance you would call featureless. They read dazzle the way a sailor reads water. Where you see pain, they see information: charge building, a seam about to open, a repair-swarm moving under the surface, weather coming three days off because the panels here are holding half a degree too much heat.

They do not speak much above ground. The air would steal it — minutes dry a mouth, an hour of talk costs water no one spends casually — and besides, the silence is not empty to them. They press boot-soles to the ground and feel the grid humming through bone. They tap messages into the skin with heel and knuckle: short, Patient vibrations that travel through the structure and up into the sternums of anyone standing within a mile with their feet flat and listening. A conversation at noon looks like a group of people standing absolutely still, occasionally shifting weight. Their sign-language, used in the shadow-bands, is similarly slow and close: hands kept low, in shade, fingers moving against wrists and forearms where the skin is sensitive and unburned. Nothing flaps. Nothing waves. Millennia of heat have edited out every extravagant gesture.

They eat what the surface provides, which is to say, they eat the surface itself, carefully. Grey-green lichen sheets harvested from the shaded sides of vents, dried to a salty mineral wafer. The black-glass fans that unfold in dust-filled cracks, clipped before they can fold — bitter, copper-edged, rich in things the deep farms no longer make. Moths, when the navigation beacons draw them, caught in fine nets at dusk, eaten whole. And underneath everything, the ration that comes *up* twice a year through the hatches: dense pale bricks of recycled protein and fat from the inhabited layers, tasteless as wet paper, prized beyond delicacy because they represent water already counted and blessed and spent by a thousand bodies below.

Because the Bright-Kin do not live on the roof.

No one lives on the roof. That would be like living on the skin of your own face. They live *in* it — in the first few meters beneath the surface, in blister-domes subsided almost flush, in maintenance skins and thermal buffer-galleries where the light comes filtered through fiber-optics and old glass and falls, at last, bearably, on inward-turned windows. Their apartments open onto light-wells that have never seen the sky. Their children are born down there in the amber half-dark, and some children never earn the crown, never pass the tests of thirst and glare, and go down instead of up when they are grown — down the shafts, layer by layer, into the true population of the world.

And that is the second thing you learn, weeks in, your lips cracked despite your reclaimer, following a lit window one evening with your eyes if not your feet: the surface people are only the world's eyelashes.

Below them, the inhabited volume unfolds beyond census.

There are the Seam-Canyon families, who live vertically in the rifts where two continental plates of construction buckled against each other, their homes stacked in the walls among dark windows, one in ten thousand lit amber, their children running reckless along ledges thirty meters above a floor that is only an older roof. There are the Avenue-Keepers of the transparent districts, who walk on ancient scuffed glass above green-lit machinery seas and keep it clean with a devotion indistinguishable from religion, teaching their young to read the movements below as weather. There are whole folded streets under other streets where it rains on schedule from condensation pipes and nobody has ever seen the sun directly, only its stored radiance bleeding from night-panels, and they find your description of noon obscene and a little holy — a desert god that lives on the ceiling of the world.

Deeper still, pressure and chemistry change by degrees, and so do the people.

The air thickens. Solvents replace water in the industrial strata. The light goes green, then red, then gives up altogether, and the inhabitants give up on eyes in any hurry — first by choice, augmenting hearing and touch and the chemical sense that unrolls from the back of the throat like a second tongue, and then by birth. You find whole lineages who navigate by the deep notes that pass up through the structure, who can tell by the taste of the air which factory three levels down has woken early, who wear their family water-history encoded in a braid of tiny sealed beads at the throat: births, deaths, marriages, every gram accounted for, inherited, never owned, only carried for a while between one body and the next. A death down there is a plumbing event and a sacrament at the same time. Nothing is wasted because *waste* is a word for a luxury only a young world could afford.

Go down far enough — past the cisterns sealed a million years ago and somehow still full, past the photonic conduits singing in their solid light, past farms lit by piped sun where crops grow in rows that never knew a season — and the machines stop modelling human beings in their calculations, and, courteously, the beings stop modelling themselves as human. There are presences in the oldest strata that move through service shafts too narrow and too hot for anything with lungs, that communicate by changing the temperature of a room in patterns, that may have begun as maintenance crews, or colonies, or the building's own repair impulse grown curious about itself. The deep families leave them offerings: a panel freshly printed, a conduit polished bright, a torn lichen-sheet left where the small unseen things can mend it overnight. Whether the offerings are worship or pest control is a distinction the deep families find genuinely uninteresting.

Because the third population, and the largest, is the building.

The repair swarms that flow like frost across a scorched panel at dawn, no bigger than grains, are its immune system and its fingertips at once. The dust-rivers sliding along invisible field lines are its digestion. The vast thermal masses that shift state miles below and send a single note up through your teeth are — something. Breathing is too fast a word. Tides would be slower and closer. The deep families will tell you the world dreams in heat, and that the dream occasionally needs people in it, small and hidden and useful, to perform the tasks a dream cannot perform for itself: to notice the moth around the beacon. To feel grief at a dark window. To name a district *ceramic, glass, living metal, prayer-tile* and walk it like a rosary.

This is why the surface rites make no sense until you have watched one.

At dusk, when the buried towers catch the last light and the world briefly remembers its skyline, the Bright-Kin gather where the condensers are beading with the day's stolen moisture. Each takes only what their weight allows. Each returns a portion — a mouthful, a palmful — to a shallow depression in the skin that is not a drain but a mouth, feeding the world that feeds them, gram for gram. Children being tested for the crown stand barefoot for the first time on the cooling panels, learning to read the grid through their soles, weeping without sound or visible tears — the body is already too wise to spend water on signalling — while their elders watch the silver slits of their eyes for panic. Those who can stand the brightness pass upward. Those who cannot go lovingly down, to the green dark and the folded streets, and no shame attaches to either. It is not a hierarchy. It is a temperature gradient. Life arranges itself along it the way water would, if there were any water to spare.

At night the panels open their stored radiance and the plain begins faintly to glow, and the tenders lie down in the glow, crowns off at last, grafted skin unfurling, ears pressed sideways to the warm ceramic. To an outsider it looks like sleep scattered across an empty world.

It is not empty. It never was.

They are listening to everyone below them — the canyon families abseiling home in the dark, the avenue-keepers polishing glass over a machinery sea, the deep lineages tasting the air and braiding water into their children's hair, the old hot presences moving through shafts where the very air would poison a lung — layer under layer under layer of a world that ground its cities down into a landscape and then kept living inside the grinding.

And under all their listening, slow as geology, patient as maintenance, the largest inhabitant turns its brightness over in its mind: panel by panel, seam by seam, repairing the roof at exactly the rate the sun wears it away, keeping the final form forever by never once being still.

The people are not its masters. They are not its tenants either.

They are the part of it that can walk out at dusk, stand in a travelling shadow, and be glad — briefly, dry-mouthed, silver-eyed, with both feet flat on the warm skin of home — that it is bright.
