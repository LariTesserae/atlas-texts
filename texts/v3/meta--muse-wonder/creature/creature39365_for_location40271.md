In the bowl, nothing lives alone, and nothing lives *in* the noise the way you would live in a house. They live *off* it, inside its standing waves, the way fish live inside a current. Take the current away and they don't get peace. They get exposed.

The first inhabitants you actually notice are not the people.

They are the ones rooted at the nodes — those crossing-points in the air where the scream cancels itself out for a hand's breadth, or piles up thick enough to lean against. There, the growth is obscene in its density for a place with no water. Ankle-high, waist-high. Teal ferns whose fronds clench and unclench on the bass note, opening their many-chambered leaves not to light but to pressure. Mats of violet not-moss, rippling without pause as thousands of dry bladders inflate and sigh, inflate and sigh, pumping gas through a communal root-mat. If you break a stem you don't get sap. You get cold oil that smells of turpentine, or a bead of silver stuff that recoils from your skin. Nothing transpires here. Nothing can afford to give anything away.

And the pale balloon-trees, barely taller than a person, are the elders of those clumps. Cork-barked, slow-seeming until you watch them. Their canopies are not leaves at all but clusters of taut, translucent sacs that drum and tighten and loosen, harvesting vibration the way a lowland tree harvests sun. Their roots don't seek water. They seek the exact depth where the rock hums truest, and when the note shifts — and it is always shifting — the whole tree will loosen itself, slew half a meter across the black grit on agitated dust, and lock down again somewhere better with a *thunk* you feel in your shins. Stillness, here, is not rest. Stillness is how you die out of tune.

Around them, never really leaving them, are the mobile ones.

Glass midges in their millions, too finely jointed to be merely evolved. Their wings don't beat against the air; they phase-lock to the dominant tone and ride the pressure itself, lift made out of sound. Alone one is a speck of chitin. Together they are a workforce and a weather system: they form sheets, columns, a sudden brief hand, they stream in bright lines under a slab of stone that is hovering on focused sound, carrying seeds, carrying black-mercury pollen, carrying granules and disassembled pieces of the bowl's own technology from resonator to resonator. At night, when the cold deepens at four thousand meters, they grow architectural — they build warm, temporary shapes out of their own swarming bodies and roost inside them.

Above them, drum-moths as big as both your hands, their bodies mostly ear. They let the noise pass through them and it leaves cleaner, amplified, tuned — living filters in the chord. Click-beetles armored like little bells that ring when they land. And higher still, a soot-colored gliding lizard with membrane wings that may not have touched the ground in generations. It folds itself into the vortex, sleeps on the wing in the warmest layer of the circling air, wakes only to snap midges from a swarm without breaking the swarm's shape, and folds again. Once in a while, from high on the wall, something unseen makes one perfectly placed cry — not louder than the bowl, just fitted exactly into a gap in its frequencies — and everything, moth and midge and human, turns its head. A warning. A mating call. A maintenance check. In the bowl those may be the same thing.

Then there are the largest inhabitants, which you first mistake for buildings.

Six of them. Maybe seven. The number changes because they walk, slowly, partly, when the wind re-tunes the hollow. Squat, windowless resonators, igloo-shaped, made of something stone-colored that is not stone — it can knit a crack shut over a minute, sweating faint heat. Their doors are membranes that beat like eardrums. Their roofs bristle with hair-fine vanes and tuning forks and dishes that track nothing in the sky, only everything in the air. They are furred with the same oil-leaved growth as the nodes. Their vents take in screaming air and exhale it a little warmer, a little calmer.

They are not housing the ecosystem. They *are* part of it. The walls unfold two centimeters to meet a shift in pressure. A ton-weight slab lifts silently, hangs, resettles in a new orientation while midges stream underneath. The resonators keep the bowl in phase. The bowl feeds the resonators. If you pass too close your inner ear knows it before your mind does — the air goes briefly thicker, and holds you up a little at the ankles.

And the people — the old hands — live the way everything else does: by never attempting quiet.

You can tell a bowl-keeper not by clothing but by economy. They move constantly, small adjustments, never standing planted in one spot long enough to be caught in an anti-node when the chord shifts and the pressure drops and empties your lungs for you. They do not shout. Shouting is nothing. Even sealed, bone-conducted speech jitters apart up here. So they press their foreheads together, bone to bone, and speak through the skull. Or they lay a palm flat on one of the speaking-plates set into the ground and let the words come up through the wrist. Most of the time they simply don't speak. After an hour in the bowl your own thoughts feel loud enough anyway.

They tend things without seeming to give orders. A hand on a balloon-tree's cork trunk until its sacs stop fluttering wrong. A forehead pressed briefly to a resonator wall. They don't take plants out of their clumps — divorced from the shared root-mat and its pocket of tuned air, a cutting goes papery in minutes, bladders caving, oil drying to varnish. They don't wait for a lull to rest in. There is no lull. There are crescendos when a gust stacks on the standing wave and every tree flattens and every moth dives for the mats, and there is the night retuning, when the resonators walk the chord down by a third into the frequencies your skeleton is built for and it becomes, somehow, more deafening, not less. But quiet is the one disaster they prepare for. Quiet would mean the vortex had failed, the resonators had fallen out of phase, and the bowl would freeze-dry and scour itself clean in an afternoon.

They are not the first to know this. Around the rim are older cairns, hand-stacked, each topped with a stretched animal skin gone to mineral — and still vibrating, faintly, in sympathy. Someone found this loud place a very long time ago and loved it correctly.

So the bowl's inhabitants are not a species. They are a metabolism, distributed across fern and midge and moth and lizard and walking house and human: the trees eat sound and make still pockets, the midges move matter through air too violent for anything larger to cross, the moths clean the frequencies, the resonators hold the whole standing wave from collapsing, the people listen with their foreheads and their palms and make the minute corrections none of the others quite can. Remove any one note and the chord thins.

At evening, in ordinary gold light, nothing settles. The plants fold their felt leaves around pockets of their own made weather. The swarms thicken. The trees thunk down into their night positions. The keepers move from plate to plate, silent, touching things.

Empty and crowded at once. Dry as powdered bone and lush as a greenhouse. Everything that can live there is living at maximum — not despite the scream, but because they have learned, body by body over ten thousand years, how to metabolize it.
