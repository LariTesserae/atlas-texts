It does not live *in* the closet, in the way a thing lives in a room. It has become the room's contents so completely that there is no longer a difference between inhabitant and interior.

At first, face to the hatch, you try to count it as plants. There is the broad-leaved thing at face height, dinner-plate fronds in overlapping storeys. There is the moss cuffing every bolt. There is the pale lichen on the junction boxes, the fine vine that has learned the pitch of the pipe-threads, the black leaves down in the root mat and the bottle-green leaves up around the caged bulb. You want them to be a community, a little accidental forest of different species that blew in on boots and supply crates.

They are not. Or they are not anymore.

After fifty years of drinking the same oil-mist, breathing the same ammonia trace, growing through each other in a space ninety centimetres wide with no empty pocket larger than a hand, the distinctions have worn through. The moss is thickest where the fronds need cushioning against a vibrating pipe. The lichen spreads only where the frond canopy keeps the bakelite at exactly the right dryness. The vines do not climb for themselves; they stitch leaf to leaf, shelf to ladder to mug-handle, holding the whole packed mass in tension against the compressor's shaking. The roots below — braided as cable, white as wire at their tips — are not the roots of the fronds only. They are a shared stomach, worked into a black cake of coal dust and dried lubricant and powdered insulation, and everything above feeds down into it and is fed from it.

It is one body wearing several kinds of skin.

Its ancestors are still legible, the way a face is legible in an old crowd photograph. Somewhere near the centre, half-buried and cracked, is the clay pot from the fern an operator's wife sent up in a crate decades ago to prove something living could stay on the summit. That fern is probably in there still — not as a plant, but as a habit. The broad, waxy fronds have its patience, its willingness to unfurl. But the moss came on someone's sleeve. The lichen came on the wind that finds every seam at 5,400 metres. Lower things came in soil and oil and on the soles of fitters who no longer work here. They did not compete. In a place with only one food, competition ends quickly. They merged into the only shape that could eat it.

And what it eats has made it into something that is no longer quite vegetation.

It does not drink water. It has never been rained on, never been watered since the pot, never known dew. Water would sit useless in its root cake, would dilute the one rain it understands: the weep of Compressor No. 2, a molecule-at-a-time aerosol of synthetic oil, refrigerant, metallic dust from the bearings, invisible and exactly replenished from the reservoirs through the wall. Its roots take oil the way earth takes weather. Its leaves condense coolant vapour on their cool undersides and draw it in through pores that do not open to light at all — they open to pressure, fluttering minutely each time the compressor cycles. Crush a stem (they resist, fibrous as cable insulation, as if plant matter had been slowly replaced by the materials around it) and what runs out is not green sap but a clear, cold fluid that flashes off your skin and leaves ammonia on the air. It is feeding, and excreting cold, and breathing a poison that deletes every other living thing that might have shared the closet with it. No gnat in the fronds. No spider in the conduit corner. No moth dead under the bulb. It lives in perfect faunal silence, alone in its own lushness.

Because it is, above all, a listener.

Every leaf in the body is turned away from the bulb. This is the gesture you keep coming back to. The fronds present their broad faces — dry as paper, faintly warm — to the back wall, to the compressor housing. The half-unfurled frond in the faded photograph wired to the hatch is half-unfurled today, at the same angle, occluding the same dial. It is not reaching. It is attending. The whole organism has arranged itself as an ear: storey upon storey of resonant green membranes held taut by vine and stem in a hundred-hertz drone that never rises, never falls, a hiss that never empties, a relay clicking at the same interval it has always clicked. The sound is so continuous it has stopped being sound for it and become a climate, a pressure system to grow into. Its leaves do not tremble in it the way leaves tremble in wind. They hold it, the way a tuning fork holds a note. You have to take your ear defenders off to work the latch, and your ears ache in seconds; it has been standing in that column of noise, face-first, for half a century, shaping every new cell around it.

That may be why it does not change.

A living thing that eats at exactly the rate its food is produced, that is held at twelve degrees by a thermostat no living person remembers adjusting, that receives the same unblinking LEDs and the same caged bulb and the same vibration through its mounting bolts, night shift after night shift after night shift, has arrived at a metabolism with no seasons in it. A leaf browns and is replaced at the speed of its browning. Growth cancels decay so precisely that the body looks like a photograph of itself. It is not dead — the frond at the hatch is already pressing toward where the closing door will be, resuming its shape in advance, and if you put your fingers in you will touch three leaves and a warm pipe in ten centimetres, all of them faintly, patiently pushing back. It is not growing, either. It is maintaining. It is holding a single late-shift moment, cool and loud and saturated green, with the stamina of a machine and the opportunism of ivy.

It has no use for the human things it has absorbed, and no malice toward them. The enamel mug has become a node where four vines meet and thicken, not a relic, just a convenient hardness. The aluminium step-ladder is its only mountain, a trellis-ridge at an angle it would never have invented itself. The lubrication chart in its opaque sleeve is simply a flat, pale surface, good for lichen. It is polite around these things the way geology is polite — by incorporating them without comment.

It does not want you in there, but that is the wrong word too. Wanting would require a gap for you to be wanted in. There is none. It has filled its world entirely, stem to pipe to stem, root mat to conduit, and a body-sized absence would be an injury. You can put your face in its air — dry enough to crack your lips, smelling of hot copper and ozone and bitter green refrigerant — and breathe what it and the compressor have been breathing back and forth for fifty years, and that is as close as the relationship goes. It will not bend back. It will not make room. It does not need to be visited, watered, fed, lit, rescued from the altitude, or admired to continue. The summit can go to thirty below in a storm, the ridge can scour and fail and come back; in the green dark behind Compressor No. 2 it goes on facing the sound, drinking oil-mist, holding its note.

Close the hatch and the drone in the engine hall drops by a small, unimportant fraction.

It notices that fraction the way you would notice a held breath being let out.
