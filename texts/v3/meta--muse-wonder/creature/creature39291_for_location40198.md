There is one who stays.

The others come and go — the harvesters with copper knives, the shepherds' children bringing salt bread and feather bundles up to the rim, the tenders from the villages below who climb in a line when rain threatens and the young cushions must be covered. They all leave again by the clean path between the leaning slabs. They have to. The silence up there gets into them too quickly if they linger.

She does not leave.

You will not see her at first. In the Ear, anything that keeps still reads as stone, and anything important reads at first as plant. She will be in the lee of a trunk with one shoulder against it, not resting — listening with the bone of her shoulder, with the soles of her feet. Or halfway up a tree on the notched cuts, a bone saw between her teeth. Or down among the cushions with her weighted skirts spread around her like another low, leafed boulder, doing nothing that looks like work for a very long time, until a cushion sighs out and she moves, suddenly and exactly, to be out of the spore-cloud.

Do not expect her to look up when you enter. She felt you an hour ago.

Your boots on the outer track sent a particular gritty rhythm down through the rim rock. Your breathing changed at the notch. Your hat, your loose buckle, your water-skin if you were fool enough to bring one sloshing — the Ear took all of that, turned it over, and delivered it to her feet long before your shadow fell into the bowl. She knows visitors the way you know weather coming in your knees.

She is small, because everyone who grows up in that wind learns to offer it less to hold. Not stunted — compacted. Her back and shoulders are broad from climbing trunks that never stop swaying. Her arms are roped with the twin lines of muscle that come from holding on and sawing at the same time. Her hands are enormous by contrast, or seem so, because every finger is calloused to a pale horn, sensitive past reason. She can lay a palm on warm pumice and tell which root is speaking underneath, and how far down, and whether it has found sulfur or only more broken feldspar.

Her feet she keeps bare inside the bowl. Shoes would deafen her.

That is the first true thing about her. She is deaf in the way the finch chicks in the hollow trunks are deaf. Not wholly — she can hear the great notes, the wall answering a gust, the trunk-sections booming too low to be sound so much as a pressure in the sternum. The ordinary scale of hearing was burned out of her years ago, by design or by simple attrition no one now remembers. People from below shout at her, at first, and then feel foolish, and then learn. She reads speech on teeth and throat if she must. Mostly she does not bother. Human talk is a clumsy instrument in the Ear. It goes away and comes back wrong.

She drums.

On wood, on stone, on her own thigh bone through the skin, on the taut fiber net strung between two trunks to add its bass to the grove. *Tok. Tok-tok. Tok* — waiting, answer, warning, a name that is not a name. Every creature in the bowl knows her rhythms the way they know updrafts. Beetles shift their hammering to make room for her. Lizards stall mid-glide, fans ticking, when she raps a trunk twice for a fall of insects. In the evening she goes from tree to tree striking the resonating chambers where the finches nest, shaking the day's last pollen loose, and the trees answer up through their whole height and for a while the entire grove is playing her back to herself, slower and larger.

She has no spoken name in the Ear. Below, in the villages, they call her something that means approximately *the Tuner*, or *Auntie Ear*, depending on the valley. Up here names only create echoes. She has a sign instead: three beats and a dragged thumb across a trunk slit, which makes a note like a throat clearing. The spiders learn it. The ground birds learn it. New plants, apparently, learn it.

Her skin is never clean and never dry. Morning and evening she works fallen sail-leaf between her palms until the oil comes, and rubs herself down — arms, face, the shaved sides of her head, the cracks that the wind opens at her knuckles and heels and the corners of her mouth. It keeps the salt wind from taking her apart. It makes her smell permanently of resin and hot pepper and old incense, so that when she does go down to the rim to receive offerings she can be smelled on the path long after she has gone back down into the gloom: a warm, edible, slightly alarming smell, like a spice that has learned to burn.

Her hair, what she keeps of it, is one black rope down her back, braided through with sail-fiber and small bored stones. It sings faintly in gusts. Not ornament. Instrumentation. Different tensions along the braid tell her how the vortex is leaning before the debris column visibly moves. Her clothes are the same — woven fiber, resin-sealed seams, hems and cuffs weighted with flat stones sewn in pockets so the wind consults her rather than carries her. Loose things belong to the column. She has watched her own tools tour the bowl for days, companionable as moons. She does not own anything she is not wearing, holding, or prepared to hear about later from an unexpected direction.

She lives in the half-buried hut in the lee of the north wall, where the baffled doorway turns the wind twice before it can touch the interior. Inside it is darker and, by Ear standards, almost quiet, which means you can hear yesterday in there only faintly. She sleeps on layered leaves over warm rock with one hand flat to the floor. She wakes whenever the one plant shifts its conversation. A root striking a new pocket. A cushion troubled in the cold. A trunk going subtly out of tune as its sap-oil thickens in a night snap. By the time a visitor hears anything wrong, she is already outside with the obsidian blade.

Because that is the work. Not farming. Tuning.

The grove would grow without her — it grew, ringed around the buried original, before there were people — but it would grow discordant. A trunk left unnotched grows too true and hums at a pitch that sets the cushion spores off early. A sail left unpruned catches the vortex wrong and begins, over seasons, to pull its whole tree into a lean that silences two others. Nets slacken. Beetles hammer a shoot to death with enthusiasm. She moves through it all making tiny, permanent corrections. Sealing a cut with black resin. Shaving a slit a finger's width longer so a young tree drops from a whistle to a breath. Tying a stone crescent against a shoot not to hold it down but to give the wind something to argue with. Building nothing that will not have to be rebuilt. Nothing here is finished; it is only, for the moment, in key.

She eats what the air brings and what the grove can spare. Beetles, roasted in leaf oil until their armor splits. Ground-bird eggs taken one from a nest, never two. The starchy hearts of burst seed-pods. Moths, in their brief silver multitudes, scooped in a net and eaten raw and powdery before the gust returns. She drinks very little and never from the cistern — that cracked, capped emptiness by her door is older than her and will stay empty while she lives; it is a reminder shaped like a container. What water she needs she carries up herself in a sealed skin, a month's worth that would last a lowlander a week, and she metes it out in mouthfuls at dawn, holding it a long time before swallowing, listening while she does it to the small shocking music it makes in her own body. Her lips are permanently split. She does not seem to mind. The oil seals them. When she smiles, which she does at beetles mostly, they crack again without bleeding.

Visitors sometimes ask — by signs, by writing in dust that blows away mid-sentence — whether it is lonely. This confuses her, visibly. She will put her hand to the nearest trunk, inviting you to do the same, and wait while the low conversation runs up from the roots through wrist and elbow into the teeth. She will point up at the turning column where her lost awl has been circling for a season. At a spider kiting past on its one bright line, reeling itself home. At the finches drumming from inside the wood, at the lizards asleep mid-air in the slow center of the wind, at the cushions breathing in and sighing out, at a shout she made at breakfast still coming patiently around the fluted wall, older and lower and not hers anymore.

Lonely would require quiet.

She is not young. She is not, exactly, old either. The oil preserves; the wind abrades; the two processes have reached an agreement on her face. Her successor is already chosen — there is always a child down below who does not startle at loud noises because loud noises never arrived for her, a child who puts her feet instead of her ears to doors. Someday that child will be brought up the teeth-colored track and left at the rim until the Ear has taken her name apart. Someday this woman will go into a hollow trunk herself, as the tuner before her did, curled in the resonating chamber with her bone saw and a bundle of feathers, and the finches will nest against her ribs, and beetles will climb her long bones in season and drop their weighted bodies and find, to the grove's satisfaction, that she still sounds right.

Until then she stays in the center of the noise that never leaves, small and oil-shining and utterly at home, one more voice in the one plant's many — the voice that listens back on purpose, and answers, and keeps the whole dry, green, impossible place in tune.
