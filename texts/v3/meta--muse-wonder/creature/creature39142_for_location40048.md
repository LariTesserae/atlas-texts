If you look for them the way you would look anywhere else, you will conclude that nobody lives here.

There is no village to find. No smoke that stays smoke, no roofline against the cloud, no path worn bright between doors. A tent left pitched at evening would be unmade by morning — not torn, exactly, but depressed, absorbed, the mat sighing up around its pegs until the whole thing lists into a pool that was not there the night before. No one with a home could survive the plateau. The plateau does not allow anything to stay where it was put.

So at first you will mistake them for something else.

A low shape in the red sphagnum, a long way off, not moving in the way an animal moves. A grazer, perhaps, with its head down. Or one of the mat's brief mistakes — a beast woven out of sedge, following a real beast's limp for an afternoon before the wind worries it apart.

It is not until one raises itself against a gust that you see the deliberateness in it.

They are low people. Not short, exactly, but built as if height were a kind of carelessness. Long through the body, broad through the hip and shoulder, and happiest on all fours when crossing open mat. Their legs fold in a way that keeps the belly close to the peat, out of the top inch of the world where the wind rules more completely than gravity. Their feet are the first true sign of them: three-toed, splayed enormously wide, webbed not for swimming but for standing — living snowshoes of bone and gristle that spread a grown adult's weight over a yard of lunging ground. Each step depresses the mat; each step is forgiven. They do not sink the way you sink. They negotiate.

Their skin is dark, tea-brown to black, and almost never visible. From the crown of the head down the back to the backs of the thighs, they cultivate a saddle of living sphagnum — rust-red, mustard, that pale exhausted green — rooted shallowly into the skin itself. It drinks the sideways rain before the rain can chill them. It insulates, it camouflages, it photosynthesizes a little in the brief amber tears of evening cloud. Old individuals carry whole gardens: cotton grass nodding at the shoulder blades, liverwort sealing the seams behind the ears, sedges with silica edges that ring very faintly when a gust combs them. A child is known by its bare, shining back. At adolescence the first moss is pressed in by its parents' palms and takes, or does not. If it does not, the child will not live a winter up here.

Under the garden, they are furnaces.

Not warm-blooded the way you are warm-blooded, evenly and wastefully. Their extremities run nearly cold — hands the temperature of tarn water, feet that can stand in frost-feathered pools without shivering. The heat is kept in the core, in a dense, slow gut where peat-fiber, sedge seed, eel-fat and a black fermented nectar taken from the furnace-flowers are broken down by bacteria that throw off a steady, composting warmth. You can hear it when one passes close. Not a heartbeat. A deeper, sequential thump — *thump-thump-thump* — vascular pistons forcing hot blood briefly out to a limb and hauling it back before the cold can steal it. In still air, their breath steams in rhythmic puffs that keep time with the flowers'.

They are, like everything true here, mostly alone.

A family is four, perhaps six, and it will hold a basin the size of a small county. You might walk three days in their country, hearing them, never seeing them: a hydraulic word sent palm-down through the mat at dusk and felt, an hour later, through the soles of a cousin's feet two valleys away; a stutter of blue-white static led down through the conductive hairs braided into their back-gardens and tapped, politely, into a sedge spire, so that the discharge jumps spire to spire across the pools and arrives as a smell of ozone and hot pennies that means *storm coming, lie down*, or *flower open to the south*, or *I am here, do not turn toward my heat.*

Speech, in air, is nearly useless to them. The wind edits everything into roar, the frogs in the warm springs chorus loud enough at night to erase conversation, and on the coldest mornings echoes come back in the wrong order besides. So they speak through the ground they are standing on, and through touch when they are close — a whole grammar of pressure and release, read not with the ears but with the feet, the palms, the sensitive skin of the inner wrist where the moss never grows. To stand in company with them is to be in constant, quiet conversation with the mat itself.

They eat what the archipelagos of mercy provide. Crane-eels, translucent and faintly lit blue in the conductive shallows, stunned with a static discharge led down a bone needle from a turbine tree's root-battery and eaten raw and crackling on the tongue. Frogs from the boiling springs, gathered by the fistful at night when the chorus is at its height and everything is vibration. The thumb-sized pollinator beetles that winter inside the furnace-flowers, scooped out scorched and drunk on fermented nectar in early spring — a delicacy, a medicine, a sacrament, all three. Cotton-grass heads. Sedge hearts. Once in a great while, when one of the shaggy grazers mires and dies, meat in such abundance that families converge from across three horizons, arriving singly out of the weather over the course of a day, eating in near silence with the starling-things screaming overhead, and leaving singly again before the carcass has cooled, each by a different route. They never retrace a path — not from superstition, though it has hardened into saying. A rebounded path holds warmth for hours. Things follow warmth. Their whole lives are a discipline of moving on at a steady pace, in enormous slow loops that follow the flowering of the furnace-flowers and the spinning-up of the turbine groves, loops so wide that a route completed in old age may pass within sight of the spring where they were born.

They are born in the springs, as it happens. In the black, carbonated water that arrives already boiling up from below, in the one kind of water they will drink and the only kind they trust. A newborn is small, blind, almost transparent for the first few days, and is kept for its first winter tucked against the hot core of a parent's belly, under the edge of the moss saddle, emerging scorched-pink and smelling of iron and peat. Children ride high on a parent's back when the mat is bad — the one moment in a plateau life when anyone holds their head fully up into the wind — gripping the garden with both hands, laughing into weather that would flay you.

That is also how you can tell them from the mat's imitations.

The mat makes people, sometimes. It extrudes a second walker out of woven sedge and white fungus, a mile behind the real one, getting the limp right, getting the steam of the breath almost right. The plateau folk do not fear these. They do not turn, they do not hurry. A mimic has no garden — the moss on its back is dead stuff, laid on, not rooted. It has no pressure in its step, and so the mat forgets each footfall instantly instead of holding it a moment the way it holds theirs. They let the copy follow for as long as the wind permits, the way you might let a younger sibling follow, and when it comes apart they sometimes stop — briefly, at a steady pace's smallest interruption — and press a palm flat to the place where it stood. Acknowledging that the ground remembered someone. It is not a small thing, to be remembered by the ground.

Because that is the real covenant under which they live. Everything else here, the mat forgets: footprints rebound, lakes migrate fifty yards in a night, channels reverse, your own reflection finishes your gesture a breath after you do, thoughtfully, as if it had considered it separately. But pressure, held long enough, is kept. A grazer dead a century sighs out its last breath when you step on its grave. And the plateau people, who press the same slow loops into the same living ground for seventy, eighty years, are remembered more deeply than any beast.

When one of them dies — out on the mat, almost always alone, almost always by lying down deliberately in a saucer far from any path the family is currently walking — the others do not come for the body. They do not need to. They come, days later, and stand at the saucer's rim, and put their feet down carefully, one by one, in the old places. And the depression retained in the root architecture sighs. Methane, warm from the half-coal seams below. A bubble of century-compressed air. And in it, faintly and horribly and unmistakably, the smell of the dead one's breath, the character of their gut-furnace, the iron of their blood, the particular sourness of the flowers they favored. The family stands in the weather and breathes their ancestor in, once, and is answered.

Then the mat begins, very slowly, to grow up through the body. Liverwort first, sealing the seams. Then sphagnum. A furnace-flower has been known to root, in time, in the warm chemistry of the chest.

They have no word for themselves that separates *people* from *pressure left in the ground.* The closest their hand-language comes, translated into feet, is something like: *that which the mat is still holding.*

So if you stay long enough — if you learn to drink only from springs you can hear boiling, to lie flat without dignity when every turbine tree stops at once, to never walk the same warmth twice — you may one evening, when the cloud tears open and the light goes raking and amber over black pools and red moss and the first arcs begin stitching sedge spire to sedge spire, see them at the true distance of their lives. One here, steam rising off a mossed back beside a furnace-flower. One a mile off, down on all fours against a gust, garden flattened, moving steadily and without any appearance of hurry. A third no more than a disturbance in a far basin, a place where the mat is being carefully, familiarly depressed and forgiven, depressed and forgiven, all the way toward a horizon made only of weather.

No roofs. No walls. Nothing built, ever.

Only held.
