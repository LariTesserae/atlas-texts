From the outer door they do not look like mountain people. They look, if anything, like divers who have forgotten to come up.

They come through the second airlock one at a time, never hurried, and stand a moment in the wet-room while the pressure settles in their ears. Hoods first. Outer shell peeled down and hung, dripping, on its peg. Boots knocked twice against the rime-bar, scraped, knocked again — not a courtesy but a reflex, learned before reading. Only then do they touch the inner door with bare fingers, cut short at the glove, and go down into their own weather.

They are human, unmistakably, and the Cup has been editing them for generations.

It shows first in the breath. Outsiders at this altitude pant and burn; Cup-born people breathe as if air were another kind of water to be portioned. Low in the chest, through the nose, with a pause between that can look like listening. Even children do it. Even asleep. Their ribs are deep, their movements rationed, their speech built to fit inside a single exhalation. A long sentence is a small extravagance. An argument is conducted in short, complete statements laid down one after another like stones, with silence in between for the other person's breath to fog and clear.

It shows in their skin, too. No one is tanned here except in a narrow band across the eyes and the backs of the hands — the well-light, caught on a terrace at the right hour, mirror-gold and unforgiving. The rest is indoor pale, washed-looking, perpetually damp at the cuffs and throat and hairline. Fingertips are soft-pruned by morning and calloused by afternoon over the same skin. Nails are kept very short. Almost everyone has the same faint pattern of darker lines at knuckle and wrist where cold metal has been handled for years: handrails, condenser shutters, drain grates, the rims of kettles.

They are not especially tall. The ramp decides bodies more than ancestry does. Seven terraces of spiral, several times a day, in layered clothes, on wet stone: it makes for strong calves, economical hips, one knee — usually the downhill one — that begins to complain at forty and is discussed thereafter like a member of the family. They walk leaning almost imperceptibly into the curve, the inner hand trailing near the wall, not for balance exactly but for information. A wall that sweats differently than it did yesterday is news. A dry patch where there should be seep is a worry. A warm patch is a small event to be reported at dinner.

Dryness, in fact, is their luxury. Not warmth — no one expects warmth outside bathhouse hour, and a person who complained about the cold would be looked at the way other towns look at someone complaining that night is dark. Dry. A cuff that has actually dried. A hood lining that does not cling cold to the neck. Gloves that squeak faintly instead of squelching. The well-off have a niche with a bench and a kettle and a floor vent that stays on a little longer than regulation. The rich, by local standards, own a second outer layer, so one can hang in the wet-room for a full day while the other is worn. Everyone can tell, at a glance, whose layers are on rotation and whose are not.

Their clothes have no bright colors to speak of — dyes fade in the mineral wet and are not replaced — but they are rich in repairs. Synthetics patch synthetics. The self-sealing alloy of the buildings has its humbler cousin in their outer shells: a tear left overnight closes to a darker seam, so an old coat becomes a map of every rock it has ever caught on. Grandparents wear decades on their shoulders. Children inherit seams before they inherit the coats.

They keep their hair close or braided tight under hoods, because loose hair never dries and mildews at the scalp, smelling faintly of iron. The school cat, long-haired and lordly on his pipe, is therefore an object of genuine, unironic envy. Small children follow him hoping some of his dryness might be contagious.

They are quiet in the way the description of the town is quiet, but it is not a temperament so much as an accumulation of small physical facts. Wet stone swallows echoes wrongly and carries drips too well; a raised voice arrives everywhere as weather. Breath spoken upward at night becomes rime on the membrane and someone's morning labor. So they speak low, and close, and preferably seated, and they have developed an etiquette of location for conversation that outsiders keep violating: not in a doorway, ever — a door held is heat lost and pressure argued with; not in the shaft after dark; not across the cistern during council. Talk belongs in niches, in bath-steam, at market stalls with your hands around something hot. To finish a sentence after your companion has stepped through a door, instead of before, marks you as either a visitor or rude.

In the bathhouse, when the waste heat comes down and the lower terraces fill with steam, this loosens by degrees. Not into noise — never into noise — but into length. Whole paragraphs become possible. This is where news travels: a new seep on the fourth terrace, clear and tasting of limestone, which means the fault has shifted a hair and the Cup crew will walk the seams tonight. A condenser running sweet. A film of bacteria in the overflow pools gone a deeper turquoise than last year, which the older people read the way farmers elsewhere read a sky. Marriages are arranged here obliquely, over several weeks of apparently casual adjacency. Recipes are debated with a seriousness that can start feuds.

The recipes matter because the food does not change. Vat protein, vat fat, the persuadable sweetness — hot, salty, gray-gold, taking the shape of whatever ceramic it is set in. So they have made an entire cuisine of temperature and texture and timing: the same block crisped at the edges in a dry pan, or steamed until it sighs apart, or chilled against cistern stone and cut thin and eaten with a sauce whose only ingredients are salt, heat, and having waited. Meals are much discussed because discussion is an ingredient. A child who invents a new way to fold the protein sheet is praised in public by the water board. Nobody in the Cup has ever seen a green thing growing, and their word for the turquoise bloom on the overflow pools is the closest word they have to *garden*.

Their authorities follow the water. The water board is not a metaphor for government; it is the government, and its clerks — patient people with waterproof sleeves and perfect handwriting, because the ledgers must survive damp that eats screens — can tell you to the finger's width what the cistern gained at noon melt and what the town breathed back overnight. Engineers are a kind of priesthood that has deliberately renounced mystery. They describe the reactors as plumbing and the folded space as a kept opening, and they train their daughters and sons to maintain what no one claims entirely to understand, by hand, by sequence, by feel: listen to the membrane struts for the tick of forming ice, watch the mirror vanes for a tremor out of true, rap the fault wall in the prescribed places and know the sound of a Cup that is holding. Theory is considered a pleasant hobby for the night hours. Competence is a hand that knows when a valve is about to complain.

Work is rotation because survival is. Everyone, at some age, has done membrane duty in an orange shell at the top of the well, scraping rime while the whole sky stands open overhead; everyone has done water-watch, walking the spiral at the melt-pulse with a listening rod; everyone has sat the quiet shift in the cistern vault watching a black surface for the pale, unhurried turn of a fish, and logged *none seen* in the book with exactly the same care as *one, near the east walkway, slow*. Good luck that is recorded becomes a civic fact.

Children are raised by the terraces. They learn the colors of dangerous water before the names of their own relatives: clear is probably safe, milky is mineral, yellow or rust means new rock has opened and you run, not walk, to tell. They can swim nowhere — the cistern is not for bodies — so they learn water entirely by ear and hand and duty, which makes them oddly reverent and entirely unafraid of it. They are called quickly to look when a raven lands on the membrane, and they press close to the well-light and watch the black shape distort in the breathing skin, and afterward they imitate its patience for days, standing too still in doorways until they are moved along.

Old age in the Cup is damp in the joints and magnificent in the memory. The old remember seeps that ran for one spring thirty years ago and where the wall sang before the last resealing. They are consulted like instruments. They sit in the warmest niches with their hoods down — white or iron-gray hair drying, for once, in rising bathhouse air — and they correct the young by tapping the stone. They are buried, when the time comes, by water, which is to say completely: the town reclaims everything it can from breath and bath, and nobody here finds it morbid that a person should end as part of the general level of the cistern. They find it untidy to imagine otherwise. The fish turn in all of them eventually.

They do not call themselves hidden people, or mountain people, or keepers of anything. If pressed they will say they are *from the Cup*, in the tone of someone naming a street. They have no romance about the folded dark that makes their town possible; they have a maintenance schedule. They have no fear of the mountain, exactly; they have a relationship with it, renewed daily, in writing, in rime scraped and seeps reported and doors never propped. The mountain moves an inch a year. So do they.

At noon, wherever they are — on the ramp, in the schoolroom, at a stall with their fingerless gloves around a bowl — they tend to stop when the melt-pulse comes through. Not ceremonially. The channels simply get too bright and loud to talk over for a moment, and there is nothing to do but listen to the whole city briefly running clear. Breath fogs. Walls bead. Somewhere far below, the black water takes its daily finger's width, and the fish, unseen, adjust their slow circles to the new depth.

Then the pulse passes. The wipers come out like patient beetles. The tram hums on its curve. And the people of the Cup go on with the unremarkable, continuous work of living inside a stone that has not finished becoming a mountain — damp, cold, quiet, and entirely at home.
