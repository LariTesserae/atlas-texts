At first you will mistake the tenants for the resident.

They are easier to see. They grain the walls. Cave crickets the color of damp paper, springtails in a pale, seething millions, midges holding their trembling station under the ceiling where the currents cancel out. Blind amphipods turning in the black pool. Caddis larvae dragging their homemade armor of rust flakes behind them. Frogs no bigger than the last joint of a thumb, peat-brown, throatless. The dipper coming and going through the slit as if it has a key.

But they all live the same way: sideways. Flattened to the rim, tucked into moss, wedged into cracks still white and sharp inside. They are crowded absurdly, furiously, because they are not allowed anywhere else. When the pressure begins to build they all turn at once — antennae laid back, wings folded, small feet braced — like iron filings finding north. They are not the thing that lives here. They are the things that live *around* it, the way eyelashes live around an eye.

The resident occupies the middle.

It has no nest there, no body you can put a hand on between events. That is its camouflage and its anatomy. The smooth bowl of dark stone in the center of the floor, washed concave and clean, is not empty. It is kept. Scoured. Nine metres of moving air held in a lens between peat above and fresh rock below, a volume far larger than the little slit in the marsh should be able to contain — that is the body.

You meet it first as a wrongness in your own breathing.

The moss curtain at the entrance bows inward. The drips on the ceiling, which have been swelling and falling in fine threads all the while, hesitate, stretch, and crawl impossibly up-slope toward the back crack. Your ears fill. The cave is inhaling, not metaphorically — through its mouth, through every stone whistle in its walls, through the stepped pools bubbling backward — and the resident is gathering itself out of thinness, drawing the cold marsh air, the charged spray, the stunned midges, the mineral rain, deep into the place under the floor where it keeps its gizzard.

Down there, out of sight, it grinds.

Flood-rounded cobbles, swallowed years ago by meltwater and trapped in a siphon loop, begin to turn over one another. Clank. Clank-clank. A rhythm, then a hammering. The bowl hums under your boots like the housing of a turbine. This is the closest thing it has to a heart, and it is made of stones it did not grow but collected, the way a bird collects grit for its stomach. It is young — this whole edge of the world is young, glacier-fresh, peat-torn, unsoftened — and so everything in it is still too sharp to be quiet. Old caves sigh through rounded throats. This one screams through split shale. Its voice has not worn down yet.

Then the pause. Loaded, trembling. The liverworts flatten scale over wet scale. The orange and sulphur and arterial-red algae shine under a skin of water drawn taut as a drum. Somewhere a side pool lifts its stone lid a fraction, tests it, settles.

And the resident becomes itself, all at once.

Every seven to eleven minutes — never regular enough to count on, because tide and meltwater argue over the timing — the siphon reverses and it exhales its whole body through the room. Twenty seconds. A piston-stroke of warm, iron-smelling, electrically alive air blasting out of the rear crack, atomizing the pools to white fog, snapping blue in the fog where wet stone points almost meet, roaring through the whistles until the walls take up harmonics and the frogs — a hundred of them, throats still, bodies positioned like reeds in a flute — are played by the passing of it, and the blast goes out the entrance chorused, amplified, animal at last, and slams the moss curtain flat against the ice outside.

That is the only time you can truly say it is *there*. Between blasts it disperses into process: a seep pushing milky heat up into the top pool, iron settling in slow rust clouds through the middle pool, clear black current waiting in the bottom pool to receive whatever has been washed down and coated and made ready. The assembly line is its digestion. Eggs laid in the warmth will be flushed into the rust to be armored, flushed into the cold to feed. Precipitate falls on the walls and is eaten. Midges fall and are eaten. Droplets fall upward and are breathed. Nothing is wasted because nothing is allowed to settle in the stomach-void and rot.

The rim-life knows the shift whistle better than any mill town. Feed on the inhale, when everything rains gently down the walls. Brace at the peak. Sing, if you are a frog, only when the air does it for you. Commute, if you are a dipper, in the warm lull just after, shaking spray in a place no dipper has any business being. It is not cruelty that enforces this and it is not kindness. Standing in the bowl during an exhale would not be an attack. It would be a misunderstanding of what the bowl *is*. You do not stand inside a lung when it empties. You do not lie down in a throat.

It is, for all its noise, a brief creature. Its lifespan is measured twice: in minutes, between one gathering and the next, and in winters, between the spring the glacier left and the winter the peat roof, groaning an inch lower every season, finally caves it in. It will silt, or freeze solid, or collapse, and at the low narrow end already a new slit is worrying open in the stone where the pressure has begun to prospect for its next body. That is how it reproduces — not by eggs, those belong to its tenants, but by abandonment. Hollow after hollow along a young, breaking margin, each one learned by the water and forgotten by the stone.

So there is no point looking for it directly. Looking finds a wet room, crowded at the edges, inexplicably empty in the middle. You have to wait, with your back in the luminous moss and the crickets flowing politely around your boots, and let it arrive on its own schedule — a sucking at the ears, a hum in the teeth, hair lifting in charged air, the taste of hot stone and green fast-growing things at the back of the tongue. You have to let the silence assemble it.

Then for twenty seconds the whole small place is one animal, loud and steaming and sparking and singing with borrowed voices, blowing its warm breath out across a frozen marsh that will never be warm on its own.

Then it is gone out of itself again, sighing inward through its slit mouth, already hungry, already beginning the slow work of the next breath.
