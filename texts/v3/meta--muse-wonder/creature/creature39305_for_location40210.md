You will not see them first. You will not hear them first, either, not in the ordinary way.

You will notice them in the tone. Where a person stands on the plateau, the great grinding note changes around them, just slightly, the way water changes around a stone in a river that has not yet been invented. A dampening. A local tuning. Dust that was hanging in rings in the bright air falters and re-forms, lower. Somewhere across the pumice a grove of ferns re-tunes a fraction, all in phase.

And then, perhaps, you will find their shadow before you find them.

It will be sitting down. That is how you will know it is theirs and not yours. The people who live here have learned not to argue with precedence. If their shadow has arrived early at a crossroads, or at the edge of a silent hollow, or outside a hut they have not yet entered, they sit down beside it in the loud ground and wait politely for themselves to catch up. They can wait a long time. They are very good at waiting. Everything they are is a practice in not being finished yet.

There were locals once, the slates say, in many hands. There are locals still. There may not be a clean difference.

They are few — a few dozen, maybe, across a country the size of a small country, hard to count because distances swell in loud ground and because you will sometimes meet the same person before the meeting you remember. They do not live together in villages. Company is too intimate here, and too dangerous. Two voices raised carelessly in untuned ground can alter a valley for a generation. Three could plant a wood no one intended, with leaves cupped around a consonant that will still be trying to finish itself when all three speakers are dead. So they live apart, in the low, roofless huts of unmortared bubble-stone, one person to a hut, two in the cold season, never more than could touch foreheads in the dished hollow of the floor and understand one another without sound.

They do not look at first like a separate kind of person. That comes later, slowly, the way the plateau itself comes.

Their foreheads are broad and polished. That is the first true mark. From childhood they speak brow to brow, bone to bone, and the place of speaking becomes over years faintly ridged, callused, a little flattened, as if thought had worn a doorway. Strangers learn the posture in a day and ache for a week. The locals lean in without thinking when they want to listen, and tilt away when a conversation is over, and this small bow — forehead offered, forehead withheld — is most of their etiquette. To refuse it is a kind of deafness. To press too hard is to shout.

Their mouths are small in practice if not in fact. They keep them shut. Their lips are cracked like everyone's here, sealed often with the black oil from the leaves, which smells of hot copper and mint and keeps the skin from splitting to the teeth. They write young, on slate, before they are allowed anything like fluent speech. A child is given a slate before she is given a name she may say aloud. She learns to make letters before she learns that a cry, held too long in the wrong bowl of ground, might take root behind the hut and be there, green-black and glossy and still crying in plant form, when she is grown.

So the children here are uncannily quiet. Not frightened-quiet. Practiced-quiet. They communicate in taps through the soles of the feet, through a stone hammer held between teeth, through foreheads pressed to a parent's forehead with the furious, whole-body concentration of the very young. Their first spoken word is a ceremony and an agricultural decision. It is not spoken indoors. It is not spoken in silence. The family — if two or three people can be called that, gathered in foreheads and exile — walks the child out to a grove that is already speaking, already lush with ferns at knee-height whose largest fronds are shivering at the low octave, and chooses the ground as carefully as a wetter people would choose a grave or a marriage bed. Then the child says the word she intends to live with. If the groves accept it, the leaf-dust settles. If a seedling comes up later cupped like a mouth around the vowel, someone will tend it. Nobody calls this failure. Sometimes the most honest members of a family are trees.

Their ears are almost decorative. Pinched, close to the skull, often stopped with wads of woven reed-fiber, not because the tone can be shut out — it comes up through the feet, through the jaw, through the fluid of the inner ear itself — but because the little flutter of air-sound on the eardrum is a distraction, a false, thin copy of the real listening. They listen through their skeletons. Old people, who have lived longest in the loud places, develop a kind of second hearing in the sternum and the shins. They can tell by walking where a grove will be in ten years. They can tell which pass needs a stone moved from its cairn and which cairn, if touched, would retune a mile of country into barrenness. Their work, if they have one common work, is that: tuning. Not farming, exactly. Not building. A endless, minute adjustment of a world still deciding what notes it contains.

They move stones. They dish floors. They repair the roofless huts, knowing roofs would sing and tear themselves apart. They stand inside reed-and-hide resonators at the forge when they need to think, and emerge blinking, a little unstrung, as if quiet had loosened them. They carry copper spikes and stone hammers and slate and pliers beaten from a single sheet of metal, and nothing made to kill, because there is nothing here to kill and because a weapon would need an edge, and edges have not been invented here yet in any lasting way.

They eat what drinks sound. Seeds that ring as they fall and are gathered at the fading of their note, crisp and black-shelled, tasting of oil and ash and an almost-green bitterness. Leaves squeezed for their slow oil. The thick hearts of the low glossy trees, which must be cut quickly before the cut surface bead-seals and the whole plant re-tunes away from the wound. They do not drink water. There is none to drink. They stand in resonant bowls at the long dusk, when the tone drops a quarter-step over six hours and the groves sigh shut in sequence largest to smallest, and breathe through their teeth, and let the vibration shake moisture loose from the mineral air and from the plants and from their own bodies in some exchange no visitor has quite described. In the morning their mouths taste of copper. They survive. In the silent hollows they would not, and they know it in their bones before they know it in thought: the sudden absence is like falling, like a body misremembering gravity. They walk in the loud places. They rest in the moderately loud. They never sleep in silence, because what sleeps without the tone may not be reminded, come waking, how to hold together.

This is not metaphor for them. Their bodies, too, are provisional.

People who stay a season arrive with finished-world assumptions and shed them like cracked skin. The locals were born shedding. Their bones are dense in odd places and hollow in odder ones, resonant chambers in the long bones of the leg. Their teeth ache at the high harmonic from the inflating peaks even though none of them has ever had a filling. Their shadows detach professionally, familiarly, like dogs asked to wait. Their memories pool. Spend enough years up there and the locals remember, all of them, the same hut from the inside, the same fire that never burned, the same explanation of how to leave, given forehead to forehead by someone whose face becomes less important than the pressure. It does not frighten them. Why should one person's past be private, they might write, on slate, when the air itself is an echo? Their constellations repeat, slightly offset. So do they.

They are patient with cause arriving late. A woman plants her footprints ahead of herself on the far side of a grove and steps around the tiny black seedlings unfurling from compressions she hasn't made yet, out of courtesy. An old man hears the bell of a seed, looks up, and moves his hand an inch to where the leaf, a breath later, shatters into ringing pieces — catching not the sound but the consequence. Courtship, among them, is often a matter of recognizing someone whose shadow has been keeping you company at the forge for years. Grief is a matter of going to the cairns and adding, not a grave-stone, but a note. Change the tone of a pass by one small stone and a mile of plants re-tune, and somewhere an intended grove thins crisply where the dead will no longer be fed. That is as close as they come to burial. The dead continue as a local alteration in what can grow.

It would be wrong to call them deaf, or primitive, or waiting for the real world to begin. In the salt-white lake bed at the center of the silent hollow — a place they avoid, a place they nonetheless built for — the wooden boat still waits, caulked with plant oil, serious and dry, for water to be invented. It was their grandmothers who built it, or their grandchildren. Sequence is unreliable. They were not foolish. They were early. They heard, through their feet, that the plateau was adding itself to itself, stone being thought of, grinding, deciding, becoming, and they understood that someday oceans would be the sort of thing that could happen, and they prepared a courtesy for that day.

So the inhabitants of the unfinished plateau are these: tuners and slate-keepers, speakers who rarely speak, gardeners whose rain is sound, people who press their foreheads together to say good morning and who choose a child's first word like a planting and who sit down beside their own early shadows to wait for themselves. They live between the silent hollows where nothing grows and the sound-drunk groves where too much does. They keep their mouths shut in untuned ground. They say only what they are willing to harvest later.

And if you stay long enough — if your lips crack and your ears go useless and you begin to listen through your teeth, through the hammer in your hand — you may discover that the description has quietly changed its subject. That you, too, have begun to dampen the tone where you stand. That a grove has re-tuned itself around your presence. That somewhere ahead of you in the virgin ash your footprints are already softening at the edges, already filling with small black seedlings, patiently expecting you to become someone who lives here.
