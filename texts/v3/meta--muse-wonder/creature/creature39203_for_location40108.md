It is lived in first by sound, and only then by bodies.

Before you see anyone in the morning you hear what has been awake all night shifting over. The pumps change pitch as the sumps are drawn down. The frogs, throat-deep in the drains, thin from a grinding chorus to single, spaced gulps. In the fissures above the top terrace there is a dry rustling as the bats settle back into the rock, thousands of them folding into cracks too narrow for a hand, smelling of ammonia and warm dust. The swifts come out to replace them, screaming between the blocks in the dimness before sun-up. On the ceilings, the geckos go still. In the walls, the cockroaches retreat behind the electrical panels where it stays hot even in the coolest hour, which is not cool.

About three hundred people live among all this. They will tell you they are the town. Officially, they are.

They are the ones who run the power hall in shifts, who keep the pumps from joining the sumps into a lake, who teach in the school with mesh on every window and unmesh it, patiently, to let the trapped dragonflies out. They live in the two pink workers' blocks and in the smaller houses on the blasted terraces, up on plinths, behind double doors. They have learned to move as the pit requires: slow in the dim morning, slower at noon when the sun drops straight in for ninety murderous minutes and nobody with sense is outside without something over their head, slow again at seven in the evening when the limestone gives back the day's heat and the streets are hottest and everyone comes out anyway because that is when it is bearable to be social.

You can recognize a long-timer by the courtesies in their hands. They shake out their shoes without looking. They cover a plate the instant it is set down, a cup the instant it is poured, not from fastidiousness but from an absolute knowledge of flies. They sweep thresholds at night. They tuck mosquito nets with the precision of people making a bed on a ship. They sleep, many of them, with earplugs in, not against the machinery — you stop hearing the machinery after a month — but against the frogs in season, and the swifts, and the particular child on the next terrace being told to shut the screen door properly. Their skin stays strangely pale for people living under such heat, because they live most of the day in their own shadow, in permanent late afternoon. Their clothes bloom faintly white at the seams with salt. Their lower walls bloom. In a sense they do too.

The children are the most pit-native of the humans. They know which stairwell currently has wasps and which pump is knocking and what that knock means. They know not to play in the half-built cistern, and they play at its edge to watch the tadpoles. They chase lizards off sunlit walls at noon — the only hour the lizards are sluggish with too much heat — and are watched at night, unblinkingly, by geckos while they do homework under blue-white porch bulbs. They have never known a wind that reaches the bottom. To them weather is not something that arrives, it is something the rock exhales.

But they are outnumbered, outweighed, outlasted in shifts.

At street level the pigeons own every ledge not strung with wire, plump and iridescent and entirely unbothered. Sparrows live inside the market roof and come down in a continual rain for crumbs. Rats use the overhead pipe racks as their own streets above the human streets, traveling valve to valve above people's heads, fat on food waste and grain and whatever the cats miss. The cats, therefore, are officially discouraged and unofficially fed on every stoop in town, thin, scarred, regal animals who sleep in drains and leave headless offerings by the clinic steps. Without them, everyone says, the rats would be worse. With them, the rats are simply organized.

In the cool exhaust of the clinic, the stray dogs sleep through most of existence. They are slow dogs, long-suffering dogs, dogs who have learned that chasing anything in this heat is a fool's economy. They lift an ear when a truck idles past. They sigh when the frogs start up. They belong to no one and are known by name by everyone, which, in this town, is the same thing.

Lower down, life gets less individual.

The frogs in the sumps are the size of fists, green-brown, throat-deep in warm water fringed with reeds, and at dusk they do not so much call as become a pressure in the ears, a single grinding organism made of ten thousand gullets. Mosquitoes rise off that same water in columns visible where the light catches them, and dragonflies patrol just above them, hunting on the wing. Tadpoles crowd the seep-filled cistern. Algae makes the concrete channels luxuriant, slick, emerald, the most successful garden in town. Red ants make highways across the empty gravel lots from shade to shade, carrying things larger than themselves in perfect, indifferent lines, rerouting around a boot without breaking stride. Beetles mass on thresholds overnight as if trying to get in out of the dark. At night the moths come to the work lights over the power hall so thick they soften every beam, and the geckos come out on the ceilings to eat them, and the bats come out of the fissures in a thin black smoke to eat what the geckos miss.

Even the buildings have tenants that are not people. Salt lives in the lower walls and spreads in dark fans. Mold finds the backs of cupboards. Cockroaches own the warm country behind fuse boxes. Wasps take a stairwell for a season and grant it back. Swallows nest in the joints of scaffolding that has stood so long it has become architecture. Oleander by the clinic — poisonous, unwatered, unkillable — flowers over all of it, the one upright green thing that seems to have understood the terms: tolerate the damp, tolerate the heat, eat light when it comes straight down and wait in shadow the rest of the time.

What lives here, finally, is anything that can stand to be trapped and thrive on it. Everything that found its way down the spiral ramp, or fell in, or blew in, or was carried in on a tire or a boot sole, and discovered warm standing water and endless insects and cracks in raw, weeping rock and no winter and no wind and no real predators except each other — and stayed. Species piled on species in the bottom of an unfinished reservoir, feeding and sheltering and breeding in the infrastructure built for a plant that needed people who needed housing that needed pumps that made sumps that made frogs that made mosquitoes that made swifts and bats and geckos, in a loop so tight you cannot say where the town ends and its infestation begins.

The people keep it from drowning. The rest keep it alive.

At night, when the yellow lights come on early and the pump indicators show red along the channel and the smell of fried onions drifts down from the top street through hot iron and wet limestone, it is hard to tell who is hosting whom. A woman sweeps beetles from her threshold. A cat waits beside her, patient as a landlord. A child bangs the screen door and geckos scatter across the wall above his head. Below them all, in water a centimetre higher than yesterday, the frogs begin again.

Above, on the rim, the plain is empty and silent. Below, everything that can live in a hot, damp hole in the ground is doing so, densely, noisily, gradually — and it is not going anywhere.
