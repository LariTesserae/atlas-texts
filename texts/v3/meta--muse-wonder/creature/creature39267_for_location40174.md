From above, it seems to own the place alone.

And it does. But that is too simple a way to say it, because it does not live *on* the plain, or *in* the city underneath it. It is both of them, and it has been outliving its tenants inside itself for a very long time.

The living thing is one tree.

Not a forest fused together, not a grove. One seed, one trunk-system, one continuous grain running for hundreds of meters in slow arcs under the wind. If you could peel it up — twelve meters thick at the heart, tapering at the rim — you would find that almost all of it is dead.

That is the first strangeness of its life. The inhabitant is mostly its own corpse.

Just beneath the upper surface there is a thin, tenacious layer that is still alive: a skin of cambium and waxy, resinous tissue, cool to the palm, faintly tacky in the hollows of the knots where the day's heat never quite reaches. It breathes there. At night it opens its pores and its old flues and draws the desert air down, laboriously, degree by degree colder, until what little moisture the air is carrying sighs out of it and is caught, condensed, hoarded in cells so laden with resin and wax that nothing else could drink from them and live. That is all it takes. A mouthful stolen from the night, spread over an acre. A sip conducted sideways through fibers that have learned, over millennia of thirst, never to let go.

Everything below that skin — the dense summerwood, the heartwood older than the ladder-hole, the pillars that go down through vanished soil, the ribbed ceiling, the polished floor of fused roots — is the tree remembering itself in wood. It laid that body down ring by ring and then vacated it, the way a snail vacates the inner turns of its shell, except that people came and carved rooms into the vacated turns and hung their houses from them.

So the dweller is enormous and, in the usual sense, absent from most of its own house. You can knock on a pillar and it rings. You can press a hand to a house-wall and feel nothing stir. And yet it is growing. That is how you know it is still there, in residence, underneath its own dead.

It grows only sideways now. It gave up height before anyone arrived to notice. No branches, no leaves, nothing to catch the wind or spend water on shade. Out at the buried rim, where the lid thins into the ground like the skirt of a bell, it adds a new ring in a good decade — a slow, pale expansion into the bone-colored desert, a few centimeters of new wood shouldering the grit aside. Inward, that same growth expresses itself as pressure. The ceiling descends by fractions no eye can catch. The pillars thicken. A doorway cut generous a century ago is a finger narrower now. A market arcade bored through a fallen trunk is closing, patient as a healing wound. Streets bend, almost politely, along grain lines reasserting themselves.

It is reclaiming the city the way ice reclaims a crack: not out of malice, not even out of hunger. Simply by continuing.

It is also, in its dryness, a thief. This is not metaphor down there. The tree lives its whole life on the edge of desiccation and anything aqueous that enters it is treated as weather — as a brief, local rain to be harvested. A waterskin lightens in the hand. Sweat vanishes. Blood dropped on the floor is taken into the grain like ink into blotting paper. Its resin, which permeates every fiber and makes the whole interior sterile — no beetles in that wealth of dead wood, no borers, no mites, no bats, no mice in the granary nets — is the other half of that thrift. Life is too expensive in water to be tolerated as a guest. The tree keeps its body clean of competitors by poisoning it, gently and totally, with itself.

That is why the city is so quiet of small sounds and so loud with large ones. The dweller has no heartbeat, but it has a voice, and the voice is structural.

Wind crosses the plain above and finds the high knot-holes and breathing pores, and the hollow skull under the lid becomes an organ. The note arrives through the soles of the feet, in the sternum, in the teeth. Each pillar answers at its own pitch according to its thickness. On a strong night the whole interior holds a chord for minutes — the tree, quite literally, sounded by the weather moving over its back.

On still nights it makes smaller, more private noises. It contracts in the unvarying cellar-cool. Fibers complain. A crack starts far across the void and travels a grain line like a distant shot, and because the speaker was the ceiling and the floor and a dozen singing walls, the sound seems to come from everywhere. This is the nearest thing it has to restlessness. A body that large cannot shift in its sleep without groaning through all its joints.

And in the tended troughs of pulverized heartwood, in the old cultivation terraces, its other selves go on blindly trying. Pale shoots, never green, never meant for light, pushing up etiolated and pepper-hot, tasting of cold ash — whether crop or offspring or simply the tree testing the dark with new tissue, no one remains to say. They grow without tenders now, overshoot their troughs, wither resin-dry, and are slowly conducted back into the floor. It eats its own attempts the same way it is eating the houses: without haste.

Which is perhaps the truest way to describe who lives there now.

Not the vanished builders — though their handprint is still in a resin patch at a child's height beside a doorway, though the guide-grooves are worn to troughs by bare feet, though every step is a saucer and every rail is silk from palms. They were, for a while, a second inhabitant, a small warm colony living inside the first one, navigating by knocking on its bones.

But they are gone, and their host is not.

It lives there still, thin and green-less just under the skin of a wooden plain that looks like nothing; vast and dead and resonant below; drinking visitors to cracked lips, closing doors by a hair a year, re-hanging nothing, letting the suspended houses settle and groan on their cables, holding the listening-basin at its center like an ear turned inward on itself. In a few centuries the ladder-hole will spiral shut, the basin will shallow and go silent, and it will be sealed again with itself, alone on the flat horizon.

Until then, press an ear flat to a knot on a windy night, up on that polished grain, and you can hear the dweller from outside: muffled, enormous, subterranean music — one organism sounding through the empty rooms it is slowly taking back into its body.
