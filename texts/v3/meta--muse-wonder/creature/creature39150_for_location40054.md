The first inhabitant you notice is the human, because you are one, and because he is easy to find.

He lives where everything else lives: at the edge. His lean-to is tucked into the one corner the rocking beam cannot sweep, behind the pressure tanks, where the wall makes a small triangle of stillness. Canvas, a sheet of boiler plate for a windbreak, a cot raised on pipe offcuts so the condensation won't rot it from underneath, a kettle that never quite leaves its tap. In winter his blankets steam faintly all night. In summer he sleeps with his shirt open and lizards occasionally asleep in the warm fold of it, and he has learned to check before he moves.

He is not, properly speaking, a resident. Keepers come up by cable car once a season, one at a time, and are relieved whether they want to be or not. But after a month up there they all begin to look like the same person. It isn't the uniform — there isn't one, just oil-dark clothes gone soft at the seams. It's the posture. The slight lean away from the center of the room, even when sitting. The habit of keeping one hand resting lightly on iron, reading the machine through the palm. The way they stop turning their heads toward the blast. The wax plugs wear a groove behind the ear.

They talk, when they have to talk to a visitor, mostly with their hands, even in the lulls. The painted signs on the wall are for pressure, for *stand clear*, for *tea*, for *look up* — swift shit, falling scale, a dropped wrench from the gallery — but keepers develop private additions in grease pencil, quickly wiped away by the humidity. They read gauges by touch when the needles blur. They can tell, eyes closed, by the tremor in a particular floor plate, whether it's the compressor or the flywheel that wants grease. Several have gone a little deaf in exactly the same frequencies. All of them, in the logbook, develop handwriting that leans the same way, as if the vibration were getting into the pen.

He is the largest thing here, and the least adapted. This is not his place. He is tolerated in the margin because he maintains the margin. He clears the drip channels, but not too thoroughly. He cuts the fig root out of the south valve, but leaves enough. He rakes ash and, without being told to, spreads the finest of it where the ferns look thin. He is less an owner than a seasonal organ the building has learned to grow — hands, arriving when the ice needs breaking from the horn lip, leaving again before he can go properly deaf, or mad, or attached.

The permanent inhabitants are smaller, and they are legion, and they have all solved the same problem in different bodies: how to live pressed flat against the walls of a room whose center belongs to a machine.

The fig is the oldest of them, unless the moss is older. No one planted it. A pip, probably, carried up in a keeper's gut or a bird's, lodging in the ash box of the dead boiler when the ash was still warm. It should have grown up. There was nowhere up to go that the beam would not take, so it grew sideways instead, a trunk as thick now as a man's thigh running horizontally along the south wall at waist height, sending out roots like slow hands into every seam that sweats. Its leaves are hand-sized and hand-shaped and they shiver constantly, in air that never stops moving. It flowers without showing it, fruits without announcement, and then all at once in high summer, after a run of long blasts, the figs swell and split in the lulls with a soft tick you can feel in your teeth. For three days the room smells overripe and alcoholic. Bees go drunk. Choughs get there first. The keeper eats a few and logs it in pencil: *figs out. Good year.*

The ferns have mapped the water. Maidenhair where the condensation first forms, delicate enough that the blast bends it double a hundred times a day and it springs back. A coarser, darker fern where the copper runs green. Another, almost black, where the rust weeps. They do not spread randomly; they occupy drip lines the way a city occupies rivers, and if you moved a pipe six inches you would, a season later, find a dying town and a new one founding itself. Spore hangs in the warm air for hours, invisible, until the great clearing blast takes it. The wallflowers that root in the mortar have learned to hold their seeds loose and wait. They are patient in a way only things rooted beside a horn can be.

Above them, in the trusses, are the choughs. They are not seasonal, exactly — they come and go through a broken louver nobody has ever been able to find from inside — but they behave as residents, proprietary, walking the ironwork two by two with their red bills tapping, riding the thermal that rises off the boilers with their wings half-closed, screaming cheerfully into noise that makes their screaming inaudible. They steal bright things. Washers, mostly. There is a cache of them, decades deep, in a disused junction box, collected for no reason anyone can devise except that they like the room and want it in pieces.

The swifts are seasonal and absolute. They arrive in spring within the same three days, poured backward out of the horn at dawn as if the mountain had inhaled them in the dark, and they nest by the hundreds behind the baffle plates where the vibration is worst. The whole colony vibrates. Eggs in nests of saliva and fig fiber lie in a shaking dark, and hatch there, and the chicks grow up deaf to any frequency below a scream. No predator will follow them in. No predator can stand it. At dusk they return through the horn mouth singularly, folding their wings at the last instant, threading a ten-foot flared throat that might, at any moment, clear itself and blast them out again. Sometimes it does. The colony does not seem to mind. A swift blown half a mile downwind climbs back in an hour, arrives shrieking, and goes to roost.

Bats live in the warm felt above the tanks, a hanging, upside-down obscurity by day. At night they drop through the green-filtered dark and take moths out of the compressor draught. The moths are the color of rust, wing and body, and rest with wings open on warm iron as if pinned there, and are — to any useful degree, as the logbook once put it — profoundly deaf. Generations of them have selected for it. A moth that startled at sound here would never stop flying, and would be dead in a day.

Lizards own the sunlit boiler lagging. They were carried up in a crate of valve packing a generation ago — someone recorded it as a joke, *stowaways, now crew* — and they have no way down. Four hundred vertical meters of catwalk have seen to that. They have not needed a way down. On the hot iron their lives run fast: hatch in a warm seam, mature in a season, scarred males with regenerated tails doing push-ups in the green light to rivals reflected in polished brass. They eat flies, and moths when they can catch them, and fallen fig when it's soft enough. They are fat in a way alpine lizards are not supposed to be. They have never seen winter properly. Winter, to them, is when the glass is cold and the dripping is loud and they crowd closer to the boiler and move slowly in the mornings, and that is all.

And in the sumps, at night, the frogs. Also carried up, also unable or unwilling to leave — in plant pots, in a bucket of seedlings for some keeper's attempt at salad, escaped into the drainage system and found it perfect. Shallow, warm, algae-green water that never freezes, that thrums. They sing in the lulls between compressor cycles, small throats pulsing in black water, and sometimes the horn gets them — catches a chorus at exactly the moment of a minor blast and throws it out over the void amplified to the size of weather. Down in the valley, on still nights, they occasionally hear it and do not know what it is. A brief, enormous croaking from the mountaintop that silences everything for a valley mile.

The bees navigate all of this — the draughts, the pressure changes, the air that moves in great slow circuits from boiler to glass to floor — by a method no keeper has ever understood, and keep a hive in a hollow in the fig trunk that it has politely grown around. They work the wallflowers, the fig, the speckled orchid-things on the copperwork. They come home laden through air that smells of oil and hot iron and green leaves, and they dance on a comb that vibrates once every seven seconds with the passage of the flywheel, and this does not seem to corrupt their directions at all. Perhaps their directions are only: *here, along the wall, where it blooms.* Perhaps that's enough. There is nowhere else to go.

That is the law they've all internalized, plant and animal and seasonal human: keep to the margin, keep small, keep many, sync your life to the blast.

Everything here breeds, feeds, ripens, or sings in the intervals. Nothing fights the machine for the center; the one vine that tried, in a wet spring, got its green arch trimmed by the beam into a perfect negative of the danger, leaves shaved flat on the inner curve, and grew thicker there. Egg, spore, pip, and pup are all timed to vibration. Even the algae in the troughs bloom after the interior rains.

And once in twenty blasts, when the governor lifts to the red line, they all participate, willing or not, in the one act that justifies the arrangement. Seeds loosen. Spore lifts. The horn clears and takes a visible breath of the room out with it — green-gold, laden, a plume of potential that hangs over the drop before the wind strips it down-valley to the crags where no soil ever was.

The inhabitants do not follow it. They stay, pressed to their warm walls, in their shaking nests, in their sumps and seams. They let the room reproduce by proxy, scattering their children to a mountainside they'll never see, while they remain here, in the only room on the capped peak, living richly and entirely in the space a machine left over.
