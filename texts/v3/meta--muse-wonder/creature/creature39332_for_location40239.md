From the rim, at first light, you might see only one of them — a figure up on a roof in the half-dark, barefoot on wet slate, resetting a stone. Small, or made small by crouching. Then another below, handing up clay in a wooden bowl. Then a third, a child, appearing at a sill two floors above the lane and crossing a two-plank bridge with a full jar held against her chest, steady as if she were walking on dry ground.

By the time the swifts leave the eaves, the whole pit is quietly awake. No bells. No shouting down lanes. They come out of doors that open onto stairs, roofs, other people's yards, and they begin, without any visible signal, the work the place requires before breakfast can be thought of.

They are marsh people who no longer live in the marsh. That is the first thing their bodies say. They are not tall — the low beams, the ducking under washing-lines and eave-bridges have seen to that in habit if not in bone — and they move with a shortened, placed step even outdoors on the boardwalk, as if any ground might be a step, a sluice, or a slab about to give. Their feet are broad from stairs and wet stone, the toes gripping. Children learn barefoot first; shoes are felt-soled things for winter, resoled often, and even adults pad about at home with their stool-feet and table-feet wrapped, out of courtesy to the neighbours below.

Close up, their hands tell more. The pit makes hands busy and keeps them nicked: knuckles cross-hatched with pale scars from flint and fresh chalk, nails kept brutally short, palms calloused from rope and bucket-chain and wooden rake. The older stoneworkers have a permanent whiteness worked into the creases of their skin, lime and clay under the nails that no washing quite removes. The women and men who tend the weep-cloths have softer, perpetually pruned fingers, and a way of wringing — twist, pause, twist again, never tearing the fibre — that small children can do in their sleep before they can properly talk.

They dress against drip, not rain. Nothing trails. Skirts and trouser-legs are tied or cut above the ankle; sleeves are close; cloaks are short and oiled rather than thick, because thick cloth drinks the pit and stays wet for days. Hair is braided hard against the head or cropped, not from fashion but because loose hair finds every wet lintel. Indoors, where ceiling beams sweat above a cooking fire and drop warm on one side and cold on the other, people wear little woven brow-bands, and shrug off a drip down the neck with the absent annoyance other people give a fly.

They speak low. It is not timidity. Sound does odd things between stone walls that share everything — a joke told in a kitchen is heard three houses over, a quarrel is public property by supper — so they have made quietness into manners and manners into survival. They knock on wood, not stone. They argue close, faces near, almost confidential even when furious. A market bargain looks like a secret being shared. Newcomers, talking at full field-volume, are hushed not by scolding but by flinches, the way you would flinch at someone stamping through a sleeping house.

And they listen constantly, often without realising they are doing it. They can tell, through a wall, whose bucket is on the rope by its squeak; whether a gutter has shifted by the change in the drip into the jar below; whether that tick on the north face is scree settling or a slab beginning to speak. Old people watch the little brown snails on the lowest lintels the way marsh farmers watch clouds. When the snails cluster thick and high, grandmothers tap the rock with a knuckle, taste the weep on a fingertip — clear or brown, sweet-chalk or iron — and send a grandchild, wordless, to move the stores up one shelf.

Because that is how a family is read here: vertically. Ask who someone is and you will be told not where they live but how far above their own history. "Two-doors-down," a man might say of himself, meaning his grandparents' sleeping room is now a sealed cistern under his floor, his parents' room a damp store beneath his kitchen, and he raises his children in the dry room he built himself. The proudest boast in the town is a dry third floor and a roof-yard that takes the afternoon sun. The poorest are not those without goods — goods can be carried in over the boardwalk — but those still trying to keep a fire lit on a ground floor, sleeping each winter a little higher on platforms as the damp climbs the wall.

Inheritance, accordingly, is not land. No one owns the lane in front of a house in any simple way, because the lane is also a drain, and the roof is also a neighbour's yard, and the wall weeps into somebody else's jar. What you inherit is height, catchment, and right-of-drip: this face of chalk for your cloth, that angle of roof for your canvas, this gutter to be cleared by your household on slate-days, that cellar never to be opened after heavy rain. Marriages are negotiated as much over eaves as over affection — a good match brings a sunnier drying-space, a stronger brother for jacking season, a grandmother who knows clay. A wedding feast is held, weather willing, across three joined roof-yards, with planks laid over the gaps and everyone sitting close, eating eel from the drain-traps and imported roots and bread baked flat so it will not take long and steam the beams.

Children are pit-wise before they are world-wise. They are carried high for their first years, passed sill to sill, and they learn the town in layers: first roofs and bridges, then stairs, only later the lowest lanes where the water runs and the cellar hatches breathe cold iron air. They are taught clear-weep from brown-weep the way other children learn berries. They know never to stand where a wall stains rusty, never to lean a ladder, never to run where the black-green film shines. Their games are quiet ones — snail-counting, drip-catching, laying a perfect line of flat stones — and their great adventure is the spring day they are first trusted alone on the dawn walk of the bottom drain, wooden rake in hand, old woman ahead and boy behind, listening for the choked sound that means: clear it now, or the lower lane floods before supper.

Work makes them urban in a way their rough tools do not suggest. No household can be sufficient here. Your roof sheds onto your neighbour's fire; his gutter feeds your jar; her wall is your wall, and if it leans after a wet winter, your house leans with it. So labour is pooled without ceremony. When the warning slate goes up at the hall, people take their turn. When a house needs jacking upright with timber wedges, six families appear with mallets and good humour, and the owner feeds them and owes six days in return. Disputes — and there are disputes, ferocious ones, over a moved gutter-stone or a goat tethered where it can foul a catchment — are settled in the hall in that same close, low voice, with grain bins above the speakers' heads and everybody able to hear a stool scrape through the floor.

They eat imported grain and roots, mostly, and are unembarrassed about it. What the pit gives is too valuable to waste on feeding itself: blue clay, cut stone with edges still sharp enough to shine, lime from the kiln in the warm wall, and that hard clear water travellers swear keeps sweet in a skin. In return come fibre, hide, timber, food. In summer there are eels, trapped where the drain narrows, and the one famous crop of the town — beans from a broken pot on a roof, leggy and suspicious, grown by whichever old woman currently holds the honour, served with ceremony and eaten with genuine pride. Goats pass through their lives two or three at a time, tethered, milked, watched, and traded on before affection can complicate arithmetic. There are no dogs. Affection, they would say, is not the question; floor-space is.

Age bends them but does not retire them. The old sit where they can read water: at alcove mouths, by cistern hatches, on roof-yards in the brief sun, hands still wringing, sorting, chinking moss into a wall. The very old move to the middle floors — low enough that their knees can manage, high enough that their bedding stays dry — and become living ledgers of which face fell in which year, whose doorway drowned when, what the snails did before the last slip. When one of them dies, the body is washed in first weep-water from the shrine alcove, wrapped dry, and carried up and out over the boardwalk before the day warms, because the pit has no earth to spare for the dead. They belong, in the end, to the flat land. The town keeps only the door below.

Newcomers either learn or leave. Those who stay acquire, within a season, the placed step, the lowered voice, the habit of glancing up at a wall before standing under it. They learn that nothing here is finished — not a roof, not a lane, not a house, not a grudge — and that this is not despair but a kind of cheerfulness. A slab falls where no one was standing (they are careful, mostly, about where people stand) and by next week it is lintels. A floor is lost to the damp and the family builds a hand's breadth higher and hangs a cloth on the old doorway for the jars. They are people who have made a home in a wound in the earth where only water should have lived, and they hold it not by conquering the seep and the settling stone, but by attending to them, together, every morning, before the jars brim over.

At night they shutter tight and show the marsh almost nothing: one lit window high in the pit wall, an eye half-open. Then even that goes out. What remains is the sound their whole lives are tuned to — water ticking into vessels in the dark, near, far, never quite in rhythm — and the soft movements of neighbours through shared walls, settling, like the scree, for the night.
