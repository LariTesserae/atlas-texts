Everything lives here, but everything lives *under* her.

Start anywhere else and you will think the chamber is a parliament of small, busy innocents. The white crickets feeling their way through the root-tangles with antennae longer than their bodies. The finger-long millipedes, glossy black, towing themselves through leaf-litter on a hundred patient feet. The emerald frogs and mud frogs and the glass frogs with their hearts showing, who court each other in semaphore — a throat pulsed without sound, a foreleg lifted and waved, slowly, like someone signaling across a crowded room. The ceiling lizards, flat as coins, upside-down their whole lives. The eyeless fish in the black pool, circling a circuit they have memorized by touch and temperature and have never once broken.

It takes a while to look up.

She is in the highest roots in the northeast corner, where the tangle is thickest, and at first she is not distinguishable from them. Pale. Thicker than they are, but the same blind pink-white, the same tendency to branch the eye astray into shadow. Then the roots resolve themselves into root, and the rest of her resolves into coil upon coil, looped and knotted and hammocked through them, making a second, lower ceiling of flesh.

No one knows if she is one animal.

That is what continuity means here, and why *she* is a guess. A snake this color, without pigment, without eyes to speak of — only two pearled-over pits under translucent scales, sealed as if by cataract or by stone — could live a very long time in a place that never changes, eating rarely, growing almost imperceptibly whenever there is surplus. Or she could be a line: a mother who laid soft eggs in the warm fern-bed where the hut was, daughters who hatched already blind and ate their brothers and took, one by one as she weakened, her place in the same coils, wearing the hollow of her body in the roots until the roots re-shaped themselves. The shed skins that hang sometimes from the tangle, whole and ghostly and five yards long before they tear, do not settle the question. They are eaten too quickly by moths and fungi. Even they must not make a sound falling.

Her middle is as thick as a person's thigh. Toward the head she narrows with a terrible, muscular elegance; toward the tail she tapers for so long that the end of her is lost, finer than a root-tip, somewhere up in the rock. Age has not made her ragged. It has lacquered her. The scales along her back have thickened and gone opalescent, pearl over pink, and where the limestone water has dripped on her for decades while she slept, a faint mineral crust has formed between them, so that in the noon shaft she does not shine like a living thing so much as glisten like the wall learning a new shape. She is becoming, very slowly, part of the cave's architecture.

She does not hiss. The fork of her tongue, when it comes, comes silently — a double thread, paler than the rest of her, tasting steam, tasting the iron in the pool-water rising, tasting the green fermentation of fallen fruit and the sudden absence where a frog has stopped moving. She hears the way the whole chamber hears: through the body. A vibration in a root is a sentence. A drip landing wrong is a sentence. The scuff of an uncareful foot on basalt would be a shout she could follow through stone, through water, through the soles of her own coils.

And so the civilization of quiet beneath her, which looks at first like mere meekness, is in fact as elaborate as any city. It is an entire evolution bent around one fact: *do not announce yourself to the ceiling.* The crickets have wings that no longer sing; they rub them to release a scent, a brief chemical whisper. The moths, a thousand at a time pressed flat to the walls in their stone-gray powder, will not fly by day at all, and by night rise only an inch and settle again. The frogs in the carved bowls tilt their heads back to catch condensation in their open mouths rather than risk the splash of jumping for a pool. Even mating, even hunger, even alarm have been translated into a visual, tactile language — a ripple passed fish to fish, a sudden stillness passed lizard to lizard outward from whatever felt her shift her weight above.

She hunts perhaps once in many days. There is no strike, in the way things strike above ground. Striking implies a beginning, a line drawn between rest and violence. She simply unspools. The great head descends on its own column, unhurried, mouth already slightly open, the loops above paying out length the way a root grows, until the air of the lower chamber is divided by her. Everything freezes — not in panic, but in a discipline so complete it looks like the place itself has paused. A bead gathers on a leaf tip and does not fall. The ant column halts, each ant with its green burden raised. Then the mouth closes on whatever moved half a second too long: a swift that came down the throat at dusk and circled once too often, a ceiling lizard whose tongue flicked at the wrong moth, a frog mid-dance. Swallowing is the loudest thing she does, and it is not loud. It is a slow, traveling contraction, visible under the pearl scales, and then the long re-ascent, coil reclaiming coil, until the corner is ceiling again.

Afterward she is still for a very long time. That is when, if you know where to look, you can see that she is not entirely a creature of the air and the roots. On the warmest nights, or what pass for nights, she lowers a length of herself — not all, never all — down to the black basalt slabs, and lies in the steam to digest by borrowed heat. The vines part for her without being crushed; she is that precise. The lowest pool steams around the one coil she trails in it. Roots overhead, freed of her weight, sway minutely and begin at once to lean, not toward her this time, but toward the space of heat she has left in them, already forgetting.

The people who cut the steps and set the bowls at sitting height must have known her, or known *a* her. Perhaps they came down to tend the water and were tolerated because they learned to move like frogs; perhaps the bowls were originally offerings, refreshed on purpose, a tithe of eggs and hatchlings paid upward for the right to drink from the cool pool unmolested. She still, very occasionally, pauses in a descent that has nothing beneath it — head cocked at an angle no living prey occupies, listening intently to the empty eastern wall where the stair ends in air. A footfall-pattern her body remembers and the present no longer provides. Then, after a minute, she withdraws, and the chamber exhales in ripples.

She is not cruel. Cruelty requires attention. She is closer to weather, or to the warm floor itself: a condition that has organized everything around it. She does not watch the frogs. She does not hate the swifts. She has never seen the sun whose brief, green-filtered forgiveness makes her scales briefly, briefly iridescent, and she would not know her own color if she could. The world to her is temperature and tremor: the hot pool and the cool, the root that thrums with sap and the root that thrums with an insect inside it, the long, dense stillness that means food is being careful, and the single false note that means it has stopped being careful enough.

Sit long enough below her, still enough that condensation begins to bead on your shoulders as it beads on the carved stone, and the distinction thins. A moth settles on your knee and folds itself into your skin. A root above, following the newest warmth in its blind appetite, inclines a fraction your way. And a fraction — this is the whole danger, and the whole welcome — hers inclines with it.
