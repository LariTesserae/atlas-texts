Everyone here is loud by profession, even if their profession is something else.

That is the first thing you understand about the people of the Acre, once your ears stop ringing enough to think. Not loud the way surface people mean it — rowdy, drunk, showing off. Loud the way a lighthouse is bright. Continuously, deliberately, as a condition of remaining where they are. A quiet person is not shy down here. A quiet person is a hazard.

So they have learned to live inside sound the way other towns live inside weather, and it has changed their bodies and their manners all the way down.

They are broad through the chest and throat from a lifetime of projecting, even the children, even the small wiry women who run the sorting tables and can be heard clear across Leafmarket without seeming to raise their voices at all. They speak from the diaphragm in short, carrying bursts, conserving sentences the way desert people conserve water. Most conversation at working distance is not really conversation. It's touch first — a hand flat between the shoulder blades, a knuckle rapped twice on a collarbone to say *I'm here, look at me* — then mouth close to ear, then hands again. They have a whole fluent language of taps and squeezes and pointed chins and exaggerated mouths for the hulling floors and the centrifuge house, where even shouting is just decoration. Lovers learn it early. So do foremen. Insults are especially elaborate.

They hear differently. Not better — many of the older Boiler Row men are stone deaf in the conversational range by forty, cupping a scarred ear out of pure habit — but through their feet, through their teeth. A child raised in the Tenements can stand barefoot on a root-ridge and tell you, eyes closed, whether the pumps are running sweet, whether a millipede train is coming up the groove behind her, whether the Acre has pushed a new feeder root under the iron plate on the next street. They sleep curled against warm pipework because the vibration comes through the wall into the mattress and tells their sleeping bodies, all night, *still safe, still safe*. Silence wakes them instantly. Silence is their fire alarm.

A baby's first outing is not to daylight. There is, functionally, no daylight. Its first outing is down to the centrifuge house, held up damp and furious in the blue sputter of the arc lamps while the crew cheers, so that its crying gets folded into the general blessing of noise that holds the ceiling up. A household that produces a lot of crying babies, arguing teenagers, clattering cookware, a grandmother who sings tunelessly while she mends, is a valued household. They get the tax credit painted as a brass leaf beside their door. A household that goes still — illness, grief, everyone away on shift at once — gets visited. Neighbors bring bells. They bring a borrowed frog in a bucket. They hang a boiler lid outside the window and take turns tapping it while they catch up on gossip, not out of unkindness, out of civic duty.

And when they can no longer contribute — when age thins the voice, when the cough from flower-oil distillation takes the shout out of a man — they do not move away from the center, as old people do in surface towns. They move *toward* it. Down to Boiler Row, where the houses are smallest and hottest and the walls themselves vibrate hard enough to rattle false teeth in a cup. Where the pumps do the shouting for them. It is considered a good death down there, to go to sleep to a noise so total it is almost like being held.

Their clothes never dry and neither do they. Everyone smells of hot iron, sweet black sap, leaf-rot, frog water, and their own clean sweat steamed out of them by the boilers. Hair is worn cropped or braided tight with copper wire against the damp. Skin goes soft-palmed and faintly green at the creases from the spore-fall; old women have beards of actual moss along the jaw if they sit still too long outdoors, and they comb them, vain as anyone. Lungs adapt or they leave on the cage while they still can. Those who stay develop a tolerance for the wet air, a kind of gilled patience. They walk root-streets in bare feet or thin-soled boots to keep the information coming up through the floor. They step over ridges without looking. They have all, without exception, learned to step aside on a catwalk for something that is not human and to do it without thinking.

Because citizenship here is not a species.

The frogs are citizens. The coin-sized ones that live inside the condensation pipes and turn every faucet and speaking tube into an amplifier for their ecstasy, and the dog-sized ones that sit in the warm outflow ditches by the cisterns bellowing up through the grates at all hours — they are not tolerated, they are *cultivated*. Frog-keepers go on rounds with oilcans and sugar water, clearing drain mouths, checking ditch temperature. A street that loses its frogs gets nervous. A street that gains a particularly tireless bellower throws a small, very loud party.

The cicadas are citizens, squatting in the steam organ when it vents, adding that perfect fifth nobody ordered to a chord that never resolves, fed sugar water from thimble cups by the organists who tune the whole town like an instrument. The organists, incidentally, are the closest thing the place has to priests. They do not preach. They listen to pressure. They keep one hand on a bored trunk pipe and know by the shudder when to feed the boilers, when to let the pitch sag and frighten the new leaves back from the Tenement windows. Their apprentices are chosen for perfect pitch and bad nerves — people who cannot stand a wrong note, who will wake in the night because somewhere a root has muffled a bell.

The pollinator bats pour out of the exhaust stacks at shift change, thousands of them, and the shift changes partly *because* of them. No one with any sense works the canopy decks when the air is that full of wings. The isopods, forearm-long, armor beaded with moisture, patrol the streets eating rust and spilled oil and fallen leaf, and children ride the biggest of them a few solemn feet before being lifted off by an embarrassed parent. The millipede trains haul pod-carts along grooves polished into the roots, legs ticking like typewriters, driven less by reins than by a man walking ahead tapping a rhythm the whole animal has agreed to follow. In the cistern pits the blind eels circle and surface, and everyone — worker with a bucket, child sent for water, frog falling in by accident — understands that the eels have right of way in the dark.

And up on the high catwalks, the lean mantis-thing the size of a cat goes clicking on its jointed legs after rats, and grown men with rivet guns step aside for it with genuine respect and say "Colleague" or touch two fingers to their forehead. It is not tame. It has never been fed by hand. It has simply found, like everything else with sense, that the loud center is where the hunting is safe, and in return its hunting keeps the granaries possible. There are three of them, maybe four. No one has ever seen one young. No one wants to investigate that too closely.

In the plaza in the middle, at certain hours, the gnats do their brief architecture — an arch, a hand, a face held together for three seconds by boiler thermals — and people passing through will pause, not because it's miraculous, they've seen it ten thousand times, but because it's a sign the air is moving right. Lizards watch this from warm pipes with the total attention of stockbrokers. Birds nest inside the centrifuge housing, raising chicks in a place that shakes their shells constantly, and those chicks emerge unafraid of anything except quiet.

All of them together — human, frog, bat, eel, isopod, bird — make a kind of dense, opportunistic, deafening reef in the sparse air between the pillars. The Acre is not crowded with trees. It's crowded with refugees from the Acre, huddled in the noise they make together.

Which is the last and largest inhabitant, and the one you have to reckon with if you want to say who lives here.

The Acre lives here. The town is inside it the way a song is inside a throat it is trying not to swallow. Its trunks are the pillars they bolt their houses to; they thicken a finger's width a year and the doorframes have to be re-cut, the catwalks re-hung. Its roots are the floor, ticking faintly as they grow in the lull after the Clamor, probing for seams in boilers with the thoughtful curiosity of a tongue finding a loose tooth. Its leaves — working leaves, shade leaves, leaves grown in the perfect serrated shape of gears in the vibration-shadow of machines it cannot quite reach — close over the light shafts and turn the sun olive and underwater. It grows different flowers for frog shift and bat shift and alters the chemistry of the whole chamber hour by hour. It pushes signage carved deep into bark out in relief on the other side, as if slowly exhaling the town's own words back at it.

It is not evil. That would be simpler, and no one who lives here uses the word. It is patient, and hungry in the way growing things are hungry, which is to say without malice and without limit. It has already eaten the streets at the edges, where you can still see a lamp post leafed in a spiral and a house front with its windows grown shut like eyelids. It is pressing, right now, with its whole slow intelligence, against the border of the sound, listening for a gap. A muffled cart. An un-sung shift. A house gone still with grief.

So the people and the frogs and the cicadas and the organ and the boilers answer it, every hour, in chorus. Not winning. Never winning. Just loud enough, together, to continue.

Stand in that central plaza long enough — damp settling on your shoulders, spore drifting, the Clamor fading until you can hear sap moving in a trunk like blood in a wrist held to your ear — and you stop being able to tell where the inhabitants end and the place begins. The workers are part of the Acre's deterrent system. The animals are part of the town's workforce. The town is part of the plant's anatomy now, a noisy, iron-plated irritation it cannot quite digest, that has learned to make itself indispensable by making itself indigestible.

They all live here, below everything, in one room. And the room, patiently, lives around them.
