First you think it is empty.

At noon, crossing one of the wide gravel yards — if you cross it at all, and most people don't — you can go a long way without meeting anyone. The loading bays are open and vacant. The bus goes around the ring road every twenty minutes whether it is carrying anybody or not. The dormitory blocks have their doors shut, the clinic has its door shut, the office flag hangs down wet against its pole. Street after numbered street, hydrant after hydrant, and no one in the street.

Then your ears adjust, or stop trying to adjust, and you realise the place is crowded. It is just not crowded with people in the open. The people are under the roofs, in the covered ways, in the amber dimness of the pump hall, behind louvered vents and steam. And everywhere the roofs and vents and drains are not empty of other lives at all.

The human town is a shift-town. There are beds for more than ever sleep in them at once, because there is no night to sleep all together in — the pumps are manned, the power house is manned, the watch on the levee and the canal levels is manned, in rotation, through the orange-lit dark that passes for night. So perhaps a third of them are awake at any hour, a third working, a third trying to sleep behind damp blackout curtains with the diesel throb coming up through the floor and into the frame of the bed and into their teeth. They learn to sleep in it very quickly. New arrivals lie awake feeling it as a noise. Old hands lie awake only when it stops — when a pump is taken offline for servicing and the sudden exposed ringing fills the room and their ears ache in the comparative quiet, and they wait, without knowing they are waiting, for the note to come back and settle them.

They are mostly maintenance people, which is to say the town itself is their work. Pump engineers and fitters in ear defenders worn smooth at the edges, the muffs leaving a permanent pale ring in the sweat and grime around their ears. Turbine operators from the power house. Electricians who run the cable ducts — and know where the rats run them too, and where not to reach blind. Welders and painters and gravel-rakers and re-graders, crews who repaint a bollard before the rust at its bolt heads can spread beyond an orange blush, who re-cut a canal bank to its template while the excavator marks from the last cutting are still visible. Brush crews in boots and gloves who shave the levee and the ditch edges again and again, and spray the hard weeds that find the seams in the concrete before they can seed.

There are cooks for the mess hall, and cleaners who hose the boot racks and mop silt prints that reappear within the hour, and laundry workers in the drying room where shirts are hung indoors because outdoors they would never dry, only sour in the wet heat. There is a clinic nurse, sometimes two, whose work is a steady rotation of the same things: heat exhaustion in new men who waited for an evening cool that does not come, fungal skin in boots that are never quite dry, infected mosquito bites and cat scratches, ears. Always ears. There is a bus driver. There are clerks in the office who post the same warnings on the notice board — hearing protection, heat, do not swim in the canals — and take the same readings off gauges that tremble at the same numbers, and paint the white waterline in the main canal afresh when it dulls, though the black water has not moved off it.

They are not, for the most part, families. The dormitories are dormitories. Food comes in on the morning truck and leaves as waste on the evening one, and so, in a longer rhythm, do the people: in on contracts, out on rotation, a little deafer, a little more used to raising their voices in the street without noticing. Indoors they shout slightly less. In the pump hall they do not shout at all for long; they give up and use hand signals, close and clear, thumbs and flat palms and pointed fingers learned like a second, quieter language that follows them out into the mess hall, where a man will signal *turn it over* or *shut it down* across the tables before he remembers to speak.

Their clothes are always damp. Not wet through, except for the brush crews and the men who work the outfalls — damp, steamed, sticking before they have crossed a yard, drying on their backs only in the sense that the sweat is newer. They move through the covered ways at noon in single file and do not linger in the blinding gravel. They keep doors shut against insects and steam as a reflex, even interior doors, even when their hands are full. They know which walkways steam will blind at which hour when a vent cycles. They know not to lean on cold pipes in the pump houses, which run with condensation and will soak a sleeve instantly. They stop, after the first week, asking when the dry season starts.

And some few stay past a first contract, and then past a second, and become fixtures in a place where nothing else has had time to weather. The foreman who can tell which of the three pumps is hunting slightly off-note through the soles of his boots. The woman in the water treatment sheds who planted the reeds in their grids and defends a certain amount of algae as necessary. The cook who feeds the cats. Because someone always feeds the cats.

The cats may be the most settled citizens. Sleek, numerous, entirely unembarrassed, they sleep the hottest hours in a row on the cool concrete under the water tanks, tails curled over their noses, and are gone at shift change to hunt the cable ducts. The rats are there before the town quite finishes building itself — rats find a duct the way water finds a low corner — and the cats follow a night later, as if issued. Nobody brought them officially. They are as much a part of the drainage system as the grates: they keep the ducts passable, they raise litters in the dry space behind the fuel depot bund, they are dosed and scratched and named, repeatedly, the same names given to new kittens as old toms disappear.

Above them, in the literal rafters, are the swallows. They colonized the pump hall while the paint was still fresh on the green plinths, darting in and out through the vents, building under the roof trusses where the rising heat keeps the mud of their nests from ever quite setting damp. They raise chicks over the flywheels to a vibration that would kill speech, and the engineers, who would regrade a bank by Friday for slumping on Monday, will take a pump's guard off more slowly in nesting season and chalk a mark by a nest to warn the next shift. At the end of the day — by roster, not by light — the swallows pour out with the bats. The bats live under every eave in their hundreds, folded into the corrugation, and when the mosquitoes rise in a visible smoke off the levee grass at evening they come down through the orange glow of the streetlights in a flickering that makes the foremen shut the mess hall doors. Geckos are already waiting around each light fitting for the moths the lights draw in, chirping faintly, unmoving for an hour and then uncatchably fast.

Down at ground level the town belongs, sonically at least, to frogs. They are in every drain and slow ditch the town cannot help making, warm permanent water filmed emerald and gold where the brush cutters cannot reach, full of insect larvae, bath-warm. After rain — warm rain, falling straight — the chorus comes up as a grinding, pulsing wall that competes squarely with the diesels, and even at midday there is an undertone under the turbine whine, under the cicadas in the staked, dusty shade trees by the dormitories. The men working the canals step through it without hearing it consciously until a pump goes offline, when the frogs, too, seem to get louder to fill the gap. Dragonflies patrol above them in squadrons, scarlet and electric blue, snapping midges out of the steam haze. Herons stand at the outfalls where the discharge stuns small fish into circling, motionless for shifts at a time, so habituated that a truck reversing past with its alarm shrilling will not lift a head. One sleeps at night on a bollard on the ring road, one leg tucked, in the full light. Egrets nest all together in the single dead tree left by the treatment ponds — left, now, because they nest there — and whiten its branches.

And behind the dormitories, despite every regulation on the notice board, someone keeps roosters. They are heard at dawn and then, confused by the cloud and the lights and the perpetual day, intermittently all day long. Everyone knows whose birds they are and no one, on the record, knows anything about them. They are the town's open secret and its unofficial proof that the inhabitants are not only maintaining an equilibrium of water. A man who has signed on to keep a polder from drowning will also keep a chicken in a crate behind a dormitory block to hear something call at an hour that isn't a shift change. The cooks save scraps. The cats are kept, by some negotiated truce, away.

So the census, if you took one, would be misleading if you counted only the people on the roster. Count instead everyone who feeds here regularly, who breeds here, who has learned the pump-note and the steam cycle and the exact level of the water on its painted line. The engineers rotating through, growing fluent in hand signals and losing a little high-frequency hearing every year. The nurse, the bus driver making his empty rounds. The frogs in the drains that the town digs and cannot drain dry. The swallows over the flywheels, the herons at the waterfall the town makes and must keep making out of sight beyond the levee, the cats under the tanks and the rats under the floors and the bats under the eaves and the geckos by the lights they are drawn to.

They all live by the same condition, which is that nothing here is allowed to finish: not a season, not an evening, not a silence, not a process of erosion or growth. The water stays on its line. The heat stays at thirty-eight, thirty-nine. The cloud the cooling towers make stays over the basin and glows at night. And the inhabitants, human and otherwise, arrange themselves inside that held moment — shouting a little, signaling with their hands, sleeping in noise, hunting at shift change, singing from black water — neither winning against the marsh beyond the clean, fresh levee, nor being taken back by it, as long as they keep on living there, at full volume, all the time.
