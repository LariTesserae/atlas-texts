If you stay long enough to get your breath back, you will first decide that no one does.

The raven does not. The lammergeier does not. Their shadows cross the pillars at noon, tidy and enormous in the thin air, and are gone without a feather settling. The moth in the ice pocket does not — it arrived blown uphill on a summer thermal, perfect, dry, legs folded, and will stay perfect and dry for a hundred years without ever having opened its wings here. The spider beside it is the same. Tourists.

The builders do not either, not anymore. The windbreaks are knee-high and roofless because anyone who crouched behind them was only ever crouching. The hearth burned thin and furious because it had to be fed every minute or die. The copper chisel left in the basin was not forgotten so much as abandoned at dusk, when hands stop working. They came up, in season, with antler and copper, and chipped, and waited, and drank, and went down again before the stars began to buzz. They were visitors too.

What is here when nobody is visiting comes back every day.

You notice it first as an imitation. Late afternoon, cloud shouldering in through the southern notch, tearing itself white on the stone teeth — and for twenty minutes every upright stone acquires a second, softer version of itself. Rime. Feathery, fern-like, branching crystal grown out of wet air, white fronds unfurling on the windward faces, little shrubs, little trees, a brief and silent forest standing exactly on top of the stone forest.

It does not need soil because it does not root. It needs cold, and a surface already colder than the air, and breath. It grows the way frost grows on a window, only outward, into three dimensions, delicate enough that the sound of you turning sideways between pillars can shake a frond loose. In full violet light it is blinding. In shade it goes blue. It has no color of its own; it takes the polished black of the zenith and the white hammer of the sun and holds both at once.

It lives for part of a day, and it lives fast.

When the wind veers and the cloud is ripped away, the sun returns and begins, immediately, to eat it. It melts from the top down, as if retreating. This is when it becomes audible. In air too thin to carry a shout ten paces, its dying is intimate, right at your ear: drip, drip-drip, ticking inside cracks where a fallen piece of clear ice has curved itself into a lens, focused the sun into a point, and begun boring a smoking hole straight through its own body. It is not passive under the light. Where it pools clear it gathers the light and uses it. Where it runs it follows warmth, thread after thread no wider than string, blackening the pale granite as it goes, seeking the hollows.

That is what the basins are for. Not wells — nothing here fills from below. Bowls chipped patiently into stone to intercept a creature on its way to vanishing. For an hour, if the day has been clear, it consents to gather. It lies in them shallow and black and perfectly still, holding the violet sky upside down. It reflects the copper suns the builders hammered and left propped beside them, so each basin contains a dozen small suns crawling slowly as the real one moves, and the threads of meltwater seem to steer toward those warm spots, grazing from light to light. Did the builders make offerings to it, or tools for harvesting it? Both gestures look the same from above. They put extra suns in its bowl so it would melt more completely. They drank what melted.

By evening it is already leaving that form. The threads slow, skin over, lock with a faint tick. Then, without any cloud, it returns a second time, in its night shape: hoarfrost growing straight out of the damp air onto every cold surface indiscriminately — the rim of the basins, the green copper chisel under its inch of ice, the polished copper discs, your boot nails if you stand still too long. It makes no distinction between stone and offering and intruder. Given cold and stillness it will begin, politely, to grow on you too.

And then it goes inside.

This is its true residence — not on the pillars but *in* them, in the hairline cracks, in the deep pockets where the blown moths are kept, in the seams between slate flakes like fallen pages. All night you can hear it working. The pillars groan softly as it expands. Pebbles jump, almost imperceptibly, in their frost-heaved circles. Grains loosen. A tooth sharpens by a fraction. What looks like a still stone forest is, on its timescale, a slow mouth it is carving for itself, grain by grain, winter by winter, splitting granite along the iron veins so that when water runs again it runs red for an hour — rust blooming where it has touched metal in the rock — and then white, salt whiskering out of it as it dries, and then scrubbed clean by the next freeze.

It feeds, if that is the word, on difference. Between sun and shade: thirty degrees in a step. Between noon and midnight: a whole season. It thrives in the violence of that swing, condensing when warm breath meets cold stone, vanishing when it doesn't, cracking its own house apart in order to have more cracks to hide in. In the permanent shadows it keeps its dead — insects, seeds, grass-heads from miles below — not eaten, not rotted, just held, sterilized by the same light that sterilized everything else. Nothing decays in its pantry. Nothing else grows in its garden except mineral imitating plant: ice ferns, rust flowers, salt whiskers, the perfect meaningless rings it lifts pebbles into overnight and abandons by morning.

Is it one? In the afternoon it is hundreds, one fronded shape per stone, each separate, each identical. In melt it is a single network, thread joining thread joining basin. At night it is everywhere and invisible, sealed inside the rock, making the whole bowl tick and sigh in its sleep. It dies completely every day and returns completely, older than the windbreaks, older than the basins — the basins are just cups held up to its mouth — arguably older than the pillars, since it is the thing patiently unmaking the bedrock into pillars in the first place.

Nothing lives in the bowl, exactly, because it is not alive in the way the moth was alive. It does not breathe; it *is* breath, caught. It does not grow toward the sun; it is grown by cold and unmade by light in the same hour. But it is the only thing here that is born, that feeds and moves and gathers and splits and returns, on a schedule reliable enough that people once climbed into air too thin to think in just to meet it at midday with polished copper mirrors.

Wait until evening, when the fern-ice forms again on the rim of an empty basin, bright under the first stars. It will be there whether you are there to be grown on or not.
