You will hear it long before you see it, which is wrong, because everything here is heard long before it is seen — and you will hear it wrong.

Not a splash. Not a strike. Just, once in perhaps twenty minutes, a single clear *toc* somewhere out in the fins, cleaner than a drip and lower than a crest-note, that goes traveling rank to rank the way all sounds travel here, getting larger as it goes. Then nothing. Then, so long after you have stopped waiting for it that you have forgotten you were waiting, another *toc*, perhaps a quarter-mile away by pitch, perhaps only thirty yards away in another aisle of glass and screened by the mist.

It has been making that sound beside you, on and off, for half a day.

It is the only large animal the basin supports, and it supports it barely, and on terms the basin dictates: be slow, be patient, spend almost nothing, and let the stone do the seeing for you.

It stands taller than you at the shoulder if you count its head — but it rarely holds its head high. It is built on the heron plan and then revised by the place. Long legs, long neck, long bill. Gray all over, not feather-gray so much as wet-stone gray, slick and close, the feathers reduced along its breast and thighs to something more like pebbled skin. In the shadowless light it has no outline of its own. Water beads on it as it beads on the fins, runs down it in the same rivulets, finds the same grooves. When the mist is down to your waist it is only a slightly denser vertical in the mist, and the fins behind it shine through the wet air on either side.

Its head is bare. That is the first thing you notice if you ever get close enough, and you almost never get close enough. Smooth, unfeathered, a little darker at the crown, beaded always with condensation. Where the eyes should be there are two covered pits, shallow, skin grown cleanly over, that twitch — there is no other word — when a note of a particular pitch passes through the ranks. It never turns toward a sound. It seems, instead, to let sounds turn inside it.

It hears with everything. This is not a metaphor the basin permits you to keep separate from fact. Its leg bones are hollow, not like a bird's hollow, light and strutted, but chambered in long uneven cells like the fins themselves. Set a palm low on a pool bank while it is standing in that pool — again, if it ever lets you — and you feel its lifted foot come down, *toc*, and then a faint answering tremor returning up through the stone, through the water, through its other leg, until its whole frame is very slightly, visibly, humming. It stands in that hum with its neck eased down and its head tipped, not listening with its ears so much as allowing itself to be played. Its breastbone will lean, sometimes, with infinite slowness, until it rests against a fin face. It stays there an hour. The fin is telling it the pool.

Because that is how it hunts. It does not look for the eyeless shrimp in the deep pools, the pale transparent ones with the white grit in their guts. It has no use for looking. It wades out — one step, then a stillness so complete that the ripples die and the dripping from its own belly becomes the loudest local sound and then dies too — and it sets a foot down deliberately, and reads the interruption in the returning ring. Somewhere under the milky turquoise or the iron film, shrimp fold at a vibration and in folding change the shape of the water against rock, and that change comes back to the bird altered. It knows. It knows not only where but how deep, and whether the cluster is worth the noise of a strike, because a strike is an event here. When it finally goes — neck unfolding in a single unhurried fall, bill spearing down — every sealed circle nearby takes up the splash and repeats it until the whole field is briefly percussion. In the sudden comparative quiet afterward, it swallows, once, and lifts a foot again.

It eats the scrape-snails too, when it finds their polished trails on a fin face — finds them, again, by the tiny dull place they make in a tone. It takes them not with a peck but by pressing the flat of its bill to the glass and waiting until the snail, fooled by a warmth or a stillness, loosens. Its claws are black and translucent at the edges, thickened, you would swear, by the same glass it walks on ground down in the gizzard with swallowed grit and the shells of its own prey. Everything here recycles stone into slightly different stone. The bird is only the largest and slowest version of the process.

It does not call. There is no need, and no room, for a voice in air that destroys nearby speech and carries a shout a quarter-mile. It calls with its feet and, in the breeding season, with its bill laid gently against a tall fin. One bird stands at the valley center where the ranks are three times human height and the booms are lowest, and taps, and waits. An answer comes back so flattened and bright with distance that it seems to arrive from inside the rock rather than across it. They find each other by pitch, correcting aisle by aisle, never hurrying, sometimes for days. By the time they meet, the mist has done all the courtship there is: two grays condensing side by side, neck against neck against ringing glass, sharing a vibration.

And it sleeps — if sleep is what you call a stillness even deeper than its waking stillness — in the dead places. Not the huts people make, exactly, though it will use one if a party has lately left, standing folded in the sand-packed lee with its head drawn down below the rim where the volume of the world drops by half. Its own places are natural: two fins that happen to face each other out of phase, a sealed circle whose lip has cracked just enough to bleed off tone. There it rests its chambered legs. There, in the one pocket of the basin where its own heartbeat comes back to it interior and private, it lays a single egg in wet grit and black sand, gray as itself, heavy as a stone of its size ought not to be.

It does not sit on the egg to warm it. The basin is cold all through, and the egg is cold-adapted, and warmth is not the point. It stands over it, one foot on either side, for weeks, and every few hours it taps the grit — *toc*, softer than hunting — and listens to the egg answer. At first there is only the dull thud of yolk. Then, one day, a tick from inside, unsynchronized, learning. After that it taps more often. The chick hatches with its eye-pits still open to a dim seam, and for its first season it can almost see: enough to follow the moving density of its parent through mist at arm's length, no more. It learns the pools by standing in its parent's fading ring and feeling where the pattern breaks. By the time the seams close over, it does not need them. The world has already moved from its face into its legs.

It lives a long time. Nothing here can afford to live fast, and it can afford it least of all. A bird marked by a white scratch on the bill — a scratch that, like all scratches in the glass, stays raw and bright for years — may be seen by three generations of travelers crossing between watersheds, always in the same broad territory of ringing pools, always apparently in the middle of the same step. It does not migrate. The basin is its whole watershed of sound, and beyond the rim, on soft ground, it would be as deaf as you are when you leave. It does not follow people. It attends to them. A party shouting across the maze sounds to it, presumably, like a clumsy new reef of animals announcing themselves clearly from very far away and incoherently from close up, and sometimes in the evening a traveler will look up from a quiet hut and find it standing just outside the cancellation, head below the fin crests, listening to their breathing with the whole of its body — the one sound in the valley it cannot make itself.

When it dies, which no one has reliably seen, it seems simply to stop being distinguishable. Old birds grow paler as silica lays itself down over them season by season, a milky film in the seams of the breast, a whitening at the joints. They stand longer between steps. A season comes when the *toc* from a familiar pool is weaker, and then fainter, and then one spring a traveler finds, in a sealed circle that rings particularly true, a standing shape grown over with precipitate, neck eased down, bill resting against the fin it used to lean on to hear the water — still conducting, long after there is anything left to listen.
