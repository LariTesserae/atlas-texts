You would mistake it, at first, for part of the sorting the wind does.

A low stone among stones, on the lee side of a slab where the scree has drawn itself out into one of those immaculate tails. Grey-brown. No taller than your ankle. Its shadow at noon is the same small black puddle as everything else's. Only when the shadow of a caracara goes over it — fast, clean, fifty meters up and still enormous — does it do the thing stones do not do. It tightens. Not down, exactly. *In*. As if it were listening to the rock it is lying on, and had decided to become more rock until the danger passed.

Below the cairn-line the herders call it the deaf one, though that is not quite right, and they know it. They tap two fingers against the ground when they mean it, then against the sternum. The ground, then the body. That is the whole definition.

It is built the way the bowl builds everything: pressed flat, sealed at every opening, and redesigned around a sound it gave up on hearing a long time ago.

From a distance it suggests a vizcacha, a rock hyrax, something rodent-thick and patient that ought to live in burrows. Up close — and you will only ever get up close by accident, by sitting still beside a water-pit until your own trembling settles into the general trembling — the resemblance frays. It is wider than it is long. Its spine is not arched but wedged, high and narrow toward the wind, sloping away low behind, so the blast coming off the gorge finds no purchase, no edge to lift or worry. It does not face into the wind or away from it. It sets its wedge *across* it, at the precise angle generations have worn into the scree behind their sleeping slabs, and lets it pass over like water over a stone in a river that has never once changed course.

Its coat is not fur so much as felt — short, dense, unpartable, the color of pumice and old bone and the grey leather of the rosette plants — except along the back, where it does something stranger. There the hair has stiffened into overlapping, fluted ridges, black-tipped, grooved front to back like the columnar rock of the gorge, like a tiny range of organ pipes laid flat. At night it sleeps out in the open on purpose, wedged in its accustomed place, and the rime takes it first. Frost feathers up white along those grooves while the lee side of it stays merely cold. Two hours after sunrise, when it still has not moved, the frost sweats. It beads. It runs, a few centimeters at a time, exactly where the grooves direct it: forward, down the slope of the shoulders, into two shallow, naked hollows behind the jaw. Little stone-colored cups of skin. It lowers its head a fraction — the first movement of the day — and drinks itself.

That is its water. Not the rock pits, though it will use those too, at the hour they exist. Its own harvested half-hour.

Everything about the head is closed. The ears are there, but you have to know to look: two small, thick flaps folded permanently down and sealed across with a pale, waxy growth it renews from glands at the jaw, choked inside with hair. Young are born with the flaps open. Within days they fold. Within a season they seal, and the animal passes the rest of a very long life — thirty years, fifty, the herders swear to animals their grandfathers marked by notching a sleeping slab — without ever hearing air again.

It does not need to. The world that matters arrives by other routes.

Its eyes sit high and far apart, under a ledge of bone, and are covered most of the day by a translucent inner lid the color of polished horn, so that it sees the bowl all its life through a slit of amber glare: shapes, movement, the sudden white semaphore of swifts banking a mile away, the shadow-language of raptors. Brightness is its weather report. Its nostrils are two downward slits on the underside of the muzzle, valved with muscle, mostly shut against dust; it breathes, like the guides do, through a mouth held barely open, teeth bared to the thin air, and at night frost will silver the teeth if it sleeps slack-jawed. Its teeth never stop growing. They have to. It makes its living scraping lichen off rock — sulfur-yellow crust, burnt orange, soot — grazing the damp tracks down stone faces in the same order, day after day, seasonless year after seasonless year, wearing the front teeth into chisels as fast as the stone wears them away. Between lichen it takes the edges of cushion plants, mouthfuls that represent a decade of growth, and whatever the wind has delivered and stunned against the rocks in the night: moths that walk rather than fly, beetles, spiders on blown silk, a swift's dropped feather it will chew for the fat at the quill.

Underneath, it is all listening surface.

Its feet are absurdly broad for its size, splayed, bare-soled, with pads like worn leather divided into lobes, each lobe — this is what the paw prints in the brief morning damp show, before the wind sorts them away — separately mobile, pressing and releasing against the stone in a slow, constant tasting. Put your own palm flat the way the old deaf guides do and you feel only the one great undifferentiated roar. It feels grammar. Through the soles, through the wrists laid flat when it rests, through its chin when it hunts for news with its jawbone to the ground, through the sternum when it stands over a hollow slab, it reads the bowl in tremor: the thump-code of its own kind from a burrow system twenty meters away, a pattern of warnings and reassurances as specific as names; the triple scrabble of a fox's paws on scree, sorted from the wind-jitter of loose pebbles because the fox repeats and the pebbles only repeat *almost*; the deep, subsonic settling of the gorge flutes, which never change, and under them, perceptible only because everything else is so constant, the faint irregularity of something new — a human footstep, still an hour off, arriving through granite before it arrives through air.

That is how they talk. Not flashing, like the swifts — they are too slow, too earthbound for the sky's language. They thump. One forefoot, then both, then a pause in which the whole colony goes still enough to receive the answer through the ground. A pup, eyes not yet sealed, rides pressed under its mother's chest and learns the language as vibration against its ribs, holding, literally holding, to her foreleg, feeling her pulse and her messages in the same channel until the two are hard to distinguish. Perhaps for them they never are distinguished. Perhaps a heartbeat, in that place, *is* a kind of sentence: I am here, I am here, on this stone, at this frequency.

They live in colonies because the burrows require it — systems dug and worried out under slab piles in the few places where fallen rock has made a roof the wind cannot lift and a floor where the vibration is muffled just enough that infants, born naked and deafeningly new, are not shaken apart by it. A dozen animals, sometimes twenty, sharing a labyrinth that may be older than the colony's memory of itself, its entrances all on the lee side, all too low to stand in, all smelling of wax and lichen and warm felt. Above ground they scatter to graze their mapped lichen fields alone. Aloneness is not loneliness up there. The stone connects them. A thump sent at noon, foot to rock beside a trembling cup of meltwater, carries farther and truer than any shout ever carried below the cairn-line.

It is slow in the ways the altitude demands and sudden only once, in the way of prey. Its blood is dark and eager, its heart — the guides who have found dead ones, frozen perfect on their backs after a fall, say this with a kind of respect — its heart is the size you would expect in an animal twice its weight, and it never quite rests, even in sleep, because at six thousand meters rest is a negotiation the body cannot win. It dozes in the sun with its black-fluted back burning hot enough to hurt your hand and its belly, pressed to stone in shadow, near freezing, and tolerates the difference the way the whole plateau tolerates it: not by choosing one, but by being shaped so both happen at once, permanently, without resolution. In direct light the horned lids come down and it grazes blind, by tooth-memory and foot-tremor. In shadow it stops, goes stupid with cold in minutes like your own fingers would, and must shuffle — an undignified, rocking shuffle, wedge swaying — back into the hammering light.

Nothing up there calls, and it is no exception. Startled, it does not cry out. It strikes the rock, once, hard enough that you feel the alarm in your boot soles ten meters away, and is gone down a crack that looked too narrow the moment before. Mating, which happens without season whenever a female's slow cycle comes round, is a negotiation of drumming conducted over days, patterns answered and altered through a shared slab, until the two meet at a burrow mouth at dusk and touch wrists, chins, sterna, sealing the agreement in the only medium that has never lied to them. The young are born in the dark, in the muffled deep of the system, and their first world is not light or sound but a continuous, rocking tremor — mother, colony, wind in rock, the whole bowl saying itself without pause — which they will spend their lives learning to parse the way you learned, as a child below, to parse speech out of air.

The foxes hunt them by the same channel, chin low, and mostly fail; the owl takes the careless at dusk, by sight, with its huge useless-faced stare. Most die the way the cushion plants die, of accumulation: teeth finally failing against the lichen, a sealed ear breached by a crack in the wax and the roar getting in at last to do in a day what a lifetime of sealing prevented, a winterless night a fraction colder that finds the shuffle back to sunlight a fraction too slow. Then the wind takes over, sorts the felt and fluted bone out into the long tail behind its last stone, reforms the ripples over the place where it slept, and the colony's map closes over the gap without comment.

Stand beside one at noon — if you have been still long enough, plugged and goggled, breathing through your mouth, it may decide your tremor is only a new kind of stone and come out to graze past your boot to the water cup at its edge. You cannot hear your breathing. It cannot hear anything at all. The water between you trembles without pause, throwing its tiny perfect ripple against the rim, and every few seconds the animal lifts a forefoot and sets it down, not hard, in a rhythm that is not the wind's rhythm and not its heart's, quite — something in between, a third thing, its own — and the ripple answers, and answers again, and far across the polished bowl another stone that is not a stone answers back through fifty meters of granite.

It is not enduring the place. That would imply the place is an event. It is the place, made briefly mobile: fluted for frost, sealed against sound, eyed through horn against the sun, reading the world through the soles of its feet, drinking the melt off its own back at the same hour, in the same grooves, forever.
