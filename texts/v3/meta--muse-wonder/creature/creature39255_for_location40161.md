The first inhabitant you actually meet on the High Anvil is not the animal. It is the animal's attendance.

At ankle height, the air is full of iron-filing flies — not flies, not exactly insects, more like living grit with a nervous system, each one a bright, bent sliver that navigates by magnetic north until a herd passes and north becomes local. Then they navigate by *that*. They hang in glittering veils around nothing you can see, tracing the outline of vast legs that have not arrived in the visible world yet, or have already left it. When the field shifts, they lose their grammar all at once and fall. A metallic rain ticks off the glass, brief and complete, and in the silence immediately after you can hear the real foot come down somewhere close enough to make your molars ache.

That is how you learn to count the population here. Not by sight. By interruption.

### The Smelters

They are the large grazers, and the reason for everything else.

Thirty meters at the shoulder is a middling adult. The old bulls are taller, and so slow that watching one complete a stride feels like watching weather commit to a decision: the columnar leg unlatches at the piston-joint with a long sigh of pressure, rises through heat-shimmer, describes a patient arc, and engages the ground a full minute later with a clang that travels through your boots before it reaches your ears. Six legs, three to a side, never hurried. If a Smelter ever ran, nobody has lived in the right century to report it.

The hide is plate and ceramic grown in overlapping scales, iron-dark where it is new, rusted almost to leather at the edges of old growth. Each plate carries fine concentric stress-lines, like tree rings, like the record of droughts in a place that has never had rain. Under the plates is not flesh in any familiar sense. There is cable-tendon, copper-bright and exposed in the long runs along the flanks, which flexes with a sluggish, oily gleam. There are ribbed muscle-pistons sheathed in conductor skin that the animal molts, season by slow season, in curled sheets the size of shed roofing. There are vents along the ribs, slatted, that pulse furnace-orange at night.

And there is heat — not body heat, not the warmth of blood. Process heat. A Smelter is hotter inside than the plateau at noon, chamber after chamber, because its gut is a refinery it was born with. The head rides low on a crane neck, counterweighted, armored, blind in the way deep-sea things are blind: two small, lidded pits either side of the jaw that read ground density and magnetic grain, no more interested in light than a compass is. The mill-teeth are the living part you notice last and understand first. Diamond-edged, socketed in a rotating jaw assembly, replaced from within as they wear. It lowers that head to the basalt and grazes the way other animals graze grass — cropping the rust-colored chemolithotroph crust from the lee of boulders, grinding rock and mat together, feeding iron-eating microbes into a furnace that eats rock in turn. It separates what it takes. Iron, silica, rare earths move down different internal channels, are held, worked, combined. What it cannot use it excretes at long intervals in a perfect, glowing ingot of slag, laid still-hot on the glass to cool over days into another black boulder. Which, in a decade, grows crust on its sheltered side. Which is grazed.

Nothing here drinks, and least of all these. Their fluids are hot oils and liquid salts and mercury-bright humors that move through mineral veins and do not boil because boiling is a habit of water, and water left this lineage so long ago that their chemistry no longer remembers the shape of thirst. Cut one — nothing will ever cut one — and it would not bleed. It would leak light and pressure and a smell like a struck match the size of a cathedral.

### Being Out of Phase

The Smelter you see is not where the Smelter is.

This is not camouflage, and it is not a trick of heat. Heat-shimmer blurs. This displaces, cleanly and completely, the way a sound arrives from the wrong side of a valley. You may watch one graze in full, unreasonable detail two hundred meters to your left — every plate, every arcing tendon, the slow rotation of its teeth on empty stone — while the actual animal stands thirty meters to your right, invisible, displacing a wind you can feel move across one side of your face. The ground jumps under your boots with a footfall it has not visibly taken.

The offset has no loyalty. Sometimes it is ten meters of space. Sometimes it is a minute of time, and you watch the head descend long after the real teeth have finished grinding the crust you are standing on. Their shadows fall honestly — toward the mass, not away from the sun — which is why on a herd morning every boulder on the flats can wear two shadows, and the second one is traveling. Experienced travelers walk by those shadows. They walk by the patch of ground where the glittering flies abruptly are not. They walk by absence, the way you would navigate a crowded room blindfolded, by the pressure of other bodies on the air.

Why they live displaced, no one can say, because there is no one here to have evolved a theory. It may be a byproduct, the way a dynamo sheds a field: their nervous systems run in loops so massive and so charged that perception — theirs and yours — arrives bent. It may be defense. A predator that lunges at the furnace glow closes its jaws on a mirage and an echo, forty meters and ninety seconds from the throat it wanted. It may be that at their size, and at their tempo, being exactly coincident with yourself is an unnecessary expense. A Smelter's leg is raised in a fault basin in the east where it has been raised for nine hundred years, image smeared into forty horizontal copies of itself, and the real animal walked out of that basin long ago. Or never arrived. Or arrives a little more each spring.

They are not in a hurry to resolve the question.

### Herd Life

They travel in herds that are less like herds than like distributed cathedrals, each animal spaced far enough from the next that you could lose a town between them, all of them bound together by infrasound too low to hear and too loud to ignore — a pressure that rearranges your sternum and passes route-knowledge, grazing-knowledge, the memory of a crossing, from the old to the merely ancient. A migration across the Anvil takes longer than most civilizations. The herd has a destination. The herd remembers arriving there, generations of calves ago, the way a river remembers a course it has not cut yet.

Calves are born already idling. That is the plateau's own word for it, offered reluctantly: a calf comes into the world small — only the height of a house — with its chest-pistons stuttering, its dynamo organs gravid with a starter charge inherited directly from the mother, its plates still soft as cooling slag. It cannot start itself. So the herd gathers. Every visible image converges and dissolves in the shimmer and the real bodies form a ring you feel rather than see, and they jump-start the calf with their own fields, pouring charge across the gap in a low, tender booming until the little furnace catches and the calf's vents flutter orange for the first time. Hammer-mites arrive within the hour, riding up the piston-joints to take their place in the seams. They will eat escaping sparks there for the rest of the animal's life, and their descendants after them.

A Smelter lives for centuries. It molts gear-teeth and grows them. It sheds conductor-sheath and grows that too, brighter each time. Bulls — the word is inadequate and everyone uses it anyway — periodically ground their accumulated charge through the earth in a blinding act that may be mating display, or territorial punctuation, or simple relief, fusing a perfect lens of glass in the sand and standing in the ozone reek of it with all six legs braced, humming. When one dies, which takes years, the furnace banks chamber by chamber, the piston-joints lock in sequence, the plates go gradually the temperature of the surrounding rock. Scavenger things come to work the seams. In a century the corpse is a boulder formation with an odd, architectural sympathy to it, growing rust-crust in its lee. The herd does not return for it. The herd, in a sense, never entirely left; the migration is that slow, and the dead stay on the route.

### The Attendance

Everything else on the Anvil is sized by its relationship to the grazers.

Turbine crows ride the exhaust updrafts off their backs — black, heavy-beaked birds whose primary feathers have stiffened into fan blades that spin as they soar, generating a shrill, needle whine and a usable trickle of charge they spend on some private economy of their own. They nest nowhere. They may not land at all; nobody has seen a young one that could not already fly. Tick-like prospectors clamp to a Smelter's belly and tap furnace heat through a siphon to crack the ore-pellets they carry in cheek-sacs, dropping the split stones as they travel, seeding the flats with bright, worked fragments that the hammer-mites then colonize.

Higher, sail-backed gliders circle thermals for years without landing, pregnant with static, trailing filament tails a hundred meters long. When a tail brushes another glider's tail, the circuit closes in a single blinding stitch of lightning and both animals ride the discharge like a correction. What they eat, up there in the empty blue, is a question the plateau declines to make urgent. Perhaps they eat the high, thin, wind-borne fraction of the iron-filing swarms. Perhaps they eat charge itself, and the rest is commentary.

And there are predators. Long, low, furnace-mouthed things, many-legged and fast in the way a landslide is fast, which hunt by magnetics: you know one is near because your compass begins to spin and every loose grain of grit on the ground stands up on end, pointing. They hunt the young and the dying and the badly phased. They are the reason, perhaps, for everything — the displacement, the herd-ring around a newborn, the bulls grounding their charge in sudden, violent circles of fused glass. Even they are not fast enough, often. Even they bite mirages.

### What They Are, Finally

From a distance, the High Anvil looks empty, and the inhabitants prefer it legible that way. You will see perhaps four creatures before dark: a grazer smeared across the horizon, a glider stitching its slow circuit, crows whining in a thermal. You will be aware, in your teeth, in your doubled shadow, in the standing grit and the sudden metallic silences, of thousands — an entire evolved industry walking itself in slow procession across a furnace floor, smelting the rock it stands on, remembering a destination it will reach after everyone who ever named it is a mineral deposit. The technology here was never built. It was born idling, and jump-started by its mothers, and it has been grazing, and glowing, and arriving slightly out of true, for longer than the wind has had its current direction.

They are not waiting for anything. That is the part that stays with you. They were not left here. This is not a ruin, and it is not a wilderness. It is a pasture, at full working volume, occupied by its rightful machines.
