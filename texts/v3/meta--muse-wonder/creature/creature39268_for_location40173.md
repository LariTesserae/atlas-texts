It lives in the middle, where nothing lives.

Not at first in the way a thing in the middle should. There is no hump to it, no back, no burrow-mouth or nest. The clearing is, as far as any ordinary eye can arrange it, bare — thirty paces of leaf-litter pressed flatter than the litter at the edges, beech leaves over beech leaves, copper and dun and black where they have gone soft with wet, flecked with the pale husks of mast. You could walk across it and walk across it again and only come away thinking that the deer have been thorough here, that the shade is absolute, that the grove for some reason refuses to seed itself in its own centre.

That refusal is its outline.

It is so low that it has taken the shape of the flatness that made it. A long, broad body, wider than it is long, spread out under a skin of its own shed layers like a pond that decided, very slowly, to stay. The edges are the hardest part to find. Out toward the root-mat the dark leaf-colour thins into real leaf, into moss, into the ordinary damp floor of the wood, and there is a place — you could not point to it twice in the same spot — where the clearing stops being ground and starts, almost imperceptibly, to be warm in the wrong way. Not warm. Less cold. Eleven degrees instead of ten. A living coolness.

The top of it is litter, but litter that does not shift. Fallen leaves land on it and are taken down into it in patches, season by season, glued by damp and by the fine, colourless slime it sweats in winter, until they make a new scale. Old scales show at the thinner places near its flanks: overlapping plates, brittle at the rim, leathery underneath, veined like beech leaves themselves, with the same tea-brown translucence. Beechmast sticks in the seams and sometimes sprouts there in spring, a dozen doomed green shoots rising straight out of its back, and the deer come in at night and crop them close and then lie down, without knowing or needing to know, on the only bed in the hollow that gives a fraction under their weight and never gives back a stone. It likes the weight. You can tell by the way the surface under a resting deer settles over an hour — not denting, exactly, but accommodating, rising very slightly around the belly and the folded legs, taking their shape the way mud takes a print. When they get up at dusk there is for a while a deer-shaped smoothness on it, which the next fall of leaves erases.

It has no need to go anywhere, because everything it eats arrives from above or walks onto it out of the edges.

This is a place where the rain is still falling days after the sky has cleared, drop by drop from the undersides of leaves, and those drops are its weather and its drinking both. They land on it all day with magnified, drumskin loudness — *too* loudly, beside your boot, for mere leaf-litter — and where they land, the surface darkens, drinks, closes. Dead flies come down out of the rookery. Rook droppings, white and seed-filled. Woodlice and beetles and small black slugs wander out of the root-mat at night and cross it on their way to somewhere else, and some of them simply do not finish the crossing: the skin under them softens, sweetens, holds. By morning there is nothing to see but a slightly glossier patch. Shrews cross it too, and deer mice, mostly unharmed; it is not a trap so much as a very slow digestion of whatever lingers. A fallen rook chick in June is gone by August, bones and all, folded down scale by scale until the black feathers are just another dark vein in the pattern of its back. Nothing here can afford to waste calcium. There is no mineral earth to lick, no stone to gnaw. So it wastes none.

Underneath, where no one will ever dig to, it is eating the grove and making it at once. Metres of rot go in under its belly year by year — leaf, root, fallen limb, the green mattress-ridges of dead beeches — and come out behind the slow wave of its movement, if movement is the word for a thing that may advance a hand's breadth in a decade, as finer, darker, more thoroughly sorted mould. The floor rises a fraction and falls a fraction because it is passing, at the pace of geology, through a body. The pump's predecessor, half-sunk by the roots at the lowest edge, is not exempt. Moss has it, and fungus, and underneath the moss this older, peripheral reach of the clearing-thing has found the iron and learned the taste of it; the gear that still shows through is going the way the rook chick goes, only over fifty years instead of a summer.

It does not hear the rookery the way you hear it. It has no ears to lift, no head, in any strict sense, to turn. What it has, spaced around the forward third of its body where the skin is thinnest and almost black, is a row of shallow pit-organs, perpetually full of water — oval, a few centimetres deep, indistinguishable from the root-pools and the filled bootprints at the grove's edge. Sound comes to it there as pressure. The cawing and bill-snapping from twenty metres up drops straight down the empty column of air and strikes those little black mirrors and goes on in, doubled; woodpeckers on the dead limbs set up a riveting it feels as weather in its own ribs; the frogs' mechanical sawing at the pools' edges in season is, to it, a near and familiar itching along its left side. And at noon, when the pump at the low edge chugs awake with its cut of hot oil through the leaf-smell and settles into that slow, inescapable beat, the whole of it answers, not by choice but by long accommodation. Its own deep pumping organ — a fist of muscle buried somewhere under the centre of the clearing, older than the diesel, older than the iron — has spent a century learning the timer's rhythm and now keeps it even on the days the engine rests. You feel it in the soles of your boots before you can name it. The root-pools at its margin tremble in rings, though whether from the flywheel or from the body under your feet, it is no longer possible, and maybe no longer meaningful, to say.

That is why it can be still in the middle of such noise. The noise is not happening to it. It is one of the instruments.

Its senses, such as they are, are mostly downward and inward: the chemistry of rot arriving under its belly, the iron tang of standing water, the exact and maintained degree of wetness that the pump has held the hollow to for decades — never flood, never drought, dampness as a constant, like temperature. It lives at flood's lower threshold the way a fish lives at a certain depth. A wet winter that drowned the clearing would lift and tear it; a dry summer that cracked it would open it to the air and kill it from the edges in. Neither happens. The engine chugs, the water stays in its pockets, the skin stays cool and faintly damp and never wet, never dry. Its whole physiology is a bet, placed long ago, that nothing here would ever change enough to matter. So far the grove has kept its side.

It is not young, and it is not, in any way that can be observed, old. Things that replace themselves piecemeal do not age in a line. A beech dies standing at the north side and is replaced over fifty years by the beech that waited in its shade; this does something similar with its own substance, scale by scale, pit by pit, the forward edge thickening as the rear edge thins into mould and is forgotten. Parish records go back a certain distance and mention the rookery. Nothing mentions this, because from any human angle it has always looked like an empty place where deer lie. Perhaps it was smaller once, a dark patch under one nurse-log. Perhaps the grove grew up around it the way the trunks have swollen together — not planted, but converged, a ring of presences deciding over centuries that the thing to do with this flat, cold, water-held bowl was to wall it, roof it, keep the sun's heat off it and let the sound build.

Once in a great while — no one has seen it; it would be a matter of decades between events, and of being in the centre, at the right hour, in the right season — it reproduces, in the only way available to a creature that is mostly a surface. In late autumn, after mast-fall, a small patch near the margin loosens. Not an egg, not a young animal with legs: a hand-sized scale, thicker than the others, dark as the pools, curled at the edge against the cold, with the pit-organs already showing as pinpricks of shine. Over a winter it works free. Over the next few years, carried a fraction at a time on the slow tide of litter, nudged by deer hooves and frost-heave and the feet of foresters, it travels outward until it finds what the parent found long ago: the spine of a fallen giant, soft as a mattress, sprouting toadstools and young holly, a place where plant matter is deep enough to live in and lie on at once. Most of them rot before they settle. Most of them are eaten, unknowing, by the parent itself as they cross it, folded back into the general dark. One in a century, perhaps, takes hold in a hollow that does not yet exist — another flattened floodplain, another bowl of trunks — and starts the very long work of becoming a clearing.

Stand in the middle long enough and your clothes go cool and faintly damp without ever getting wet. That is its breath, such as it is, leaving the skin all around you at the pace of evaporation off a pond. A drop falls from somewhere high and lands too loudly beside you, and the place where it lands darkens, and drinks, and closes. The flywheel turns at the edge of the hollow. The organ under the centre turns with it. A rook crosses the column of air overhead, black against ordinary bright sky, calling once, and the call comes down and goes through the little black pools and through the soles of your boots and into a body that has never once needed to look up.

There is nothing else to arrive. It has been here the whole time you were looking at the emptiness.
