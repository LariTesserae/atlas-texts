From below, in the lowlands, they are supposed to be a people apart — plateau people, table people, the ones who live in the white city and never come down. From inside, there is no apart to be. You cannot draw a line where the human population stops and the rest of the animal population begins, not by sound, not by smell, not by the hour of waking.

They wake before light because everything else does. The dawn chorus is not an alarm so much as a pressure change in the walls. In the rookery districts the tenements themselves begin to vibrate — thousands of birds shifting on ledges, in nesting holes, in the warm cavities of the brick skin — and the vibration passes through to the interior rooms where the people sleep, windowless by design, in the dark core of the building. You learn as a child to sleep through ordinary screaming and to wake at a change in it. A silence at the wrong hour means fire, or a hawk, or a facade about to come down. A good sleeper here is not a deep sleeper. A good sleeper is a discriminating one.

They emerge ducking.

Everyone on the plateau ducks, slightly, permanently, even in open sky. Generations of porters have grooved the lintels with yokes; the stone stairs are dished into bowls; the doorways were made for shorter loads and shorter people and then never raised, because raising a doorway means disturbing the nests above it, and you cannot disturb the nests during nesting, and it is always, somewhere on the face of a building, nesting. So they have become a compact, stoop-shouldered people without being particularly short — broad through the chest, thick in the neck, legs heavy from stairs, calves ropey from a lifetime of inclined stone. Old men and women are bent like hooks. Children run at a crouch under web-laced alley mouths even after the morning sweepers have cut the webs down, out of pure inherited caution.

Their skin takes the color of the city. Up at two thousand meters the sun is hard and the guano terraces are white enough to hurt, so roof-workers, scrapers, loft-keepers, goat-herds on the parapets go dark as lowland leather, but the interior people — the machinists, weavers, tanners, cooks, clerks of the nest-rights courts, the families who live three rooms in from any outside wall — keep a damp, cellar pallor, greyish, faintly yellow around the eyes. Everyone, dark or pale, is streaked. Ammonia bleaches hair before age does; it is common to see a twenty-year-old with white forelocks and eyelashes crusted pale from working the drying terraces. Foundry crews are freckled black with soot that never quite washes out of the pores. Canal families have a rust line at the wrist, where the water gets in under the glove, year after year.

They smell, to a lowlander, intolerable. To each other, they smell like information. Hot feathers, wet leather, coal smoke, boiled grain, tallow soap, the sweet rot of a tannery two districts over carried on rising afternoon air, the iron tang of canal water on a coat that has dried and been wetted a hundred times. Their own bodies contribute without embarrassment. Fleas, lice, bedbugs, the small mites that live in pigeon down — these are not a failure of cleanliness here, they are weather. Bathhouses are communal, steaming, cockroach-haunted places where you go precisely because everything lives on everyone, and for an hour the heat makes it all stop biting. You come out pink and temporarily empty, smelling of sulfur, and by the time you have crossed the laundry yard the midges have found you again.

Breathing has shaped their faces. The nose broadens; the nostrils flare and stay flared. Long-term guano workers breathe shallowly through the mouth behind canvas hoods, and their voices come out hoarse, flattened, strangely carrying — they learn to speak from the diaphragm, in short phrases, timed between foundry thumps and steam releases and sudden collective lifts of starlings. Conversation on the plateau is not continuous. It is tidal. People pause, without awkwardness, mid-sentence, while a flock goes over or a lift cable screams somewhere or the swifts in a chimney hit a pitch that makes talk pointless, and then they resume exactly where they left off, sometimes twenty seconds later, sometimes after both parties have done something else with their hands. Lowlanders think they are slow. They are not slow. They are interrupted at a frequency lowlanders cannot hear as interruption.

Their hands tell their trades more plainly than uniforms, though there are uniforms of a sort, all canvas, leather, oilcloth, feather-stuffed quilt where the wind cuts on the rim. Loft-keepers have forearms crosshatched with fine white scars from beaks and claws, and a permanent gentleness in the fingers even when the rest of them is coarse — they can sex a squab, clear a blocked chimney vent of a dead swift, coax a sitting hen off eggs, all one-handed, without looking, while arguing about roof rights. Guano scrapers carry their shoulders unevenly from the sacks and keep their fingernails cut to the quick because the dried stuff works in and splits them. Masons — the great hereditary caste, who begin a reservoir tower their grandchildren will fill — have palms like horn, stone dust ground so deep into the lines it looks tattooed, and a habit of tapping any wall they pass, listening for cavity, for nesting, for rot, the way other people check a door is locked. Canal children can pole a barge before they can properly write; canal grandmothers steer with one foot while mending nets that catch nothing but frogs and lost tools, the frogs thrown back, the tools kept.

They eat what the loop provides. Almost nothing they eat photosynthesized here; almost everything arrived on a lift or grew on something that did. Breakfast in a communal kitchen is boiled lowland grain, salt, tallow, a pigeon egg if the loft is laying well, tea the color of canal runoff made from imported stuff no one can name anymore. Meat is preserved, dried, smoked, potted — goat from the roof herds, mule when a mule's route is finally done with it, squab by the thousand, swift and starling in season, eggs in quantities that would sicken a lowlander and here pass as ordinary. The one great luxury, hoarded for weddings and for women after childbirth and for the sick, is pickled green stuff from below: sour leaves packed in brine, tasting of a world none of them has seen, eaten in tiny amounts like medicine. Scurvy is an old enemy kept at bay by routine rather than cured; gums bleed commonly, old scars reopen in the late winter, and nobody makes a tragedy of it. The frogs in the drains are not eaten, for reasons that are part law, part disgust, part gratitude — the frogs eat the flies, and the flies, if uneaten, would eat everyone else.

Their children are raised inside the larger raising. A tenement infant is laid in a room warmed on one side by a foundry flue and on the other, in winter, by the literal body heat of roosting birds through the wall, and grows up able to tell, before it can speak, the difference between pigeon warmth (steady, cooing, safe) and a wall gone cold because a colony has been lost. Small children are given ledge-tasks, gutter-tasks, egg-counting. They learn the litigation calendar alongside their letters: you cannot re-roof during nesting, you cannot seal a cavity without building its equivalent, the chimney is built after its rookery, the factory owes the birds first. A child who seals a hole for fun is punished not as a vandal but as a kind of arsonist — he has reduced the city's capacity to be itself. By adolescence most of them have a species, the way lowland children have a trade chosen for them. One is good with bats and works the canal bridges at dusk, counting the river of them by ear. One has the patience for goats on the parapets. One goes down into the cellars where the white molds bloom and comes up coughing and content. It is not romantic. It is aptitude, noticed early, because a wrong placement wastes a lifetime and lifetimes are the main currency.

For their time is the city's time, and the city is slow in a way that enters the marrow. A lawsuit over nest rights begun by a woman in her marrying year may be settled, amicably, by her daughter, and neither party thinks the court has failed. A marriage is likewise an accumulation — families merge lofts, merge roof access, merge the obligations their grandfathers owed to particular colonies of swifts that have since died and been replaced by their own descendants, both avian and human tracking the same ledges. Love here expresses itself practically: I will cut the webs on your mother's stairs, I will take your father's night watch during the swift arrival, your name added to the loft book in the hand we both learned from the same keeper. Funerals return the body to the economy without sentimentality and without waste: bones to the mills, fat to the lamps, the rest, after the formalities, going where everything goes, out onto the black terraces under the sun, to dry and whiten and be sacked and sent down the cliff to feed fields the dead person never saw, which will send up grain their grandchildren will boil. People find this comforting. The loop holds. Nothing is required to leave it.

They are not cruel, and they are not kind in a lowland way either; both words assume a separation they do not feel. A keeper who will sit up three nights with a sick racing hen will wring two hundred necks before breakfast without changing expression. Dogs sleep in the doorways because the doorways are warm and because a dog asleep is a dog not costing anything, and also — this is said plainly — because they like the company, the weight of another animal breathing nearby in a city that is, at every scale, about the weight of animals breathing nearby. Rats are not exterminated, they are managed, displaced, outcompeted by cats kept deliberately hungry at a precise, professional level. Even the house spiders are tolerated until morning, their alley-veiling webs acknowledged as a kind of overnight weaving that must be cut and will be remade. The people live as one more numerous, long-lived, tool-using species in a stacked habitat, and they know, without needing to say it, that the buildings are habitat first. They happen to be the animal that builds the cavities. That is their niche. It confers duties more than privileges.

Late in life they go, many of them, a little deaf in one ear — the tram side, the foundry side, the side turned for sixty years toward the screaming chimney — and their eyes, squinted against guano glare on the roofs and accustomed to darkness in the interiors, adjust poorly to open distance. Those few who ride the freight lifts down, for law or marriage or the fertilizer trade, come back shaken not by the lowlands' greenness, which terrifies some of them outright, roots and weeds and soft ground that gives, but by the quiet and the emptiness of the sky. Down there, they say, you can hear yourself. Up home the sky is occupied; the air has traffic; the walls have tenants; your own wall is warm in patches at night with other lives.

And in the late afternoon, when the plateau cloud forms overhead and the rain falls only inside the rim, they come out as everything comes out — onto ledges, parapets, steaming cistern edges, shaking water off, bathing, calling, resettling — the people among the pigeons, indistinguishable at a distance in the grey veils of steam, another species crowding the wet stone, flourishing briefly and loudly in the runoff, and then settling again, patient as masonry, onto a city their ancestors have been softening, whitening, and inhabiting together for longer than anyone up there bothers to keep records.
