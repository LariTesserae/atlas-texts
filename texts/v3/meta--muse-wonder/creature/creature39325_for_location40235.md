First you meet them with your ears, and only later, separately, with your eyes, and never all at once.

There is no one animal in the valley. There is no king of it, no dominant species clearing the others out of the good terraces. What lives there is an occupancy so dense and so partitioned — by stone, by shade, by hour — that after a while you stop thinking of it as parrots *and* monkeys *and* frogs and start thinking of it as a single population wearing thousands of different bodies.

They have it floor by floor.

The topmost skin of the place, the wall-crowns and the rim terraces and the fig canopies that have replaced the roofs, belongs in daylight to the loud ones. Parrots by the thousand, scarlet and green and blue-headed, roosting shoulder to shoulder along cornices that were cut to shed water and now shed feathers. They do not nest so much singly as continue a roost that seems to have a memory longer than any one bird: the same ledges whitened, the same fig branches polished by feet, generation settling exactly where generation settled before. Below them, toucans work the fruit trees in heavy, bouncing flights, swallowing figs whole. Hoatzins clamber through the shrubs over the cisterns, wings clawed, graceless, entirely at home. Egrets pick through the shallow tanks. Herons stand in canals built, by coincidence, to precisely heron depth, one leg tucked, waiting for the frogs that live in the plumbing to make a mistake.

Because the water system has its own tenants, and they are its voice. Every drain mouth has its frog, throat pulsing in the dark round opening. Every cistern, half full under its mat of ferns, is rimmed with them at dusk. They live in bromeliad cups in the niches, in the inch of algae-shining water in an aqueduct, in pockets where a spout has failed and made a permanent puddle on a stair. Small, brown, green, speckled, they are not *in* the city’s water so much as they are a condition of it, and at night the whole stonework thrums with them, as if the canals themselves were calling.

Above the frogs, continuous even over their chorus, are the swifts. They nest in every drain hole and under every eave and cornice, so that the buildings are stippled with them, and their twittering in daylight is not a sound that starts and stops but a high, electric weather. Swallows have mudded their cups onto carved faces, beakful by beakful supplementing the stonework. Weaverbirds stitch pendant nests under every surviving lintel until doorways hang full of them, swaying green-and-yellow clusters that cheep when the wind moves them. In the one great plaza where the sun actually lands at midday, vultures hold the center — wings spread on the bell-ringing slabs to dry the night’s damp out of their feathers — and around their feet the pavement moves with finches and doves, pecking a century’s worth of seed and dropping and grit out of the joints. Nobody hunts them there. Nobody has hunted anything there for a very long time, and they know it in their bodies. They do not flee you. They flow, annoyed, six feet sideways, and resettle, and watch you pass with the bright, entitled stare of tenants whose lease is older than your species’ memory of the place.

The wall-tops are roads, and the monkeys use them as roads. Capuchins in trooping parties, picking, squabbling, carrying infants and stolen mangoes, their nails clicking on stone worn smooth by exactly this traffic. Howlers in the higher, heavier branches, letting out a roar that rolls down the terraced bowl and sets the parrots off again. Below them, where they drop rinds and split seeds into the courts, the ground shift works: coatis with their tails up, nosing through leaf litter in single file; agoutis darting and freezing, darting and freezing; peccaries rooting in family bands, snuffling through the damp fill of a collapsed house until they have turned it as thoroughly as a ploughed field. Tamarins, tiny and maned, sleep in the layered thatch that is no longer quite thatch and not yet quite soil. Every hour-of-sun stone has its lizards doing push-ups over territory measured in inches, its butterflies puddling at the wet mortar.

And then there is the population that never sees sun at all, which may be the largest.

Under the terraces, in the covered galleries and nesting chambers and undercrofts where it is true warm dark, the city is packed. Bats in colonies that pour out at dusk in rivers and pour back before dawn, their guano deep and soft underfoot, sweet-smelling, feeding beetles without number. Kinkajous sleep rolled tight in the roof spaces, unfolding at night to walk the beams upside down. Owls and nightjars take the dark the bats vacate. Whole chambers are given over to bees — you smell the honey before you reach the doorway, warm and fermented, and hear the interior seething in its comb-darkness. Wasps have papered the undersides of lintels. Termites have built a second city directly over the first, mud towers and sheeted runways earthen-skinning the stone towers, inhabited in their own right. Ants maintain permanent highways along the carved gutters, thousands bearing green leaf-flags upstream in a line so constant it looks like the aqueducts themselves are flowing with vegetation.

They are not merely sheltering in the ruin. They are continuing it.

That is what distinguishes them, finally, from animals in a forest that happens to have stones in it. They build where the builders built. Weaverbirds thatch with stitch and saliva where thatch has rotted. Swallows re-mortar with mud. Monkeys drag branches into favored sleeping corners and trample them, season after season, into platforms. Fig roots split a wall and then thicken until they *are* the wall, load-bearing. Moss stitches what feet loosen. Dung and leaf-fall and collapsed nests fill the fill behind the retaining stones a fraction at a time, holding the terraces up from inside. Maintenance here has passed from hands to beaks and claws and roots, but it has never actually stopped.

They live slow in the midst of all that noise. A parrot explodes off a wall and settles on the same wall. A bat colony returns to the same black chamber its ancestors blackened. Frogs call from plumbing that has held frogs since the last human voice was there to hear them. Fruit comes — fig, then mango, then wild avocado rotting sweet on warm stone and drawing iridescent flies — and the same processions shift their routes from orchard to orchard along the wall-roads, as they must have done when the orchards were tended and the fruit was a harvest instead of a fall. Nothing is in a hurry to change its address. Death is mostly unobserved under leaves; vultures and ants and beetles process it quickly back into the general damp.

By day they are birds and monkeys and ringing cicadas you feel in your teeth. By night, seamlessly, fireflies over the lower canal pockets, and frogs louder, and the rustle and squeak and thump of ten thousand nocturnal bodies moving through rooms and passages sized, conveniently, for bodies. The same niches hold different tenants on different shifts. The city is never empty and never quiet and never, in any meaningful sense, abandoned.

What lives there, then, is an inheritance made flesh: a many-species tenancy so old, so settled, so unafraid, that the animals are not reclaiming the place. They are the place, in its second, feathered and furred and rooting incarnation — still inhabiting the stairs, the drains, the cisterns, the dark under the terraces, keeping a stone town alive by living in it exactly as fully as it was built to be lived in.
