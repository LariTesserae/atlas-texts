You hear them before you see them, too, but differently. Not talking. The pit doesn't allow talking.

Halfway down the iron stair the pumps take your chest and the looms take your ears and the air thickens, and the people appear out of steam the way things appear in green water — first a movement, then a shape, then a hand raised close to a face. Palm flat: *wait*. Two fingers hooked: *line moving*. A fist tapped once on a forearm: *hot*. They are fluent in a language that only exists fifteen meters down.

It was painted on placards to begin with, a safety code. It didn't stay that. After a century of twelve-hour shifts in a place where shouting has to be done mouth-to-ear to be heard at all, the hand-signs acquired grammar. They have signs for kinds of wet, for the smell of a retting tank turning, for *that pipe will knock in a minute, don't lean on it*. Above ground, at the rim lockers, they speak like anyone else in the district, hoarse and a little too loud. Below, their hands do the work, quick and economical, never lifted higher than the shoulder. It saves breath. The air is too warm and too used to waste on sentences.

They do not live down there. That is the first thing to understand, and it isn't true.

No one sleeps in the basin. No one is allowed to. On steam-through night the whole place is evacuated and sealed and scalded lane by lane to keep it from rotting and to keep anything animal from settling — no rats in the store-rooms, no frogs in the canals, no nests, no eggs, no burrows. The workers climb out before it starts and the pumps are left to beat alone in the mist. In the morning they climb back down to a world that has been reset to plants and iron and water.

And yet they are not visitors.

They come down wet and they leave wet. The change is only in degree. The crews work knee-deep as a matter of course — skimmers leaning on long poles to fold the duckweed back from the settling ponds, retters in canvas waders turning flax in the waist-deep tanks by feel, their arms bare and slick to the elbow, knowing a stem is done by the way it gives between finger and thumb. Terrace crews go drier but never dry: pruners and cutters and bamboo-men soaked through from the waist up by leaves, by drip from the glass nave, by their own sweat in the boiler heat. The taro tenders move the slowest of all, calf-deep in the clean channels, unhurried as herons that aren't allowed to exist.

Their clothes have been decided for them by the place. Canvas, undyed or dyed with things that don't mind mildew. Leather that would rot in a week is unknown below the rim. So is wool, so is paper, so is any pocket left unemptied overnight. They wear caps or tie their hair tight and flat; loose hair mats, molds at the scalp, picks up fiber. They wear brass at their wrists instead of watches — the pumps are the clock down there, three flywheels in the tiled pump cathedral turning behind railings polished black by palms, their beat slowing fractionally when the pressure from above drops, quickening before a pipe knocks. An old pump-keeper will stop mid-sign and listen with his whole spine, the way you or I would listen for a name called in another room. The pump-keepers are the nearest thing the basin has to priests, or to family. There are three of them a shift, always, one for each engine, and they do not rotate to other work. Some of them have kept the same pump for thirty years. They oil by smell. They know their machine's idiosyncrasies in their molars.

Everyone else rotates, because the pit wears particular parts of a person down. Loom-tenders in the long east shed go deaf in a specific band first, the band of the shuttle's tick, and learn to feel the belt drives through the soles of their feet. The nave crews — mostly young, mostly apprentices — work in the green underwater light among the seedling trays and hanging ferns, lifting, tying orchid clones in coconut husk to the warm vents, propagating everything by cutting because nothing down here is permitted to seed. Their eyes get very good in dimness. Up top at shift change they'll blink and shade their faces on an ordinary overcast day as if it were noon in a desert. Their skin takes on the basin's palette, not sun-brown but steam-pale, softened, the hands especially, permanently pruned at the fingertips, nails kept cut to the quick because soft nails tear on wet fiber.

The slate-keepers are the oldest, usually. When a loom-hand can no longer hear the warning signs even signed urgently across a lane, when a retter's lungs begin to object to a lifetime of warm wet air, they are moved to the tin-box rooms off the canteen where the records are etched on zinc and slate with a stylus. Births are not recorded there. Neither are deaths. The tablets list cuttings taken and from which mother-plant, tanks turned and when, which fig root has been bolted to which failing gantry and accepted as structure. It is a genealogy of plants that are not allowed to have descendants, kept by people who very often do: son following mother down the stair, daughter following father, not out of sentiment but because basin work is a literacy. A child from above ground, put on the duckboards for the first time, will flinch at the noise and grab at every rail and be useless for a month. A basin child already knows not to step where the moss is bright, already knows a root mass will hold and a half-rotted board won't, already reads the lanes by the feel of moving water against the ankle — clean channel left, effluent right, don't put your weight there when the line is moving.

They eat together in the canteen at the level where the smell of lime and hot iron is thinnest, food brought down every morning in sealed tins from the street because almost nothing grown below is grown to be eaten — the taro is a filter first and a crop second — and they eat quickly, still signing with their mouths full. There is no lingering. Lingering is what the plants do. The whole labor of the place, underneath the pumps and looms and vats that supposedly justify it, is a holding action performed by human hands: lanes kept a shoulder's width clear, pipes kept findable in the philodendron, vents kept from closing over with monstera, bamboo cut and cut and cut again before it shades out the tanks. Skip a corner for three days and purslane takes a coiled hose. Skip it for six and you will have to cut your way in to learn what you have lost. The workers understand, in a way no one at street level quite does, that they are not tending a garden or running a factory. They are negotiating, daily, with the only permanent residents.

Because the plants do live there. They live there the way the pumps live there — rooted, bolted, plumbed in. The figs that send flat ropes of root down from the rim and across the brick like slow lightning are older than any crew and are, by now, load-bearing. When an iron support rusts through, the root that replaces it is not removed. It is accepted, shaved flat, bolted to, built around. The monstera holding the pipe-end wall together is not decoration that got out of hand; it is the wall. The mother-plants in the nave, the great sterile orchid stocks and fern banks from which every cutting in the basin descends, have individual slate numbers and individual tenders and small brass plaques wired to their stems, like names. They will never flower usefully, never fruit, never complete the cycle that would require a pollinator, a bird, a wind that isn't full of steam. They grow, and are cut, and grow from the cutting, and are cut again, in a green immortality that goes nowhere.

That is the population of the pit, in the end: a race of clones that cannot die properly, tended by a race of people who cannot stay. At night, after the last line has climbed the shaking stair and come out blinking into dry air and sudden, miraculous quiet, the basin continues without a single animal eye to witness it. The pumps beat on the tiled floor. Condensation gathers under the glass and rains on the taro. The bacteria in the retting tanks — never named in the rule about no animal life, because no one down there counts a thing they cannot see — go on patiently rotting the flax soft in the dark. Fiber loosens. Algae films a clean surface. A fern unrolls exactly where it will have to be cut away in the morning.

The workers go home to houses that must feel, to them, unnaturally still and unnaturally dry, their ears ringing for an hour with a noise that isn't there, their hands still making small, involuted signs at the dinner table — *wait, hot, line moving* — before they catch themselves and laugh, too loud.

And in the morning they go back down, because if they didn't, in a week there would be no place to go back down to. Only growth, closing over the lanes like water over a stone.
