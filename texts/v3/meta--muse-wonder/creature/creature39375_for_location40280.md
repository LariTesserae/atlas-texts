First you notice how they walk.

Not wading — there is too much water in the lanes for that word, and too little. They walk as if the water were part of the pavement. A flat, unhurried placing of the foot, a slight roll outward so the black film breaks around the ankle instead of splashing, a pause so small you only learn to see it after a day of watching, while the foot finds the firm cinder under the silt. Then the other foot. They never step where the duckweed is unbroken. That is not superstition, quite. It is habit so old it lives in the legs: unbroken green means deeper water, a missing slab, a grating left open.

Their shoes tell you most of what a census would. Wide shoes, softened to the shape of the foot inside them, the leather permanently dark and swollen. Many of the older people go barefoot indoors and out from May onward, and their feet have broadened with use, toes splayed, soles pale and ridged as fingertips left too long in a bath — except it isn't too long, it is always. Toenails gone soft and translucent. Between the toes the skin stays tender no matter the age, and mothers check children's feet at night the way other mothers check for lice, turning them to the lamplight, drying each fold with a warm cloth that will not, in that air, ever get them dry. Just less wet. Just warm.

They are, like everything in the town, without sharp edges.

The people are not tall. The roofs are low, the pipe-bridges lower, and generations of ducking under a hot condensate line at the turn by the turbine house have put a slight, permanent inclination into shoulders and neck — not a stoop, exactly, more a readiness. Their hair is kept short or tied up in cloth, because loose it never dries and mildews sweetly against the scalp. Their clothes are wool and heavy cotton in undyed browns and boiler-blacks and one startling, repeated blue — doors, shutters, a woman's dress, a boy's cap all painted from the same Works stores, a blue that goes almost black with damp and shows handprints where it is touched often. Nobody owns a garment that could be called crisp. Collars soften, hems soften. Buttons are bone and wood because metal frets cloth in that wet — or else large iron buttons greased along with the tools.

Their hands are what they do for introductions, if you know to look. Polished palms. Grease worked so deep into the fingerprint whorls that it survives washing. Short nails, because long ones split in the wet. Rings, if worn at all, worn on cords round the neck rather than risk a finger swelling in the heat. Everyone's hands know, without looking, the temperature of various pipes by the soundness of a knock, the correct give of a leather belt, the difference between silt and reed-mat and a bolt gone to rust in a grating. Children are judged grown not by years but by whether they can be trusted to wipe down a bearing without being reminded: stroke, turn, wipe again. Like feeding something, one of them will tell you, entirely without metaphor feeling the need to announce itself.

Their speech has been shaped by the pump more thoroughly than by any schoolmaster up over the flywheels.

*Thud — hiss.*

They talk in the hiss and rest in the thud. A sentence arrives in two parts, or three, with the joints neatly placed. To an outsider it sounds phlegmatic, as if everyone here were half-asleep or thinking of something else. In person it is rapid, even witty, but syncopated: name, pause, verb, pause, the funny bit saved clean for the steam. Old people can carry on three conversations at once this way, restarting each exactly where the stroke cut it. Younger ones fill bad silences — a bearing screaming, the sluice-gear grinding its hour — with a whole fluent second language of signs over shoulders and across lanes: *bring, hold, hot, look under, your turn to eat, the boy is in the cistern vault again*. Babies learn some of the signs before words. You will see a grandmother at a doorway answer a baby's flapping with one greased fist, tender and precise.

It has changed their voices, too. Low, to stay under the hiss. A little hoarse at the edges from warm damp air breathed from birth, steam and coal-smoke and the green exhalation of leaves. They have superb hearing for machinery and surprisingly poor hearing for anything high and thin; more than one visitor shouting has been gently taken by the elbow and brought closer, not from deafness exactly, but because a shout is simply the wrong shape of sound here. It gets eaten. Their own laughter is low for the same reason. It travels through brick better than across air anyway — everything here does. Put your palm to a cottage wall in the evening and you can feel who is home.

For these are not houses so much as warm cells in a larger body.

A family might have two rooms over the coal store and a kitchen borrowed heat from the boiler flue year-round, which means soup is always just possible and bread dough always rising somewhere in the town, and in winter — such as winter is, a thicker cloud, a colder rain — nobody freezes because nobody is ever really outside the Works. Privacy is a curtain, a habit of not listening, a way of turning one's chair. Intimacy is unavoidable and therefore mannered. They smell one another constantly: soap when there is soap, wet wool, hot iron on skin, the faintly mushroom smell of people who live where clothes dry only to a negotiated dampness. It stops registering. What registers is a *wrong* smell: a dry smell. Burnt dust. A stranger come in from beyond the marsh whose coat is actually shedding water instead of holding it will turn heads in a lane, pleasantly, as a spice would.

They eat what can be persuaded without wings or teeth to help it along.

Greenhouse beans and peas pollinated flower by flower with little brushes, by women and patient men who can spend a bright-grey morning moving down a row at pump-stroke pace and not find it tedious. Squashes. Roots that like their feet wet. Mushroom beds in the dark under-street vaults, where the black water mirrors the lamps and the caps come up pale and enormous overnight. Reed shoots, pickled greens, grain brought in sacks along the one high causeway and stored in tin-lined bins; flour swells like everything else and is baked often, in small loaves, before the mould blooms blue on it. Almost no meat — there is nothing to make it from, never has been in living memory, and the dried or salted stuff from outside is a festival oddity, chewed with interested suspicion. Protein is beans, is mushrooms, is a whitish curd set in steam. Meals are warm rather than hot, seasoned with vinegar and smoke. Nobody leaves food out for anything. There is nothing to leave it for. A crust on a sill just softens, fur with green, becomes part of the general negotiation between rot and use.

That absence is not a topic. That is what you learn slowest.

Ask about the perfect leaves — figs by the boilers with not a hole in them, ferns three storeys up untouched, reed-heads full-seeded year after year — and you get a shrug and someone showing you how to clear a channel. Ask why there are no midges and a man will slap absently at an ankle out of a politeness to your question, then look surprised at his own hand. Children have a word for duckweed that contains no duck; if you tell them about ducks they receive it as they receive stories of deserts and snow — as true, probably, elsewhere, and faintly badly designed. *It would break the green,* one girl said, not unkindly, considering. In school above the flywheels they learn the names of animals from swollen books kept in tin boxes, and pronounce *spider, rat, frog* in the careful voices of children learning dead languages. Several of the smaller ones believe a pigeon is a kind of valve.

They are not lonely for other life. They have the plants, which they tend with an attention an outsider could mistake for pet-keeping: this fig by the vent whose roots must be eased yearly from the hot brick, that balcony fern descended, cutting by cutting, from a great-grandmother's. And they have the Works.

Everyone works at maintenance. This is not a figure of speech for employment; children clear duckweed from outflow channels with rakes taller than themselves, the very old sit at benches shelling peas and mending gloves and keeping an ear, an actual cocked ear, for the loose inspection plate that has rattled for a decade — because if its tempo changes, someone must be told. Shifts are measured less by clocks than by water levels in the header tanks, by which cistern has been drawn and which bed strain. The pace is the pump's pace, and it has gotten into more than conversation. People sleep in rooms where the stroke comes up through the floor every twenty seconds all night, and their breathing, if you lie awake listening in a borrowed bed over the coal store, slowly finds it. In the evening cycle, forty seconds, the town's breathing lengthens with the machine's. Lovers time privacy to cycles. Arguments cool between strokes. The dying, the midwife says, tend to go on a hiss, as if let out.

Bodies here last well in some ways and poorly in others. Lungs complain early but adapt; there is a characteristic soft cough in middle age that nobody treats because everybody has it, treated instead by standing a while in a particular dry-ish eddy by the turbine where the air moves. Skin infections are constant, minor, managed. Eyes are excellent in dimness, pupils quick, and a noon visitor squinting under the leaf-roof will be guided solicitously by a six-year-old who can see a dropped gasket in green shadow at twenty paces. They sunburn badly and rarely. Their teeth are good from soft food and bad from constant sweet tea kept warm on pipes. Nearly everyone is missing the tip of some small piece of themselves — a fingertip creased flat, a toe shortened by a dropped slab in childhood — and nearly no one is missing anything large. The place is dangerous at the speed of geography, not accident. It wears, it does not strike.

It takes them accordingly. The town sinks a finger's width a year and they add a threshold; a grandmother dies and her window becomes somebody's coal hatch and her fern is divided among the lane. The dead go out to the southern reed beds, to the strained, gravelled ground where the water leaves slower and cleaner than it came — what the old men say of water they also, in the same tone, say of people. Nothing is wasted that can be returned. Ashes in the mushroom vaults. Names reused before the books have finished swelling shut over them. If that sounds cold from outside, from inside it is the opposite: nobody here is ever really allowed to become abstract. Your great-grandfather is a step you still stub a softened toe on. Your mother is the reason that pipe does not knock.

Newcomers either leave within a season — dried out, they say of them, coughing, homesick for weather that finishes happening — or they slow down. It is observable, almost measurable against the flywheels. The stride shortens. The voice drops into the hiss. They stop slapping at insects that are not there, stop expecting a bird to startle from the eaves when a sluice clanks, stop trying to get paper, boots, tempers completely dry. They learn which lanes are warm underfoot at evening. They learn to sit there with the others, chairs in a row where the outflow pipes heat the stones, feet almost in the black shining water, watching the amber bulbs double themselves below knee-height in the second, watery town, while condensation runs down every wall and the leaves overhead — perfect, unbitten, indifferent — drip warm rain that never reaches the marsh.

And late, when the pump slows and the talk does too, what lives here becomes simplest to see: a few hundred people neither sealed against their place nor quite separate from it, held at the exact sinking point between marsh and machine by unending, unhurried care. They are the only animal the town permits, and it has spent generations permitting them into shape — soft-footed, low-voiced, grease-handed, patient as water, breathing in strokes — the warm, moving parts that the Works grew for itself, tending it back, stroke after slow stroke, so that it can go on tending them.
