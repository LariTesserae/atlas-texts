The first time you see one, you may not register them as a person at all.

A shape in a doorway a quarter-mile down a steaming street, standing perfectly still in the amber murk. A figure on a catwalk three stories up on the side of a cooling tower, unmoving while fog pours past them. Someone sitting in a lit boiler-house window at what might be midnight, might be noon, with their hands folded.

There are a few dozen of them for the whole of Sarrat Alto. Boiler-tenders, channel-walkers, filter-men, ledger-keepers, and the one woman who maintains the clocks. Enough to keep the flywheels turning. Not enough to make any street feel peopled.

They have learned to be unhurried in the particular way the city demands. The pumps beat every four seconds, through the pavement, through the soles of the feet, into the teeth. The caretakers walk in time with it without thinking about it. Three steps, a pause. Three steps, a pause. Their speech falls into it too, short sentences with damp air between them, because sound does not carry well here and shouting only makes more steam in the throat.

They are always wet, and they have stopped trying not to be.

Not wet like people caught in rain. Wet from the inside of the air. Their clothes — loose linen shirts, canvas trousers cut off below the knee, aprons of oiled cloth that rot and are replaced — are dark at the seams by morning and never entirely dry again. They hang things to dry the way other people hang things to keep them from mildewing: hopefully, and without expectation. Most go barefoot indoors, where the floors run warm and the gutters run along the skirting boards. Outdoors they wear thick-soled sandals, because the basalt can scald through leather at mid-afternoon.

Their skin takes the color of the place. The sun never quite reaches them, so they are not browned, but they are not pale either. They are the grey-gold of people who live under sodium lamps and wet cloud, stained faintly at the hands and forearms where the mineral water has dried on them day after day, year after year — white at the knuckles with salt, rust-red in the creases, a greenish tinge around the nails from handling copper that never gets scrubbed clean. If you shake hands with a channel-walker, their palm will be soft from the perpetual damp and hard underneath, callused not by friction but by heat. Their hair they keep shaved close or tied up in oiled cloth, because loose hair mats, and mildews at the scalp. Eyelashes bead. Everyone blinks slowly, as if their eyes have learned there is nothing sharp-edged enough in the light to be worth startling at.

They smell, all of them, faintly of the reservoirs: wet iron, sulfur, hot stone dust, and underneath that a clean, boiled smell, like laundry that has been in the pot too long.

You will not see children playing in the courtyards of raked glass. Now and then there is a child — born up here to a boiler-tender and the ledger-keeper's daughter, raised on the second floors where paper can be kept, taught early which pipes are blue. They grow up fluent in a literacy no school down below would recognize: reading the microbial mats that ribbon the cooler edges of the channels. Mustard-yellow thickening means the water is cooling and safe to wade. Milk-white means silica is about to choke a bend. A rust-red bloom tearing loose in sheets means a surge is coming from underground, hours before the pressure gauges admit it. They learn not to seal a door, ever, the way other children learn not to touch a stove. A closed room is not private here. It is a lung with its mouth shut.

Privacy, as a consequence, means something different. Houses breathe through their occupants. Doors are pinned open, vents pinned open, interior rain chains run through hallways where guests are expected to step around them. People live on the upper, drier floors of buildings that are mostly empty below them, one family — one person, more often — to a whole terrace, sleeping not in beds but in the hammock rooms slung high in the ventilated halls. A mattress would grow a skin of mold in a week. A hammock lets the hot air move under the spine. They rest through the worst of the artificial afternoon, when the heat stacks up under the condensate ceiling and the whole city seems to hold its breath, and do their walking at the hours that would be evening if the light ever really changed.

Their work is mostly a refusal to fight.

A new well weeps up through a courtyard seam where no well was drilled, and the nearest caretaker does not call for concrete. She comes with a pole and a bucket, watches it for a day to learn its temper, notes the color of the crust it leaves, and cuts it a shallow channel with a chisel — basalt remembers water, so you might as well give it a line to remember. If it lasts a month it gets a name, entered upstairs in the ledger where the paper keeps: after a dead aunt, after a tool lost in a sump in 1963, after the saint whose feast day it flooded. If it lasts a year it gets a small roof. This is not sentimentality. A named well is a well someone checks.

The boiler-tenders work by sternum more than by ear, leaning their backs against warm iron to feel whether a piston is laboring. The woman who maintains the clocks makes her round with a case of instruments that must be measured twice, because a brass rule is longer at three o'clock than it was at nine, and a clock that was true yesterday is slow today because its pendulum has grown in the heat. There is no sun worth setting a clock by anyway. The clocks are not for the sky. They are for the timed discharges, for knowing when the reservoirs will let go their steaming floods down the southern runnels, when a tower is about to purge and erase a street in fog for a minute. Time up here is a maintenance schedule, and she is its priest.

They drink only from the blue pipes and remind each other of it without nagging, the way you remind someone to duck under a low beam. The wrong water would not kill you quickly. That is the trouble with most things in Sarrat Alto. It would take the enamel, then the teeth, then make a slow mineral project of your kidneys. So their teeth are stained a little, bluish at the gumline, and their smiles — they do smile, dryly, at the visitor who tries to dry his notebook inside his shirt, at the well that has appeared in the middle of the railway station stairs and been promoted, by plaque, to architecture — come and go in the steam.

And they are not, by any true count, the majority of the life here.

Under them, around them, preceding them, is the slime in the cisterns that keeps the copper sweet and must never be scrubbed entirely away. If a new hand gets zealous with a brush, an older hand stops him, almost gently. Leave a handprint of it. Leave the corners. The pipes remember that too. In the channels, the bacterial mats thicken, tear, regrow in three days, in their bands of yellow and white and red — acre upon acre of them, if you added up every gutter and settling bed, a slow, warm meadow with no leaves. In the deepest reservoir, where the water is finally cool enough and dark enough, live the blind shrimp, translucent as fingernails, sightless, unbothered. Nobody fishes for them. When a filter is pulled and one comes up twitching in a bucket, the work stops for a moment. Someone carries it back down and tips it in, the way you would return a bird that had flown into a house. A rumor of animals, confirmed just often enough to be treated with courtesy.

Moths live at the substation lamps, wings edged dark with damp. A lizard lives — perhaps several live, perhaps it is always the same one, reported on different walls — on the sunless hot stone by the markets. Everything that can live here has simplified itself to suit: no green, no song to speak of, nothing that needs dryness or direct light or a sealed place to hide its eggs.

The caretakers have simplified, too, though they would not put it that way. They eat tinned and dried things brought up the switchback road, and fish sometimes from the coolest outflow, and bread baked in steam ovens that never needs to prove because the whole kitchen is proofing temperature. They keep very little below the second floor. They write little, and keep what they write high. They mend the cooling fins, they rake the glass courtyards, they cart pans of lithium salt to a siding for a market that may no longer exist, because if the separators stopped filling, the wells would not stop filling, and the city they tend would go back to being what it was before anyone tried to make an apparatus of it: a hot plateau, weeping from every seam.

Late in the day, when the local rain comes — sudden, warm, strictly theirs, drumming for ten minutes on every iron canopy at once — you can see them all, if you know where to look. Not sheltering. Just pausing. A channel-walker under his well's little roof, watching his gutters fill. The clock-woman in an open doorway with her case at her feet, letting the hour go inaccurate. A boiler-tender with his forehead against a pipe, listening to the pitch change as the runoff gathers and the deep rock shifts under the plateau.

Then the rain thins. The pumps settle. Somewhere across the empty terraces a new seam darkens, blooms white, begins to drip.

Someone will notice. Someone will fetch a chisel, and the ledger, and think of a name.
