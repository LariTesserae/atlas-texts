There is almost no one.

That is the first true thing about the inhabitants of the High Sea, and the longer you stay, the truer it gets. You can sail for a month beneath that white, ordinary sun, through rain that hammers and thins and hammers again, and meet no other living presence at all — no bird working the wind, no fish turning under the swell, no insect drowning in the scuppers. Only, very rarely, at a distance that makes it seem like another temporary island, a hull.

Low, black, broad-beamed, riding deep. No mast worth the name. No bright paint. From far off it looks like one more piece of new basalt that has decided, inexplicably, to move against the current.

Aboard it are six people, or nine, or sometimes — on the anchored raft-tenders — only two. There will be perhaps a few hundred of them on the whole sea at any given time, scattered across an ocean large enough to lose hurricanes in. They are the pour-crew, the sounders, the keepers of the recipe. They do not call themselves settlers. Settling is precisely what they are forbidden to do.

They come up from the low country by stages, because the air at the top of the world will not receive them all at once. Weeks in pressure camps on the plateau rim while their blood thickens and their breathing slows and learns to work in the thinness. Even acclimatized, they move up here like people perpetually at the top of a long stair climb. They sleep badly. Their lips split. Heavy work is done in short, planned bursts, then they stand with their hands on their knees in the rain, mouths open, throats burning, while the deck hisses under the downpour.

It changes their bodies in smaller ways, too. Their voices go first. On deck everyone shouts — over rain on swell-top, over cataracts falling miles away, over thunder that is not near or far but simply a condition — and below deck no one speaks loudly at all, because the hull carries everything: rain, wave-impact, the deep irregular drumming of collapse and uplift far below. So they develop two voices. A deck-voice, hoarse and flat, stripped of nuance, good for numbers and warnings. And a hull-voice, barely above a murmur. Many of the long-timers have extensive sign, whole conversations struck out with wet gloves against the rain, and hearing that has learned to sort human meaning from continuous noise the way an eye sorts one gray from another gray in fog.

They are kept sterile with a thoroughness that would shame a surgical theater, and this, more than the altitude or the weather, is what makes them native to the place.

Nothing may be introduced before its time. That is the law that governs their lives. Ships are scoured before they rise. Food comes up sealed and irradiated, bland as ship's biscuit and nutrient paste. A beetle found in a crate of gloves is not released, not flicked overboard laughing; it is killed, logged, incinerated, and the crate quarantined. Their own clothes carry no soil. Their boots have never touched a living coast. Even their gut flora is thinned by the diet and the antimicrobials in the desalinated drinking water, until the older crew joke, without quite joking, that they are becoming like the sea itself: full of chemistry, short of life.

They drink the sea only in the way a chemist drinks. Each morning watch, someone leans over the rail with a sampling cup, tastes — a drop on the tongue, spat out — and enters it beside the instrument readings. Blank. Metallic. A little more calcium in the southern gyre this week. Salt rising a fraction in the eastern province, almost where it should be, falling again after rain. They know this unfinished water the way farmers elsewhere know soil by smell, except there is no smell to know. Rain, hot stone, clean mud. They keep, some of them, a private and entirely unscientific vial of old seawater from Below, from a real ocean, and open it now and then below deck just to breathe brine and rot and low tide. Younger crew find it almost unbearably strong. Like opening a room where something has died, and lived, a very long time.

Their work is mostly waiting made technical.

They take soundings that will be wrong next month. They chart an island of steaming, knife-edged basalt, name it for the chart — a grandmother, a lost shipmate, a town in the dust Below — and return in summer to find it split into five teeth, or sunk to a reef that can take a hull off at the waterline. They maintain the invisible machinery they cannot see: seeding a current with mineral packets where a reef is scheduled to one day be possible, correcting a boundary-field buoy at the rim where the sea pours fifty meters off the edge of the world and then curves, steaming, impossibly back into itself. Near the boundary your skin crawls, your back teeth ache and sing, and the rain in front of your face falls sideways in a sheet. The rim-crews are the quietest of all, and the shortest-rotated. They come back with white lines at their wrists where the field has raised the water on their skin in beads that stood up too tall and would not flatten.

And they tend the plantings.

A raft of reed-kelp a thousand kilometers from the next, dark green, town-sized, trembling on the swell and anchored to a drowned spire. Cold moss spreading a hand's width a season across fresh basalt, black until the sun strikes it emerald. A sparse, translucent forest in the shallows, leafless stalks swaying in a current that reversed in the night. The tenders know these places stem by stem. They prune, they re-anchor cable that new rock has cut clean as a knife, they log the exact green of things. They talk to the kelp while they work, out of pure human reflex, and are a little ashamed of it, and do it anyway. Nothing talks back. At dusk they listen, every one of them, every time, for a call across the water that has never once come.

They have no children up here. No graves, either — the dead go Below, because a corpse is an ecosystem and an ecosystem is contamination. No pets, no gardens but the assigned ones, no fishing, because there is nothing to fish for and because the first mouths are being decided elsewhere, in committee, in sequence, years down the recipe. The keepers eat nothing the sea makes. They are not yet permitted to be a link in a food chain. They are a hand that stirs and then withdraws.

Which is why the long-timers are regarded, by the authorities who rotate crews and enforce the quarantine, with a growing and specific unease.

Stay up here five years, seven, ten, breathing rain that has been fixed and edited as it fell, drinking water the nanomachines have handled a thousand times, working waist-deep at the raft edges in cold so thorough it numbs through gloves in minutes — and the place begins, very slightly, to edit back. Water stands on their forearms in high, clear beads. Cuts from the virgin basalt, which should be filthy and are instead eerily clean, knit in strange, glassy seams. Their thinning gut flora does not recover on leave Below the way it should. Some of them stop being able to digest unsterile food at all. Some of them can tell a change in provincial salinity by the prickle at the back of the tongue before the instruments register it. One rim-worker, it's said, could feel an uplift coming an hour out, a slow shrug rising through miles of water, as a pressure in the healed bones of her hands.

They do not become monsters, or prophets, or anything so legible. They become provisional. Like the moss. Like the leafless stalks filtering minerals for a light regime that hasn't stabilized. A first draft of an inhabitant, made mostly by accident out of the only animal that could be persuaded to come up here and be lonely on purpose.

And so there is, increasingly, a split in those low black hulls murmuring through the rain. The rotated crews, who do their seasons, keep their vials of true seawater, and go back Below to thickness of air and the smell of life. And the others, fewer every year and harder to replace, who ask for extension after extension; who name the new islands and stay to watch them drown; who have let the sea make them a little too clean inside, a little too attuned to currents that reverse in the night. Who understand, in a way no one Below seems willing to say aloud, that when the rain finally stops being constant and merely becomes frequent, when the salt arrives and the green rafts tangle and begin to stink like a real shore, when something is at last allowed to eat and be eaten here — they will have to leave.

The finished sea will have no place for its midwives. They were trusted up here precisely because they brought nothing living with them.

Perhaps some will go obediently, back down the continental refusal to the pale dust, and spend the rest of their lives unable to bear the funk and clamor of a living coast. Perhaps some, on the last night of the last rotation, will simply open a hand over the rail — a hand that has killed every stray beetle for a decade — and let something small and winged, smuggled all the way up in a seam, go free into the silver rain.

For now they are only this: a few hundred hoarse, thin-blooded, half-sterilized people in black hulls, spaced farther apart than nations, shouting numbers into a deluge, listening through the hull at night to an ocean being hammered into shape — the first inhabitants of the High Sea, and, if the work goes right, the last ones who will ever have to live in a world with no other life in it.
