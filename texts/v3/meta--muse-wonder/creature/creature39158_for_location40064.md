First you notice that they are not deaf. That would be the easy explanation.

The keeper in the ascent shaft shouts because everyone shouts, up there, from ten in the morning until the evening diminuendo — but when the doors close behind you and the cork vestibule swallows the clatter of the Table, she drops, without thinking about it, to a voice so soft it startles. Intimate as a hand to the ear.

"First day?" she says. "Breathe through your nose. It'll learn."

Natives have learned in the body.

They are short, most of them, and broad in a way that has nothing to do with fat. Big ribcages, developed over generations in thin air, that rise and fall even when they are standing still, as if they are always a little way up a hill. Their necks are thick. Their throats are thick. A Table-born longshouter can put a clean note across a market square in a full wind and hold it while a trunk answers, and then come indoors and whisper over dust-tea as if shouting and whispering were two entirely separate instruments, separately kept in tune.

They keep their ears shut outdoors.

Not with plugs — plugs are for visitors. Their outer ears are small, heavy-lobed, folded close to the skull, the cartilage ridged like the edge of a leaf-plate. By the time a child is six she has learned to tense a muscle downhill children don't have a name for, and seal herself almost shut against the high ringing. What hearing remains to them outside is low and bodily. They hear through their jaws. Through their molars. Through the soles of their feet, if they stand still in their thin-soled indoor boots on the mat. A herd coming home is not a sound to them so much as weather arriving in the ankles. A trunk preparing to lean is a pressure behind the eyes.

Inside, in the cork rooms, they unseal. It is an oddly private gesture, almost tender, and guests are expected not to stare. Freed, their hearing is unnervingly precise. In a city built of double doors and sealed curvatures they live by interior nuances: the change in a kettle of oil as it approaches heat, a filament singing half a tone flat three conduits away, a partner's heart shifting in sleep. They can tell, blindfolded, who has entered a house by how long that person stands in the first vestibule, letting the loud come off them.

Their skin is dry in a way that makes visitors itch in sympathy and then in fact. No sweat that you would recognize. Their bodies have closed that door. What they excrete through the skin is a faint oil, smelling of resin and pepper and hot iron, not unlike the Plant itself; children born on the Table are rubbed with it by their parents' hands until their own glands learn. Their knuckles crack white. Their lips crack, are sealed with amber balm. Their eyes are narrow, not from squinting at brightness — the light is never bright — but from wind, and their lashes are pale with CO2 frost every morning. Many go grey-eyed early. The dim has let their irises widen. In the quiet indoors you see how large their pupils are, how they find faces in what to you is dusk.

They do not waste water upon themselves because they do not, in a cultural sense, believe they contain any. That is the cleanest way to put it, though it is biologically untrue and they will cheerfully correct you, shouting, if you suggest it outdoors: of *course* there's water, locked, bound, triple-skinned inside the stupid bag, but it is nothing to do with you until you die.

Everything fluid is enclosed. Drink is rationed from sealed bulbs and taken, by etiquette, facing a wall. Funerals are freeze-driers, humming for three days behind double airlocks, and profitable with it — a dried elder can be carried out onto the mat and laid down without waking a single pale shoot. Funerals are also the only time Table people are fully, ritually silent outside. They lay the paper-light body down. They walk backwards away from it, heel-toe, heel-toe, making no useful noise at all. Because silence is camouflage and they do not want the Plant to hurry. They want it to take the offering slowly, on its own time, plate by plate.

Children are the opposite. Table children are encouraged to be appalling. They are armed as soon as they can walk with knock-sticks, ribbed gloves, shoes studded with different woods to make different degrees of clap. They learn the pattern-language of the mat by running on it — long knock, short, long means a tunnel clear; a rapid trill through a listening-spike means new growth under a path. They lie flat in the evening, cheek to cork-mat, and shout *through* the ground to a friend two streets away rather than walk it, translating the vibration back up through their teeth. School is mostly music, but not as downhill understands music. It is architecture. It is weather forecasting. It is ethics. A child who cannot hold the street's A-note is not tone-deaf, she is a danger to the neighborhood. She will be loved, and taught by hand on her ribs until she can.

Which is to say the Tuners are not a priesthood set apart, whatever visitors from the shaft think. Everyone tunes. Some just do it at scale.

The ones who walk the mat in resin-hard suits, with listening spikes longer than they are tall, are the closest thing the city has to an aristocracy, and they claim the opposite. They read the score that the node-network revises nightly — not on paper, paper is hung in strings to flutter in the vents and be heard from the kitchen — and they go out and alter the city by a breath here, a closed market vent there, a new thoroughfare opened so the wind will howl another street into usefulness. They prune, and planting means bending a shoot by standing beside it for an hour, drumming a lullaby with soft mallets until it turns.

The oldest of them have silver in them. Not jewelry. Nodes.

It begins, usually, behind the jaw, where the pulse of the network is already felt. A small, beetle-egg bud under the skin that the Plant has learned to grow because for four generations it has grown them in stems and the Tuners have held their heads, hand-tended, against the trunks at night. Not everyone develops them. Those who do hear a little of what the Plant hears: the city as a warm knot of noise in the middle of a huge, slow attention. They become poor indoors. They miss the subsonic creak. They sleep, toward the end, out on sleeping-platforms between trunks, oil-sealed against the cold, ears open, smiling occasionally at a sound miles off that walks through them root to root faster than air.

Do not mistake this for worship. Worship is too quiet. Table people argue with the Plant, incessantly, instrumentally, at top volume in market squares designed to roar it away from the stalls. They curse it when a new bud loves the smell of a kitchen and starts splitting the wall to get closer to the people's warm noise. They cheat it with empty houses built only to clap and ring and lure growth out of the suburbs. They depend, with a clear and unsentimental biology, on its components growing inside their machines — and now inside a few of their bodies — and they know it depends on their combined racket for direction. It is less a god than a vast, dry, deaf-toward-silence spouse. You keep it from smothering you by never quite shutting up.

It has made them blunt. There is no room for insinuation in a culture that has to be understood through wind. Irony outdoors is impossible; sarcasm is a safety hazard. Love songs, marriage proposals, apologies, municipal complaints — all delivered fortissimo, face tipped into the scour, hands spread so the gestures will read at distance. Which means the tenderest things they say, they say indoors, in this other voice they keep separate, in rooms lined so thoroughly that you can hear cloth move. A visitor who only meets them on the street thinks them joyous and brutal. A visitor who is invited past the second airlock understands that public loudness *is* privacy. Everyone is shouting; no one can possibly be overheard.

Their food tastes of smoke and salt and oil because it must: grazer meat cured without a drop, leaf-plate milled to a black flour, beetle resin tapped by rhythm and traded as sweets, blocks of mineral salts the grazers and humans alike lick to make up for a world quarried from stone. They eat communally because a kitchen is a loud, attractive thing and should be anchored by many voices. They burp into their sleeves. They spit into sealed cups at their belts with the automatic delicacy of snuff-takers. Babies are taught the cup before they are taught the street note, and both by song.

At the one un-walled hour, when the wind drops past the rim and every plate on the Table settles and rings itself out in that long traveling diminuendo, they pour into the streets in their indoor clothes. This is not leisure, though it looks like a festival. It is maintenance of the self. For an hour they can speak at ordinary volume and be heard by unaided, unsealed ears; they can hear their children exist without drumming; they can hear each other swallow, laugh without projecting, quarrel honestly. Old people time their confessions to that hour. Teenagers time everything to it. Then the ticking underfoot starts up, small and cool, and one by one the houses reseal.

If you stay long enough they will stop shouting *at* you and start shouting *for* you, angling their bodies to make a lee, cupping a hand to throw the words along the wind, interpreting the Trunks back to you as if translating from a relative. If you stay longer still they will leave a sealed bulb by your bed without comment when your lips split, and teach you the muscle that closes your ears, and not look when you practice unsealing them indoors.

And if you ask them — indoors, so you can say it properly, so it is not ripped away and turned into performance — whether they ever want to leave, to go downhill where it rains on people's actual faces, they are puzzled in a way that feels rehearsed until you realize it isn't.

They dream of rain nightly. All of them. The Plant's old forbidden memory comes up through the floor of the one animal they live inside, and their own older memory answers. They wake dry-eyed, dry-mouthed, strangely rested. Then the frost ticks off the roofs, the plates begin to lift, and they go cheerfully out into a land with no water and no quiet, to shout the forest away from the door and to be, in their bodies and their cities and their songs, the noise it leans toward.
