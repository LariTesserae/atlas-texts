At first you assume no one does.

The sterility crews come up in rotation, three at a time, and go down again before their skin has even pruned properly. You have seen the roster in the gondola. You have seen the lockers, the row of thin wetsuits hanging dripping by the airlock, hooks numbered, towels folded. Everything accounted for. And the brief is explicit in the way briefs are when they are trying not to sound strange: no permanent habitation, no food brought inside the wet volume, no animal life to be introduced, however inadvertently.

Then you start noticing the exceptions.

One wetsuit is not on its hook. Not once, when someone is out in the water, but always. Its place is empty and the concrete under it is dry in a building where nothing else ever dries.

There is a cup on the upper gallery, dented tin, set down on a control cabinet that the moss has claimed. Not left — kept. Rinsed. Turned upside down against the condensation rain. Beside it, pruning shears laid blade-in on a strip of hydroponic felt so they won't scratch the paint. The handles are warm.

Cuttings in the nave are not all drifting where the slow current left them. Some are gathered. Kelp ribbons trimmed from the intake grilles are bundled by length and wedged, neat as laundry, into the mangrove roots to soften and break down. Fern fronds blocking a sight-glass have been folded back, not cut, and tied with a twist of their own stem. A channel through a thicket that was closed last month is open again, but not in the straight line an engineer would choose. It curves. It gives the new growth room. It is the shape of someone who passes this way often and has learned to compromise.

You look, after that, for a person, and for a while you find only a disturbance in the place itself.

A slow widening in the kelp where something has just gone through, too low and too unhurried for a crew member on a task list. Bubbles that are not from the aeration lines, rising irregularly from behind a turbine casing, pausing, resuming. On the surface gallery, among the mangrove crowns, a hammock of woven mineral mat strung between a pipe bridge and a trunk — only it isn't quite a hammock, because its lower edge trails in the water, and green has begun to grow through it, and every morning, you realize, someone has to cut themselves free of it, gently, a few white rootlets at a time.

She lives at the waterline.

That is the first true thing about her. Not above it in the steamy air, not below it in the bright green volume, but where the two meet and keep changing places across the twenty-centimeter tide.

She was intake crew once — is still, on some manifest that no one has bothered to correct — a broad-shouldered woman in her late fifties with close-cropped grey hair that she shears shorter with the pruning scissors, and forearms mapped with the pale, bloodless lines where fronds and cable ties and old gasket edges have pressed into skin kept too long underwater. She wears the missing wetsuit cut down to a vest and shorts, patched not with neoprene but with felt, with ceramic bead sewn in little pockets at the seams for weight. When she is working low in the nave she goes without it entirely, and then she is harder to see and somehow less startling: just another large, slow shape moving through the shafts of light, dappled by caustics, her skin filmed in places with the same olive algae that velvets the turbine casings.

She has learned the building the way the plants have. By interval and obstruction.

She does not swim, exactly. Swimming implies intent to cross water. She drifts and pulls, hand over hand along the guide ropes, along pipes that have become branches and branches that have become pressure lines, kicking once in a long while with a whole-body undulation that barely stirs the particulate. She can stay down — not impossibly long, not freakishly, just longer than the crews are comfortable timing, because she has slowed everything in herself to match the place. Her breathing, when she surfaces under the crane rail among the dripping leaves, is not a gasp. It is a resumption. Her ears clear. She listens a moment with her head half out, cocked toward the heat exchanger's tick, toward the hourly valve, and only then does she haul herself up onto a catwalk that vanishes into fern.

She is the animal the system was designed to exclude, and she has taken up, one by one, all the jobs its absence left empty.

She grazes. That is not a metaphor down there. She eats the young kelp she prunes, the tender fern tips, the waxy submerged flowers after the brushes have done their clumsy weekly pass and she has done a better one by hand — stroking pollen loose in those fine visible puffs, moving it blossom to blossom on her fingertips, letting it hang around her like dust in sun. She eats slowly, underwater sometimes, surfacing to chew among the mangrove leaves with the unselfconscious patience of something that has nowhere to hurry to. Because of her the water is no longer sterile. It cannot be. Her gut, her skin, the warm creases of her, have seeded this summit sea with a microbiology it was never supposed to have, and the plants, first- and second-generation and unchecked by any other mouth, have responded to her as to a season finally arriving.

And she rots things, in a place where nothing had yet learned how. Cuttings tucked into root masses. A dropped glove left deliberately on the bottom grating until it softens. Her own shed hair caught in the filtration racks. Small, warm, animal processes, introduced at the exact pace the warm water can bear.

The crews know. That is clear once you have seen her, from the way they whisper on the surface gallery even when she is twenty meters away under the turbines, from the extra towel that gets folded and left, from the manifest that continues to show a rotation with her name always in the next gondola down, and the next, and the next. She tends what they can only maintain. She clears sensors by touch in silt-clouds that blind the cameras. She knows which pump's long minute of stillness is rest and which is beginning to fail, because she rests her teeth against its casing and feels the hum. She knows where the floor plan has changed this week because a trunk has completed its grip around a coolant pipe and the pipe now sings at a different pitch. When engineers say the tree is now part of the pressure system, she is the one who told them, surfacing long enough to say it in a voice gone soft and hoarse from disuse and humidity: *Listen. It's carrying.*

At night — for there is a kind of night even under glass, outside wind worrying the towers, light gone blue and then gone — she sleeps in the cut-free hammock with her body submerged to the ribs and her head pillowed above the falling tide-line, so the artificial sea breathes up and down across her belly in its slow cycle, covering and uncovering a band of slick skin, a row of small scars, a fringe of rootlets that have mistaken her, not unkindly, for infrastructure. Condensation rains on her face. She wakes without startling. She cuts the night's new growth away from her wrists.

At noon, when the whole interior is at its most itself, you may not find her at all, and that is when she is most completely living there: not a figure in the water but a function of it, a warmth moving through warmth, a slow shadow rearranging the kelp by millimeters, a hand trailing along a boiler seam where a flower is growing, checking — the way you would check a sleeping child's forehead — whether it has opened yet, underwater, pale and waxy, into the bright, silent, crowded green.
