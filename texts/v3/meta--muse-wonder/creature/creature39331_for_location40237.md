You would not see them from the rim. You would not see them for a long time after coming down, either, and not because they are hiding.

From above they are part of the packed glitter: another wet pillar, another dark shoulder of stone with white mineral dried on it, another rust seam, another false green-blue bloom where the copper has stained something that is not quite rock. Up close they still are. It is only after an hour down among the slots, when your mind has begun to edit the roar out and the world has gone strangely silent while everything visibly shakes, that you begin to notice a few of the stones are shaking differently.

They live low. Knee-high to a person at the shoulder, sometimes less, and built wide in the wrong direction: narrow when seen from the front, so they can turn sideways and ease through a gap a body wide, but deep through the chest and hips, heavy-boned, set close to the ground on short legs that end not in paws so much as hands. The feet splay. The toes are broad, blunt, rough underneath, and they place them the way you might place a palm to feel a wall — flat, deliberate, testing. They are always touching. Two feet in a cold run, one forefoot pressed to a pillar, tail — thick, flattened, the color of wet ash — braced in the current behind them. If one contact lifts, another has already taken its place.

Because for them the noise is not noise. It is the medium they see in.

They have ears, but not on the outside of their heads. No flap, no opening the spray could drive into. The skull is thick and low, the jaw broad, and the hearing is in the bone itself: through the soles, up the legs, through the teeth when they rest their chin to stone, as they often do, quite still, with a look you would first mistake for exhaustion. Near the main channels the rock hums at a low note you can feel in your hand. In the higher jumbles at the edges the note rises, thinner. To them that difference is a landscape. Pitch is distance. Pitch is depth, current speed, a hollow under an arch, a loose slab, another body standing round a corner with its own feet pressed down and sending itself through the ground.

The constant roar illuminates the valley for them the way the sun illuminates it for you — evenly, relentlessly, without shadow except where a pillar blocks the vibration and leaves a quiet lee behind it. That is why they do not leave. At the rim, where the sound at last becomes something the valley is doing rather than the condition of the world, they would be half blind.

Their actual eyes are small and pale, set under a ridge, filmed over as if with a permanent bead of water. They seem to register glare more than shape: light stone against dark, sun against slot-shadow, and perhaps the stationed rainbows, which hold their place hour after hour in the spray and shift only a hand's breadth by afternoon. Fixed colors in fixed places. Landmarks, for anything that needs them. Their skin makes its own landmarks and then erases them. Wet black along the belly and throat where the water sheets over them. White crust across the shoulders where the sun dries them while they stand. Copper blooming blue-green in the creases of the neck and behind the legs, bright enough that from twenty feet away you would swear something mossy had gotten a hold there. Nothing has. It is metal out of the water, gathered in them, shed in flakes when the crust gets too thick, and gathered again.

They are wet and dry at once, held, the way everything here is held.

They eat slowly and without leaving their station. In the quieter side-pools the amber mats grow, opalescent, slightly gelatinous, grazing on dissolved stone — and the mats regrow in exactly the same shape because these animals graze them in exactly the same reach, tongues rough as stone rasps, licking round the edge of what one body can reach without lifting its feet from a known vibration. They pick drowned insects from foam with two fingers, with a finch's indifference. They do not drink from the main channels if they can help it, where the water runs too mineral and scouring; they lick condensation off shaded rock faces, bead by bead, in the bright sun, or put their mouths to the weep where a pillar sweats cold through its own shadow.

Nothing here sings to be heard, and they do not either. They have voices, buried deep enough that what comes out is more thud than sound, felt in the sternum of another animal standing close. Mostly they speak through stone. A forefoot lifts and comes down, not stamped — nothing here needs to stamp — but settled with a particular weight, and the note of the pillar changes under it. Another answers two slabs away without turning its head. Sometimes two will come together in a slot so narrow they must arrive sideways, and press forehead to forehead, or both foreheads to the same wet wall between them, and stay like that while the mist makes and remakes itself at waist height around their legs. To a watcher it just looks like resting. The droplets jumping on the pool beside them jump in a slightly altered pattern.

The young begin in the pockets the current forgot. Under a ledge in a sunlit backwater, the color of ash under water, motionless, skin beaded, external gills fine as wet thread in a place where the stream only sighs through a crack. What you might take for a salamander, and is gone without a ripple you can separate from the other ripples, is them before the bone thickens. The ones that survive come out heavier each season, less and less aquatic, more and more of the boulder field. They learn a channel the way light learns it: by following a slightly warmer run against their legs, by knowing which pillar sings up and which sings down, by keeping to the stations where the spray crosses at the right angle and the food films pearl again after being scraped away.

They sleep wedged. In the drier — only slower-dripping — jumbles near the edges they back into a narrowing between slabs until the stone takes some of their weight, and then they stop. Upright, eyes filmed, feet spread, vibrationally awake even then, because to sleep out of contact would be to sleep in darkness. The wind cut to a constant tone through the slots moves their damp flank-hair, which is not quite hair and not quite a fringe of skin, and does not dry it.

Aging is encrustation. The white spreads down from the shoulders. The copper deepens in the joints. They shift their station less often, and then only a hand's breadth, like the rainbows. An old one is almost indistinguishable from the leaning slabs it has settled among, interlocked at the base, rounded by water, humming at the pitch of its own pillar. When it dies — wedged, usually, in the place it slept — the current does its patient work. Nothing loose is allowed to stay. What is soft is carried away. What is mineral remains a while longer among the stones, unnoticed, one more shoulder in the pack.

They are not visitors here, and they are not hiding in exception to the valley. They are what the valley produces when it is left to be itself long enough: deaf to the roar because they hear by it, blind to the glare because they see through feet and jaw and skin, at home in water too cold and too charged to settle in because their bodies have become a slower, denser kind of current moving through it. Motion as a state, not an event, with a pulse inside it.

Climb back to the rim at evening, when the white stone goes gold and the shadows pool blue-black in the slots and the sound still does not deepen, and they will already have resumed their place in the field below you. Thousands of wet pillars. Thousands of white threads. And among them, uncountable because unseparated, the low grey lives going on touching the world to find out where it is.
