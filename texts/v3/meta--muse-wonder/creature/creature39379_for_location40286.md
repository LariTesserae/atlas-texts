At first you think the inhabitants are the people.

You see them at dawn, which is when they are most visible: water-carriers moving through the alleys with jars balanced and both hands free, herders coaxing goats out between the prickly-pear fences, porters with bundles of reed, grain, clay pots, cloth, all of them flowing around each other in passages that look too narrow for one body, let alone two passing. Nobody pushes. Nobody raises a voice to say *move*. A shoulder turns, a bundle tilts, a donkey waits half a second with one ear cocked, and the stream divides and rejoins.

They are built, or have built themselves, for conservation. Not of energy exactly — they work enormously hard — but of anything that would be lost to the open air. Water first, then sound, then temper, then motion in the heat. Their walk is soft and flat-footed on the sand streets, unhurried even when it is fast, the walk of people who know that running raises dust and dust raises thirst. Their gestures are small and close to the body. When they talk across a courtyard they do not shout; when they talk across a street they come closer first. It is startling, standing among millions of them, how near everyone sounds.

Their clothes are loose, pale, layered against a light that does not so much shine as occupy: undyed cotton gone cream with washing, lime-white wraps, ochre and dusty rose overshirts the color of their own walls, head cloths wound to shade the neck and leave the face in a movable pool of shadow. Skin at wrist and ankle is darkened and fine-cracked by sun and clay dust; hands are stained in bands — white from limewash, red from mud brick, green at the fingertips from crushed moringa and fig leaf. Many go barefoot indoors and in the alleys near home, and their feet know the ground the way other people's eyes know a page: where the sand over a buried cistern stays a fraction cooler, where a courtyard threshold has domed up a thumb's width since spring, where the street has gone soft over a place the old lakebed is still exhaling damp.

They are long-distance lookers. Raised on ground with nothing in the way, they read the basin the way sailors read water. A child of seven can tell you whether that white column twenty kilometers off is dust or rain failing to reach the ground, whether a cloud building over the western quarters will travel or dissolve, whether the warm wind at evening will come strong enough to sleep on the roof without a reed screen. Their eyes rest naturally at the horizon. Indoors, in the dim green shade of a courtyard, they blink and adjust slowly, and prefer it.

What forms them earliest is not speech. It is the cup.

Before a child can be trusted to carry water, she can be trusted not to spill it, which is different. She learns to fill from the cistern mouth in the courtyard floor by ladle, in counted dips. She learns that rinse-water from greens goes to the aloe, that water from washing grain goes to the sunken beds, that the last cloudy mouthful in a jar goes around the stem of something, never onto open earth — pouring water on bare ground is the one waste that makes adults actually stop a child's hand, gently, every time. She learns the sound a full cistern makes when you tap its sealed mouth, and the hollower sound in the third dry month. She learns which well her quarter draws from, what its daily measure is, why no one — not the well-keeper's own family, not a traveler, not a rich household with many jars — takes the last of it. The deep damp needs the night to gather again through clay that is still deciding what it is. A person who took the last measure would not be punished so much as remembered, which in a place with no center and no anonymity is worse.

By the time she is grown she carries an invisible map that has nothing to do with streets. The alleys rearrange themselves as houses slump and are remade; roofs change hands nightly in the hot season; markets rotate by weekday from square to square so that cloth and peppers and salt walk themselves through the city. None of that is confusing. What she navigates by is trees and wells and gardens. The baobab where her great-grandmother's generation tied goats. The fig by the cistern that three houses share the fruit of and take turns re-sealing the crack for. The moringa that belongs, in the only sense that matters, to grandchildren not yet born — the woman who planted it may prune it hard for leaves every season, but she would no more cut a living branch in the dry months than she would lift a neighbor's jar, and neither would anyone else. Shade is different. Shade belongs to whoever is standing in it, arrived a moment ago, a stranger, a dog. You do not ask. You move over, because the tree's owner is the future but its shadow is the present, and the present is for whoever is hot in it.

That distinction runs through everything they make. A house is private at its heart and public at its top: the courtyard, where the cistern sleeps under the floor and children sleep in the hot season's first hours and the cooking fire makes its brief careful smoke, is never entered uninvited. But the roof belongs to the night and the neighborhood. Families sleep up there under stars so dense the sky looks granular, and a stranger crossing from roof to roof at midnight, stepping over the low parapets, murmuring a greeting to the shapes lying in the dark, is ordinary traffic. In the morning the same roof is a work floor again — grain spread to dry, peppers reddening, laundry, weaver birds arriving in ribbons to steal exactly the amount of theft the household has already budgeted for.

For they do keep budgets for other species. This is where the first idea — that the inhabitants are the people — quietly fails.

The geckos are not in the houses. They are *of* the houses: pale, translucent patrols on every interior wall, stationed by the lamp niches, snapping moths out of the warm air in movements too fast to look like effort. Nobody feeds them and nobody would dream of clearing one off a wall; a room's gecko population is commented on the way other places comment on a roof — a good house has them, holds them, passes them on. Cats hold hereditary tenure in particular granaries and defend them with a seriousness that is respected in law-adjacent ways; the kittens of the granary cat are spoken for before they open their eyes. Dogs sleep in the precise center of alleys, in the deepest shade-line, and the entire traffic of a crowded city steps over them without breaking rhythm or sentence. To move a sleeping dog you would have to have a reason. No one, most days, has a reason.

Donkeys know the well schedule better than some of their drivers and plant themselves accordingly. Pigeons keep the hours of the grain roofs. Bees hold the fig trees until nine and yield them to the bulbuls by an arrangement that predates everyone alive and is simply observed, the way you observe that the market is in the next square on Thursdays. At dusk the swifts give the sky to the bats coming out of the baobab hollows — no fuss, no collision, a changing of the guard over millions of roofs — and the bats take the insects that rise off the gardens in the first cool, and below them the cats take the rats that come for the gardens' leavings, and the whole relay runs nightly without a single participant needing to understand it.

Even the eaters of the city are inhabitants of a kind. Termites are forever consuming the oldest beams from inside, hollowing the skeleton the city walks on, and are forever being smoked out by specialists who can hear, tapping, which wood is still honest. The beams are replaced, the smoked insects feed chickens, the chickens feed courtyards, and the people describe this not as an infestation but as the city digesting itself at the correct speed. Everything here is on a maintenance schedule measured in lives: walls re-mudded, beams renewed, cisterns re-sealed, gardens inherited intact by neighbors when a house comes down, because a living garden kept alive costs almost nothing and a dead one costs five years of saved water to raise again. A garden, therefore, is never demolished. It is fostered. People will tend a plot of prickly pear and moringa and sunken greens for an absent household for a decade, eating the yield, keeping the roots wet, and hand it back when someone builds on the plot again, with the accounting of those years done entirely from memory and considered binding.

Their work, seen whole, is the work of keeping a wet thing alive in a dry place by never letting the wetness gather anywhere it can be lost. So they are plumbers of cupfuls, gardeners of shade, masons of a ground that will not sit still. There are well-tenders who can taste the deep water and say how the clay is recharging. There are women and men who know which of forty greens can be kept producing on rinse-water alone and which must be allowed to die back and be mourned for a season. There are fig specialists, cistern divers, roof carpenters who can lift a settling beam and reset it true by eye, granary-cat negotiators — these are not quite trades and not quite family roles, but a person becomes the one who is good at it and then is fetched, alley by alley, at need.

They eat accordingly: grain ground soft and rhythmic, stone on stone, heard through two courtyards at midday rest; goat milk still warm; dried dates from camel trains that cross the plain with salt and pots and leave with cloth; peppers dried on their own roofs; greens by the handful, bitter and iron-dark, cooked fast over fires that are lit, used, and out again before the sun is fully up, because a fire after nine is water spent from every body standing near it. Feasts, when the three or four true rains come and the alleys run ankle-deep and children are allowed — encouraged — to shout, are less about food than about the sound of abundance: jars filling, gutters running, the whole city briefly loud in a way it cannot afford to be the rest of the year, and then dry by evening, smelling of wet dust, everyone already re-counting.

By midday they vanish. That is the truest portrait: millions of people, and the streets empty, shutters closed, markets cloth-covered, only donkeys standing in the thin shade with their ears rotating, a bustard perhaps striding down the noon street like an official who knows everyone is home. Inside, in rooms cooled ten degrees by vine and courtyard tree, they rest in the practical silence they have made together, and the city does the quiet half of its living — grain digesting in cats' bellies, water settling in cisterns, leaves transpiring their cupfuls into a humidity just thick enough for the geckos to begin gathering toward, termites chewing in the dark of the beams, roots finding the cracks beside the stored rain and widening them by a hair.

They are not a quiet people by temperament. They laugh on roofs at night at a volume carefully fitted to not wake the roof two houses over. They argue about markets, trees, marriages, well-measures, with real heat — at close range, in courtyards, where the mud walls will keep it. They simply live in a place that has taxed every excess out of them over generations and returned it as attention. They hear a wooden well-bell muffled by design two alleys away and know whose draw it is. They smell the evening wind come off the cooling clay and can pick out, in the single household smell of the whole basin settling — dust, resin, woodsmoke, goat milk, crushed leaf, hot limewash cooling — whose cooking, whose garden, whose quarter.

And at night they climb to the roofs and lie down in the open, in the semi-public dark, above courtyards that are sealed and private below them, and look up at a sky unlit by any glow of theirs. From far off, their city is only a darkness denser than the plain. But it is a darkness with a texture: breathing, rustling leaves, soft hooves, a child turning over, a gecko chirping from a wall, bats working the air between the baobabs. Life packed into life, human and otherwise, each keeping the other alive by agreement and by appetite, going on quietly in the warm — not separate populations sharing a place, but one population, many-shaped, that the new dry land took in all at once, and that learned, together, how to stay.
