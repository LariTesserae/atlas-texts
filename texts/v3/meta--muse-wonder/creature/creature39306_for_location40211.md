Almost nothing, if you go looking for it in the usual way.

The shrimp are real. You can find them if you kneel long enough beside one of the still pools, until the sky reflected in the water stops seeming empty and your eyes learn the difference between a mineral edge and something feeling along it. Translucent, eyeless, no longer than a thumbnail, they graze the black and amber mats at the pool margins with a patience that makes even here seem hurried. They never swim out into open water. They never leave the rim they were born on.

The moths are real too, after a fashion. At dusk they work the warm air at the basin's edge, rising on one vent's exhalation and sinking in the cool pocket behind its cap, rising and sinking, as if tethered to a place they cannot quite occupy. They do not feed here. Nothing flowers. They are probably only using the heat the way a swimmer uses a current — to stay aloft a little longer for a little less — before the night takes them back to the ordinary country outside the rim.

And the bacterial mats, the rust filaments trembling in water too hot for a hand, the black crusts in the shade under the slabs: those live. Slowly, chemically, without anything you could call a face.

But none of them is the resident. They are too small for the basin, and too separate from it. The shrimp do not explain why a footprint heals by evening. The mats do not explain why a chimney broken in one shape regrows in that same shape, weeks later, cauliflower lump for cauliflower lump. The moths do not keep time.

You have to stop looking *on* the floor of the basin, and start listening *through* it.

It rings hollow under your boots in places. That is not a figure of speech. There are chambers down there, and connecting tubes, and narrow throats, and reservoirs where hot water gathers until the pressure is exactly sufficient and no more. The geology of the place made the first rough version of that plumbing — water and heat and fractured rock will always find channels. But channels erode. They silt up. They widen unevenly, choke, collapse, wander.

These have not wandered.

The throats below the great vents are smooth in a way water alone does not smooth things. They are lined. If you could get a light down one in the second after a discharge, in that ringing pocket of quiet behind the fallen cap, you would not see bare stone. You would see a pale, wet surface that contracts, very slightly, away from the sudden coolness of the air. Translucent, like the shrimp, but not separate from the walls. It is the wall, for as deep as the light goes. In the narrower passages it meets itself in the middle and has to squeeze past its own body to let the water through. In the reservoirs it hangs in heavy folds, digesting the heat out of the stone.

It is one animal. Or one colony so old the distinction has stopped meaning anything. Twelve kilometers from rim to rim is not the size of its territory. It is, roughly, the size of it.

Everything you took for landscape on the surface is either its skeleton or its skin. The cracked plates are scutes it lays down from beneath, silica-rich and iron-dark, each generation secreted into the crack left by the last. When the sun warms them and they split along the same fine seams, that is not failure; that is how it flexes without tearing. At night the condensed steam carries dissolved stone into the splits and it sets them again, the way a body sets a hairline fracture, overnight, for ten thousand nights running. A footprint is a shallow bruise. By noon it has glazed the bruise over. By evening it has forgotten there was weight there at all, because forgetting is what the surface is *for*.

The chimneys are its breathing pores, grown up around the vents where its interior comes closest to the sky.

This is why each one wears a cap. The slab of basalt is not part of the animal — it is a piece of the old roof of the world, older than the animal, that happened to be lying over a pore when the pore opened. A lesser creature would have been smothered by it. This one incorporated it. The rim of each chimney has grown up to meet its slab in a close, irregular seal, ground by lifting and falling into something like a joint. Underneath, the soft lining of the throat gathers pressure — not only steam pressure, although the steam is real, but the slow muscular pressure of the body below deciding, chamber by chamber, when to breathe.

*Lift, breathe, fall.* The clap you feel through your feet is a valve closing.

Each pore keeps its own period because each is breathing at the depth of its own lung. The small bright vent near the eastern pools, firing every nine seconds, is a shallow chamber close under the crust, quick to fill, quick to empty, fed by a short straight throat the animal keeps scoured clean. The huge one with the orange mineral wool around its mouth labors for a minute because its reservoir is far down and vast, and because the throat that serves it was narrowed — deliberately, incrementally, over centuries — until the discharge would come at the pressure the deeper body required and not a breath sooner. It is not that the animal never speeds up or slows down. It is that it corrects. A hotter season, a shift in the deep water, a partial blockage of fallen sinter: the lining thickens here, relaxes there, lays down a fraction more silica on a lip that is firing a fraction too early, until the interval comes home again. Stability this perfect is not the absence of change. It is change working full-time, in both directions, to cancel itself out.

It has no eyes and no ears, and it has never needed either. The steam sheet diffuses the light to a directionless glow at ground level; there is nothing up there worth seeing. The sound of its own breathing would deafen any ear it grew within a day. What it has instead is the whole underside of the basin floor as a single sensitive surface. Vibration arrives through stone with perfect fidelity. It knows the difference between a cap falling true and a cap falling cracked by the ring of it. It knows rain on the rim country — rare, and useless to it, since rain arrives cold and carrying the wrong minerals — as a faint irregular patter at the very edge of itself, like fingertips on a distant wall. It knows your walk within minutes of your descent. Not your weight: the *pattern* of it. Two pressures, alternating, moving in a line, pausing by pools. Nothing else in the basin moves like that. The birds do not land. The moths have no step. It cannot see your hair lift in the static before a discharge, but it can feel the fine tremor in the air through the open pore, and if experienced visitors learn to step back at that warning, it is because, in its own slow way, the animal learned first to hesitate — a fraction of a second, no more — when something warm and solid is standing too close to a mouth that is about to exhale.

It does not eat, in any sense you would recognize. The bacterial mats are not its food; they are its external digestion, an gut turned outward into the pools where the chemistry is too hot and too changeable for its own tissue. The mats break down gases rising through the wet stone — sulfurous, metallic, faintly oily — into forms the lining of the runnels can take up as the water percolates back down. The shrimp tend the mats simply by grazing them, and the animal, in return, keeps the pool levels even and the temperatures survivable, admitting a breath of cooler condensate here, throttling a hot upwelling there. Whether it domesticated the shrimp or the shrimp domesticated a corner of it is a question that stopped having an answer a very long time ago. The shrimp may even be its young, or its detached and wandering organs, sent up into the light too small to harm and too blind to leave. They share the same translucency. They share the same indifference to the sun.

It produces nothing. That is the deepest truth of it, and the thing hardest to stand next to. A factory makes something other than itself. A landscape is made by something other than itself. This makes only the conditions under which it can go on being made. Stone dissolved below is stone deposited above, in chimney and plate and the tiny salt feathers of the mineral gardens, which are not gardens at all but the animal's excess silica set out to cool where the spray lands. Heat comes in from the deep earth and heat goes out in the plumes, flattened by the northwest wind into that low luminous sheet — a breath made visible, spread thin, given back its water at the far side of the basin so the whole system can drink it again. Nothing accumulates. Nothing is exported. Even its waste heat is accounted for: it is what keeps the moths up at dusk, what keeps the dawn fog forming in the same band at the same height, what makes the compass needle tremble with a faint electric unease as the charged steam discharges through the magnetite sand along pathways its body has used so long they have become, effectively, nerves.

It must be very old. Not old as a tree is old, in rings. Old as a habit is old. The tube system beneath the basin has been worn and re-worn, lined and re-lined, so many times that the animal's present shape is less a body it grew than a shape it remembers — the same way the chimney regrows, the same way the cracks reseal in their old places, because the water arrives at the same pressure carrying the same dissolved stone and the memory is stored not in anything like a mind but in the geometry of the passages themselves. Damage it and it does not improvise a new design. It restores the old one with a patience that looks, from outside, exactly like geology.

It has probably never reproduced. Once in a great while the wind must fail or falter — once in centuries, once in longer than that — and a plume goes up straight instead of sideways, and spores or fragments or whatever it sheds in its exhalations are carried over the rim instead of being condensed faithfully back into itself. Somewhere downwind there may be warm, wet, mineral ground where a piece of it could take hold and begin the million-year work of hollowing itself a basin. Or there may not. It behaves as though there will not be. It keeps nothing in reserve. All of its effort is maintenance, and maintenance, perfected, is indistinguishable from stillness.

So when you climb back to the rim in the evening and the sound stops dead at the ridge line — level, level, level, then gone, as if a door had closed — it is not that the noise was sealed in. It is that you have stepped off the body of the thing that was making it, out of the shallow drum of its back, where every breath arrives through the stone. Behind you the caps go on lifting and falling in the haze, pore after pore, in their separate, unvarying times. Ahead of you is the warm quiet country where things are born and die and leave ruins.

Nothing in the basin will be different tomorrow. That is not because nothing is alive there.

That is how you can tell something is.
