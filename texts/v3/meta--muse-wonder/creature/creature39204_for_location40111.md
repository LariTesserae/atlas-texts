The people of Sallowmere are, to a visitor first meeting them on the rim road, disappointingly ordinary. And then, after an hour down in the bowl with them, not ordinary at all — not in anything they have become, but in everything they do without noticing they are doing it.

They walk differently. On the boardwalk they walk in the middle, light-footed, listening through their boots. Off it they pick a line between root-knots the way townspeople elsewhere pick a line between puddles, never quite looking down. They duck in places where there is nothing overhead yet — a cableway wire taken down last week, a stem that will be there next week. They step *over* at ankle height without breaking conversation. Their children do it before they can properly read. School in summer term is half lessons and half going out in a crocodile with hooks on their backs, blunt for the littlest, to learn which pale jointed thickness under the leaf-mould is a feeder you may trim and which, whitewashed and numbered, is Arterial and must not even be nicked. A cut in the wrong place is not vandalism in Sallowmere. It is plumbing. It will make taps sigh air three streets away in the Terraces, and everyone will know whose child, or whose new son-in-law from outside, did it.

They are a pale, damp-handed people, not sickly but shade-kept. The sun in the bowl arrives as coins through leaves, and for most of the working day they are under that even, green-grey light, with their sleeves rolled to the elbow and their forearms mapped in fine, pale scars where a stem-edge has drawn across. Their fingernails are stained dark at the quick with bitter sap that soap lightens but never quite removes, and they smell — their coats, their hair, the inside of their houses — faintly medicinal, of crushed stem and wet iron and tarred canvas. Newcomers find they go on smelling it on their own luggage for days after they have left.

They dress for the morning rain that is not rain. Broad hats, not so much waterproof as drip-shedding, with a little spout at the brim evolved over generations. Boots that are expected to come up dark. Coats hung by the door on pegs that are moved, year to year, as the house drifts a few centimetres on its raft and the coat begins to brush the jamb. Nobody in Sallowmere leaves a door unlatched, and nobody thinks this has anything to do with locks. A house that has thickened on one corner in the night will present you, at six o'clock, with a door swung wide and a floor taking the drip, drip, drip from the leaf-cups overhead, six until nine, while the barrels fill and someone goes along the boardwalk in the wet light tapping pipes and reading meters as calmly as a postman.

Everybody cuts. That is the first social fact. The clerks from the Top End, where the air is driest and the Co-op and the school stand, the pressmen from the rock at the centre, the grandmothers in the Terraces whose gardens are not gardens at all but boxed planters of imported soil up on legs, safe from the sap — all of them owe hours, and all of them are on a roster painted up in the town hall beside the great root-map. The map is in ink and in pencil and in everyone's head besides, and it is always slightly wrong. Winter evenings are for arguing it right again.

So their work is not really separable from their housekeeping. A Sallowmerer mending a cable will, as a matter of course, cut back the fronds slowing the drip in a gutter. A woman hanging washing — which will take two days to dry, and will smell green when it comes in — will pull a new curling fist of leaf away from the line with one hand while she pegs with the other. Two men lifting and re-laying a bowed section of boardwalk will stop, feel a creak travel up through the screw-pile, and wait a moment with their hands still on the board, not alarmed, just attending, the way you attend to a sleeping animal shifting under a blanket. They hear the town growing the way other people hear weather. At night it ticks in the pipes and settles in the rafts under their beds, and they sleep through it. They would sleep badly, most of them, in a truly still house on stone.

They are quiet, because the bowl is. The leaves take sound and do not give it back, so no one has learned to shout across a distance. Voices stay close. Gossip stays close, too, carried along the boardwalks at walking pace, trolley bell ringing once at a crossing, the Press House thumping not in the air but in the soles of your feet. Visitors remark on the lack of birdsong and expect the people to mind; mostly they don't, or they mind in the abstract, the way you might mind never having seen the sea. A heron in a glade is news. Moths in August are a week-long festival nobody organises. A moorhen nesting by the outflow gets its name in the shop window.

They eat imported and boxed things — potatoes and greens coaxed out of clean soil in planters, tea and paraffin and tinned stuff from the rim railway — and they are fierce about tea, because tea tastes of somewhere dry. They send out board and pellets and paper stock and terrible preservative on that railway, and are vaguely puzzled when outsiders think that is the point. Those are leftovers. The product is the glade around the Press House, the space under a roof, the boardwalk kept level for another month. The clearing they stand in. It took a hundred and fifty years to make, and it has to be made again tomorrow morning, at ten centimetres a day.

That gives them a particular temper. They are not pioneers and they are not custodians of a wilderness, and they dislike both descriptions. They are maintenance people, settled in a place that will not settle, expert in a negotiation nobody else has to conduct. They expect things to drift and plan for the drift in the placing of a step, the slack left in a cable, the extra hand's breadth left between houses because everything here needs room to be cut back — plants, and tempers, and neighbours too. The Terraces are spaced far apart and the friendships are correspondingly un-crowded, long-lived, low to the ground, intergrown underneath where you cannot see.

In winter, when the leaves are down and the bowl stands revealed in pale stubble and lamplight, they look startled even to themselves, and walk a little faster across the open. Then the first curled fists push up again, the map goes wrong again, the houses take up their slow travelling again, and the Sallowmerers take up their hooks and go out under the new shade — not conquering it, not worshipping it, just keeping, together, the exact amount of space a life requires.
