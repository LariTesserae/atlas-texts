No one lives there in the way you first look for.

There is no village under the trees. No smoke — smoke would be redundant, and impossible to tell from steam. No clearing, no hearth, no paths that stay paths after the afternoon rain. When you cross the rim and go down into that held 38 degrees, you find deer, monkeys, bats, stalk-legged birds, frogs ticking in bromeliads, and above it all the slow condenser barges drifting with their red night lights, unmanned and never landing.

And then, after a few days, you start finding the work.

A travertine lip that should have sealed a hot pool over has been kept open, not broken — thinned, neatly, from the inside. A rock flute that whistled choked at dawn is clear by evening and sounding a half-note lower, as if it had been scraped clean with something soft and patient. A fallen trunk across a spring channel has not been moved, exactly; it has been rolled just enough in its melting to let the water under, and packed with moss on the downhill side so it won’t roll back. The single river at the south notch never silts, though the whole valley is trying to deliver peat into it.

They are very hard to see, because seeing is a dry-country habit. Here everything is already wet, already dark, already steaming, already moving slowly.

The keepers live belly-deep.

That is their country: not the canopy, though they can climb; not the caves, though they shelter there at midday; not the hot center of the pools, which would cook even them. Their country is the margin where temperature becomes topography — the bath-warm edges, the bog meadows that rise and settle a hand’s breadth on the deep pressure cycle, the terrace where the water is exactly as hot as their own blood.

They are about as tall as a person sitting down, even when they stand, which they rarely do fully. Long in the body. Heavy behind, narrow in front, with limbs that fold rather than bend. Their feet are the first thing you’d mistake. Broad, splayed, almost round in soft peat, the toes webbed to the second joint and tipped not with claws but with thick, blunt nails worn to the quick. In mud they print like a great soft paw with no claw marks, and the print fills with tea-colored water while you watch it. Their hands are the same, only cleverer. They go on all fours through the bog as a matter of course, and stand only to reach into a tree fern or haul themselves onto a travertine shelf, dripping.

They have almost no hair, and what they have is not for warmth. Along the forearms, the back of the neck, the long ridge of the spine, there are fine, pale, separate filaments that lift when the static builds. Before a vent sighs, before blue sparks start running soundless along the quartz seams, the keepers turn their heads — all of them, across a pool, at once — as those filaments rise. They read pressure in their skin. They read electricity the way you read a change in wind. When the charge gets too high they simply put both palms flat in wet peat and let it go through them into the ground, untroubled. After rain their shoulders sometimes steam with tiny, soundless discharges as they move.

Their skin does the rest. Dark, not with pigment alone but with living stain: iron-red at the joints, green-black across the back where algae films it, sulfur pale around the mouth and nostrils. No two are colored alike because no two work the same pools. The old ones who tend the upper terraces are rimmed white at the shoulders and elbows with travertine, mineral laid down year after year until it forms a kind of loose, pitted armor they have to crack at the joint to keep moving. The young are slick and brown as river stones. In the water they vanish by becoming it. On land a keeper sitting still is a hummock, a root buttress, a wet rock covered in liverwort, until an ear moves.

The ears move constantly, like the deer’s. The nostrils are theirs alone: enlarged, folded inward in wet pleats like the deer’s steaming breath apparatus, but closable in sequence, one fold at a time. They can put their faces right into the sigh of a vent — never over its center — and sort the smell of sulfur, iron, rot, clean water, approaching rain, sick algae, another keeper, by which folds they open. Their eyes are small, deep-set, amber, with a second clear lid that never quite stops moving. The light down there is not light so much as green glare and mist; they do not need to see far. They need to see near, and under water, and in luminous ground-cloud at night.

They do not call.

Nobody with sense does, in that heat. Calling costs breath, and breath is hot. Instead they tick: a nail drawn down the edge of a travertine shell, a tooth clicked against a wet stone held in the mouth, a bubble deliberately released at a pool’s edge to break with a soft glassy note. The forest at morning is full of frog sounds like a fingernail on glass. Some of those are frogs. Some are a keeper on the far terrace saying *I am here, the vent is open, the water is rising* to another he cannot see through the trees. At night they go lower still. They put their foreheads to a root or a hollow flute column and hum, one note, until the column answers when pressure hits it. The whole eastern terrace can be mapped after dark by those tunings — which flutes they keep clear, which they let narrow to raise the pitch, which they have abandoned to sulfur. It sounds like machinery because it is tended like machinery, by bodies.

Their day is a migration of a few meters.

Before dawn they are already standing in the coolest terrace pools, if cool is a word that can survive here — 34, 35 degrees — letting the night’s condensation run off them, drinking by simply opening their mouths to dripping leaves. As the bog begins its slow lift and the springs strengthen, they move up-pool to warm, grazing as they go: emerald algae combed from the crust with their nails, pale blind shrimp scooped from the shallows in cupped hands and eaten alive and wriggling, water beetles, the soft mineral jelly that forms where iron water meets air. They cook nothing with fire. There is no fire in them at all. But a fish or a handful of large grubs knotted into a leaf and weighted with a cobble at the hot edge of a pool will be perfectly, tenderly done by midday, and they are very precise about which edge, and how long the ground cycle will hold it there.

At noon they disappear into the held breath.

Some go down — literally down, into the root-mat beneath the bog, into flooded hollows under buttress trees where the darkness is total and the water barely moves. Some go up into the travertine caves on the west wall and sleep among the bats in a heat so close and mineral that a human would faint within minutes, hanging their arms, letting their filament-hairs settle. They do nothing, superbly. Their temperatures, which track the valley’s within a degree, coast. Even their hearts seem to wait for the exhale.

When the vents along the east terrace let off steam together, they get to work.

That soft collective sigh is their signal, the shift change of the whole factory. In the slackening that follows, when springs ease and the meadow sinks, they move along the system doing with hands and weight and teeth what would otherwise need tools: clearing a spring mouth of fallen fronds before they can mat and seal it, breaking a new travertine dam with the mineral-crusted heel of a hand so the next terrace gets fed, rolling deeper into a wallow to keep a channel from silting, packing peat away from a sulfur fan with the side of a forearm (close, never touching its heart), biting through a vine that has begun to strangle a flute column. They tend pitcher plants the way gardeners tend something half animal — moving insects toward them, relocating their bucket-sized, steaming pitchers when a pool changes course. They know each hot meadow the way you would know a sleeping animal: where it can bear weight today, where the crust is a lie over scalding water, where to place a palm to feel the rock trembling and judge whether it is the good tremble or the building one.

No one taught the barges about them. The barges’ sensors read a keeper, when they read anything through canopy and steam, as a deer, a warm rock, a localized change in conductivity. The keepers, for their part, regard the barges the way they regard the emergent trees: a very high, very slow layer of the system that does something useful with what rises. When a tether line comes down and fouls in the tree ferns, they free it without curiosity, biting away the vine, guiding the cold metal — cold shocks them; they handle it with moss wrapped around their hands — back into the updraft. They have never seen what harvests the water. They know only that if the high dark shapes did not drink, the charge would spend itself worse in the lichen and the mist at night would sit heavier, and so they leave the high layer alone.

They are born in the water, at the travertine edge, into hands and algae-slick arms. Children spend their first years in the cooler terraces with the tadpoles and iridescent fish, nearly amphibious, eyes mostly lidded, learning temperature with their whole skins: this step safe, this step the deep heat breathing through. They ride on a parent’s travertine-white back when the bog is in its rising phase and the channels run too fast. They grow by staying, learning the valley not as a map — fifteen kilometers of ring wall, side-valleys, hanging marshes too large to see whole — but as a sequence of temperatures and notes and trembles that leads, pool to vine to cave to pool, all the way around and home.

They age into the mineral. The oldest keepers move less and less far from a single cluster of pools, and the pools take them into account; travertine grows faster where they habitually sit, shaping itself into a kind of seat, then a shell. Their skin splits white at every crease. Their ticking gets slower and is answered from farther away. One season they are a shape in the mist at evening, breathing with the meadow. The next, something soft is melting into the bog where they used to sit, going the way of the fallen trunks — not rotting so much as becoming sponge, green, held together by mycelium and roots, feeding the mat from underneath. There is no grave to find. There is, for a while, a warmer place in the peat, where the shrimp crowd thicker and the algae comes in especially bright.

They are not many. Perhaps a dozen families in the whole caldera, one to a side-valley, meeting mostly by sound: a flute retuned overnight, a tick answered across water, two mineral-crusted strangers standing motionless in a pool at dusk with their heads up, listening together to something you will never hear, for a full silent minute.

If you stay long enough you may see one, once, at night. The valley will have condensed around you, the forest standing in its own luminous ground-cloud, warm water reflecting moonlight from below so everything is lit backwards. A frog will tick. The bog will settle under your feet. And between the trees, lower than a deer, slower than a monkey, a broad dark shape will cross through the glow with its pale filaments lifting in the charged air, trailing sparks off one wet shoulder, smelling of iron and leaf mold and hot stone — going unhurriedly about the maintenance of the world it lives inside — and be gone into the steam before you are sure it was ever separate from it.
