At first you would say that nothing lives here — not in the way you expect living things to announce themselves.

There is no movement at eye level. No herds, no flocks lifting, no animal in the open. The trunks stand empty. The leaves hang unstirred. You stand still in the brown-green twilight until your shirt is stuck to you and your eyes begin to fail at adjusting, and the whole valley seems to be plants, water, and rot, holding its breath.

Then the occupancy resolves.

It resolves on the trunk you have been leaning toward without touching. The bark shifts. What was lichen is a line of moths, wing overlapping wing, each one the exact grey-green of wet wood, each with a crack-pattern down its back that continues the crack in the bark. Under them, smaller moths. Under a curled edge of bark, a beetle the size of a thumbnail, black, polished, waiting with its antennae laid flat. Farther up, where you cannot quite make out the shape, a katydid has arranged its legs to be the veins of a leaf, and is a leaf, until it decides not to be.

This is the first rule of the inhabitants: they do not occupy space, they *become* surfaces.

Almost everything here is the color of something else. The lizards asleep head-down on saplings are new-growth green down one side and dead-leaf brown down the other, and they turn, very slowly through the day, to keep the right color to the light. The frogs in the bromeliads are bark, or moss, or the wet inside of a flower, with only the slow pulse in the throat giving them away — and even that stops when you look. Tree snakes sleep in coils that read as lianas. Owls in the day holes of trunks are knots of wood with eyes closed to slits. Even the jaguar, when it comes down the old dry creek bed that everything uses as a road, is not so much spotted as made of the dapple itself, patches of dark and patches of spent light moving together through more dark and light, dissolving at ten metres.

They live this way because sight is a poor currency here. In a permanent noon-dark, sound carries and betrays, motion carries and betrays, but stillness is almost perfect safety. So this is a waiting fauna. Ambush is its grammar. Things sit for hours, for days, in the exact posture of hunger, and let the world come to them.

The frog waits in its cup of water thirty feet up, in the heart of a bromeliad, and eats what falls in or comes to drink. The snake waits along a branch that monkeys use, tasting the air with a tongue that brings it a smell-map more detailed than any eye could make — warmth ahead, sex, fear, a rat that passed an hour ago and will pass again. The soft-shelled turtle waits buried in the mud of a pool with only its nostrils up, for hours, for a whole afternoon. The leeches under the stones wave slowly at nothing, testing for the heat of a body that has not yet arrived. A fruit thuds down somewhere and everything within ten metres freezes for a full minute, because everyone knows everyone else heard it.

They can afford to wait. Heat and wet are never withdrawn, so nothing here has to hurry to stay warm, or migrate to drink, or gorge before a winter. Metabolism is a slow fire that never goes out. Things digest for weeks. Things grow by increments. Things live, by the standards of their size, improbably long, in territories so small that a lifetime's map can be learned by smell alone.

A sloth is born in a tree and may spend most of its life within sight of that tree. It moves two metres an hour, hanging upside down, and it is not really an animal travelling through the forest so much as a piece of the forest that relocates itself. Its fur grows the wrong way, sheds water downward, and is green — actually green — with algae living in the hollow hairs, and in the algae live moths found nowhere else, and when the sloth descends, once a week, with terrible slowness, to bury its dung at the base of its own tree, the moths ride down and lay in the dung and ride up again, fertilizing the algae that camouflage the sloth that carries them. A whole garden, animal, plant, and insect, completing a circuit at walking pace slower than moss.

Below it, the monkeys do move, in the middle canopy in the early dark, hands and tails finding branches they do not need to see, dislodging a brief rain of stored water wherever they pass. Below them, or above them after full night, the bats pour out of hollow emergents in a soft river, thousands of them, leather wings almost silent, each one returning before dawn to the same roost that smells, unmistakably, of itself. Under them go the owls, on wings fringed to silence, hunting not by sight but by the rustle a beetle makes under a wet leaf.

Yet the true population is not these. It is smaller, and it never stops.

At face height the air is occupied by midges and tiny stingless bees that find the moisture at the corners of your eyes and stay there, patient as dust. Every leaf has an underside, and every underside has tenants: sleeping lizards, katydids, clusters of eggs like glass beads, a spider holding its web flat to catch what walks. The leaf litter, three inches deep and fermenting under its white fungal threads, is not ground to the things in it — it is a city in cross-section. Millipedes go through it like slow armoured trains, eating the rot and making soil of it. Beetles shoulder through in pairs. Earthworms surface in the wet. Land crabs sidle from burrow to burrow with their claws tucked, grazing, retreating backward at a vibration. Ants — always ants — cross it on highways worn bare and smooth, a two-way current that does not pause at night, outbound empty, inbound carrying green crescents many times their size, tiny sails bobbing in the dark, a conveyor that runs for seasons until the trees at the end of it are spent.

For the inhabitants here are not only eating the forest. They are running it.

The leafcutters do not eat the leaves they harvest. They carry them down, into chambers under the soil big enough, in aggregate, to hold rooms, and there they feed them to fungus gardens they have cultivated longer than any human agriculture. They weed the gardens. They tend them. They harvest the swollen tips to feed their larvae. They carry the spent, dangerous refuse to separate waste chambers staffed by workers who will never surface again, who live their whole lives in the warm, sour exhaust of the colony. A big colony exhales. It processes a tree's worth of foliage. It turns canopy into underground, leaf into fungus into ant into soil.

The termites have built up out of that soil into fluted towers taller than a person, hard as poor concrete, and the towers breathe. Inside, a fungus comb must be held at an almost constant heat in a valley that never cools, so workers endlessly plaster and re-plaster chimneys and galleries, opening and closing vents, driving air in at the base and sighing it out the top, a million bodies air-conditioning their crop. Put your hand to a vent at night and what touches you is not forest air. It is manufactured air — warm, damp, faintly fermented, the product.

Wasps laminate grey paper nests from chewed wood and hang them from branches. Bees in a hollow trunk fan the entrance in coordinated shifts, wings blurring, so the trunk hums under your palm like an idling engine. Dung beetles find a dropping within the hour, assess it, cut it, roll it away in sealed balls to underground stores, burying fertility before flies can waste it. Vinegar flies cloud over fallen fruit fermenting to alcohol in the hollows, and butterflies come to drink and fly drunk, slow and erratic through shafts of lesser dark. Nothing falls that is not met. Nothing dies that is not disassembled in order, by specialists queued for each stage: the thing that eats the leaf, the thing that eats its droppings, the fungus that takes the wood it lived in, the mite that rides the beetle that buried the dung.

And in the water — which is never a lake, never a river you could follow, only pockets and stalls and cups — the same density holds in miniature. Every bromeliad pool has its tadpoles grazing algae off submerged leaves, its mosquito larvae, its one small frog who owns it. Every oxbow hollow has water beetles rowing, leeches waving, fish trapped when the waters joined briefly in the wetter weeks and cut off again, laying eggs that will wait buried in mud through the dry contraction until the pools reconnect. The creeks, tea-brown and bankless, are roads and nurseries and hunting grounds at once, easing through root mats that grip the clay like fingers.

This is how they reproduce: not in seasons, exactly, because there are no seasons strong enough to command them, but in opportunities. When a giant emergent finally falls and a shaft of true sun hits the floor, seeds that have lain dormant for years germinate in days and saplings that have waited a decade in the dusk begin to race each other upward, and for a few weeks that bright patch converges with insects, and the birds that eat them, and the snakes that eat the birds, a slow-motion riot over light. The rest of the time they breed in the dark, by scent and by single calls — a frog giving one liquid *plop* from a bromeliad and not repeating it, sufficient in a world where every territory is known — and they lay their young in water held in leaves, in foam, in mud, in the bodies of other things, wherever wetness can be trusted to last.

They map it all without seeing it. At night the forest is laid over with a second, invisible geography of smell trails, territory marks, ripeness, danger, readiness, far more detailed than the visible one. That is the map the jaguar reads walking the creek bed. That is the map the ant highway is written in. That is the map the moths, patterned as bark, are waiting inside of.

If you stay long enough you understand that no single one of them is the inhabitant. Not the jaguar, rare and solitary and mostly absent, whose territory simply encompasses everyone else's. Not the sloth, not the colony whose individual workers live weeks while the colony itself outlives trees. Even the termite mounds outlast the giants that shaded their founding. Lifespans overlap like the canopies — emergent, continuous, understory — three layers of time as well as light, the short-lived swarming and feeding the long-lived, the long-lived making the shade in which the short-lived can afford to be slow.

What lives here, in total, is a processing. A self-maintaining dark full, at any given moment, of things doing almost nothing, very slowly, all at once. Every leaf, pool, burrow, branch, and body is occupied, and occupied by something exquisitely specialised for that exact inch of wet heat, that exact stage in the breakdown of a leaf or a fruit or a corpse, that exact quality of twilight. When one vacates, the space does not stay empty long enough to be noticed. The abundance is not in motion. It is in tenancy.

You will leave by climbing — up through the young palms and tree-ferns, up through the knitted roof where the monkeys travel, up into the sudden white hammer of sky above the flat crowns of the emergents. Behind and below you the place will not call after you, or flock, or fall silent, because it was never loud. It will go on dripping at irregular intervals, ticking faintly under the wet leaves, exhaling warm air along its creeks, carrying green sails along bare highways in the dark, digesting meals that will take weeks, waiting in the shape of bark for what comes next.
