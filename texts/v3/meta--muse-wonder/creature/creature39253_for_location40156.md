At first you would say that nothing lives there. Not really.

The moss campion in the sound-shadows behind the cairns lives, after a fashion, but it has taken a century to make a cushion the size of a hand and has decided, apparently, that this is enough. The black lichen lives so slowly that living and being a stain on stone may be the same activity. The snow finches live in the cairns for a season, feed their gaping young beak-to-beak, and go down again to where a call can be a call. The ibex come up in the poor years and stand with their heads low and their felted hooves spread, listening through their feet to something that has nothing to do with food, and then they remember themselves and leave.

None of them are *of* it.

The people who cross are not of it. They come up the three days of simplifying country with wool in their ears and grease on their faces, they tap their messages into the flat rocks with the flats of their hands, they sleep in the roofless windbreaks and in the hut with no door, and they go down the other side altered only in the way that a bell is altered by having been near another bell. They leave footprints in the frost that will not melt. They leave their breath hanging in a dotted white line at face-height along the trail. They do not leave anyone behind.

And yet the windbreaks do not fall.

That is the first true sign, if you stay long enough to notice — and no crosser stays long enough, which may be the arrangement. Dry-laid stone at six thousand meters, in frost that splits granite like pottery, should work itself apart. Instead the curved walls are kept. Not rebuilt in any human, urgent way. A plate that has slipped is lifted and reset. A hand's breadth of wall that has begun to lean into the roar is leaned back. The cairns change more subtly. One that stood taller than a man on the eastern crossing will, by the next crossing a decade later, be lower, its plates spread in a neat fan at its base — not fallen, *laid down*, as if some judgment had been made that it was standing in the wrong place for the sound and needed to be taken out of the way.

The hut floor has two hollows worn by sleepers. Only one is ever dusty.

Whatever lives on the high table, it maintains the instruments. Because that is what the cairns and windbreaks are, once you understand the bowl: not shelter, not markers. Tuning. A cairn throws a sound-shadow as well as a shadow of light, and in that comma of quiet the white flowers grow. Move a cairn, and the quiet moves. The plateau is twenty kilometers of ear, and someone tends the ear.

Its tracks are in the braided trail with all the others, and have been there so long that at first you read them as ibex. The frost keeps everything. Among the boot-scars and the darkened prints of generations, there is a print that is neither boot nor hoof: long, broad at the ball, the toes barely separated, as if the foot were wrapped in felt or had *become* felt, had grown its own silence to walk on. It does not come up from the valleys. It does not go down to them. If you follow it — and you can only follow it by walking, because sight lies about distance here and will show you the whole loop at once, meaningless — it makes a slow circuit of the bowl. Rim to windbreak to cairn to hut. Cairn to cairn. Always approaching the ring of seven slabs at the center, always veering, describing a spiral that takes days at the pace the print is set.

The pace is very slow. The stride is a human stride, but sunken, considered, each step placed the way you would place a cup of water you did not want to spill. And here and there the trail stops entirely at a flat rock, and the frost around the rock is disturbed in a fan shape, as if something had stood there a long time, perfectly still, with its hand down.

Listening through the stone. The way the ibex do. The way the sleepers in the hut do, involuntarily, dreaming without words. It stands and puts its palm flat and feels the roar come up clean through the skeleton of the place, unshredded by the air. The rock is honest. It has learned everything it knows through the rock.

Because it is made — and this is the thing you do not understand until you have been in the returning of your own voice — it is *made*, mostly, of what the air does here.

The air will not carry a shout honestly. It folds it, learns it, gives it back a breath before your lips have closed, in your own voice improved by lifelong acquaintance. The air will not carry a whisper at all. A whisper is taken. Breath will not dissipate. It holds its cloud-shape at head height, sometimes with the curve of a mouth still in it, until it looks, to the altitude-sick, like the dotted line of everyone who has ever panted across is still crossing, very slowly, beside you.

Some of those breaths never thinned. Some of those whispers went somewhere.

It wears the cold the way the rocks do: by sweating rime in the loudest parts of the bowl. If you saw it at the edge of your vision — and it is only ever at the edge, because looking straight at anything here tells you where it is without telling you how far, and it has advantages it did not ask for — you would see a figure the height of a tall person, stooped a little, in the perpetual northward lean of everything that has stood in one weather too long. Its outer shape is travelers' leavings: greased fur gone grey, wool darkened at the cuffs and collar with that impossible damp, layers on layers, patched with nothing imported, bound with lichen-colored cord that is probably also wool, probably also very old. Weather and wardrobe have been happening to it for so long that the distinction is academic. Slate dust has worked into every seam. It rings, very softly, when it moves. Not the coat. *It.* Under the coat. As if its surface, too, had split along its grain into small plates that shift against each other at each step and add their note to the chord without ever swelling it.

Its face is covered, as the crossers cover theirs, against a cold that is not extreme but is eternal. What shows is the breath-mask: a hovering whiteness that does not come and go with breathing the way yours does, but stays, a soft held cloud in the place where a mouth and nose would be, renewing itself at the edges, rime beading on it and beading on it until the lower half of its head seems to be made of the same fog that marks the trail. Sometimes, in that fog, a mouth-curve forms. It is not always the same mouth. It has had a great many mouths lent to it, one breath at a time, by people who are now dead in valleys it will never see, and it keeps them the way the frost keeps footprints: without malice, without emphasis, because nothing here is in a hurry to become something else, including an ending.

It has no visible ears. The sides of its head are packed smooth — wool, fur, a pale sulphur crust of lichen that has mistaken it, reasonably, for a boulder that moves too slowly to object. Sound does not enter it by the air. It has sealed the dishonest route. When it wishes to know, it kneels — the kneeling prints are there too, beside the flat rocks, beside the pools, a double crescent in the frost — and puts its ear, or the packed side of its head, or its open palm, directly to the ground. The two hollows in the hut floor fit it the way a bed fits a body that has slept in one position for a very long time. One hollow for the head. The deeper one, polished by contact, not for a head at all, perhaps. For a hand, held down all night, taking the pulse of the note while the rest of it slept.

It does not speak into the air. It may not be able to afford to. A thing assembled out of returned voices, out of shouts given back and whispers absorbed and breath that held its shape too long, might come apart at the seams if it spent itself into that treacherous medium — or worse, might hear itself returned, pronounced correctly, a breath before its own lips closed, and not know which of them was supposed to go on being it. So it taps. Travelers who have slept badly in the windbreaks sometimes report, afterward and only afterward, a tapping in the rock beneath them in the dim unchanging light: three, a pause, two. Not a code they knew. They answer it without deciding to, half asleep, hand flat in the dirt-feeling cold of the floor — and the tapping changes. It learns the weight of a hand the way the air learns a voice. It can tell crossers apart through six inches of stone. It probably knew your name before the bowl gave it back to you. It had been listening to you your whole way up, through the sole of the mountain, feeling the plates ring under your boots.

It eats almost nothing, and what it eats takes a long time to happen. In the loudest parts of the bowl, where moisture beads directly out of the vibrating air, it licks rime from the sweating rocks the way an animal licks salt, patiently, hoarding a cold that leaves the tongue feeling scalded. It drinks from the centers of the pools, where the water stays improbably open, colder than ice, kept disturbed by the roar. It may gather the white flowers from the sound-shadows, one five-petaled bone-colored blossom at a time, in a harvest measured in years. It may not eat them. It may move them. The cushions of campion behind the cairns have the tended look of things that are planted in commas, deliberately, where the quiet falls.

It is not strong, by valley standards, and it is not fast. Things fall strangely in the bowl, slowly and a little sideways, and it has learned to lean on that consideration the way the lammergeier does, letting the pressed air — the chord you can feel in your sternum and the small bones behind your jaw — take a fraction of its weight at each step. That is why the footprint is shallower than a body in all that fur should make. It walks partly on the sound. A dropped tool considers its descent; it has become something that lives in the consideration, in the pause between a thing let go and a thing landed. Time declines to complete its transactions up here. A cut stays bright and wet, neither clotting nor flowing. Hunger comes and forgets to grow. It is not immortal. It is unfinished, continuously, at a rate too slow for the place to have noticed and frozen. That is close enough, over centuries, to fool the lichens.

And it is afraid — if *afraid* is the word for a body deciding, the way your body decides to walk backward out of the ring — of the center.

It goes there. The spiral track proves it goes there, eventually, at the end of its long circuit of tuning and tending. But the prints inside the seven slabs are different. They press deeper, as if the sound had stopped holding it up at the threshold, as if it had to be entirely its own weight in there, most of it borrowed. They go in backward, often. They do not stay.

In the silence at the center, it would be able to hear its own blood, and then stop hearing even that properly. Worse: in the silence, nothing is returned to it. No whisper is absorbed and kept. No breath holds. It thins in that quiet like a cloud in a wind that isn't blowing. And the pool in the middle of the ring does not reflect the sky, and does not reflect it either — not while it stands there. It reflects the ring with no one in it. It reflects, sometimes, the ring with everyone in it lying down, but slowly, as if the light had a long way to travel up. Perhaps if it waited, its reflection would arrive, years late, lying down in the stone circle in water that shows a greener slope and a brighter sky than this plateau has ever permitted. Perhaps that is why nothing is carved here, why there is not a mark anywhere: it has seen the delayed picture and understood that the place is keeping a version of every visitor for later, and it does not want to hurry the development.

So it leaves the center, backward, and puts the roar around itself again like water closing over a stone, and the plates under its felted feet ring softly, and the chord takes the note without swelling, exactly in tune, exactly as loud as it was before there was anyone to hear it.

It is the mountain's echo, given enough centuries of cold and attention to stand up in. It is every name shouted across the bowl and returned correctly, deciding at last to stay and listen for the next one rather than fade on the far rim. It keeps the cairns so the quiet will fall in the right places and the white flowers can grow. It taps on the rock under sleeping strangers so that someone, through the honest medium, will tap back and confirm that the crossing is still happening, that voices are still arriving to be learned. It lies awake with its hand in the hollow, feeling the single struck note go on and on beneath the stone, in the hollow second space under the world where the roar lives.

It does not know what struck the mountain. It does not need to. It is what the striking left listening.
