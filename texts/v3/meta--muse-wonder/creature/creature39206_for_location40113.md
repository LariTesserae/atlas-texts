At first you would say that nothing lives there.

Not the builders. The builders belong to the rim, to the wet country behind them, to the lines of baskets and the dark, newly laid ground still squeaking underfoot. They come out in the cool hours and retreat before the heat. They make the Table; they do not inhabit it.

Not the grass in the joints either, nor the moss on the north side of a pillar, nor the seedlings yellowing in a pan that stayed wet a week too long. Those are accidents with roots. They happen to the place. They do not yet know how to stay.

The thing that stays is farther in, and so sparse, and so nearly the color of the ground, that for a long time you mistake it for another of the leavings: a stack of bricks, a survey pillar, a low shed standing miles from any other shade.

Then the shed moves.

It is a person, carrying shade on their back.

From a distance they look broad and low and unnaturally slow, a dark speck with a pale halo moving across rose panels, then across chalk-white ones, then stopping altogether while the heat is up. Up close the shade resolves into a frame of light wood and reed matting, clay-daubed, weighted at the edges, worn like a tortoise shell or a roof that has learned to walk. It is hat and house and sail and water-catch at once. By day it keeps the sun off the head and shoulders. Tipped at noon, propped on its carrying poles, it makes the only useful shadow for miles, a thin black tent just high enough to lie under. At night, inverted and set into a panel joint, it collects dew in its clay hollow until there is a cupful beaded in the weave, tasting faintly of smoke and reeds.

The people underneath it — the seam-walkers, the fillers, the tenders of the curing — are the Table's only interior population. If population is the word. There are no villages on the panels. No families settled in clusters, no fields, no graves, no wells, no paths worn by generations. Density here is like everything else on the Table: hours of nothing, then one, then hours of nothing again.

A tender works a territory measured not in land but in decades. One rose province of clay, laid forty years ago, shading into the bone-colored work of the next building generation. They know the panel numbers the way farmers elsewhere know fields. They know which year's mix runs salt, which cures brittle, which blisters if a fire is lit on it, which old course was dragged in on mats and still shows the paired lines. They navigate by stamped pillars and by color underfoot and, increasingly as they age, by sound.

Because their real work is listening.

In the morning, while a fresh section is sighing vapor and the night's dew is still in the seams, a tender walks with their head cocked and a small hammer loose in one hand, though the hammer is more for confirming what the ear already found. The ground ticks as it warms. It pings deep in the older fill. A crack starts somewhere under the surface, traveling — *tink, tink,* a fingernail dragged over pottery — and they follow it the way a tracker follows a sound in brush, except there is no brush, no cover, nothing between the sound and the sky. They mark the hairline with a smear of dark ash. By afternoon, when the crack has opened enough to take a fill, they are back with a skin of wet slurry carried against their hip, kept workable in the shade of their own body. They kneel on padded reed, never bare-kneed on the hot panel, and work the new material in with a wooden trowel. Palm flat beside the seam. Fingers smoothing the lip so the next rain will pond rather than run.

Some of the palm prints cured into the ground are theirs. Some are the palm prints of tenders dead before the panels they are walking on were laid.

Their bodies have been edited by the place, slowly, the way the panels are edited by weather. They are dusted white to the elbows and knees — lime, ash, fine clay worked into the skin until wiping it off becomes a special occasion, done only at a fresh pan, and then only to apply it again wet as a paste. It is sunscreen and camouflage and uniform at once; from a pillar top a tender in full sun is nearly invisible, just a moving flaw in the glare, betrayed by the small hard shadow of carried shade. Their hair is cropped close or bound under reed and bleached at the ends. Under the eyes they wear narrow slit-shades of pale fired clay, polished inside with soot, because here the light comes up as hard as it comes down and ordinary squinting is not enough over years. The gaze that develops is level and unhurried. They look at the horizon the way sailors do, and mostly they look down.

Their feet are famous, in the rim settlements, in the way things become famous that rim people never expect to need. Thick-soled sandals of layered reed and hide, re-soled with unbaked clay that fires a little more each day they walk. The soles have to be thick: by mid-morning no bare palm can rest on a panel, and a tender stranded without footwear in the interior would be in trouble by ten. Over years the sandals shape the walk, a flat, rolling, economical stride that spreads weight, avoids the raised lips of the joints, makes almost no scuff on a finished surface. Their tracks are among the few tracks the Table ever holds, and even those are tidied by wind within days, dust settling preferentially into the print until it reads as a faint, temporary joint in the geometry.

They eat what can be carried and what will not spoil in heat that cooks as it preserves. Flat cakes from the wet margins, dried strips, salt they harvest themselves from the rings of vanished pans — that salt is theirs by right, gathered into little bone-colored piles and pinched into food with a connoisseur's attention, because pan salt from a three-day pan is clean and bright and pan salt from a six-day pan is bitter with green film. After a rain they skim algae from the oldest edges and dry it on their shade-frame into dark flakes. In a good wet season, at a pan that holds, they eat something fresh and faintly vegetal and are briefly, almost visibly, glad. It is never enough to live on. Nothing on the Table is enough to live on. That is the first rule of their existence: they are residents who cannot be self-sufficient, and so their lives are stitched to the rim by supply caches in the low sheds — grain sealed in clay, spare reed, trowel wood, water skins replenished by whoever last passed — and by long, planned returns to the edge that feel less like going home than like visiting the country that makes their material.

So they do not settle. They *circulate*.

A tender's year has no planting and no harvest. It has the daily tower of cloud over the center and the judgement of pans. Too fresh and the water tastes of lime, raw on the tongue. Too old and it is salt skin and algae. Just right, for a day, sometimes three, the shallow water is drinkable if you wade out past the cracked margin and dip where the mirror is unbroken. On those days there may be company. All the tenders within sight of the same grey curtain dragging across the white will angle toward it, not hurrying — no one hurries in the interior except the newly arrived, and they learn or they leave — and by evening there will be three people, or five, standing at the edge of a new-made temporary lake miles wide and inches deep, looking at their own reflections come back at them out of the sky.

This is as close as they come to a town. They wash lime from their faces. They trade news in the flat, uninflected way of people who have each been alone with expansion-ticks and rain-hiss for weeks: which pillar has leaned, which province of panels is drumming hollow, where a blister rose after some fool lit a fire directly on the surface, what the new work at the eastern rim smells like this year. Sometimes children are conceived at a pan. Sometimes a tender who has not been seen for two wet seasons is simply not there, and someone else quietly begins walking his decades, filling his cracks, drinking from his caches, and the news of the absence spreads at the speed of rain.

They are not many, and they are not, strictly, born to it. Most come in from the rim crews when they prove to have the ear — or when the noise and wet crowding of the margins become less bearable to them than silence. A few come because they cannot stand to be buried anywhere ordinary, though that concern is premature. Out here there is no burial to speak of. The ground will not receive you deeper than the fill; dig and you find older work. When a tender dies in the interior, if they are found in time, the body is carried panel by panel toward the advancing edge, days of slow transport under a shade-frame now used as a litter, and given to the builders. There, where new ground is still wet and steaming and squeaking underfoot, the dead go into the making. Not under the Table. *As* it. A decade later someone may walk across a whole rose territory and never know that a gram of the color is a person who once listened for cracks in it.

Aging, for a tender, looks like curing. The skin cross-hatches at heel and knuckle and the hinge of the jaw; the fine lines fill with white dust until face and panel share a vocabulary. Hearing sharpens in one direction and dulls in all others — old tenders can identify, blindfolded, the decade of a slab by the pitch of its afternoon tick, but may not notice you speaking the first time. They sleep warm. That is one of the few mercies the place offers outright: after sunset the great mass gives back its heat slowly, and a tender lying in the lee of their frame on a finished panel sleeps on a floor that holds the day long into the night, listening to it contract, ping, settle, like a huge animal turning once in sleep and being still.

And perhaps that is the real description, arriving late the way everything arrives in the interior, hours of nothing and then one made thing. The tenders are not conquerors of the Table and they are not its victims and they are not, in the old sense, its inhabitants — there is no food web here for anything to inhabit, no soil, no succession, only opportunistic grass in a seam saying *water was here once.* What the tenders are is the human part of the curing. The Table ticks and they mark. The Table cracks and they fill. The Table lays down salt in its pans and they take a pinch and leave the ring. From the top of a survey pillar they are invisible within the faint grid the wind keeps drawing — white on white, a rhythm rather than a presence, one moving shade to every few thousand panels.

Stand in the center at noon, when the cloud has not yet built and the silence is broken only by expansion sounds and your own steps coming back at you off the hot flat, and you might conclude the place is empty.

Wait until evening. When the tower lets go twenty kilometers away and the grey curtain comes hissing over the white, erasing the horizon, you will see them turn — all of them, at whatever distance, like needles — and begin, unhurried, to walk toward the rain.
