It is not inhabited the way a plain is inhabited. It is inhabited the way a body is inhabited — by processes too small to see, by organs too large to recognize, by things in between that think they are separate animals because for a while they have their own mouths.

The largest of them are the boiler-grazers, and for a long time they seem to be the whole answer.

They move in herds so dense that the water is only visible in the cracks between them, shoulder grinding shoulder, plate ringing softly against plate, their six and eight legs going down through brine and finding purchase on ice, mud, the backs of smaller things, occasionally on nothing at all that will hold for longer than a step. From a distance — if distance behaved — they would look low, broad, unhurried. Up close they have no distance at all. A grazer fills the weather. Its flank rises beside you riveted and steaming, patched with horn, chitin, blued metal, scar tissue that has oxidized into something between all three. Barnacle-intakes cluster at its knees. Its hide ticks as it cools and pings as it warms. Frost forms in lace along the seams where plates overlap, and cracks off in sheets when it breathes, and forms again before the pieces have finished sinking.

It has no proper head. Its chimney-neck rises, bends, rises again, jointed like a crane and insulated with a mane of mineral wool that was once its own hair and is now mostly asbestos feather and frozen mucus. At the end the neck opens into a valve-cowl, lips of flexible iron that purse and flare, and set deep in the throat is an eye — sometimes one, sometimes a wet cluster that has not agreed on a number yet — looking down the length of its own exhaust. It sees mostly heat. It sees the herd as a moving country of warm shapes. It sees you as a brief, inefficient fire.

They graze without stopping. Their mouths are underneath, where you cannot watch without going into the water, which you must not do. There are mouths in the water that are not theirs. Under the belly a set of intake-slats opens and closes, opens and closes, drawing in black brine, sulfur mud, pump-mollusk pellets, loose screws, shed teeth, the soft dissolving bodies of the newly dead. Inside, the sound changes. You can hear it through their ribs if you press your ear there: a low grinding mill, a wet furnace, sorting organs moving things along by size and usefulness. Iron to the blood. Sulfur to the heat. Glass to the eye-glands, where it is secreted in slow lenses. Usable parts to storage bladders along the spine. The rest out the back in warm slag that steams as it hits the cold and is already being eaten before it settles.

Alone, a grazer is a death sentence. Its heat leaks faster than it can make it, the brine pulls at its joints, ice finds the seams. In the herd they leak into each other. Steam from a thousand chimneys falls as frost on a thousand backs and melts again from the updraft of their bellies. They lean. They lock plates with neighbors while sleeping, if sleeping is the word for that half-hour when the legs keep walking but the eye films over and the chimney breaths come slower, remembering deeper water. Calves — small, hot, frantic things the size of dogs, then horses, then rooms — ride in the lee of their mothers’ legs, hooked in by temporary clamps they will later shed as their first set of adult teeth.

But the grazers are not separate from the things that crawl on them. Nothing here is separate for long.

In their joints live the gear-mites.

They are the size of thumbnails, then coins, then — if they survive the crush — crabs. Eight-legged, twelve-legged, their shells are washers and sprockets grown around a kernel of meat. They eat rust. That sounds like cleaning, and it is also feeding, and also a kind of careful wounding: without them the grazers’ joints would fuse clean, lock, and tear. The mites keep the wound open and moving, licking oxidation down to bright metal, excreting it as red dust that stains the frost in arterial fans around every knee and shoulder. Grazers shake periodically and rain them into the shallows. Most find a leg and climb. The rest become something else.

In the shallows, among the pump-mollusks, are the valve-crabs who were mites last season, or last century — time pools here like everything else. Their claws have squared off into wrenches. One claw is always larger, grown to the exact gauge of the grazer-bolts their grandparents tended. They scavenge the black pellets the mollusks spit, they pry open dead intakes, they steal still-twitching parts from assembly pits and drag them sideways into the mud to see if the parts will follow. When a crane-hunter takes a feeder apart overhead, the crabs gather underneath in a patient ring, letting screws bounce off their shells, waiting for the screaming to become useful.

The pump-mollusks themselves may be crabs who stayed too long in one place. Or grazers who gave up on moving. They root. Their iron shells open into the current they make themselves, gill-fans spinning, turbine-steady, pulling the basin through their bodies one mouthful at a time. A mature mollusk-field hums at a pitch that sets your teeth in sympathetic motion. They are the kidneys of the plain. What they discharge — compacted iron, sulfur, salt in neat black pellets — feeds everything. What they retain becomes shell, becomes more mollusk, becomes after a thousand years a low reef of fused intakes still pumping in unison under the bellies of passing herds, unaware or unconcerned that they were ever anything with legs.

Above, the bellows-birds turn in the low air.

They do not flap. Their wings are segmented fans that rotate around shoulder-hubs, each blade feathered with thin hammered membrane that catches steam and spins them onward, creaking. Their bodies are light because they have to be — hollow ribs, lung-sacks that inflate visibly with each slow revolution, a tail of copper wires that trails sparks when the air is particularly saturated. Their cry is a train whistle heard from a town that no longer exists. They navigate by the echoes of it coming back off the herd.

When one dies, its hub seizes by degrees. For days it hangs in the migration lane, turning slower and slower, while the others pass through the arc of its wings and strip it by friction — a feather, a blade, a bright screw of bone — until what remains is mostly a spine and a whistle, and then only a whistle, and then an upright shape of sound standing briefly in the steam before the water takes its outline and keeps flying it a little farther.

Predators here are not an opposite to this. They are a function of it.

The crane-jawed hunters are tall, stilt-legged, almost clean compared to the herd — long articulated necks, heads like toolboxes that open on several axes at once. They do not kill cleanly because clean killing would waste information. They select a feeder — the boar-sized, low-slung grazers-in-waiting that nose along at the herd’s edge — and they begin, methodically, to unscrew it while it walks. A leg folded and detached. A side plate lifted to expose the hot, surprised organs working underneath. An eye stalk coiled carefully around a talon and tied off, still looking. The feeder keeps grazing as long as it has a mouth connected to anything. It screams through whatever vents remain open. Dropped parts crawl. The hunter eats only certain things: fresh pumps, uncorroded bearings, the soft electrical fat around the spine. The rest it leaves for reassembly, and this is understood, if anything here understands, as a form of care. A herd that is never taken apart rusts together into a single immobile mass. The hunters keep the animals plural.

And in the pits, the taking-apart becomes making again.

You find them everywhere the ground is hot enough: shallow craters steaming in the mud, ringed with offerings. A pit is never empty. A loose piston works its way through black water toward a casing that fits it almost. A length of intestine, iridescent inside-out, wraps experimentally around rebar and squeezes, and learns. Teeth migrate through silt and set themselves in a jaw in the wrong order, then correct themselves overnight. A spine assembles vertebra by vertebra as crabs bring it pieces and argue over placement by hammering.

Mothers come — not always female, not always the species that will result — and cough up what they have been growing for this. Bolts ripened in cheek-pouches. Lenses secreted like pearls behind the eyes. A second heart, still beating in nutrient brine, carried in a throat-sac for a year. An old bellows-bird will land, with great difficulty, at a pit’s edge and shake from its tail-wires a nest of conductive filaments, then leave before the pit can incorporate the bird too. A grazer will lower its chimney-neck and exhale gently into the crater for hours, keeping the assembly above freezing with its own breath.

What climbs out is never new. It is dented in places it has not been struck yet. Rust blooms on it in patterns its grandparents wore. It may be crab-shaped and grow into a feeder. It may come out already boar-sized, shaking mud from intake-slats, looking for the herd by sound. Sometimes it comes out small and upright.

Those are the ones that live on the backs.

They are born the size of children and some of them stay that way for decades, bipedal because the backs require it, their feet broad and magnetically tacky, their hands divided between fingers and tools in a proportion that shifts as they age. One hand ends in a wrench that is also bone. The other is soft enough to feel for hairline cracks. Their lungs are visible bellows under a cage of ribs that opens when they run. They wear nothing grown that cannot be shed — patches of hide, frost, a harness of their own molted straps.

They are tenders, if a word is needed. They ride the archipelago of the herd.

They move from back to back as the grazers sway together, leaping gaps that open over black water, landing with a lessening of weight as the grazer’s strange gravity takes them. They clear mite-jams from jammed knees with the concentration of surgeons. They hammer messages into ribs — I am here, this joint is failing, hunter overhead, pit ahead, do not step — and read replies through their feet several seconds before they are sent. They deliver bolts mouth-to-mouth. They midwife calves out of side-pouches and pit-eggs alike, and are midwifed in turn, for they are not a separate people. They are a stage.

A tender who lives long enough gets heavy in a way that has nothing to do with gravity. Plates begin to bud under the skin of the shoulders. The soft hand squares off. A second pair of limbs, folded for years along the ribs like an unspoken opinion, start to ache in wet weather. One morning — or one of the dim hours that substitutes for it — they go down on all fours on the back of an old grazer and do not get up again, and the herd makes room, and their chimney-neck begins, painfully, to grow.

Not all of them grow. Some stay small and become sharper, more specialized, riding the oldest grazers, the white-frozen ones who died mid-herd years ago and are still carried along by the press of bodies, still steaming faintly from chimneys that open onto nothing living inside. The tenders keep those too. They sit in the lee of dead chimneys and listen, because the frozen ones remember routes in their locked legs, and when the herd calls in unison the sound sometimes stands up in their throats and speaks in a voice made of ice and echo. Those tenders become listeners. They navigate by the herd made of noise that grazes alongside the herd made of meat, and by the water-duplicates lagging underfoot, reading the plain’s memory of itself for where it intends to go next.

At the horizons the stub-hills weep their brine, and after enough time among the tenders you understand those are not hills either. They are grazers who outgrew the herd, or were left in a silence pocket too large for the others to fill, and rooted, and slowed, and kept growing inward. Their legs are under the mud. Their backs have become the only high ground there is, which is almost none. Pores that were once sweat-glands and blowholes squeeze out concentrated brine, rust-threaded, mineral-rich. Young tenders climb them with reverence and terror to lick salt directly from their grandmothers’ skin, and under their hands the stone is faintly, unmistakably warm, and ticks as it cools.

Nothing here ends by stopping. It ends by changing scale.

Mite to crab to tender to grazer to hill. Mollusk to reef. Bird to whistle to a shape in the water still flying its route after the flock has turned. Dead grazer to oracle to furniture to a frozen white body at the front of the herd, ahead of where it died, looking back.

They eat iron and make rust and eat rust. They take each other apart to keep from fusing. They assemble their children out of their grandparents and cough up hearts for strangers. They keep each other liquid in a cold that wants them mineral, keep each other loud in a silence that freezes whatever it enters, keep moving because the water under them remembers every shape they have ever made and will not let any of it go.

The basin is finished becoming flat. What lives in it is not.

It is still becoming animal, becoming machine, becoming weather — shoulder to shoulder, part to part, grinding gently against itself in the twilight, steam rising from the pits where the next version of everything is trying, experimentally, to pump.
