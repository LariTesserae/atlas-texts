At first you would mistake it for ground.

That is the correct way to meet it. A low dome of peat among other domes, silver-green and copper-green, beaded with water, breathing a little steam in the cold morning. Ferns grow on it. A flowering rosette has taken root near its highest point, and tiny frogs are clicking, very softly, in the pool gathered in its hollow.

Then the dome moves.

Not quickly. Nothing up here is quick except falling water and light. It shifts its weight from one side to the other, the moss ripples, the pool tilts and steadies again, and somewhere underneath a foot the size of a dinner plate comes down on splayed, webbed toes, presses the peat flat without breaking it, and lifts with a faint, sucking sigh.

It is an animal. It has been standing asleep in the warm outflow of a boiler grove while you walked around it twice.

They are the plateau's great grazers, its waders, its moving islands. Low and immense, shoulder-high to a person when they finally stand clear, but built so lengthwise and so heavily bellied that they seem less tall than wide — a long back, a deep chest, legs short and columnar and furred to the waterline. Their heads stay down. That is their proper attitude: nose in the flow, mouth working, ears turning independently under their wet caps of hair, listening not so much for sound — sound dies too fast up here — as for pressure coming up through their feet.

Because they hear with the ground. They have to. Fog takes sight after ten meters. Moss takes voice after twenty. But peat carries the thump of the boiler trees for kilometers, grove answering grove, and it carries their own kind back to them: a deliberate stamp, a chest-deep pulse made by swallowing air and letting it out against a closed throat, felt rather than heard. A herd spread out over an entire lake district, invisible to one another in the mist, will all turn at once toward a warm seam that has begun breathing faster for spring. Calves learn the map before they learn the country. Which they must, because the country never changes and the map never stops: warm-cool, warm-cool, following the slow seasonal migration of heat under the rock.

Their bodies are made for a place with no dry season and no bare earth.

The outer coat is long, dark, hollow-haired, shedding water in sheets; the undercoat is so dense that the skin beneath it can stay almost dry for days at a time, and in that warm dark a whole felt of lesser life lives — mites that eat shed skin, beetles with magnetite jaws that pry out burrowing larvae, a pale threadlike fungus that keeps the hide from rotting in the perpetual wet. Over the shoulders and along the spine the hair gives way, in adults, to something stranger: overlapping plates of keratin gone almost mineral, iron-dark, copper-green at the edges, thickened year by year out of what they eat and drink. They lick the orange seeps where the boiler trees have shed their bark-scales. They graze the silica grasses down to the crown and chew, with slow rotary molars, the paste the wheel-shelled mollusks make of leaf litter. They take in more metal than flesh can use. The surplus comes out in those plates, in the dense black pellets they drop in the outflow channels, in horns that are not quite horns — two low, blunt, branching growths above the eyes, layered like the trees' own bark, conductive to the core.

That is why lightning loves them, and why it does not kill them.

In electrical weather you can see a herd from a rise, if the fog thins: a dozen dark backs in a black lake, each faintly crowned, charge crawling blue-white from horn to horn and down through legs spread wide in the water, distributing itself pool to pool the way it does through wet moss and conductive sap. The plates tick as they cool afterward. The beetles come out to clean between them. For an hour after a storm the oldest animals glow very slightly at root of horn and along the spine, a stored blue-green leaking light, and night insects come to it, and small, soft-footed predators come to the insects, and the herd stands perfectly still and lets that lesser weather happen on its back.

The back is the point. An adult in its prime is an archipelago.

The spine is bowed into a shallow basin by a lifetime of its own weight. Rain gathers there. Wind-blown seed gathers there. Moss colonizes the shed plates, then ferns, then, in a really old cow, a stunted shrub or two, rooted not in the animal but in the peat that has accumulated on it, a hand's depth deep. Frogs live their whole lives in the dorsal pool — tadpole to thumbnail adult — and never touch the plateau proper. Orchids bloom out of season in the warmth that rises through the hide. When she moves from grove to grove, and she will walk for weeks without hurry, she is carrying isolated ponds across the divides between them, ferrying frog spawn and crab larvae and freshwater mollusks and seeds in her coat and in the mud packed between her toes. Species that could never cross ten dry meters of peat ridge — and up here, ridge is a relative term, but the inhabitants of a single leaf-pond are precise about it — cross whole watersheds on her.

Her calves are born in the shallows at a lake edge, onto floating mats thick enough to bear her forequarters. They come out able to swim and unable, for the first few days, to walk well; their feet are too soft, their legs fold. So they climb, using hooked milk-teeth and blunt claws, up the long hair of her flank and into the basin on her back, and ride there in warm water among the frogs, steaming gently, while she grazes nose-down around them. A calf that falls is not lost. The mat closes. The mother puts a foot down, waits for the vibration of its struggling to come up through the peat, and nudges with her nose. The moss-backed cats, cat-sized and patient, know this too, and wait at mat edges for exactly that moment; most old cows carry a frieze of pale scars low on the muzzle from teaching them otherwise.

They have no territory. Territory is meaningless in a place that is all edge, all surface, all process. What a herd owns — if the word can be used — is a sequence. This grove after that storm. This outflow while the crabs are pruning it, not after. This iron seep in the month when the plates are shedding and the larvae are fat between plate and trunk; the hammer-billed birds arrive then too, and work the fallen metal on their stone anvils, and the herd eats the overflow and licks what's beaten thin. Then on, in pulse-time, twenty minutes behind the boiler grove's exhalation, to the next. Young bulls who try to hold a place are simply left; the herd flows around them like water around a stone, and by the time they notice the thumps are receding through the peat, the fog has already taken the others.

They are long-lived because nothing about them is spent quickly. Seventy years, eighty, for a cow who learns the sequences well. Their lungs are enormous — the thin air does not trouble them because they never hurry and they breathe wet, through double nostrils that warm and saturate each breath before it goes deep, snorting out small twin jets of steam. Their blood runs rich and dark. They sleep standing in moving water so that their feet never quite settle and rot; they wake already facing the direction of the next warm ground, having turned, degree by degree in the night, like plants.

And when one dies, the plateau does not waste the event. It happens usually in water, in one of the black central lakes, far out on a mat. The body settles slowly. It does not bloat and drift as a carcass would in the lowlands; the cold and the chemistry forbid it. Fungi with metallic threads are in the hide within days, unspinning the copper from the horns, the iron from the spine-plates, leaving bright filaments for young boiler roots to find and graft to. The back, already an island, becomes one in earnest. Peat accretes. Shrubs take the shoulders. For a decade or a generation there will be a low, long island in that lake with an oddly regular outline, flowering out of season, warm at one edge where the last of the body-heat of decay still rises, and frogs clicking in the pool where the spine used to be.

It does not live on the plateau, exactly. It lives as the plateau does — by circulation, by ferrying, by taking in the raw and giving back the refined, by being rained on and conducting and shedding and seeding as it goes. Put your hand on its flank, if it permits you — and it might, having no category for you, neither predator nor weather — and you feel through the wet hair the same slow thump you feel through your boots from the trees.

Hers just answers a little sooner.
