At first you would say the bats live there, because at the far end they are so complete. But that isn't true, or not true enough. The seam is lived in the way a body is lived in — in layers, in zones, each tenant pressed so close to the next that the whole narrow mistake in the rock has learned a single, shared temperament.

It begins at the mouth, in the glare.

The lizards own the first meter. Dust-colored, almost the exact pink-grey of the fresh andesite, flattened as if the seam had made them in its own image. They lie with bellies spread on the ledges, legs splayed, chins down, only their throats moving — a quick, papery pulse, pulse, pulse, to shed heat they have worked all morning to gather. High-altitude lizards live thriftily; nights up here freeze even in summer, and their blood is only as warm as the rock they can find. Here the rock is never cold. The crusher house at their backs leaks heat through concrete all night, so they can start earlier and finish later than any lizard on the open plateau, and grow fat on accident.

They are territorial in inches. An older male — scarred along one flank, tail regenerated blunt and darker than the rest of him — holds the highest ledge where the sun strikes longest. Below him, younger animals stack in descending order of boldness, each with its crack to vanish into when a shadow crosses. They do not really flee anymore. A boot, a voice, a human shoulder scraping past sideways will get a lazy eye opened, a shift of a centimeter deeper into shade. They have learned the size of the place. Nothing with wings that eats them can turn in here. Nothing with teeth wants to come through the crusher roar. Flight would be wasted motion, and they do not waste motion.

Over their heads, under the slight overhang where the cut face leans out just enough to shed the rare rain, the cliff swallows have glued their world.

Their nests are retorts of mud and spit, gourd-shaped, the entrance a small dark mouth pointing down. The mud itself is imported — there is no wet earth on the plateau worth the name, so they work the sumps and the dust-settling ponds below the plant, carrying it up beakful by beakful mixed with grit and diesel sheen. It dries here the color of the yard. Inside, out of sight, the chicks keep up a chatter that the rock multiplies until it sounds like a much larger colony, a constant sewing-machine twitter that rises whenever a parent flicks in through the roar.

The swallows come and go as if the plant were a cliff in a storm they had evolved for. They time the conveyors. They cut between belts, ride the hot updraft off the exhaust ducting, snatch the moths and midges that gather stunned around the floodlights even in daylight. Their wings clap off walls a handspan apart without touching. When they leave, their droppings spatter the lizards' ledges, white over tan dust; when the lizards stir, they knock shed skins and beetle husks down into the deeper dark. Nothing is wasted because nothing can afford to be.

Deeper in, where you have to turn your shoulders and duck, and the light goes from glare to twilight to a brown dimness your eyes have to manufacture, the tenants get smaller and more numerous.

Darkling beetles pack the fissures in the day, black cases polished dull by dust, shoulder to shoulder like the bats further in, waiting for dark to move. Earwigs thread the same cracks, pincers folded. Wolf spiders sit with their long legs drawn tight against their bodies to fit the width of their chosen split in the rock, eyes catching any stray photon in a brief green shine. A female in late summer carries an egg sac almost as large as her abdomen, wedging herself so the sac does not scrape; when the spiderlings hatch they will ride her back in a living crust until the seam is too crowded even for them and they disperse on silk that the hot air immediately steals.

These are the seam's processors. Nothing grows in here — no lichen, no moss, no green shoot in a crack — so everything eats something that arrived from elsewhere and died, or was dropped. Moths blunder in at night, drawn across kilometers of cold plateau to the plant's white floodlights, batter themselves against the hot wall of the crusher house, and fall papery into the crack. Flies come for swallow droppings and bat guano and lay their eggs in it. Beetles come for the moths and the fly larvae. Spiders come for the beetles. Lizards come down, sometimes, as far as the light will let them, for the spiders. It is an import business, a food web hung entirely on the plant's waste heat and waste light, and it runs with the steadiness of a shift roster.

And then, where the seam widens into that wardrobe-sized pocket, the bats.

Mouse-eared bats, hundreds of them, in a single continuous mass. Not a colony arranged on a ceiling so much as a second ceiling made of animals. They hang head-down from the unweathered rock, hind feet gripping blast fractures no wider than a fingernail, wings folded tight and wrapped forward over their chests like cloaks, each back pressed to the next flank. Brown-grey fur gone tan at the tips with dust. Long ears — absurdly long when you see one alone — folded back or twitching upright in slow rotation, so that across the mass there are always a dozen ears up, listening, while the rest sleep.

Their heat together is not metaphorical. It lifts off them in a visible shimmer when cooler air is drawn in at shift change, and it smells — warm animal musk, old leather, the sweetish undertone of insects digested and re-digested. One bat shifting its grip sends a ripple through all of them, a slow readjustment shoulder to shoulder, claw to rock, that travels the width of the cluster and dies out. A pup born in the warm season clings first to its mother's belly, then, as it grows too heavy for flight, is parked in the densest part of the mass while she hunts, kept warm by other bodies on all sides. If it falls, it falls soft, onto guano.

The guano slope beneath them is their architecture as much as the rock is: dry pellets over powder over older, compressed powder, built at perhaps a centimeter a year, crumbling at the edges, warm all the way through from slow decomposition. It steams faintly on freezing mornings. It is also a larder — fly maggots move in its upper layers, beetles patrol its foot — so even the bats' waste feeds the seam that feeds, indirectly, the bats, when the beetles emerge at night and are taken on the wing at the mouth.

They have recalibrated fear, all of them, because the pocket allows nothing else. A bat colony in an open cave would pour out at a torch beam. Here pouring out would be suicide, a hundred wings trying to unfold in a space forty centimeters wide. So they watch instead. Torch off, movement slow, no voice — voice is useless over the crushers anyway — and they will let a human stand under them at arm's length, ears tracking, a thin social squeaking going on constantly among them, too high and too crowded to parse, like the sound of wet fingers on glass. It sharpens if dust falls on them. It settles again. The spiders hold. The lizards hold. Calm is not tameness here; it is physics, learned animal by animal and taught by the walls.

At dusk — or at what passes for dusk, which is less a darkening than the brief lull when the crushers idle for shift change and the constant pressure in your molars eases — the order of leaving asserts itself. The swallows make their last sorties and crowd into the mud retorts, heads out. The lizards go torpid on still-warm stone. Then the pocket begins to peel.

One bat drops, opens, finds the exit by a memory of turns laid down in complete blackness. Then another. Then a loose, unhurried stream of them, never a rush, each waiting for the airspace ahead to clear, banking out of the seam into the floodlit yard and gone almost at once into hunting circuits over the plant: around the light cones where moths orbit, along the conveyor gantries, low over the settling ponds where midges rise even on cold nights. High, thin calls bounce off steel and rock indifferently. By full dark the pocket is empty and somehow larger, the guano ticking as it cools, the spiders coming out of their cracks to reclaim the walls, the beetles beginning their slow patrol.

Before dawn, when the plateau outside is at its most murderously cold and wind is scouring dust across the yard in sheets, they return — one by one again, fur cold-smelling, bellies tight with insects — and rebuild the mass in reverse, claw finding fracture, wing folding over a neighbor's back, ripple after ripple settling until there is no bare rock to see. The lizards are already waiting for a heat that will not fail them. The swallows are already muttering in their sleep. The seam breathes out once more into the morning, hot and ammoniac and alive wall to wall, and holds.
