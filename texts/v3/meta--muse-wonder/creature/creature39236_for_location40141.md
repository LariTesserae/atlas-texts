Nothing in the Bellows Cup lives alone, and nothing in it is soft for long.

The lake is only fifty years old, but its inhabitants are not. They came up out of the mountain's older plumbing — out of the flooded fissures and lightless spring-chambers that fed the crack long before the summit fell open and made a bowl for the sky. That is why so many of them are blind, or nearly blind, and why those that have learned the new daylight still behave like creatures feeling their way through a cave. They were cave animals first. The sun is a recent accident above them. They have met it with shell, plate, mineral skin, and slowness.

Everything here eats precipitate, or eats something that did.

The base of it is the bacterial film on the young stone — amber, milk-pale, the color of old gunmetal — a living tarnish that pulls sulfur and dissolved iron out of the springwater and turns geology into food. Above that film, tier upon tier of bodies convert stone into more bodies. Their blood runs heavy with metal. Their shells are not the clean calcium white of seashore things; they are banded with rust, blackened with manganese, flecked copper-bright where excess metal has been worked out of the flesh and laid down in the armor. Old individuals look less grown than manufactured and then weathered. Young ones are almost translucent, and darken as the lake writes itself into them.

At the top, in the hammered silver ceiling of the surface, live the raft-snails.

They hang upside-down from the underside of the water's skin, each on a broad, rippling foot that spreads its weight so the surface tension holds. Their shells are shallow domes, hydrophobic along the rim, and they lock edge to edge with their neighbors until whole rafts of them dimple the light. They are the lake's first gasket. When wind — felt here only as a disturbance traveling down through pressure — worries at the surface, the rafts tighten. When the floor-plate lifts on its seven-minute breath and the whole Cup swells, they loosen just enough to let the meniscus move without tearing. They graze the surface film where bacteria gather against the air, and their feet are always busy, always adjusting, hundreds of small muscular hands holding the roof on the water from beneath.

Just below them turn the millers.

A miller is a fist-sized spiral that has decided to live inside its own pump. Water enters the mouth of its turreted shell, passes over sieving combs, and leaves behind carrying everything too fine to eat in a slow exhalation of white silt. They rotate as they feed — not swimming so much as screwing themselves gently through the water column, click, pause, click, the vanes of the shell catching and releasing. The sound is everywhere and never synchronized: a rain of small mechanical ticks at different depths. Much of the Cup's famous clarity is their work. They eat bacterial floc and mineral grit, digest the living part, and rain the rest down as flour-fine sediment that the other animals will use to plug the mountain's leaks. A miller's shell grows in visible increments, one pale band for every season of good spring flow, and when it dies the empty spiral does not dissolve. It sinks, rolls into a hollow with others, and becomes part of the lakebed's bright rubble.

The walls belong to the grazers.

These are the giants of the place, if anything so slow can be called giant: dog-sized, many-legged, plated across the back in overlapping shields of dark, glass-scarred mineral. Their undersides are a crowded engineering of legs, each tipped not with a claw but with a blunt pad that can grip unworn andesite without being cut by it. They have eyes only in the sense that two pits at the front of the head distinguish light from dark and the looming shadow of a predator from the shadow of rock. They navigate mostly by taste and vibration, through the soles of their feet.

A grazer does not bite. Inside its mouth are two opposed stones — true stones, swallowed young and held in muscular pockets, replaced over a lifetime as they wear — and it feeds by pressing those stones together over a mouthful of bacterial crust and volcanic glass. Grind, shift, grind. The rasp travels through the water and through the stone and into the bones of anything listening. Glass becomes paste. Sulfur bacteria become flesh. Iron becomes plate. The grazers are why the walls, for all their sharpness, bear broad polished paths where generations have worked the same rich fractures; their own feeding smooths the mountain just enough for their young to climb.

They are courted the way they eat: slowly, by abrasion. In the mating season two grazers circle one another on a wall for hours, plates striking plates, feet testing, the stones in their mouths clicking out a rhythm that answers the millers without ever joining them. Now and then a struck edge throws a visible spark in the dim water. By the end there is a clean ring scoured in the bacterial film around them, and the female has cemented a clutch of mineral-dark eggs into the warmest part of the fracture, where the spring breath keeps them from freezing.

Deeper down and quieter are the piston-eels.

Each lives almost its whole life in a single tube of secreted lime fixed to the floor or a low wall. The tail stays anchored. The long blind head drives out and back, out and back, once a minute in ordinary times — a slick, pale piston with gill sieves flared behind the skull, pumping springwater through itself to strip oxygen and food from the current. When the central plate lifts and bubbles race up from the seams, every eel in the Cup strokes at once, pulled into synchrony by the pressure wave, and for a moment the lake has a single pulse. Their tubes grow at the mouth as the animal lengthens, ring upon ring, and the oldest stand knee-high off the floor, slightly bent by years of the mountain's movement. They are also living caulk: the lime they secrete creeps out over the cracks around them, sealing the fractures they feed beside, generation stacked on generation until some eastern leaks are plugged more by eel-tube than by stone.

Among them, clustered on the floor plates, the hammer-mussels wait with their shells agape on dark filaments. They are not dramatic until they are. A change in pressure — a plate shifting, a predator venting ballast overhead, the first tremor of the seven-minute lift — and every mussel in a cluster slams shut in the same instant. The sound is one enormous submerged *clang* that rolls around the bowl and comes back off the rim. That closing is defense, feeding, and signaling all at once: it traps a mouthful of enriched water, protects the soft body inside, and tells the whole lake that the pressure has turned. Their shells are thick beyond any ordinary need, knobby with coppery nodules, and they too work themselves into the leaks of the floor, packed so tight in places that the basalt plates move against mussel shell rather than against each other, and squeal.

Leaping between the walls, rarely touching bottom, are the gear-backed crawlers — hand-sized, bright-eyed newcomers to the light, the Cup's restless children. Their front legs are ordinary jointed legs. The rear pair is not. At the hip and knee, hard teeth mesh tooth-for-tooth with a precision that looks machined because, functionally, it is: cog against cog, so torque passes through the leg without slip on wet glass. A crawler crouches, the gears engage, and it throws itself from stone to stone with a dry click and absolute certainty of footing on landing. They scavenge what the grazers drop. They steal eggs not yet hardened. They flee the electric fields by trajectories calculated in their legs. Broken gear-teeth are shed with the molt, and the lakebed is littered with perfect tiny cogwheels that were once parts of living animals.

Over all of them drift the predators.

Iron-dark, rivet-shaped, no bigger than a loaf, they do not swim in any way a fish would recognize. They hang in the water column on adjustable ballast bladders, flooding a chamber to sink, venting it with a small flank-hiss to rise, turning by minute shifts of internal weight. Their mouths are short presses lined with crushing plates. They hunt by patience refined to geology: one will spend an hour positioning itself, bubble by bubble, directly above a grazer feeding on a wall, and then simply empty its bladders and descend. No chase. No strike. A slow, irresistible coming-down, the press closing over the grazer's back while the grazer's many padded feet scramble for purchase and the stones in its mouth grind once, twice, against nothing. Others are fast enough to get clear. The ones that are not become dark meat, then shell fragments, then floor.

And threading the rock spires at mid-depth, the bright fish — the only quick things here, and quick only in one way. They lay an electric field between two points of stone and hover inside their own invisible architecture, pale bodies pulsing faint blue at the gill edges. Anything small that blunders through the field takes the discharge whole: the water flashes white, the smell of burnt copper blooms, and the stunned body sinks turning into the fish's waiting mouth. Their arcs sometimes leap wrong — to a shell, a wet fracture, a heap of metallic debris — and in that instant of accidental welding, dead shell fuses to dead shell on the lakebed. That is how the Cup builds its strange reef: not coral, not nest, nothing intended, but layer upon layer of gear-teeth, miller spirals, mussel valves, crusher stones, and copper nodules arc-cemented into a floor that is quite literally made of former inhabitants. To walk on the bottom of this lake would be to walk on the compacted machinery of the dead.

Every seven minutes, all of it answers the mountain.

The plate rises a hand's width. Bubbles race. Mussels clang. Eels stroke together. Millers stutter and resume. Grazers brace with all their feet and keep grinding through the lift, because the bacterial film tastes richest just after the spring pulses through the cracks. The predators ride the swell up without moving a fin. The raft-snails loosen and tighten, loosening and tightening, holding the ceiling.

None of them cooperate. None of them needs to. The snail holds the surface where it feeds. The eel seals the crack that feeds it. The miller shits silt into the leak it hovers over. The mussel wedges itself into the moving floor because the moving floor delivers food. But added together, body by body, habit by habit, they are a single distributed creature with ten thousand mouths: a refinery that eats dissolved metal and excretes bright nodules in the hollows, a pump that keeps a small sea from draining out through a shattered rim, a foundry whose products fuse themselves into its foundation.

That is who lives in the Bellows Cup. Not visitors to a lake. The lake's own organs, walking around inside it — armored, mineral, patient animals, breathing at the speed of stone, holding their improbable water up above the clouds one slow stroke at a time.
