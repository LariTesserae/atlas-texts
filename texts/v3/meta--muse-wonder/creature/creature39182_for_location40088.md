At first you would think they were only people, perhaps people who had become very quiet.

That is the first mistake newcomers make in Vael-Mora, and it is a kind one. The inhabitants encourage it. They speak your language, after a fashion. They wear clothes — loose, pale sleeves that tighten almost imperceptibly as the humidity climbs, trousers cut high above the ankle for the wet meadows, soft-soled shoes everywhere except indoors, where the floors still remember shells. They have hands and markets and arguments about whose turn it is to tend the cistern marsh. If you arrive through one of the folded arches, one of them will be waiting on the other side to offer you water that tastes faintly of copper and green leaves, and they will not comment when you drink it too fast.

Only afterward do you notice that they were waiting *before* you arrived, turned slightly toward the place where your shadow would appear.

They live by anticipations. The city taught them, or they taught the city, until there is no clean seam between the two. A Vaeline wakes a minute before the eleven-minute cooling, without any bell. You can see it happen in a tower at the dimmest hour: one by one, in windows kilometers apart, figures rise and go bare-armed onto the balconies as the temperature falls. Their skin prickles all at once. It is not cold — nothing in the basin is ever really cold — but it is *less warm*, and that difference is their dawn, their bell, their rainfall. They stand in the condensation with their faces lifted while the buildings around them open thousands of ceramic petals and drink. Some of them collect the water on their own shoulders without thinking about it, the way you might warm your hands at a fire. Children try to catch it on their tongues. Very old people simply let it bead in their hair, which they wear long and unbound in the wet hour, and shake out afterward over the balcony plants.

Their eyes have adjusted before their manners did. Under the lattice there is light enough for color, but never a source for it, no point in the sky to squint against. So they do not squint. Their pupils stay wide, black and reflective as meadow water, and their gaze has the disconcerting steadiness of the wader birds in the cisterns — not staring, you realise, but locating. They identify each other at a distance less by faces than by a compound of posture, scent, and the particular warmth each body gives off into the damp air. In the temperature-theatres this has become art; in daily life it is simply courtesy. To approach a Vaeline directly, face-first and speaking, is faintly clumsy, like grabbing a hand before it has been offered. You arrive at an angle. You let your shadow or your warmth arrive first. You pause. They make room for you in the order they sensed you.

They move at the speed the place permits, which is to say they have made a philosophy out of plant-speed. This does not mean they are slow. Through an arch they can cross twenty kilometers between one breath and the next, and they do it with the absent-minded ease of stepping into the next room — checking the etched mark the way you would glance at a house number, turning their head slightly against the pressure in the sinuses. It means that between arches, hurry has lost most of its uses. If a branch has grown across the orchard path in the night, heavy with unripe plum-dark fruit, a Vaeline does not push through or curse the gardeners. She reads it. The district is replacing a root main; the district prefers her elsewhere; the fruit, in a week, will be an apology. She goes the long way through the fern forest, trailing one hand at leaf-height to feel which fronds are still curled into their night-cups and which have already been emptied. She may be an hour late. No one receives this as lateness. Arrival, like water, is understood to gather where conditions allow.

Their voices are pitched to the city's acoustics, low and unhurried, with long vowels that carry pleasantly through ceramic halls. They leave pauses in conversation that a newcomer rushes to fill, not understanding that the pause itself is a place where the other person may be listening for something: a root clicking through warm stone, a reservoir ticking full, the faint premonition of a wall changing its mind. They almost never shout. Partly this is breeding. Partly it is experience. Everyone in Vael-Mora has, at least once in adolescence, shouted something furious across a courtyard and had it returned to them hours later, softened and lowered, in a bedroom or a bathhouse or beside a parent's deathbed — their own young voice arriving like an uninvited memory. They learn early to speak as if their words will outlive their mood. Many of them develop a habit of not finishing sentences that have turned unkind. The city will not finish them either, exactly. It keeps only the volume it was given. But why risk the echo.

Names sit lightly on them for the same reason. A district is renamed when its cistern dries, and so, often, is a person. A woman you meet beside the Eastern Cisterns may introduce herself by a name that means *third cup after cooling*, and apologise, in the same breath, for the name you were told to ask for — that was her dry-season name, from the year the reservoirs went low, and she answers to it still but with a small delay, as if hearing it through water. Children collect names the way children elsewhere collect scars. Old maps embarrass them, not because they are wrong but because they are too confident. To name a thing permanently, in Vael-Mora, is to pretend it has finished becoming. Most things have not. The land is younger than the grandparents.

You can tell which district someone was raised in by watching them enter a room. Those from the ordinary towers walk as if up were a promise. Those from the three old Experimental Generosity districts — where gravity was laid in as a municipal kindness and never quite laid straight — come in testing each step with the ball of the foot, light, lovely, faintly apologetic even on reliable floors, one hand drifting toward a rail that is not there. They are teased for it all their lives and marry, disproportionately, dancers and maintenance workers. The maintenance crews themselves are a kind of citizen-priesthood, though they would hate both words. They wear grey-green that darkens with damp. They can stand before a tower wall for the length of an afternoon, doing what looks like nothing and is, in fact, a negotiation conducted in patience: waiting for living wood and layered ceramic to settle on a shape that will hold a doorway, a balcony, a child's weight in ten years' time. A good negotiator is recognised not by tools but by temperament. Buildings open for them a little sooner. Arches bud near their houses.

At home — and home is a word with more architecture in it than elsewhere — they keep their dew-rooms the way other peoples keep altars or libraries. A small, deep-shaded chamber. A beloved object at its centre. In one house a copper bowl, dented where it was dropped in some earlier, harder place before the basin rose. In another, a stone from the old coastline. In more than a few, a grandmother's preserved hand, grey and perfect and unembarrassing, resting palm-up on a cloth. Through the cooling hour, water condenses out of the warm nothing onto that surface and runs, drop by drop, into a channel worn smooth by use. They make tea with it. They serve it to guests without explanation, and to the dying without ceremony. It tastes of almost nothing, which is the point. Who you love, in Vael-Mora, is partly who you are willing to drink time with.

They are not mystical about this. They are not mystical about most things. They built the lattice, or their grandparents did, and then they forgot some of the mathematics on purpose because the lattice worked better as weather than as doctrine. They print tower-segments in chambers of held lightning and regard the process with the fond irritation you might feel toward an elderly relative: powerful, necessary, not to be stared at directly. If you ask how the fold in the arch works, a Vaeline will explain politely and completely for as long as you seem to want, and nothing she says will be false, and at the end you will understand no more than that the city is folded, politely, in a few places, and that it is rude to stand exactly in the fold on a humid day. Some knowledge they have converted into etiquette. It keeps better there.

What they fear is not darkness or distance but unaccommodated space. This is why they go, deliberately and a little bravely, to the uninstructed forest beyond the last tower — young people especially, and lovers, and the recently bereaved: to walk for an afternoon where no path will reroute itself for their comfort and no arch will bud knuckle-like from the soil when they are tired. They come back quieter, scratched by ordinary branches, obscurely proud. The city that anticipates their thirst and shade and shorter way home has made them gentle, and some of them worry, in the way healthy creatures worry, that gentleness arranged from outside is a kind of sleep. But by evening-that-is-always-evening they are home again, smelling of mineral steam and green sap, and the tower is opening its petals around them, and someone has left a cup in the place their hand will naturally reach for when the temperature drops.

And perhaps this is the truest thing to say about them: they do not believe they live *in* Vael-Mora, exactly. If you press them — late, kindly, over dew-tea, when the greenish stored light has come on in the towers and each window is its own separate star lying low among the trees — they will correct you with no heat at all. The towers are where they sleep. The orchards and cisterns and folded arches are where they conduct the practical business of being bodies.

The city itself is smaller and briefer and much more beautiful. It is the film of water that gathers each morning in a clay hollow that will be dry by afternoon. It is the reflection caught for an hour in a leaf-cup, veil and towers upside down and close together, everything the basin keeps apart briefly allowed to touch. It is the moment, just after the cooling, when building and garden and skin are all damp at once and indistinguishable in purpose — all of them drinking, all of them holding light without shadow, all of them about to let it go.

The inhabitants stand around those moments the way other peoples stand around a hearth. They point them out to strangers. They teach their children not to drink from them.

Then the water evaporates, the greenest patch remains, and they get on — slowly, attentively, at plant-speed — with living in the part around it.
