The inhabitants are outnumbered by what they tend, and they know it. That is the first fact of life here.

There is a crew — small enough that everyone knows whose boots are whose when they are lined up to dry, small enough that a conversation begun on the first terrace in the morning can be picked up again, word for word, on the fourth terrace in the afternoon, because the flood has moved both people up. A dozen, perhaps, on a shift. Fewer at night. No one was born here. No one has died here. No one, yet, quite *lives* here in the way people live in a hamlet, though the place is the size of one. They are its first tenants and its midwives at once, and they are as new and self-conscious about it as the soil.

You can recognize them by their adaptation to water that is not weather.

They wear rubber boots even on the dry hours, shirts that go dark between the shoulder blades by ten, trousers rolled to exactly the height the standing water reaches on a flooded terrace. They carry everything. Hand tools in crates, coiled hoses on shoulders, lunch boxes, laminated checklists gone a little curly at the edges from the steam — nothing is left on the ground, because the ground, in rotation, disappears under an ankle of shining water. They move ahead of the sluices the way shore people move ahead of a tide, unhurried because they have memorized it. The clunk of the electric gates is a clock. So is the sigh of the grating when the pumps underneath start to pull a terrace dry. They feel that through the soles before they hear it, and shift their weight, and step up a level.

Their work makes them both industrial and embarrassingly tender. In the morning they check pressures and relay switches and winch up another soil-pod from the loading bay, stenciled numbers still bright on the steel. An hour later they are on their knees in a flooded row, pollinating flowers by hand with soft brushes because the pollinators have not been allowed to arrive yet, going blossom to blossom with a concentration that turns, without anyone commenting on it, ritual. No leaf is turned without a glance. Vines that found a handrail in the night — and they always find it in the night — are unwound finger by finger and coaxed toward a trellis wire. A tray that shifted when leaned on is shouldered back true. At every threshold between terrace-systems they stop, automatically now, and wash their boots, so that no soil from one young ecology is tracked into another. The ecology is still too young, in their protocol, to be mixed with itself.

They still read the lists out loud. That will not last. Already they are developing the private language that replaces lists: terraces called not by number but by flood-time, pipes called by the sound they make when they expand at noon, the cistern simply The Top, as if it were a hill. They name things provisionally, half-ashamed of the affection. A place this new embarrasses people who care for it; it has not given them permission to be sentimental yet, so sentiment leaks out sideways, in how carefully they redirect a stem, in how quiet they go when something else makes a noise.

Because most of the real population cannot move.

The plants are the true inhabitants here, tens of thousands of them, and they are all, unnervingly, the same age. They were not grown in succession the way a forest grows itself, elders and seedlings together. They were planted all at once, at full intention, a whole cohort lowered into made soil in black trays and capillary mats. Broad-leafed things whose leaves have outrun their stems and nod too heavily in the dawn rain from the vault. Ferns packed in the wettest corners, frond against frond, as if for company. Vines in a permanent exploratory adolescence — testing pipe-rack, irrigation boom, tool handle, boot left a minute too long — and being gently, daily, educated. Young trees stand in deep pods with their root balls still wrapped in pale fabric, like arrivals who have not yet unpacked their bags, who have not been told whether they may put their feet all the way down.

Under them, smaller and earlier, the pioneers who were not invited on any manifest. A bright dusting of moss on the north side of the curing concrete. A slick of algae filming the rills and making the water look older than it is. They are the first inhabitants who chose this place, however blindly, and the crew regards them with disproportionate respect. What arrives on its own feels like a promise that the rest might one day be real.

Animal life is mostly still a rumor, a plan, or an accident, and therefore each actual animal has achieved a fame out of all proportion.

There are the moths, pale ones, no one knows from where — eggs in matting, a crack in a service vent — who gather at the work-lights after the sun goes level and amber through the panels. They make the night shift less sterile. They flutter in the beams while someone checks a valve, and in that moment the place stops looking like a facility and looks, briefly, inhabited in the ordinary, dusty way inhabited places are.

There are the bees, who live here and do not live here yet. Their boxed hives are stacked by the airlock in quarantine, sealed, humming faintly if you put your ear close — a whole future citizenry on hold, audible through wood. The crew, passing with their brushes, sometimes talk past them or to them. When they are finally released, the hand-pollination will end, and the sound of the mornings will change forever. Everyone knows that will be a threshold. No one has heard birdsong layered here; now and then a single high bird crosses the translucent roof as a moving shadow and is gone, a visitor from the world below the clouds, reminding them how high their garden is hung.

And there is the frog.

One frog. He — they have decided he — must have come as an egg in a shipment, a contaminant the sterilization missed, and he has taken up residence in the rill on the third terrace, where the water runs clear over new concrete. He sings at irregular intervals, not at dusk like a proper frog chorus should, but at odd, startling moments in the working day: a lone, clear note in all that green quiet and soft pump-thud. Work stops for it, the way work stops for a bell. People turn their heads. Someone, inevitably, marks the time. He has a log, this frog, kept with more fidelity than some of the pressure gauges. He is fed, surreptitiously, on moths. He is stepped around during floods by men and women in heavy boots carrying crates, who would rather get wet to the knee themselves than disturb the one wild thing that has volunteered to certify that this machine might become a landscape.

At night the crew thins to a watch of two or three, moving slow under the fans, listening to dripping settle into the leaves and condensation bead bright overhead for tomorrow's small artificial rain. The plants go on breathing out moisture in the dark. The pumps rest in rotation. The bees hum in their boxes. Somewhere below them, far below the deck and its braced legs and its grating void full of shadow, cloud moves under their feet and valleys go blue and invisible.

They have not settled. The roots have not bound the soil, the vines have not been allowed their first choices, the trees have not shed their wrappings, nothing has died in quantity enough to make leaf litter and feed the next thing. The human inhabitants walk causeways between floods with their tools on their shoulders, washing their boots at thresholds, brushing pollen flower to flower, listening for a single frog — caretakers of an population that will, if they succeed, outlive their checklists and their names for the terraces and need them less and less.

For now, though, everything that lives here needs to be carried, coaxed, released, or forgiven for arriving early. And the crew, damp-backed and careful, moves one terrace ahead of the water, learning in their bodies the rhythm of a place that is learning, with them, how to be inhabited.
