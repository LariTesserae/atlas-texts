Almost no one lives there, and for a long time you might think no one does.

You can swim a flooded valley from one basalt shore to the other, moving storey by storey through the lily forests, pushing aside the dark water-leaves of trees that were flowering on dry land before the damming, and see no sign. A hollow pressed in the silt, already softening at the edges. A vine curtain tied — too regularly to be accident — into a loose knot at just the height where a wave, arriving the next morning, will pass through it and not tear it. Orange fruit ripening underwater, heavy and unpicked, except that one has been opened very neatly along its seam and closed again empty.

Then, late in the day, the doubled note. Low, unhurried, from across the crater. And an answer, much later, from somewhere you cannot place, because it had to travel up through twenty feet of green water and leaf before it reached the air.

They live at the boundary. That is the first true thing about them, and the least sufficient.

Their heads, most of the time, are in the thin wind. Their bodies are in the warm lake. They hang there in a vertical rest that is neither swimming nor standing, feet drifting well above the drowned forest floor or just brushing the uppermost floating leaves, hair moving in the dry air while their shoulders move, if they move at all, in water that has not yet decided to become a current. At noon they rise a little to cool themselves. At night, when the altitude reasserts itself and the emergent crowns tick and shiver, they sink until only the crowns of their own heads break the surface, and then not even that. They sleep wholly under, in the blood-warm dark among the tree trunks, breathing so seldom and so slowly that a watcher from above would take them for another pale shape the flooding had failed to kill.

They do have lungs — large ones, quiet ones. A child surfaces for air the way the forearm-long salamander-things do, rolling one eye first above the waterline to check the light, taking a breath without haste, subsiding without a ripple worth naming. An adult at rest can stay below through a long conversation conducted entirely in touch along a vine, or through the slow work of re-weaving a floating mat, and come up at dusk with bubbles still lodged, unrisen, in the folds of their clothing and the hollows of their collarbones, each one filmed faintly green.

Seen on a rare dry ridge, out of their element, they look wrong in a different way than a swimmer out of water usually looks. They are tall, because most of their lives their spines have not had to carry them, and thin in the long-boned manner of the wading bird that works the travertine alone. Their legs are longer in proportion than their arms; they place their feet with that same exaggerated, ash-grey care among the obsidian shards, not from fear of cutting themselves — their soles are like wet horn — but from an unwillingness to disturb silt that may take a week to settle clear again. Their skin below the throat is darker than above it, and grows darker with age, waxed and almost leaf-like, patched in places with the fine red algae that furs the submerged trunks. It does not seem to trouble them. Some of the oldest have algae living in the hair at their napes and along their forearms in definite, tended patterns, as another people might wear braiding.

Above the waterline they are paler, drier, feathery-looking. Their hair bleaches almost white in the hard noon light and lifts in the wind into a fine, separate weather. The contrast is so exact that from a distance, at the amber hour when lake takes sky's color exactly, you see only a small pale crown floating disembodied on the reflection, and below it — if you are also under, in green dusk — a long dark body completing itself downward like the reflection of an island completing a mountain.

They are not numerous. There may be fewer of them across the whole interrupted ocean than there are emergent peaks. The flooding is too recent and the climbs from below too sheer for anything like a migration to have happened; their own stories, insofar as stories can be extracted from people who may take an afternoon to finish a sentence begun at breakfast — the first words exhaled underwater into a hollow lily stalk, the rest spoken to the air hours later when they happen to surface near you — suggest that their grandparents, or perhaps some ancestor so remote the word grandparent is a convenience, came up a waterfall that no longer exists, following vapor, and found the warm sea waiting and the animals not yet arrived. They stayed because the cold of the high air, which would have killed them exposed on the ridges, stopped at the surface. Because the fruit ripened without season. Because down among the trees it was already, impossibly, sheltered.

They did not build on the islands. The islands are for the dead, for drying things, for the few necessary fires made from aerial branches whose smoke goes straight up into extraordinary thinness and can be seen, a single thread, from three valleys away — which is how you know someone has died, or been born, or decided to marry, the difference being in how long the fire is kept. Daily life happens lower. They make their dwellings, if dwelling is the word, inside the architecture the forest already provides: a place where three drowned trunks lean together and their submerged branches make a rough, leafed room, its ceiling the underside of the surface itself, bright with trapped bubbles and upward-fallen leaves rotting in the light. They enlarge it by persuading vines down from the aerial canopy and anchoring them to root again in the silt. They reinforce the floating mat above until rain pools on it an inch deep, and in that warm second lake, no longer than a body, they keep their smallest children and their most delicate tools and their seeds.

Their children are born underwater, in the green silence, and are not brought to the air at all for the first days — only lifted, now and then, in cupped hands just to the boundary, so the scalp learns wind while the throat stays warm. They learn the two foliages of the world before they learn any distinction between them. They learn that leaves may fall up. That seeds sink on parachutes of mucilage and must be followed down, at a child's pace, an afternoon for ten feet, if you want to know where the next grove will be. That a bubble is not necessarily going anywhere; it may lodge in the underwater canopy and stay all season, growing its green skin, and you may mark a month by it, or leave messages against it, or, when very young, treat the inverted silver meadow overhead as a fruit orchard belonging to the underside of the world.

Work, for all of them, is mostly a form of anticipation.

They tend the travertine lips at the hot springs, breaking a lip here, encouraging it with a placed stone there, so that a pool rises an inch this year and a saddle floods in their grandchildren's time. They plant the dark water-leaves of young emergent trees well above the present waterline, on speculation — tying saplings, already putting out waxy underwater foliage into mere humid air, along shores that are still dry rock, because the plateau is still tilting and the dame is still rising and what is ankle-deep this decade will be a double-canopied forest thing in fifty years. They clear channels between crater lakes not for boats, which they do not have or need, but for the slow currents, meters per day, that carry warmth and fallen seeds and news from valley to valley. A channel cleared this spring may bring a stranger's doubled call, undistorted, by autumn.

They eat sparingly, and mostly from the submerged abundance that nothing else has arrived to contest: the splitting orange fruit, eaten where it ripens so the seeds drop from the mouth straight into their slow descent; new lily discs, silica-crisp, bitter as mineral water; the transparent crustaceans grazed by hand from algae mats in such numbers that a meal is less a hunting than a skimming. They are not, so far as can be told, incapable of killing — the obsidian they work, underwater, knapped by pressure between two stones so the shards do not scatter but sink in neat fans, would cut a reed or a throat with the same glass ease — but meat has not yet become an idea for them. The one wading bird is known individually to everyone within three passes. It has a name that is also the word for *patience on one leg*. No one would eat the other regular at the table.

Because they are so few, their social life has the stretched, attended quality of the lake's own sound. A courtship consists largely of agreeing, over several weeks of swimming, to be in the same flooded forest. A disagreement is conducted by moving one's sleeping place to a different cluster of trunks and waiting for the current, rediverted by everyone's daily passage, eventually to bring the other person news of the new address. They gather fully perhaps once in a generation, when a fresh lava levee dams a wholly new valley and the whole plateau audibly — which is to say, over a month of ticks and bloops and rising drips — prepares to extend its sea. Then they come in from the crater lakes, walking the bottom in the manner of the moon, pushing gently between stems nearly touching, their heartbeats the loudest thing in their own ears, to stand along the new black shore and help the water in: planting ahead of it, rooting vines ahead of it, teaching trees that have never been wet how to put out a second kind of leaf.

Old age, among them, is an increasing permission to stop distinguishing. The oldest no longer bother surfacing except at evening. Their bodies have taken on, in patches, the rigidity of the silica-fed plants; not a disease, or not spoken of as one, but a slow mineral patience in the joints that makes bottom-walking easier than swimming and stillness easier than either. They choose a trunk — often one where they played as children when it was only waist-deep — and over years they stay near it, then against it, then within the tangle of its rooting vines, surfacing less, eating less, the algae patterns on their arms spreading into something less like decoration and more like occupancy. Children leave fruit within reach and speak to them through hollow horsetail stems, jointed, dripping inside, that carry a voice down remarkably clear. Whether the very old are still answering, or whether the doubled note heard late across a crater, attended to by the whole plateau, is simply the lake moving air through those stems at the temperature change — this is one of their few disputed questions, and they dispute it enjoyably, over years.

They bury no one. When the stillness is complete they carry — walk, along the bottom, bearing a body grown strangely light with trapped plant air — the dead up onto a basalt island, the only sustained climb of a life, and lay them in dry wind among black stone that has never been underwater, under the smaller, paler, feathery crowns of the emergent trees. It reverses everything and is meant to. A life spent with the head in the air and the heart in the water ends wholly in air at last, returning to the thin place it only ever visited, while below, at the waist of the nearest double-canopied tree, a bright raft of floating leaves closes over one more absence.

And afterwards the place, which was a plant place before them and is learning to be an animal place very slowly, does not look emptier. It looks, if anything, more inhabited: another hollow kept clear in the silt, another vine knotted at the right height, another grove putting out speculative dark leaves above a shoreline not yet flooded, waiting for grandchildren who will hang with their faces at the boundary and feel two climates at once, and find that perfectly ordinary, and never think to ask which of them is the surface and which is the sky.
