Nothing lives there, for a long time after you arrive. That is the first answer the Cup gives you, and it gives it completely.

You look for the tenant the way you look anywhere: for a body among the bodies, for movement crossing the stillness. A flicker in the fern shade. A disturbance in a pool. You listen for a call and an answering call. You find the plants spaced too far apart for a jungle, the pools too clean for fish, the air too empty for gnats, and you conclude — with a small, unreasonable ache — that the place has been left to itself.

Stay longer. Stay until your clothes have taken on the Cup's humidity and stopped changing, until your own breathing no longer sounds like an interruption. The tenant is not hidden. You have been mistaking its scale for the place, and its speed for weather.

It begins, if it begins anywhere you can point to, in the air at the height of the fern crowns.

That is where droplets appear without falling, brightening one by one on filaments you can barely see. You told yourself they were spider-silk without spiders, plant-hairs, a trick of mist catching on mist. Over days you see the pattern is not random. The filaments run from frond to frond, from the horsetails to the rim, from the great door-sized leaves down to the lips of the pools, a web so fine and so thoroughly wet that it is only ever visible where water has gathered on it. It is not built over the Cup. It is strung *through* it, in every direction, at every height, down into the sphagnum, down through the peat to where the stone sweats warm and cool by turns.

That is its body. Or the nearest thing to a body a mouthless thing requires.

It does not sit in the Cup. It is suspended in it, permeating it, a single filamentous creature coextensive with its bowl, each thread too fine to cast a shadow, the whole of it gathered nowhere and absent nowhere. When the mist thickens against the north wall in the morning, that is not weather passing over it. That is it, thickening. When by afternoon the fog has sunk to your ankles and the crowns stand clear and dripping, that is it settling lower, thread by thread, at a pace your eyes were not made to follow. Its circuit of the bowl takes a day. Its gesture — a frond bowing under gathered droplets, a dozen bright beads merging and going down together without a sound — takes an hour to complete.

The plants are not its food, and they are not its neighbours. It took you a month of watching a furled frond not change, and then change entirely, to understand: they are its organs, grown slowly outward from the filament-web, each one a different way of doing the only things it ever does. The tree-fern is a raised hand for catching light where the light falls straightest. The horsetails are chimneys, ribbed and weeping at the joints, drawing the mountain's warmth up from the peat and letting it sigh out into the cool spill of air from the rim. The low plant with leaves like doors is its great drinking skin — though drinking is the wrong word for a thing that never swallows. Water runs uphill along those veins toward the raised centre and gathers in a lens, and for as long as the lens holds, the leaf beneath it is magnified, green cell upon green cell, as if the creature were looking at itself very closely, very patiently, through the only eye it needs.

And the pools are where it rests its attention. Nearly circular. Descending one to another. Each holding the sky without a ripple until the overflow passes, an inch deep, silently, to the next. Nothing moves in them because they are not habitats. They are surfaces of exchange — stone to water to air to thread — kept glass-smooth by ten thousand years of the faintest possible touch, the way the rim was polished, the way the basins interlock. Erosion did not finish here. It was taken over. The creature learned the water's habits so completely that wearing away became a kind of grooming.

It feeds, if you must use the word, on everything that is not food. Warmth coming up through the ground enters the lower threads and travels, almost imperceptibly, into peat and pool and leaf. Light enters the fern crown and the thick-petalled flowers — those flowers open over weeks to no pollinator because they are not invitations; they are slow, perfumed exhalations, the creature spending a little of its gathered sweetness back into the air it breathes. Water enters everywhere at once: condensing on a filament, beading in a pore of basalt, sheeting down stone a finger's width a minute, rising again. Nothing is bitten. Nothing is torn, hunted, carried off, or buried. Fruit swells and drops and rots where it falls because rot, for this tenant, is not waste. It is a change of address. What softens in the peat rises later in a horsetail. What yellows intact at the bottom of a pool is, leaf by leaf, being read into the water.

It has no mouth, and it has never needed the idea of one. Its skin is all of its surface, and its surface is most of the Cup.

That is why there are no animals. It did not drive them out. There is simply nothing here arranged for an animal to use. No seed is offered without rotting first. No leaf tolerates a bite — not because it is poisoned, but because no bite has ever been part of the bargain the place makes; an insect crossing the rim into that warm, saturated air would find its wings filmed with condensation, its hum absorbed by moss, its hunger faced with surfaces that give up their substance only at the speed of dissolution, over seasons. Birds do not sing in because the rim cuts the song, but also because there is nothing below worth announcing. Over uncounted years the Cup and its tenant refined away every spare thing: noise, haste, appetite, excess. What remains is a circuit so closed and so thrifty that a second, hungrier life would be a leak in it.

It knows you are there the way water knows a new stone has been set in it.

Not at once. On the first day, perhaps, only as a local thickening: condensation forming on your skin and not running, because the filaments nearest you are learning your temperature. By the third day your footprints in the sphagnum fill with clear water at exactly the rate the pools overflow, neither faster nor slower, as if your weight had been entered into a very old ledger and balanced. Your warmth is taken up without greed. Your exhaled breath becomes, briefly, a slightly denser patch of mist near the ferns, and is politely dispersed around the whole bowl by evening. It does not approach you. It does not recede. Both words imply a front and a back, a creature that could be in one part of its home and not another. You are simply, for a while, an additional surface on which the Cup can condense.

And it remembers the last surface that was offered to it, down by the lowest pool.

The hammerstone fits a palm because a palm once taught it the shape. The obsidian scraper still keeps its edge under the algae because the filaments, decade after decade, have drawn the green film up to the tool's rim and no further — not preserving it, exactly; preservation implies intent toward the past. Rather, the tools became part of the circuit so slowly that they were given the same treatment as a smooth stone or a drowned leaf: touched constantly, altered almost never. Whoever knelt at that slab and ground something in the shallow depression — pigment, seed, medicine, nothing the Cup could use — was, for the length of a visit, condensed upon, learned, and let go. The tenant kept the shape of the work the way a pool keeps the sky: by holding still while it was reflected. Long after the hands went back over the rim, the depression went on filling with a spoonful of clear water, emptied only by evaporation, filled only by weeping stone. An offering bowl that offers its contents to the air.

If you speak, it will not answer, but the sound will not be wasted. The moss will take the consonants. The pools will take the vibration and return their surfaces to smoothness a fraction sooner than stillness alone would manage. It has no ear, but hearing, like drinking, is something it does with its entire extent. Your voice becomes a brief pressure on ten thousand wet threads. Perhaps that is registered, at the creature's timescale, the way you would register a single droplet cooling your wrist: not a message, but a confirmed aliveness, somewhere in the shared medium.

It cannot leave. This is not a sadness in it — sadness, too, would be a kind of spending — but a fact of its construction, as plain as the rim. Outside the bowl the wind is thin and bright and cutting, and a body made of suspended moisture and patient filament would be torn to invisibility in an hour, dried on the basalt, carried off over the cloud-sea. Inside, it is effectively immortal, not because it resists death but because it has arranged to do almost nothing that requires repair. The mountain keeps it warm. The rim keeps it small. The water keeps it wet. In return it keeps the mountain's warmth from venting, the rim from crumbling, the water from stagnating. Each is the other's condition. It may be that the Cup grew its tenant. It may be that the tenant, thread by thread over ten thousand years, grew the Cup into the only shape that could hold it. By now the distinction has rotted down, softened, become soil, come up again as moss.

When you finally climb out — hungry, as it never is; noisy with your own heart, as it never has to be — nothing follows you to the lip. The mist does not reach after you. The fronds do not turn. But on your last evening, if the light is right, you may see the whole bowl at once as you first saw it from above, and for a moment the spaced plants, the linked pools, the weeping walls, the bright beads appearing in empty air will resolve into a single posture: not a face, not a figure, but the unmistakable arrangement of a living thing at rest in the only room it has ever occupied, breathing once a day, growing once a season, touching everything it owns with everything it is.

It was never empty.

It was only ever too slow, too fine, and too complete to need a mouth — in a place where nothing has ever been eaten, and nothing has ever gone to waste.
