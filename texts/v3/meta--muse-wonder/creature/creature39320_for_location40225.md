If you stay long enough — and here, *long enough* is a difficult idea — you begin to understand that the forest has not, after all, left one of its stations empty.

At first they look like the glassy fish do. A vertical shape in a pump upwelling, hanging a little apart from the shoal, swimming without travelling. Or a shadow crossing a light-bar on the open plain where there should be nothing between the towers. Or, on a breathing-hump, something upright among the fused trunks, drying in the thin air, black against the permanent ten-o'clock sun, before it goes over on its belly and is gone down the wet root-slope again.

They are the people of the sea. Not its operators. Nothing up here needs operating. They are another of its fitted animals, only they are the one that fits everywhere, a little, and nowhere completely.

They are built long, because everything here is either rooted or carried, and they have elected, over whatever abyss of time made them, to be carried beautifully. A full-grown one is well over two metres from crown to heel, most of it spine and ribcage, with limbs that seem too thin until you see them work against a pump-current. The hands and feet open in the water into fans — fingers elongated, webbed not with skin but with a fringe of pale, sensitive root-hairs, the same that span the empty water in invisible webs. They feel their way as much as they hold with them. Swimming, they go fan, fan, and a slow undulation that starts behind the ears, their hair streaming out and tasting the current ahead of the body.

For they do taste with almost all of themselves. The water is plant-broth, resinous, faintly green, alive with microscopic things, and their skin is made to read it. It is translucent at the throat and belly and the insides of the arms, and shows the blood dark green underneath — not metaphorically. There is plant in them, in the blood, in a fur of fine algae that grows in the light across the shoulders and scalp and the long backs of the thighs. In the open places they will hang for what would be hours, if hours moved, spread in a bar of sun coming down through the leaf-ceiling, doing nothing that looks like anything, and be faintly fed by it. Their backs, which take the most light, take also the most damage over a life, and thicken into ribbed, grey-brown plates, shell-hard, very like the bark of the root-towers. Old ones — though *old* is hard to judge — look carved when seen from behind, and entirely soft from the front.

The cold, which puts an ache in a visitor's teeth in minutes, is simply their temperature. Eight degrees, ten degrees, is the heat of their blood slowed to match. Their hearts go at a pace that would frighten a physician: a beat, a long consideration, a beat. They can shut the fans of their hands down to paddles and work furiously for a short time, in a stunning-field or escaping an intake, and the heart will rise to it and then, unhurried, settle back to the one rhythm everything else up here also keeps. They breathe water most of their lives, drawing it across a frilled, feathery structure that opens under the jaw like a gill and like a throat at once, filtering out broth, plankton, oxygen from the bubble-columns. But they have lungs too, old ones, capacious and inefficient, and they need them. Every so often — at intervals as regular as everything else — each one rises through the leaf-ceiling by one of the breathing-holes that the leaves maintain around the humps, and lies a while in the mountain air with only its lower body in the sea, coughing softly, filling itself with cold that smells of sap, while the algae-fur on its shoulders dries to a pale, crackling green.

Their eyes are made for a world that is always mid-morning and never noon. The pupil is a horizontal slot, the better to keep the permanent bars of light from dazzling across it, and the iris is the exact blue-green of deep water at the edge of visibility; when one of them looks at you from a kilometre away across an empty plain — and they will, long before you have resolved into anything to them — all you see at last are two small pale gleams, aligned, patient. They do not blink often. Underwater, there is nothing to blink against.

They are not numerous. The sea spreads them as thin as it spreads everything, and abundance, when you find it, is always local and appointed. A family — if family is the word — holds a pump-grove: five, perhaps nine individuals, spaced down the ranks of the hollow trunks, each at the vent or intake or lateral jet where it has always been. They clear what the grazers cannot reach. They ease fallen husks out of the turbine vanes with their fanned hands before the vanes can jam, though the vanes have never jammed. They turn, very slightly, in the boiling upwelling, and sing into it: a low note felt in the sternum, pitched to sit inside the roaring of the grove rather than above it, by which mothers find their young in green murk and lovers find one another and a swimmer gone too far down-current is told, without urgency, which water to take home. Their language, when they lift their heads into air on the humps and use the other voice — a wet, clicking, surprisingly birdlike speech — seems to have no past tense. Translating it, if you could, everything would come out in the continuous present. A thing is falling. A thing is regrowing. A child is being born into the water downstream of the sorting-reefs, where the current is slow and warm and rich with sorted rot, and is already turning its face into the flow.

They do not raise their children so much as attend their arrival at competence. A newborn is the length of a forearm, blind as the eels in the digestion pits, all gill and fan and instinct, and it knows — born knowing, literally, in the nerves — where a boiler vents and at what rhythm, where a pylon will discharge and after how many crackles of corona, which channels rim-ward run too fast to swim. It knows this the way it knows peristalsis. Teaching consists of being nearby, in the water, while the knowledge settles into the limbs. Within a season that never turns, the young one's eyes have opened to the green dusk, its back has greened, and it has drifted, or been sung, to whatever station in the grove stands thinned by a death. For the numbers do not grow and do not fall. A death is absorbed by the root-mat with the same quiet that takes a stunned fish. A birth loosens from the reef in the same slow turning path births have always taken. Each replaces each, not exactly, but at the rate. When you ask — in air-speech, with gestures — how many they are, they are puzzled in a way that has nothing to do with counting. They name the stations. Intake. Third Vent. The Fan That Faces The Light. The Rim Between Two Falls. The people are who is there.

And they do move between stations, which makes them, perhaps, the sea's one restlessness. A grazer will patrol its hills forever. A bird will dive at its pylon forever. But a person may, in the middle of its life — a life fifty or eighty or a hundred turns of some unmarked count, spent mostly in water that has not changed its temperature in living memory — leave the pump-grove and take the great permanent currents across the pelagic plains, travelling for distances that empty the mind, hand over hand along no rope but the tug of root-hair webs, resting in the lee of a lone electric pylon with the corona building audibly around them and moving on one clean count before it fires. They visit the digestion pits and knot themselves briefly in the warm outflow with others who have come from other groves, and this is how blood moves around the sea and does not stagnate. They go out to the rim and hang there, as you might, at the lip of the world, fans spread against the outflowing water, looking down four thousand metres through waterfalls into cloud, while the cold goes through them and their algae-fur streams rim-ward and the whole continent of green light roars and ticks and conveys and grows behind them.

They look at that drop a long time. That is the most human thing about them, and it is not very human, because they look at it with no expression you can read and, in the end — in so far as anything ends — they turn and take the in-current back to a station, and take up the note inside the roaring again, and clear a husk from a vane that is already clear.

They are not wise custodians of the forest, and they are not its victims, and they are not a fallen people who once ran the machinery and have forgotten how. They never ran it. They are what the standing wave produced when it had produced everything else: a creature that can leave its place, and therefore can notice that nothing ever leaves its place. They carry that noticing the way they carry their own green blood — quietly, throughout the body, without it changing their temperature.

Come back in a year and the one you met will be mid-turn in the upwelling at the third vent, or will be absorbed, and another will be mid-turn there instead, greener or less green, and will raise its horizontal-pupilled eyes to you out of the bars of light with a patience that is not waiting, exactly, because waiting implies an afternoon. The light will be late-morning light on its plated back. The water will taste of sap. It will be, deafeningly and peacefully, itself.
