At first you would mistake them for visitors who have simply stayed too long.

They have the same shape, after all. Two arms, two legs, hair cut close because loose hair drifts and is combed out by the motes as waste. They wear the same grey-blue suits visitors are issued at the peak station, featureless things that shed water and scale and shed, in general, the idea of fashion. In the amber under-light of a street, swimming the long blue-black interval between one tower and the next, a resident looks like any human who has learned to breathe water.

It is only if you stay — if you watch them for what the station calls a day, and then another, in a place where day is only a ten percent dimming — that the sameness starts to peel.

They do not fidget.

That is the smallest, truest thing. A visitor, even a calm one, makes a thousand corrections in the water: a kick to hold position, a hand waved to turn, shoulders rising with the old, useless instinct to surface for air. The residents hang. Absolutely. Where they stop, they remain, upright or tilted at whatever slight angle they chose, hands loose, heads up in the twilight, until they decide, minutes later, to go on. The field thins around a moving body here, but around a still one it seems to thicken in approval. They have learned how to be filed.

They move, when they move, without hurry and without wake. A resident crossing the plaza will take three minutes to do what you would do in thirty seconds, not pushing through the water so much as persuading it, in advance, to let them pass. Their kicks are short, their arms barely sweep. Old women of ninety — and many of them are ninety, or a hundred, or a hundred and eleven — travel this way to the market arcade for their ration of paste with the same unlined faces they had in the archived photographs in the council hall. They are not young. That would be a change. They are *preserved.*

The equilibrium has got into them the way it has got into everything else.

Their skin is the first ledger. Washed continuously by filtration motes, stripped of bacteria, spores, shed cells, oils, it has lost most of its ordinary defenses and most of its ordinary color. In Sere Calm light they are the pale grey-brown of river stones that have never seen a river, translucent at the ears and the web of the fingers, veins showing as a faint blue map. They have no scent. They leave no scent. If you press your forehead to theirs to speak — jaw to receiver, as you must, because the field eats voices — you smell only cold water and the mineral ghost of their last meal.

Their eyes have widened to the available dark. Pupils that never quite contract. A tapetum-like sheen the throat-implant people grew for them, generations back, so red light dies even sooner for them than it does for you, and amber becomes a kind of white. They blink very slowly. Some of the oldest have stopped blinking at all while awake, and let the water do it.

Inside, they are quieter still. Their permanent implants are not the temporary bud a visitor grows in an hour. Theirs are braided through the throat, the sinuses, the inner ear, a second, patient anatomy for breathing a thick cold draught at eight degrees, forever. Their lungs have learned not to panic. Their hearts, listened to through contact, beat forty times a minute, and slower asleep in their mist-sealed rooms, where they lower the water to a dense fog so they can feel, for a few hours, the unfamiliar drag of their own weight in blankets. Many of them dream, reportedly, of falling. It is the only motion their bodies remember that the field cannot correct.

They keep their microbiome in a vault down in the peak station, frozen samples in labeled vials: gut cultures from before entry, skin flora, the whole vanished ecology of being a person who could rot in the proper way. It is tended like the archives. None of them can ever use it again. To leave Sere Calm would be to re-enter a world of weather and decay all at once — bacteria, sunlight full and unfiltered, air that moves without permission. The doctors who built the town calculated, long ago, that exposure would not kill them quickly. It would kill them *interestingly,* and in cascade. So they do not leave. Visitors think of this as a tragedy. The residents, if you stay forehead-to-forehead long enough to earn the longer clicks of their private speech, will correct you. Leaving would be a change.

There are forty-three of them, in a town built for three hundred, and that number is also filed. It was forty-three last decade. It will be forty-three next decade, because a birth — displacing water, introducing a new and helpless metabolism that cries, or would cry if sound carried — is not forbidden, exactly. It is scheduled with the same care as a repair to the lens. Every twelve or fifteen years the council permits one, and the child is raised communally in the wide spaces between towers, taught first to hang still, then to swim without wake, then to speak by touch. They grow up never having seen green. Never having been dry except in mist. Never having felt wind that was not a rumor through three hundred meters of edited water. By the time they are ten they find visitors exhausting — too fast, too warm to the touch, trailing bubbles of outside air that strip off them with humiliating little hiccups, always trying to get the town to answer them.

The old, when they are finally not preserved enough, do not die in the water. That experiment was done early and is not discussed. They go down, voluntarily, through the underside of the lens, feeling that pressure change through teeth and sinus for the last time, to the hot sealed chambers in the peak station, where a body can be composted at a decent, private temperature, far from the equilibrium. The transit takes an hour. Everyone knows who is going. In the streets the amber guides do not dim for it, the sonar ping at the hour is not silenced. What the town does instead is hold position. Forty-two people, hanging motionless between their towers at the appointed minute, casting their shadows upward, being still together more completely than usual. It is as close as they come to a ceremony that admits time.

Work is mostly a form of that stillness, practiced professionally. Someone must watch the desalinators that never desalinate. Someone must walk the filtration canals and count the seven blind glass carp, forty centimeters each, silver-translucent, circling a route older than most of the counters. Someone must sit in the council rotunda — which fills, like everything, with unmoving water — and maintain the saved version of the town against the handrail-carvers and the tea-spillers. Someone must be archivist, and answer visitors through bone conduction, telling them there has been no measurable net change in one hundred and eleven years with a pride that sounds, after a while, less like pride and more like a prayer said correctly.

They do have the eighth carp. Only they do. A visitor will never see it; a visitor is still making ripples with his attention. But ask three residents separately and you will get the same non-story, down to the loop it joins at the north canal and the loop where it leaves, unchanged in fifty years of telling. It may be the one thing in Sere Calm they have allowed to grow because it grows without changing. A folklore kept at constant temperature. Sometimes, late in the dimming, you may find a resident alone at the canal glass, hanging, forehead nearly touching it, watching the seven go round and waiting in perfect patience to be wrong about the count.

And underneath the people, or perhaps instead of them, there is the other population, which outnumbers them beyond counting: the motes. Invisible filtration engineers in their trillions, eating seed and spore and stray cell, healing polymer within the hour, taking a cup of spilled tea apart molecule by molecule. They are not alive in the way the carp are alive, nor dead in the way the town would like a corpse to be dead. They are maintenance as an ecosystem. The residents speak of them the way farmers once spoke of weather — as the condition that decides what can live here. Which is to say, almost nothing. Which is to say, exactly this: seven fish, forty-three slow humans who no longer quite photosynthesize, digest, decay, or age at a natural rate, and a rumored eighth shadow that sensors never record.

If you stay long enough, your own pulse begins to sound loud and then, gradually, less loud, because you are learning to beat more slowly in your ears. Your skin pales. You stop kicking to hold your place. The town does not welcome you. Welcome would be a response. It simply stops correcting you quite so often, and files you, temporarily, in one of its long empty intervals between lights, upright in the cold twilight, breathing stone-tasting water, casting your shadow up toward a surface that is in every direction, while far below the plaza the real clouds go on having weather without you.

That is what lives in Sere Calm.

Not a people who conquered a strange place, and not a people defeated by it. A people who have been successfully kept — cool, dark, plantless, nearly silent, nearly changeless — in the sample they were meant only to tend. Forty-three specimens of the standard ocean's keepers, suspended forty meters above a mountain that never finished forming, aging so slowly that the joke about naming the fish for dead council members has outlived the council members, the joke, and nearly the difference between the namers and the named.
