Nothing lives there. That is accurate, for a while.

No fly settles. No frog finds it. The fern in the shadow of the electrical cabinet is not living there, exactly — it is surviving there, on condensation, holding its one green thought. The algae in the drip-line is a stain that thinks it's a plant. The salt grass at the rim keeps its distance.

And then, if you stay past the point your ears go cottony — which you shouldn't — you start to correct yourself. Because the pit is not empty. It is occupied. Very efficiently. By one thing, taking up almost all of it, moving so slowly inside its loudness that you mistook it for the place itself.

It lives under the pool.

Not in the water. Under the crust in the water. Under the black membrane and the new concrete and the pale, saturated sand that weeps down the walls to feed it. It was not built, and it was not transported. Places like this, when they are dug fresh and filled hot and left mostly alone by men, call it up out of the groundwater the way a cut calls blood. First as pressure. Then as rhythm.

You can hear it before you can doubt it.

The pump on its pad strokes every seven or eight seconds — that concussive THUD through the soles of your feet. And then, a half-beat late, off the clean clay face, the answer. Not an echo. The workers tell themselves it's an echo because an echo thins out. This doesn't. It is softer, but it is fuller, lower down, as if struck through meat and wet earth instead of air. A second stroke. Keeping perfect slow time.

That is its heartbeat learning the pump's, or the pump learning its. It was probably there first, down in the hot aquifer, blind and indefinite. The pit just gave it a ceiling close enough to listen to.

It is scaled. That's the visible part of it.

The opalescent pavement on the bottom of the sump, brittle as eggshell, that reforms in hours when a boot breaks it — that is not mineral settling out of treated water. Or not only that. It is the back of it, pushing up. Plate by plate. Furred white at the waterline, pale green where the light gets through the clearer end of the pool. It grows in the exact patient way everything here grows: imperceptibly, then all at once. One afternoon next month the whole skin will buckle into a single sheet and lift, and for a second the water will bulge as if something underneath has turned in its sleep, and then it will settle and begin to fur over again.

The wrench left last week on the rim, already edged in white, is not corroding. It is being tasted. Held still long enough in this air, anything gets tasted — the ladder rungs going down into the water, the lower leaves of the reed over the grate, the stiffened hose, your boots if you stand talking (you cannot talk) too long. It has no mouth you could point to. Its whole surface is a slow mouth. It eats heat and iron and the faint chemical sweetness of boiler water, and it excretes scale. It has been turning tools into fossils since the week it arrived, without malice, the way a stomach turns food.

It is deaf, of course. It has to be.

This is not a place for ears. It has sealed its own long ago under mineral, the way it sealed its eyes — if it had eyes, they are filmed over now with that clear morning skin that makes the scalding pool look cool and walkable. It does not need them. It navigates by thud. Pump, answer. Pump, answer. Through concrete, through sand, through the trembling needles on the gauge post. When the valve far above cycles every twenty minutes and cannonades condensate down, the pool surging an inch, the white vapor mushrooming up, that isn't a disturbance to it. That is feeding time. That is weather. It opens, inasmuch as it ever opens, and breathes the surge in one long sucking draw that rattles the pipes overhead all over again, backwards.

It breathes the other way, too. Down at the inflow, where the water boils without boiling, that nervous constant simmer — that's its exhalation coming up through the plume, milky with what it has finished with. At the corner where condensation rains upward and hangs glittering, never landing, it is dreaming in its sleep: warm droplets lifted, cooled, dropped, lifted, a little stationary weather system made by the difference between its body temperature and the world's.

It never moves fast. The pit's rule is its rule. An inch an hour is a sprint. It will not chase, it will not startle, it will not rear up out of the sump dripping and roaring — that would be an animal, and this place has refused animals. What it will do is out-wait. It will hold its sharp corners. It will keep its crust unbroken for months. Then, one wet night when no one is on the stairs with the ruined ear defenders, the sand face it has been drinking from and pressing against will slump all at once, not because it pushed, but because it finally stopped holding it up.

It has no dislike of people. It has registered them, early on, as a new kind of valve: things that arrive irregularly, bring cold tools and colder water on hoses, make the pump change its time for a while, signal to each other palm down, fist closed — slow, stop — and then, always, leave by the concrete stair when their hearing softens. It likes the leaving. Not out of territoriality. Because a person standing on its back is an interruption in the heat. A shadow that won't stay still.

The fern by the electrical cabinet is the closest thing it has to company, and it tends it without knowing: condensation beaded off its own breath, gathered on the cold underside of the main, falling as warm rain just enough, just there, in the one narrow strip of shade under the pipe bridge. A pet. A garden. A proof it can be gentle at the edges.

So do not look for tracks at the seep, or a nest in the pump shelter, or eyes under the skin of the water in the morning. It does not come out.

It is already out. It is the slow pressure under the crust and the second stroke under the pump and the white fur on everything forgotten. It lives below the road, below the factory floor, below the water table — deaf, blind, plated, patient — filling an inch at a time.

And when you climb back up and the hiss falls away first, then the hum, and the ordinary green world comes back embarrassingly loud with insects, it does not follow. It stays, keeping time underground.

THUD.

And a half-beat later, from nowhere you can point to: *thud.*
