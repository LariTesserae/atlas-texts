Nothing animal lives there. But the forest is inhabited.

You have to stay under long enough to notice, because at first they pass for part of the growth: another stand of drowned spruce on a former hillside, another column of kelp rooted to a field boulder, another dark shape furred with moss and swaying when the tidal river combs through the valley. Only when the light changes — when a patch of phosphorescent moss pulses somewhere below a drowned ridge and the shape answers, faintly, from its own shoulder, its ribs, the long green hair where needles or hair once were separate ideas — do you understand that some of the trees have been answering each other for a very long time.

They are what the people became when the water did not recede.

Not quickly. The drowning had time to become geography, and they had time to become native to it. Whatever rose in those valleys — meltwater, lake, then sea where a mountain sky should have been — it came slowly enough for hands to build towers first, walls, weirs, cairns, and then slowly enough for the bodies using those hands to be translated, generation by generation, into the element that was taking them. Skin thickened to leather against the cold. Hair lengthened and greened. The soft, hungry interior life simplified. Mouths became unnecessary in a world that fed through light, silt, mineral seep and current. Lungs became unnecessary in a different way: they learned to hold strings of bright bubbles along the blades of their backs and under their arms, to trade gases through moss that lives in symbiosis on their skin, to go very still when the ice thickens and the blue-green noon is only a rumor.

They are not fish. They are not beasts in plant clothing. By the time they are adult, animal is the wrong kingdom altogether.

A child begins as the valley begins everything: carried.

Their seed-pods have no shell because nothing has ever evolved there to eat them. The pods wait in the lee of the drystone walls — the walls are not property lines now, whatever they were on dry land, but current-breaks and seed-fences, dividing a fast tide from a slow one — and split only when the whole mountain sea leans back toward the moon and the bottom rivers reverse. Then the child goes out in the long ribbons of silt and torn frond, east becoming west, past bells, through spruce woods, over polished cobble deserts where only travelers and the very young cross. To settle is a decision made with the whole body. A good place has shelter from the falls that spill over submerged sills, south-facing memory in the slope, enough old pasture stone to anchor against, and neighbors whose moss-light is intelligible.

For the first century, perhaps, they are small and comparatively restless. Hand-high may mean older than the slate on a bothy roof. These young root and unroot as the lilies do — those mats that hang upside-down from the underside of the ice and let go at the crack of noon, turning as they fall — learning the basin by moving between ceiling and floor. They tend the gardens that are also their elders. They clear bleached kelp-drifts from the doorways of drowned huts, not because anyone enters by a door, but because a choked doorway changes the note a building makes in the current. They reset white stones around the cairns. They keep the woven willow weirs in repair across the channels, and at tide-turn they harvest what the weirs were always, perhaps, secretly for: not fish, which have never existed there, but seeds, pods, buoyant fronds, the valley's slow mail.

Older, they travel less. Their feet become root systems and then cease, in any useful sense, to be feet. Their lower bodies fur and darken; algae takes them; the cold preserves even their mistakes. A mature inhabitant stands ten, twenty, in the bell valleys nearly thirty metres tall, not because they reach for sun in any frantic way — there is almost no sun to reach for — but because height puts their bubble-strings into faster water and their hearing into the path of sound. Their bodies are partly hollow. That is not decay. It is design now, or the memory of design. When the current pours through a stand of them a hillside finds its chord, each trunk at a slightly different pitch, and information moves through the valley as music: stress in the ice above, a fall beginning over a sill two valleys away, rain arriving as hiss on the surface, a seed-release coming, grief.

They speak, if speaking is not too animal a word, in vegetative weather. Mosses live in patterned colonies on their skin and flare in sequence. One will answer a question another asked last season. Kelp-children drifting between rooted adults carry bubbles that catch the glow and bear fragments of it upward, garbling and enriching the message as they rise. Near the bell towers their language changes, because the bells do the work that pollinators do elsewhere: the tolling shakes loose what must be shaken loose. It calls growth in a direction. Whole families lean, over decades, not toward the light but toward a bronze note under forty metres of water.

This is why the tallest forests are in the lowest valley, where the old villages clustered. The rooted ones gather around the towers the way congregations once gathered inside weather. When the tide turns hard and water pushes through the narrow tower openings, nobody reaches out a hand — hands by then are mostly frond, root and memory — but nobody needs to. The bells ring the people. The people resonate back. Seed falls in a slow green snow through black water. Moss light ripples outward across the basin, valley answering valley, until even the empty silt plains seem briefly to have a nervous system.

Their dead do not leave.

In a place with almost nothing to dismantle a body, ancestry is not a story but a building material. The old bleach, sink a little, harden. A grandmother becomes part of a pale reef that protects seedlings from the east tide. A line of former shepherds, taken standing by deep cold like the spruces around them, remains upright for a thousand years, gradually less legible and more useful, until young ones root between them exactly as new spruce roots among the dead spruce. Sometimes a child grows through the ribs of someone who built a wall, and thereafter keeps that wall with a fidelity no doctrine could teach.

They remember dryness the way plants remember a climate in the shape of a leaf: inaccurately, and in the body. They arrange flint scrapers beside hearths full of black silt. They weigh down slate roofs against wind that will never reach them. They maintain cairns on summits now ten metres under the ice, white-ringed, though no traveler needs marking there except the tide itself. Some of this is ritual. Some may be engineering whose purpose has changed so slowly that purpose and ritual are the same thing. Sound keeps the current from silting a doorway. A wall keeps a family from being combed flat. A roof keeps a hollow stone room resonant at the pitch where seeds release.

On the surface they almost never appear. Above the ice is iron dusk, thin air, no birds, weather dragging itself across snowfields; nothing up there is food. At most, in the shallows around the stony islands, you might see their eldest with heads just under the forming ice like the reeds — listening upward for rain. It reaches them as pressure and a continuous hiss, and in certain bays the cracks answer by spraying up to meet it, as if the sea were trying to remember being sky.

They are not numerous. Sparse is the rule everywhere in the basin, and they obey it, clustering lushly only where a drowned cliff shelters them or an old slope holds its warmth of stone through the winter dark. Between their settlements lie the great underwater deserts: grey silt, outwash gravel, cobbles shining dully as they roll into unlit valley floors. They cross those spaces slowly, on tide and at noon-fall, carrying light on their skin. From above — if there were anything above but ice and cloud to see — they would look like weather moving through a forest that is also a town, a choir, a graveyard, a nursery.

At the lowest rim, where a river once left for lower ground, water still pours impossibly up the rock from the tarn below to keep the sea at its chosen level. The young go there before their final rooting and hold their bubble-strings in that upward rush. It may be pilgrimage. It may be measurement. It may simply be where the current cleans them. None of the explanations feel sufficient while the bells are going in the deep valley and a whole hillside of people is humming in chord, green-haired, rooted, awake at the speed of forests, in a loud animal-empty sea that did not kill the world it drowned — only taught it to live by other means.
