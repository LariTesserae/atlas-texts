You would not see it first.

There is nothing to see with. You would feel it, the way you feel everything else down there — as a change in the arrangement of the dark around you.

If you stood between two electrical trunks long enough for your hair to lift, you would notice the current alter. Not stop. Nothing ever stops. But it would hesitate, detour, thicken in the vessels on one side of you and thin on the other, as if something had stepped into the field and the field, politely, had made room for it. Then a warmth against the side of your face that was not furnace-warmth. A displacement in the saturated air. A hand, if it is a hand, laid experimentally against the same trunk you are touching, a little higher up, so that its fingers close over a living valve you had only felt beating under the bark.

It does that for a long while. Reading the valve. Reading you, perhaps, in the same way — as a difference in conductivity.

It is a department, like everything else. Only this department walks.

In shape it has been constrained by the same conditions that made the trunks: there is only up, and down, and narrow passage between close-grown columns in standing water. So it is upright, and narrow, and taller than you expected once you have traced it from the waterline to its crown. Not tall in the way of the canopy. Tall in the human scale it coincidentally occupies, because that is the scale of service-channels, of root-mouths, of junctions on a trunk that need to be reached and opened and cleared. Six feet, six and a half. The same, when you encounter the next one, and the next. A stem is not roughly like the stem before it here.

Its legs go straight down into the black sheet-water and do not end in anything you would call a foot. Below the knee — if it has a knee, and it does, a swollen turgid joint that bends by swelling on one side and easing on the other — the limb divides, and divides again, into slick rootlets that spread when it stands and gather when it moves, questing for purchase in the root-mat. It does not step so much as it plants itself, leans its hydraulic weight forward, plants itself again. When it comes to a conveyor-frond it will simply stand, roots loosened, and allow itself to be handed along at the speed materials are meant to flow, upright in the dripping dark, hairless head turned neither left nor right.

Because there is nothing to turn toward. Its face is smooth.

Not blank in the manner of something unfinished — finished, rather, to a template that has no use for sight. There are shallow sealed seams where, in another lineage, eyes might have opened. They never do. The skin slides uninterrupted over them, warm, fibrous, very faintly ridged like the underside of a young leaf. It does not need to adjust to the darkness. It was never expected to.

What it has instead, all over, is a finer attention. The skin is not a covering but an instrument panel grown thin. Along the forearms and the long, root-divided fingers, along the throat and the sides of the ribs, copper-rich nerves run just beneath the surface in insulated sheaths, the way current runs in the trunks themselves. You can see nothing of them, but if you were permitted to touch — and it will permit it, with the neutral patience of a plant permitting rain — you would feel a faint, slow pulse that is not a heartbeat. It is borrowed. When it lays its palms to a trunk, its pulse matches the trunk. When it steps into a plating marsh, it slows with the electrochemical pools. When it touches another of its kind, palm to palm in the absolute silence, something passes that has never needed to become sound. Information, instruction, a correction in load. A conversation held entirely as current.

It has no mouth in any practical sense. There is a small, shuttered seam below the smooth chin that opens, not to bite — there is nothing to bite, no fruit that is not already scheduled for the presses, no animal at all — but to couple. It presses that seam to sap-ports, to bladders of distilled floodwater, to the soft feed-nipples that grow at standardized intervals along certain service trunks, and it drinks by osmosis and slow peristalsis, taking in exactly the mineral broth it will spend. The rest of its feeding is continuous and unremarkable: its skin drinks the standing water it wades in. Its rootlets taste iron, silica, copper, heat, as they go. Its body is always exactly as wet, and exactly as warm, as the air — because to be otherwise would be wasteful, and would make it legible as a separate thing.

For the same reason it does not smell, to itself, of anything, and in time it ceases to produce the faint green smell it does have for a newcomer: warm wet lignin, slow fermentation, a trace of ozone from its own conductive tissues. It has nostril-slits that sample humidity and the chemical weather of a district, but the smell of the world never strengthens or fades, and so it does not experience it as smell. It experiences it as place. A furnace-province is a baked-resin tightness high in the throat. A distillation crown is a cooler bead-work running down the inside of the forearms. A rain-column, a mile away, is a textureless hush it already knows, and a minute lessening of the electric prickle on its skin.

It hears, after a fashion, through its feet. The forest is padded to muteness — every bearing moss-grown, every fall of water landing in vegetation — but vibrations travel in the mat, in the water, in the pressurized bores of the trunks. A rotary flower meshing a mile off sends a rhythm up through its rootlets so slow it registers as a change in pressure behind the face. Its own movement makes no sound worth designing out. It is made, like everything serviceable down here, of soft wet fiber and turgid wood, oil-fed at the joints, and it moves at the pace the biggest machines move, slowly enough that sound never assembles.

It works. That is too active a word, and too purposeful, but no other will do.

It tends the junctions where leaf-belts meet chopping mouths and sometimes bind with pulp. It clears root-mouths after a rain-column has fed them silt. It lays its long fingers over valves that have begun, fractionally, to open late, and coaxes them — by pressure, by current, by a chemical exhalation through its palms — back to schedule. It carries seed blanks in a woven sling grown from its own hip, a sheet of living cloth that is periodically harvested from its back and regrows, and it sets each machined seed into the descending drills with a care that is not reverence and not habit but simply exactness: the seed goes in at the angle its predecessor went in. In the plating marshes it wades chest-deep among millions of hanging nodules, turning, testing skin-thickness with its forearms, stripping the ones that have finished so they can be taken to be stripped again elsewhere. Nothing it does produces anything that leaves. Nothing it does finishes. By evening — there is no evening — it has maintained, in its small radius, the precise condition that existed before it arrived.

It is not intelligent the way a thing is intelligent when its world can surprise it. Its world has arranged, over continental distances and uncountable repetitions, never to. What it has instead of intelligence is a vast, embodied memory of process, grown into it before it was ever detached. It knows every province because every province is the same province arranged again. It needs no map and carries no history. If you could ask it — palm to palm, in pulses — about the sky far above its stacked black canopies, it would have a response, but the response would be a diagnostic one: an increase in current, harvested, spent below, belonging to the canopy department. Above is a function, not a place. Before is a function too. The belts were creeping. The valves were opening. The rain was falling in its appointed columns. It has no tense for it being otherwise.

And it has no childhood, and will have no old age.

In districts assigned to that purpose, hollow silo-trees grow them the way they grow dormant seeds: in rows, in darkness, in warm nutrient dark, identical blanks curled inside fibrous pods that split along a seam when internal pressure reaches standard. It emerges already full-sized and already knowing the valve-rhythms, already able to find a sap-port with its shuttered mouth, because the knowing was stamped in the way the seed-presses stamp everything. It steps down into the water and takes its place on the nearest belt, and the belt takes it toward the nearest junction that requires a tender. For a long time — decades, perhaps a century; the difference is not recorded anywhere in its tissues — it will be replaced piecemeal as it goes: skin sloughed into pulp channels on schedule, a finger worn thin regrown from the knuckle, the woven sheet on its back harvested and regrown, leaf after leaf of itself. Then, on an ordinary circulation, its own current will begin to thin, its rootlets will answer the mat a fraction late, and without distress or ceremony it will allow a conveyor to hand it, upright and still sensing everything, into a district of chopping mouths. What returns to the feedstock will be reprinted. The next tender to step from a silo-tree in that province will lay its hand on the same trunk, a little higher up, and close its fingers over the same valve.

There are no others. No mate, no rival, no young clustering at the rootlets. Pollination is a coupling of shuttered flowers elsewhere and does not concern it. Affection, if it has it, is the matching of pulses when two of them stand together between electrical trunks and share a load so neither is strained. Loneliness would require an expectation that the configuration might change. The configuration does not change.

So it lives, if living is the word for a self-maintaining process that has learned to walk slowly from one part of itself to another: blind and exquisitely aware, silent and in constant communication, fed by the forest and made of it and indistinguishable, at last, from a piece of the factory that has become mobile in order to keep the rest of the factory still. In the rain-districts its smooth head beads with condensation that runs down its conducting neck in slow lines and back into the standing water. Over furnace-roots its skin shimmers faintly with hotter air. Between the great trunks it pauses, hairless, eyeless, root-footed in the body-temperature flood, feeling through its palms the endless, soundless current go to where it has always gone — and then it is handed along again, deeper or less deep, in a world with no edge and no outside, doing the same work its predecessor did, under leaves that will drip the same bead onto the same place on its shoulder, tender after tender after tender, each one new, the event identical.
