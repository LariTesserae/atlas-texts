If you stay long enough for the smell to stop being a smell — sulfur, hot stone, and underneath it something sweet and close, like old leather left in the sun — you begin to pick them out of each other.

At first it is only carpet. Then it is kinds.

The first life is not animal at all. It crusts the mouths of the vents and creeps a hand's breadth out over the black glass where nothing else can stand. Sulfur-yellow, bone-white, iron-orange, it grows in slicks and felts and hanging threads, bacterial mats that make their food out of heat and poison gas and mineral water that never quite becomes water. They are chewed down to a stain and they regrow overnight. They have no season. In the Pan they are grassland, forest, and seaweed bed at once, and everything else is arranged according to how close it can bear to stand to them.

Closest are the worm-things.

You will not see one whole. They live under the pile, against the actual stone, where the floor of molt is thinnest and hottest and your shoe would blister through its sole. Now and then a length of one surfaces between the beetles — pale, eyeless, segmented, the color of a grub turned toward light for the first time — and its sides sigh open and closed, open and closed, drinking heat directly. They do not graze so much as filter. Bacteria, molt dust, dead legs, fluids pressed out of the layers above: all of it passes through them and comes out softer, richer, edible again. They are the Pan's stomach. The others tolerate their slow surfacing the way soil tolerates a root turning. When one dies down there, which you will also never see, it is not wasted and it is not mourned. It is simply incorporated, from the inside out, before it can cool.

Above them are the beetles.

Their wing cases have fused shut over generations that had nowhere to fly to, making each one a low, matte-black dome. They use those domes as shields. A beetle inverts the logic of armor: the hard part is not to keep predators out, though the centipedes find the seams readily enough, but to keep heat from settling. They go down into the pile headfirst and present their backs to the vents, and a dozen crickets will stand on them for the fractionally cooler air in their shadow. Their legs are short and spaded for pushing through molt. They eat the felted mats and the leavings of the worms and, when they can get them, each other's shed skins, which are softer than they look. They mate without ceremony underneath everything, in the dark under the dark, and lay their eggs in packets glued to the underside of a warm stone at the Pan's edge — never the center, where they would cook, never the wall, where they would freeze. The larvae hatch blind and immediately begin to dig inward toward warmth, eating as they go.

The millipedes are flatter and slower and older in their manner. Each one is like a coin given legs, or a little armored raft. They do not seem to hurry even when the current of the carpet carries them. They ride mostly in the middle layers, curling at a touch into perfect, sealed discs that the crickets climb over without pausing, uncurling again a minute later to resume grazing. What they graze is mostly the past: compacted molt, fungal threads no thicker than breath that run through it after a body has lain still too long, the husks of their own ancestors. Their digestion is what makes the floor springy instead of foul. Without them the Pan would choke on its own skins. With them it becomes horn, bedding, nursery, food again. A millipede can live — if the rings that sometimes show when one surfaces are true — through more turnings of the carpet than a cricket can imagine. The crickets treat the very large ones as geography. Clusters form behind them where they stall the flow. Young ride in the lee of them to cool.

And then, everywhere, inescapable, are the crickets.

They are the visible surface of the Pan, and therefore the part you think of as the inhabitant, and they let you think it. Finger-long, the color of wet cave clay, with hind legs folded like origami and antennae three, sometimes four times the length of their bodies — antennae so long and so constantly in motion that the air a foot above the floor is also occupied, woven. They are blind, but blindness is the wrong word. Eye is simply not the sense that was kept. Their antennae taste humidity, temperature, the chemical panic or contentment of the cricket in front of them, the faint electrical change when a centipede passes. They talk incessantly in touch. A wave travels across the carpet faster than any individual moves: here, here, hot, turn, food, hold. That is the language you hear as an almost-silence, ten million tiny taps absorbed by ten million bodies.

They stand six and eight deep not out of desperation but out of architecture. The top layer feeds and watches — if watching can be done with antennae — and ventilates, lifting its wings, which no longer open for flight, to rasp a dry, soundless fan. The layers below rest, molt, digest, are carried. The bottom layer endures. Then the spiral takes them: in along the cool wall where they can press their bellies to stone that still remembers the mountain night, out along the slow outward current, up through the pile as their turn at the bottom ends, rested and warmed by degrees until they crest again at the surface. No cricket stays at the lethal center. No cricket stays at the safe edge forever, either, because there is nothing to eat at the edge except each other. The rotation is not kindness. It is the shape hunger and heat make together when there are too many bodies and exactly enough room to keep moving.

They are born into the rotation and they never leave it. Eggs are tucked into the warm molt. Nymphs, translucent miniatures with antennae already too long, are nudged by adults into the slow lanes until they learn the current. They learn it the way you learn a river: by being taken. By their third molt they can hold position against it, make the little side-step that keeps the spiral from bunching, form with their own bodies the towers that rise where the flow stalls — bodies locked leg to leg, climbing each other simply to get a body's height higher into cooler air, swaying there, shedding heat, before the tower deliberately sighs apart and reforms a pace away. The same towers hang from the low ceiling, grape-clusters of crickets gripping stone and each other, upside down over the vents, dropping one by one back into the carpet when their grip warms.

Above them, surfing, are the centipedes.

Hand-long, wet-ash grey, improbably flat, they do not so much walk on the carpet as be passed across it, their many feet barely dimpling the backs beneath. The crickets make way without scattering — there is nowhere to scatter to — a local parting, a ripple, a lifting of antennae toward a threat that has already decided. A centipede takes a cricket cleanly, from the side, without breaking the spiral. The neighbors resume their step over the small, sudden absence. The centipedes are necessary in the exact proportion they are terrifying. They take the slow, the sick, the ones whose antennae have begun to wave out of time; they keep the carpet from becoming a graveyard that still moves. They also scavenge the little falls from the rim, the tower-collapses, the still-warm dead. In return the carpet tolerates their young, pale threads no longer than an eyelash, riding protected in the middle layers and eating mites too small for anything else to bother with.

Because there are smaller things still. Mites in the joints of the beetles. Springtails in the condensation. Things that live only on the mats, and things that live only on the things that live on the mats, in a narrowing tower of dependence that goes down past visibility. The Pan does not end at the size you can see. It just becomes less distinguishable from the stone.

And once or twice an hour, or once in a night — there is no rhythm a visitor can keep — they all stop.

Not startle. Not scatter. Stop. The cricket mid-step, the millipede mid-uncurl, the centipede flattened to the backs it hunts, the hanging clusters locked and swayless. The worm-things close along their sides. Even the faint rasping of useless wings ceases. What is left is the vent breathing against rock, and your own heart making an embarrassing, meat-loud noise in a place that has evolved out of noise.

In that pause you understand, in your body rather than your mind, that they are not many separate populations sharing a bowl. They are one thing, distributed. The pause travels through antenna touching antenna, leg against shell, body pressed to body pressed to warm molt pressed to stone, faster than thought and older than any individual in the pile. Whether they are listening to a shift in the mountain's heat, to a pressure building under the vents, to each other listening — it does not matter. They hear it together because, at the density they live, together is the only way there is to hear. And then the instruction lifts, and the same leg completes the same step, and the rotation resumes as if the world had not briefly shown its seam.

They do this in their feeding and in their thirst as well. At night, when cold air spills over the rim and meets the rising heat, the inch-wide stain of condensation darkens on the high back wall. It never drips. It only gathers, a cold sweat on stone. And they come to it in silent rotation — cricket, beetle, a millipede uncurled at last, cricket again — each to press mouthparts to the damp for a count you cannot predict but they do not violate, each making way for the next without pushing. No one cheats during the freezes. No one cheats during the thirst. Cheating would be a kind of solo, and there has been no solo in the Pan for a very long time.

That is who lives there. Not a king, not a queen, not a colony with a single womb hidden at the center — the center is empty because the center would kill a queen in minutes. The inhabitant is the turning itself: bacteria making food from poison, worms making food from waste, beetles making shade from their own backs, millipedes making floor from the dead, crickets making weather from their bodies, centipedes making room. Each one entirely itself, with its own hunger. Each one also a cell in a larger animal that is exactly the size of the bowl, that has been maintaining its temperature and feeding itself and listening through the rock for longer than the cairns outside have been sinking and being restacked.

It does not need the sun. It does not need the trail. It tolerated you the way it tolerates a stone falling from the rim: as a brief, warm irregularity in the current, worth tasting for salt, worth climbing for the rain of your sweat, worth letting go when you climbed back toward the cold.

Behind you the floor closed over the place where you stood. It is still closing. It is still opening. It keeps turning, wall to wall, rim to vent, in the dark the noon cannot enter — not waiting, not finished, just alive at the density where aliveness becomes a surface, and the surface, patiently, becomes a home.
