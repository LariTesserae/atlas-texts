At first you would say that nothing lives there.

Nothing lives in the middle, at any rate — and the middle is almost all of it. No one makes a home in that great column of cool, dim air. The lives are all at the rim, pressed into the curve of the wall, under the floor, above the shutter, in water you can hear better than you can see. To live here you have to live sideways, flat against an edge, and leave the center to the sound.

The oldest residents are underneath.

Below the planks, in the black water of the cistern and the slow clay channels feeding it, freshwater mussels sit in their deliberate rows, half-buried like cobbles that breathe. Some of them are very old. Their shells are thickened and ridged and filmed with algae, and they open and close on a time that has almost nothing to do with day or night — only with the fill and sigh of the water coming in from the low fields. They filter what passes. They clarify it by living in it.

And because the mussels are there, the bitterling are there: thumb-length fish, dull until the light coin crosses the inspection pit and they turn and flash a sudden blue-green stripe. They need the mussels. In season the females lay their eggs down into a mussel's gill, safe in that other body, and the mussels in return send out their own larvae to hitch, briefly, on the fish. It is an arrangement so old and so mutual that neither could now easily be the first.

Above them, at the exact level of the floor, are the frogs.

There are two kinds, and they do not quite belong together. The green marsh frogs come in as accidents through the sluice mouths, big-shouldered, loud, opportunistic, and stay because the eating is easy and the roof keeps the herons off. They can leave. Sometimes they do.

The others cannot, not really. They are smaller, paler — the color of things grown in a cellar, a wet bone-color, pink at the throat where the blood shows through. Their eyes have shrunk to dark points under the skin. Generations of them have been born in the gaps between the boards, have fed on midges in near-darkness, have mated to a chorus amplified beyond any marsh their ancestors knew. Take one out into sunlight and it would be lost. They are not trapped here. They are *of* here, in the way a fish is of a particular river. The made marsh has made them in return.

They are the loudest life in the dome and they know its acoustics better than its builders did. Each male finds a pocket — a hollow under a plank, a sunk clay pot, a notch in the vaulting — that gives his call back to him larger. They don't sing all at once, not truly. They take the gaps between sluice-surges, between drips, breathing in轮 turns around the perimeter until the separate throats knit into one physical pressure that trembles in your teeth. Then, for no reason you can hear, they will drop out in patches, and the sudden quiet feels like falling.

What they eat rises to them. Midges and craneflies hatch in clouds from the scum at the cistern's edge, spiraling up toward the three light slits, uncertain fliers in the damp. Most never get there. A wet smack from below, a tongue in a plank-gap, and they are a frog. What escapes the frogs meets the swifts.

The swifts do not live here all year, but when they return they return precisely. High in the wall the nesting holes wait, each one thumb-shaped where someone pressed wet clay before firing, generations of someones. The birds come screaming through the light slits at dusk, fold what seems an impossible wingspan, and vanish into holes they could find blind — and in that green dimness, blind is close to how they do find them. They sleep clinging in the curve. They raise naked, gaping young on insects caught both outside over the fields and inside on the rise. For a few months the dome has this second, faster hunger at its top, and then one morning the holes are empty again and the place seems, impossibly, quieter at the crown.

Below them live the bats, in the perpetual wet dark behind the vertical wainscot boards. In the day you would not know their number. You might smell them, a close animal musk under the smell of clay and cold water, or hear a shifting, a dry rustle as hundreds resettle against one another. The wood there seems furred if your eyes adjust. They do not leave at evening so much as detonate — a vortex filling the forbidden center for less than a minute, wings loud as hands clapping in a hall, the frogs falling silent under the rush, the hanging shutter knocking in the draft. Then they are gone out through vents you cannot see from inside, and the center empties again.

And above the shutter, in the dead space of its housing, the owl.

There is always exactly one. No one has ever found two, and no one remembers a year without one, though they must die — pellets accumulate, feathers whiten and fall — and be replaced. A new one comes, perhaps drawn by the sound of swifts in the wall, perhaps by a vacancy shaped like an owl that the place itself advertises. It keeps to its own tradition better than people do. It drops its pellets, compacted swift-bone and frog-bone and the indigestible shine of beetle wing, onto a single spot by the east wall, always the same spot, as if the floor there were marked in a language only it reads. It is the only litter allowed to remain. All year it grows, a small grey monument to what the dome eats, until the keepers clear it away with wooden spades — never metal — at the turning of the year.

For the keepers are inhabitants too, though they would be the first to deny they live here.

They live up in the village, in dry houses, but part of every week of their lives is spent down in the damp learning how not to disturb it. You can tell a keeper outside, in ordinary air, by small things: they pitch their voices a little higher than other people when they are indoors, without thinking; they whisper by turning their face to a wall; they touch wood as they pass it, flat-palmed, checking its temperature. Children learn early to move around the edge, never to run across the middle. Old ones know by leaf-curl on the reed baskets by the door when the air has gone sour, know by a change in the frogs' pitch that flood is coming a day before the fields know it, know which plank will need replacing next spring by the note it gives underfoot.

They bring nothing in that they do not bring back out. They replace board with identical board, tile with uneven tile, re-daub clay with flat paddles, clear the owl's place, lift a sluice pin, check the central black post with an absent hand to confirm it is still cold. It always is. Their work is not to improve the place. Their work is to keep it from becoming a room. A room would fill. A room would hear only itself.

Even the plant life here lives by permission, and only just. Algae varnishes the runnels black-green. Midges graze it. And behind the main sluice wheel one fern — a single fern, inexplicable, unplanted, therefore protected — pushes fronds from a crack in the wet masonry. Keepers who would pull any other green thing from the center without hesitation step around that one. It has become a person, in the small way a lone living thing in an unlikely place becomes a person. If it dies, something in the roster of residents will have been lost that cannot be re-ordered from the village.

So the census, if you stayed long enough to make one, would not be a list so much as a layering: mussels and fish in the water, frogs in the floor, midges in the rising air, swifts in the upper wall, bats behind the lower wall, an owl in the roof-dark, a fern behind the wheel — and people, intermittently, humbly, at the door with paddles and spare planks, learning the two rules by hand: keep the center clear, and never raise your full voice to answer the singing.

Because the last resident is the one all the others are arranged around, and it occupies the emptiness everyone else avoids. Call it the echo. Call it the listening of the building itself. It feeds on drip and sluice-groan and frog-call and returning birds, gathers storm pressure from the low land days before the rain, shivers in the shutter when flocks pass overhead. The animals time their feeding and sleeping to its surges. The keepers time their work to it. The frogs were changed in body by it over generations.

Nothing lives in the middle. Everything lives by leaving the middle for it.
