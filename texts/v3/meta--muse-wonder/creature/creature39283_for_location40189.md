The people of Anath Hollow are not blind. That is the first mistake an outsider makes, coming down in the cage with his eyes aching for light.

They can see. They have simply stopped using sight the way you use it.

Their eyes, unmodified, are large and wet-looking in the gloom, pupils permanently dilated until the iris is a thin, dark ring. They blink slowly, and often, because the warm air dries the cornea and the vent-mist stings. Children are fitted early — some families choose the grown lens, a second, translucent lid seeded in infancy that thickens over the eyeball through puberty, clouded to daylight and exquisitely sensitive to heat. Others wear the machined kind, set into the orbital bone with fine gold contacts. Either way, by ten or twelve, a Hollow child sees the world first in infrared.

To them a street is not dark. It is textured grey. Doorframes breathe at the edges. A kettle is white. A hand, held up in greeting, is a bright, articulated star. The districts have temperatures the way surface towns have colours: the Foundry Band flares and pulses, the Cistern Steps run in cool black rivulets, a sleeping house is a dim rectangle with the warmer, shifting smudge of bodies inside. They learn very young not to stare across the void. Looking *up* — which is across, which is down through four hundred metres of nothing at the town on the far side — brings the vertigo on faster than any drug. Their inner ears know that out is down, but their old monkey brains still see a city hanging over them, about to fall. So they keep their gaze low, on feet, on hands, on the metre or two of warm pavement immediately useful to them. Their whole posture follows from that: a slight crouch, head tucked, shoulders rounded forward, not from deference but from orientation. They walk as if perpetually ducking under a low ceiling that is, in fact, the ground on the other side of the world.

They are short, most of them, and broad through the feet and hands. Generations on a curve have not changed their gravity — it pushes at something close to a steady one g toward the shell — but it has changed their gait. The surface under them is never trusted to be level in the visual sense, and is always dished, grooved, slick with condensate, grown over in places with a skin of warm ceramic that has healed itself crooked. So they walk flat-footed, toes splayed, soles calloused into a kind of pale leather. Many go barefoot indoors and out; shoes are for the Foundry, for vent-work, for the chill metal around the heat sinks. A Hollow adult can read a street with his feet the way you read print. The warmed strips in the pavement at a junction, the change from basalt to ceramic to the spongy matting laid down near schools, the teaspoon-hollows worn into every step by a thousand previous feet filling after a Wet cycle — all of that arrives as information. They step, without thinking, exactly where everyone has always stepped.

Heat has edited the rest of them. Thirty-one, thirty-three degrees, always, and humid in the Wet phase until shirts are a second, heavier skin. Nobody wears much. Loose wraps of dried fungal fibre, grey-brown and faintly sweet-smelling when new, rotting pleasantly when old. Pockets everywhere. Sleeves rarely. Hair is a liability — it holds water, it mats, it harbours a fine black mite that lives in the roof-trough succulents — so most adults shave their heads or crop them to a damp stubble, and what hair remains grows in thin and colourless. Their skin is pale, not surface-pale but subterranean: a bloodless, yellowish pallor in raw light, flushed readily, slick always with sweat that does not cool them so much as announce them. You can smell where someone is from before you touch them. Foundry people carry ozone and hot oil in their pores for days after a shift. Cistern families smell green, faintly rotten, of algae and wet stone. The fig-tenders by the heat sink smell, impossibly, of leaf-dust and cold root-water.

They touch each other constantly, and with great formality.

In a place where voices are swallowed by the sphere and returned smeared and useless, and where shouting died out generations ago, they have built a language for the noise and the dark. It began as sign, and became tactile. To speak to someone in the street is to take their forearm — offered, always, wrist up — and sign against the skin, fingers pressing, tapping, stroking in quick, economical patterns that the recipient feels as much as sees. Conversations in a market, during a Loud, look like a crowd of people gently holding each other up. Arguments are faster, harder; you can bruise with grammar. Jokes land as a particular flutter against the inner elbow that children learn before they learn the brass tones for venting and water. Intimacy, real intimacy, is still voiced. Indoors, inside the metre of unnatural quiet that a house's cancellation field maintains against its inner walls, people speak aloud to each other, low and startled by their own clarity. Lovers reserve their voices. So do parents, singing. So do the dying, very often, who ask to be brought close to a wall where the city's roar eats itself down to a flutter, so they can be heard without effort for the first time that day.

They live on the city's cycles because their bodies have been entrained to them. There is no day to wake to. There is Loud, when the six ventilation plants cycle up and wind falls outward from the centre in all directions at once and the mist forms and rains warmly onto every roof, and Less Loud, when the pumps take over their long hammering and talk is easier and the figs close. People sleep in the quiet pockets, in shifts, in hammocks strung close to shielded walls where the silence is thickest. To sleep in Anath is a skilled act. Children are rocked not to a lullaby but to a pump rhythm, and adults wear, even at rest, the faintly startled look of people whose hearts have learned to beat around a machine noise that never entirely stops. Hearing goes, predictably and unevenly, in middle age. The older residents are not deaf so much as selectively tuned — they miss voices entirely and can tell, by a vibration in their molars, which of the six vents is labouring, or whether a freight lift three bands away has taken a bend badly.

Work is mostly tending. The Hollow was built for five thousand, and perhaps nine hundred live there now — nobody keeps an exact count, because the housing rolls are never updated and the empty houses on the High Curve still cycle their locks each evening and air themselves for the dead — so the population is spread across a whole interior world built for five times their number. A person may walk an hour to a shift and meet no one but drones on the way, and think nothing of it. Solitude here is not an event. It is the medium. What community exists gathers deliberately: markets under awnings when the central rain comes, lessons crowded into shielded schoolrooms during the hammering phase, the brief, sociable frenzy of the drip-points when children are sent out with skins and come back soaked and laden and bright in infrared, delighted to be useful.

They harvest pale fungal sheets from behind the cisterns, and eat them fresh — rubbery, faintly metallic — and dry them for insulation and clothing. They keep the protein vats, and the grove of engineered figs whose leaves turn and turn toward a sun that does not exist, tracking the vent-wind, and whose roots must be sleeved in coolant lest the Hollow's warmth cook them. They mend the self-healing walls when the healing goes wrong. They mind the drones that hang, unsupported, in the void, and follow routes that no longer correspond to any need anyone can name; the drone-minders are a quiet, superstitious guild, who talk to their charges by touch when they dock, and who are always the ones sent up — out, in, across — in shielded pods to net a cave swift when one finds the old prospecting bore and spends a night screaming at its own echoes returned from every direction at once. By strong custom, the swift is apologised to, formally, forearm offered to empty air, before it is released. They apologise a lot in Anath, in that formal way, for intrusions of noise, of touch, of presence. It is how a people who live pressed against the inside of a sphere, and yet increasingly alone in it, acknowledge that even nine hundred is a crowd.

Children are raised communally and peripatetically, passed downslope and upslope between households keyed to different sleep shifts, so that there is always an adult in a quiet phase to watch them. They are taught, before reading — written text is large-print, high-heat-contrast, and mostly for signage — to interpret echoes. A Hollow child can throw a click off the curve and tell you, eyes shut, whether a corridor is occupied, whether a cistern is full, whether a wall has healed hollow behind its ceramic skin. They dare each other, inevitably, to look up too long across the void, or to lean into a service duct and call for the blind rats, who are hairless and pink and exactly as adapted as they are, and who flee the drones' culls with the same persistent, unglamorous success.

Old age comes early in the lungs and late in the joints. The damp gets in. Fungal infections bloom in armpits, in groins, behind knees, and are treated as weather. But the constant, even gravity and the necessity of walking — everything is walking, up the curve and over the top of the world and down the other side, always the same effort — keeps them wiry into their seventies. The very old migrate, by unspoken agreement, to the High Curve, to the empty tenements that have been maintained for them by nobody in particular, and live among houses that tend themselves. Visitors bring food and sign, slowly, into hands that have gone almost entirely to callous and can still read a sentence at a touch.

They do not bury their dead in the shell; there is nowhere in the shell that is not also someone's floor. The dead are composted, formally and without squeamishness, into the fungal beds behind the cisterns, after a night kept in a shielded room where anyone who wishes may come and speak aloud to them in the manufactured quiet. Then their house, if they lived alone, is simply not struck from the rolls. Its doors go on cycling. Its shutters go on testing themselves. Somewhere on the far curve, a room airs itself each evening for a person who has become insulation, protein, a slightly richer patch of pale sheet behind a dripping tank.

They are not a failing people. That, too, is an outsider's mistake. The steps are worn, the walls scarred in crazed webs, the figs restless, the population thin — but nothing in Anath suggests an ending being resisted. It suggests a settlement, negotiated over generations between bodies and a place that was never meant to be looked at, only inhabited from within. Warm, dark, resonant people, who navigate by foot and forearm, who reserve their voices for love and death, who live pasted around an emptiness and have learned, as their central discipline, not to look directly at the sky — because the sky is full of streets, and lit windows, and people exactly like them, walking upside down over their heads, not looking back.
