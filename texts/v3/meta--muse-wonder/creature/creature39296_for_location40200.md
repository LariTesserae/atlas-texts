From above, during the long midday drowse, it seems as if nobody lives in the Cup at all.

That is partly camouflage, and partly timing. The inhabitants fold themselves away when the canopy turns its silver undersides to the sun. They hang in shade. They curl in the cool hearts of their gourd-houses. They press into hollows in the barrel-trees where the air is still and damp from stored water, and if you are looking for movement, for faces in windows, for anything upright and busy declaring itself a people, you will conclude that the green bowl is only plants and a few drowsy animals keeping each other alive.

Wait until evening.

When the leaves open again, the town fills — not suddenly, not with any sound of gates or voices, but the way a forest floor fills after rain. Shapes uncurl from under roots. Eyes open in doorways at ankle height, at knee height, at the height of a stooping child. Bodies that were indistinguishable from bark or shadow or a heap of fallen fruit begin, unhurriedly, to be separate from the ground.

They are not one kind of creature. That is the first mistake to give up.

There is a people of the Cup, if by *people* you mean those who make and keep the town, but they are not a species standing apart from the others and ruling them. They are a relationship. A choreography so old that every participant has been shaped by its part.

At the center of it — if there is a center — are the gnawers.

You would see them first in the lanes at dusk: low, heavy-bodied, warm to the touch even through their fur, moving on four limbs as readily as on two. When they rise up to inspect a roof-vine or exchange something in the market-hollow, they stand no higher than your waist, their weight settling back over broad hind feet. Those feet are remarkable. Almost hairless underneath, padded, spread wide, the toes webbed with loose skin — not for swimming, for there is nowhere here to swim, but for feeling. They walk on the whole listening surface of themselves. A step is also a word. Two soft pressures, a pause, a drag of the heel, and the message travels through the moss-mat and root-hair to a neighbor three houses away, who answers without lifting her head by pressing back.

Their forepaws have long, furred fingers and blunt, flexible nails, good for parting leaves, carrying fruit against the chest, combing a companion’s coat, guiding a tendril into the notch where it should take hold. They have never made a tool, and after a while among them you understand that this is not a lack. Their bodies are so variously equipped that a tool would be a clumsy extra organ. What needs cutting, their teeth cut. What needs binding, a living tendril binds. What needs carrying travels in cheek, paw, mouth, or the loose fold of skin at the throat. What needs remembering is grown into a house-wall or laid down in scent along a threshold, where every foot that passes reads it again.

They are furred in the colors of the town at different hours — moss-brown, bark-black, a greenish grey along the back that catches leaf-light and breaks up their outline, pale almost to silver on the belly. Each has a patch of longer, lighter fur at the throat which can be puffed up or flattened tight at will. In conversation — and they are in conversation constantly — that patch flashes like a small moon opening and closing. *Here. Not here. Come nearer. Enough. Mine. Yours. Safe.* Combined with posture, with the angle of the ears, with a breath of musk released from glands at the wrist and jaw, it is a language denser than it first appears. A whole negotiation over a bladder-fruit in the market may consist of two animals standing forehead to forehead, throat-patches flickering, occasionally touching tongues to the fruit-skin to taste whose water it carries and how old it is.

They have large eyes, dark and wet-looking, set wide for the green dimness under canopy, and ears that are less ears than furred funnels, constantly swiveling not toward voices but toward creaks, to the tick of cooling rock, to the faint answering tone that sometimes stirs in the glassy blades of the rim. Their muzzles are short. Their front teeth are broad, orange-enamelled, and never stop growing, which is why the doorways of the gourd-houses must be gnawed and re-gnawed, and why gnawing is not labor to them but maintenance of the self. An old gnawer whose teeth have at last worn down to smooth, useless pegs is fed pre-chewed moss and soft fruit pulp by its grandchildren, and sits all evening in the doorway it can no longer widen, feeling the traffic of the lane through its feet.

And they carry their water visibly, as other creatures might carry wealth, or pregnancy, or shame.

In the heat of the Cup their cheek pouches hang full and translucent, the skin stretched so thin over stored water that at dusk a faint green light passes through them. A mother crossing a lane with her pouches slack has recently given drink to her young. A stranger arriving at a market-hollow with both cheeks bulging is rich and can be generous without a word. To offer water they do not pour. They press a bladder-fruit mouth to mouth, or, between intimates, pass it directly, tongue to tongue, with a gravity that makes you look away. A young one who dribbles is not scolded — nothing here is scolded aloud — but is gathered up instantly by three or four adults, paws spread to catch the drops before they reach the mat, and licked clean with an attentiveness that is half tenderness and half accounting.

Their young are born blind, pink, and entirely silent in nests of shed fur and dry fern-feather deep inside the house-gourds, where the wall is thickest and the mountain’s vent-warmth comes up through the floor. They are hushed not by being taught quiet but by being constantly touched — a paw across the back, a warm body folded over them, a throat pressed to their ribs so they learn the world first as vibration. By the time their eyes open they already know how to go still when the rock teeth hum. By the time they can walk they are reading foot-pressure through the mat the way you would read a familiar voice from another room.

But the gnawers could not live here alone, and do not.

The hare-sized grazers who crop the street-moss are not livestock. Nobody pens them, nobody drives them. They have their own routes, older perhaps than the gnawers’ streets, and the streets exist because the grazers keep passing that way, cropping the moss to a springy lawn and leaving behind dry, nearly odorless pellets that the burrowers carry down into the compost almost before they cool. The grazers have blunt, continuously growing molars and broad padded feet like the gnawers’ own, though they walk always on four. They sleep in heaps in the gathering places and are groomed by whoever passes; in return their bodies carry seeds in their fur from district to district, pressed into the mat wherever they roll. Some have lived so long in one neighborhood that their backs are gardens — seedlings sprouting in the felted hair along their spines, tended idly by climbers as they pass overhead.

The climbers are smaller, leaner, bright-eyed, with prehensile tails and furred gripping palms, and if the gnawers are the town’s ground-keepers, the climbers are its roof-line. They run the vines above the lanes all evening, pruning with their teeth, tasting a leaf here and nipping it off there, redirecting a tendril with a hind foot while they hang upside down and exchange throat-signals with the household below. They sleep curled in the forks of barrel-trees with their tails locked around branches, and their children ride them everywhere, clinging so tight and still that at a glance a climber may appear to have two heads, one looking forward and one, smaller and more solemn, looking back the way they came.

At dusk the gliders come down from the high rim-side trees, launching themselves in a slow, unhurried traffic between rooftops. They are the size of cats, perhaps, though most of that is membrane — soft, down-edged wings of skin stretched between elongated limbs, patterned underneath in the pale ultraviolet mottling that the night flowers also use, so that from below a glider crossing the canopy gaps looks briefly like a flower opening and closing against the sky. They eat moths. They eat, also, the parasites that gather at the mouths of water-bladders. They carry pollen on their bellies from roof-garden to roof-garden and are welcomed everywhere, even though they are dreamy, vague creatures who will fall asleep mid-grooming wherever they happen to land, hanging from a vine by one foot with their wings wrapped around them like a second, softer body.

Below them all, and mostly unseen, live the burrowers: blind, naked-skinned, huge-toothed, smelling richly of compost and warm earth. They are the Cup’s digestion. They turn the litter under the lanes, drag fallen leaves and dead roof-palms and the bodies — for the dead of every species are given downward, without ceremony and without waste — into the deep dark where everything softens into the food of seedlings. The gnawers leave fruit rinds at burrow-mouths the way you might leave a plate by a door. The burrowers leave behind tunnels that drain and aerate whole neighborhoods, and sometimes, at the turning of a season, a burrower will surface at night and lie for an hour on the warm mat, sightless face lifted, while small hands of several species pick the mites from its folds.

The tiny bright lizards keep the leaves. The hand-sized moths, with felted wings that make no powder and no sound, keep the night flowers. The flowers, in their turn, feed the moths in ultraviolet silence and pay the gliders in roosting shade. Even the barrel-trees can be counted inhabitants of a kind: individuated by the gnawers, named in scent at their bases, their inner cisterns tapped only at certain seasons by certain families who have tended those trees for longer than any living memory, trimming their roots, sealing cracks in their bark with chewed pulp and resin. When a great tree dies the neighborhood does not so much grieve as rearrange itself around the new fall of light, planting in that bright wound first the quick soft things, then the slow ones, then, years later, the seedling gourds for houses yet unneeded.

This, then, is who lives in the Cup: not a race, but an agreement among races, renewed nightly in grooming, exchange, pruning, cropping, pollination, burial.

They have no stories that can be overheard. If they keep history, it is kept in what is grown where — in this terrace bent aside for the rock that fell before any living grandmother was born, in the inner skins of a house that has housed eleven generations and whose walls, sliced by a falling tooth in some past year, would show eleven green rings around a sleeping chamber. Elders are not those who speak longest; there is no speaking. Elders are those whose scent is layered deepest into the thresholds, whose footfalls through the mat bring answers from the most houses at once.

They have no wealth separate from water and warmth and the health of the mat, and no poverty except thirst and a house-wall growing too fast for a family small in numbers to keep the doorway chewed open. Such a family will take in young from a crowded house across the bowl — adoption passes through the town as easily as water, mouth to mouth — or climbers will move down for a season and help gnaw, hanging in the doorway at evening, shaping it wider between bouts of grooming, while the children ride their tails and learn the taste of green gourd-rind.

They mate in proximity and gesture, in the long evening when the whole bowl opens. They court by carrying water further than it needs to be carried, by arriving with both cheeks full and offering the first drink, by sitting so close their throat-patches brush. They fight rarely, and fighting too is silent: two gnawers pressed chest to chest in a lane, paws locked, each trying to push the other’s foot out of true contact with the mat, while neighbors shift aside without apparent interest except that every foot is lifted clear of the ground between them, so the quarrel has no audience and must end quickly for want of witnesses. What cannot be settled by pressure is settled by smell — one leaving a mark so final on a threshold that the other simply begins, the next evening, to take the long way around that house.

As for what they know: they know fog the way you know a relative’s step. Before the night breath slides down from the cooling rim-stone, the whole canopy begins almost imperceptibly to tilt — not because anyone commands it, but because climbers have spent the evening checking angles leaf by leaf, and gnawers have opened the root-mouths, and the mat-keepers have chewed yesterday’s moss dry enough to hold tonight’s gathering. They know which rock tooth leans and how far it has leaned in their lifetimes. They know which vent breathes too hot and must be capped with wet litter, which house is ripening too hard to survive another season, which tree is keeping its water selfishly because a crack has sealed a cistern away from its own roots. All this knowledge lives below language, in paws and teeth and the daily routes of bodies, and is therefore never argued with and never entirely lost — only composted, when its keeper dies, and grown again in whoever ate and slept and was groomed in that keeper’s place.

On your last evening, from the notch in the rim, they are hard to pick out at all. The town below is a dense stirring of greens and browns, a market of fruits laid out by color, a hundred pale throat-patches opening and closing in the dimness, feet whispering messages through the mat, wings crossing the gaps like flowers. Then the fog begins to slide down the black glass teeth of the rim, and one by one the inhabitants go still — grazers folded, gliders hung, gnawers curled in living doorways with their cheeks full of the day’s gatherings — while millions of leaves tilt above them to drink.

That stillness is not absence. It is the whole population, at once, listening through the soles of its feet for the mountain to make a sound — and choosing, together, in the oldest civic act they have, not to answer.
