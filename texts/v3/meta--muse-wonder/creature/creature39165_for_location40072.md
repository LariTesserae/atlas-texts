They are still there.

That is the first correction to make to Vaith, and the hardest to keep. The city is not abandoned. It is unmemorable.

You have met its people. You met them on the first plaza, again by the cisterns, again — with a small, polite nod, because by then you were becoming familiar — on the stair that took you up into a cellar. Each encounter was perfectly ordinary while it lasted. It is only afterward, at a lower turning of the street, that your memory thins to a swept threshold, a shutter easing shut, a bowl still humming on a table though you do not remember anyone setting it down.

Above a certain height, the most recent thing goes first. In Vaith, an entire citizenry lives inside that fact the way valley people live inside weather.

The Vaithin are low people, to begin with. Not short in the way of being diminished, but built close to the ground they are always, carefully, not rising from. Barrel-chested, broad through the ribs and shoulders, short-legged, with calves knotted from staircases. A child of Vaith learns to crawl early and to walk late and to stand up all the way later still, and some of the oldest inhabitants return to a kind of considered crouch, not from weakness — they can carry stone all day — but from wisdom. Upright is a kind of altitude. They do not waste it.

Their skin is the pale of the upper rock after a winter of rime, and it does not burn under that small white sun so much as it gradually takes a print of the light, a faint blue shadow that stays for days. Their hair grows slow and mineral, dark in youth, going blue-gray at the temples exactly the color of the felt in the wall-joints, and about as fast. They harvest that felt for their bedding, and stuff their own clippings back into it; over generations it is never entirely clear, in a Vaithin house, which part of the mattress was grown by the wall and which by the family.

They have large, soft noses that can close entirely, like a held breath made flesh. They have ears too thin for the cold — great, translucent, veined ears, lit pink from behind, that they fold flat against their skulls in wind and open, startlingly, indoors. It makes them look always to be listening slightly past you. They are. They are listening for your shadow to arrive, for your footsteps to come up from the stone, for the delayed parts of you to catch up before they answer. Among themselves, to speak to a person's shadow while the person stands in sun is a grave intimacy, and to ignore the shadow entirely is how you address a stranger you intend to keep strange.

*Sleep with the mouth closed* is not a proverb there. It is pediatrics, ethics, hygiene. A Vaithin sleeps sealed, breathes through the nose in long, rationed tides, and wakes with the inside of the mouth dry as paper. Speech costs. Every word spoken at that height has weight and weather; it leaves the body and it does not dissipate, it pools, it thickens in high corners, it goes stale in unopened rooms. So they speak low, and low in both senses: pitched down, and from the lowest room that will serve. They say what must be carried downhill and leave the rest as gesture — a shutter opened with the others to ring an hour, a stone weight set on a market counter to mean *this much, and I am still owed the pause after it*, a hand pressed once into the dust inside a dry cistern to record that the invisible level of the household stood, that season, this high.

Children are given names late and lose them early, and seem untroubled by it. A name said too often in the upper city loses letters, and the Vaithin have decided, sensibly, that a person should not. They are called instead by route: *third door below the dyers*, *the one who comes by the cistern steps when the bowls hum*, *younger than her shadow*. The names work. Their maps work too, street by street, breath by breath. It is only when you try to hold two of their maps in the mind at once that the far wall recedes.

They eat sparingly and without romance. Mineral felt, soaked in well-air until it softens. Dried moth, which tastes of mica and dust and very faintly of the window it wanted to enter. Hare, on those rare occasions when the silence has left enough of one to cook, eaten entire — ears crisped, bones ground to a pale grit indistinguishable from what collects on the market counters, because in Vaith very little that is lost stays lost; it settles, it is swept, in time it is served back. They do not drink. There is nothing so crude as drinking. At dawn, before the rime can flee straight from crystal to nothing, they go along the north sides of statues with cold scrapers and harvest the night feathers into covered stone cups, and they breathe over those cups through the day, mouths closed around a private weather. A treat, for a child, is a smell released from an upper room under supervision: river mud. Green leaf. Rain on another person's hair. The child stands downhill of the door with her ears open and her eyes streaming and tries, politely, not to remember all of it at once.

Their work is the city. It would be wrong to call it maintenance, because that implies an original state they are failing, slowly, to preserve. Vaith is not decaying and being repaired. It is being continuously *agreed to*. Every fifth door is swept because every Vaithin household sweeps its own threshold and the thresholds of the four houses nearest to it that are currently unoccupied — not empty, *unoccupied*, which in Vaith can mean for an afternoon or for a hundred years. Houses move, on nights when the stone contracts and the streets re-negotiate their heights. A family may wake in a pumice facade by the dyers' pits that yesterday stood by the cistern steps, and they take it as valley people take a change in wind: they check the kindling, they weigh the door they love, they learn the new lowest way to market, they press a fresh handprint into the cistern dust at whatever height their life now makes. The house, for its part, has been swept. It is always swept. Somewhere in its upper rooms the stored ends of their songs will have shifted in the dark, like sleepers turning toward a voice.

And they age upward.

That is the thing the valley stories never get right, because the valley imagines height as ambition. In Vaith, rising is what happens to a body that can no longer keep its density. The old get lighter. Not frailer — lighter, measurably, scandalously, so that a grandmother must weight her door against herself, must carry market stones in her sleeves on windy plazas, must sleep under a felt mattress instead of on it. Their footsteps stop arriving altogether. Their shadows spend longer and longer away, resting against far walls, flattened and content. They move into the upper stories that younger families use only for storage, and live among the pauses and unfinished sentences, adding their own. An unfinished sentence, left by a very old woman in a high room, can keep its shape for decades. Her great-grandchildren climb up, breathless and elated, forgetting by the landing why they came, and sit in the thickening of it anyway, companionably, until it releases — not the words. The *posture* of having meant them.

At the end — and they would not say end; their language, what survives of it downhill, has a term closer to *being shelved* — the lightest of them simply fail to come down in the morning. The bed keeps their hollow a long while. The felt closes slowly. A shutter somewhere in the quarter opens with the others to ring an hour, and the air above the market counters grows for a moment dense at elbow height, and some transaction no one can quite follow is, to everyone's evident satisfaction, concluded.

They are not ghosts. They cast their weight on scales, when they consent to stand on them, and they stub their toes, and they stain their fingers for weeks in the dyers' dust and rinse it off instantly in the memory of water. They court by exchanging lowest routes to one another's houses, which is to say they give each other, breath by breath, a way to arrive unexhausted. They marry by learning to wait, at the foot of a staircase, for the version of the beloved who is higher than she thinks, and letting her pass without comment. They bury no one. The city gains another few feet it will never admit to, and the boulders in the gravel gardens grow their beards of needle ice on the lee side, and the ice sings at a pitch most people hear as pressure behind the eyes.

If you stay long enough — and nothing prevents you; the houses are sound, the instructions are cut plainly into the lintels, readable whenever you are properly out of breath — you will notice your own routes shortening, your own name wearing smooth at the edges, your calves learning what your map cannot keep. You will sweep the doors nearest yours. You will stop trying to remember whether you have met your neighbors.

You have. You are meeting them now, halfway down, where the air is just thick enough to hold it: low, broad, pink-eared, patient people, rising with their whole city so gradually that only the stars, large and near and refusing to blink, could be trusted to say whether it is Vaith that is climbing out of the top of the world —

or its people, carrying it, one remembered breath at a time, toward whatever has been waiting above them with its mouth closed, listening.
