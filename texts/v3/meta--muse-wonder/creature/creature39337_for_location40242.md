They are not tall people.

It isn't only that the houses make them seem so — low doorways worn smooth by a century of shoulders, lintels you duck under without thinking, stairs built for short strides. It is the bodies themselves. On the plateau, tallness is a waste: more skin to feed the wind, more length to keep warm at the far ends. They grow compact. Short legs, short arms, broad through the chest and shoulders, thick-necked, with hands that look too large for them until you watch those hands knap stone, lift it, set it true without mortar.

The chest is the first thing you notice when one of them strips to work in the summer noon. It is deep front to back, barrelled, the ribs flaring like the hoops of the casks they do not have. They were born breathing thin air and they go on doing it without ceremony — a slower, fuller breath than a lowlander's, drawn down and held a fraction longer, as if the body were sieving the air rather than gulping it. Children learn the rhythm before they learn to talk properly. You can hear it in a sleeping house in winter: a roomful of people above the stable, breathing in unison almost, unhurried, while frost feathers across the inside of the shutters.

Their blood runs thick and dark. Cuts are slow surprises. Their cheeks and noses and the backs of their hands are permanently reddened, not with sunburn exactly, though the sun at that height will peel a newcomer in a day, but with a dense net of vessels close under the skin, a kind of built-in weather. Old people's faces are maps of it, purple-red against skin tanned the color of old leather, split and healed a hundred times around the mouth and eyes from smiling into wind.

Their eyes are narrowed by more than ancestry. Everyone squints here. The light off snow, off pale scree, off summer cloud built over the plateau's own center, is a white hammer, and from childhood the lower lid rides high. When they travel any distance they wear the slit bone goggles, and come home with pale bands across their faces where the straps sat. Indoors, in the dim roofed alleys and common halls, they are famously sure-footed in darkness, feeling their way by a hand trailing a wall they already know stone by stone.

They move at the pace the air permits, and have made a virtue and then a manners of it. Heavy work goes by spells: lift, drag, heave — rest. Lean on the haft, breathe, talk a little, heave again. A lowlander trying to keep up will be sick and ashamed by mid-morning; a plateau man will still be going, at exactly the same pace, at dusk, and will have got more done. Nothing in their gesture is wasted. They do not wave from across a market. They do not shout, except to animals, and even that is a short, hoarse note meant to carry through felt and hide rather than air. Speech is low, close, economical — not because they have little to say, but because talking is breathing spent on purpose. Sentences are short. Silences are not awkward. Two old women shelling barley will conduct half a conversation in glances over the bowl, and what is said aloud will be the part that needed a sound.

It makes them seem, to visitors from below the rim, taciturn and then suddenly, disconcertingly funny. Their humor is dry, physical, at close range: imitations of a lowlander gasping on the road up, of a calf born in a doorway during a market, of a husband who harvested the frost off the wrong side of the wall and froze his sister-in-law's wash. They laugh with their whole chests, and then stop to get the breath back, still grinning.

They dress in layers that are less clothing than portable architecture. Against the skin, woven shirts — wool, mostly, undyed or dyed in the greys and browns the plateau provides. Over that, heavy tunics and coats of felt and sheepskin, wool grease still in them, smelling faintly of lanolin and smoke. Over that, in wind, a stiff outer wrap belted tight, so the body's warm air is trapped and carried with you. Boots of layered felt with leather soles, resoled for years. Mittens on cords. Hats with ear-flaps that tie under the chin, for everyone, without vanity. A woman's hair is braided tight and oiled and often wound with strips of cloth that carry, in the color and knot, which town, which family, whether she is married, whether the marriage house is building this year. A man's beard is not ornament, though they are proud of a good one; it is insulation, and in winter a white mask by breakfast, frosted with his own breath, which his wife or child will crack gently off for him indoors like shelling an egg.

They grease exposed skin with rendered fat in the worst months, and smell, as houses do, of that fat burning patiently in lamps, of dung-smoke — wood is far too dear to burn except for the finest work — of boiled grain, animal breath, wet wool drying by a hearth. It is not a clean smell, but it is a warm one, and warmth is the real currency. Everything social follows from it. No one with sense lives alone. Houses share walls because a shared wall loses half the heat. Families stack: grandparents, parents, children, a married brother and his wife not yet able to build out, all around and above a ground-floor stable whose cattle breathe the ceiling warm all night. Privacy is not a room. It is a curtain, a turned back, the elaborate courtesy of not hearing what you plainly heard through felt and floorboards. They are masters of that courtesy. A quarrel that would empty a lowland house is conducted here in whispers over three nights and settled, because there is nowhere to storm off to that will not kill you by morning.

Children are born into arms and stay there. At this altitude birth is slow, dangerous work, and infants are small — kept skin to skin under the mother's coats through the first winter, carried inside her clothing on the road, so that a woman traveling appears, absurdly, to be breathing for two, which she is. They grow slow too, and are not expected to carry a full load or a full conversation until late, but they are set early to the plateau's first chore, the one that begins every day of a life: gathering frost before the sun steals it. Small children with small buckets, scraping the inside of the outer wall, the ropes, the cattle's broad backs — the animals standing patient, steaming, accustomed — learning the exact economy of it, what a household of nine and a byre of four needs, drop by drop, to drink and wash and cook. Nothing makes an adult faster than a child wasting water. Nothing makes one gentler than watching a child learn not to.

Their food is what patience can store. Black barley, bitter and hardy, ground on saddle-worn stones, boiled into porridges and flat griddle cakes that keep. Fat-tailed sheep, plateau cattle — meat for feasts and for the deepest winter, milk soured and salted and dried into stones of cheese that will outlast the maker. Thin-shelled chicken eggs are not food so much as news: someone is well-off, someone's daughter is recovering, someone wants to be thought generous. Tea, traded up the long road at ruinous expense, is brewed weak and drunk scalding and salted, less as stimulant than as a way of getting hot water into the body without apology. They eat close around the hearth, from common bowls as often as not, and the measure of a house is not whether its people go hungry in March — everyone is thinner in March — but whether the granary mound is opened in the right week and shut again with something still in it.

Work is in their hands in a way that makes lowland tools look impatient. Iron is for edges only. The rest is stone, bone, horn, antler-polished and bone-needled, and wood that came up the road on a sledge and will be a lintel, a loom-bar, a grandfather's staff, and a child's gate in turn. Every third house has its loom and the tick of it is a house-sound. Their weaving is dense to the point of cruelty — cloth that stands up by itself, cloth that sheds wind the way slate sheds melt. Their building is slower than their weaving: a house is a fifteen-year argument conducted in stone, a wall a season, a roof when the children are tall enough to hand slate up, a new room when a marriage requires it, bridged over an alley the family also owns half of. A man may cut the first stones for the room his grandson will be born in and consider that a normal span of ambition. Debts, marriages, journeys to the rim and back, are all reckoned the same way — not in weeks but in seasons, in thaws. News arrives at cart-pace and is treated accordingly: as story first, fact later, to be weighed against the season it left.

They are herders as much as townspeople, and the two conditions are not separate. The great cattle have right of way through the streets by law older than the streets, and a man will move his market stall onto the steps, still bargaining, while a hundred steaming backs fill the way below him, and count it no interruption. Every family keeps something close — sheep in the courtyard pen, dogs thick as bears asleep across thresholds, cats in the granary, pigeons communing in the eaves — and the boundary they observe is not between human ground and animal ground but between the animals that are *of the house* and the herds that are of the plateau, passing through on their own old business. They read weather in the herds coming down early, in marmots whistling from the burrow-towns, in ravens gathering. They are buried, when fuel and frozen ground make other rites unworkable, on high stone platforms beyond the towns, given back to the lammergeiers and the clean air, so that even in death they do not waste wood or occupy soil that took a hundred baskets to make.

What they are not is hard in the way songs below the rim like to imagine. They complain about the cold constantly, inventively, as a form of affection for anyone who has to go out in it. They hoard small luxuries — a bronze bell with a clear note, a piece of real wood that smells of the forests, southern salt, an egg — and display them shamelessly. They weep at weddings held in roofed courtyards with the cattle audible below. Old age comes to them as an enlargement of the heart in both senses; the literal one that altitude demands, and the social one, because an old person who can no longer haul stone can still mind the cistern, mind the children, remember which year the spring failed and which cousin owes what. They die oftenest in winter, quietly, in the warmest room, with the breath going slower and fuller until it simply does not return — and the house gathers a little more frost than usual that morning, and carries it, like everything else, to where it is needed.

In the evening they stand, sometimes, in a market court among thousands, and listen. You can hear meltwater ticking into a cistern two courtyards away. They consider this one of the sounds of wealth.

They have never had much water. They have never had much air, or wood, or warmth that they did not have to keep. What they have had is each other, wall to wall, and time at walking pace, and they have built out of those scarcities a dense, patient, immensely populous world — people who breathe as if air were something to be husbanded, speak as if words were, and lay one sharp stone on another in the full expectation that their great-grandchildren will be warm inside it.
