It is not, at first, that they are many. It is that they will not make room for the idea of one.

The first thing that resolves out of the amber dimness and the pain in your ears is a frog on the nearest valve wheel. Thumb-length. Olive-brown, a dark mask through the eye, a stripe down its back like a wet seed split open. Its throat is blown up to a bubble, translucent, thrumming so fast the skin is a blur, and it is singing — you can see it singing rather than hear its particular note, because its note is already inside your sternum. Then you see the second frog under it, clamped to the same spoke, and the third under that, their forefeet braced on each other's backs, all three bubbles going in phase. Then the wheel is not a wheel, it is a cluster. Then the pipe is not a pipe.

They are boreal chorus frogs, *Pseudacris maculata*, thousands of them in a room nine feet by six, and they have used every surface the way lichen uses stone. They line the faux boulders at the waterline shoulder to shoulder, damp sides touching. They coat the copper runs two and three deep, the bottom layer holding on with splayed toe pads, the top layer holding onto the bottom layer. They crowd the grout lines and the conduits and the rims of the ceramic drain tile. In the sump they float with only the gold-rimmed tops of their eyes breaking the surface, patient as crocodiles at a scale where patience has shrunk to fit in a teaspoon. When one moves — a leap from pipe to valve, legs unspooling — a hundred eyes swivel with a minute wet click you feel rather than hear.

Individually, they should not be doing this. At two degrees Celsius they should be shut down. Chorus frogs are freeze-tolerant hibernators; in a real cave, in a real winter, they would be lodged in mud and leaf duff, hearts slowed to a rumor, glucose flooding their tissues like antifreeze, letting ice form in the spaces between cells and waiting, in perfect abeyance, for thaw. This was a hibernaculum. It was built for sleeping.

They have not slept. They have not woken, either. They have found the margin between the two and made a life there, at the exact temperature where torpor ought to begin, kept from it by the one thing they themselves make: vibration. The chorus shakes them. One hundred and seventeen decibels, constantly, reflecting off tile and water and copper, does not let the cold settle into them completely. Their skins — permeable, shining, never dry — tremble continuously. Their tiny hearts are being massaged by sound. So they sing through a winter that never deepens and never breaks, throats tiring and recovering and tiring again, each animal dropping out for minutes or hours to sit with its bubble deflated and its mouth a thin, exhausted line, and then inflating again exactly on pitch, because its neighbors have held the place in the chord for it.

That is why they all face the same way near the loudest junction. Not worship. Orientation. They are turning their bodies into the standing wave the way trout turn into a current. At the nodes, where the mist droplets hang jittering and refuse to fall, the pressure is least; at the antinodes it is a physical push. The frogs arrange themselves by feel along that invisible architecture, densest where the sound holds them up. They are both the instrument and the audience, packed into the resonant box of the room until source and listener are indistinguishable.

And they are not alone, because a single species cannot close a loop this tight without unraveling. The room has furnished the rest of the choir out of whatever was stocked, stowed away, or arrived in the moss pads.

Under the frogs' feet, where the tile is not submerged, the floor breathes with cave crickets. Camel-backed, eyeless or nearly so, the color of wet cardboard, their antennae longer than the rest of them and never still. They wave those antennae in slow figure-eights, reading the air, reading the vibration off the concrete, stepping with exaggerated care between puddles. They graze the green film that coats everything. When a frog snaps — a movement so fast it registers mostly as a small, separate percussion inside the big sound — a cricket vanishes. The rest do not scatter. There is nowhere to scatter to, and scattering has apparently been bred, or learned, out of the economy here. They resume waving.

In the water, smaller still, is the grain that makes the whole thing fed. White isopods, blind, translucent, curled like punctuation, by the hundreds and then by the thousands where the sump deepens to eleven centimeters, picking at detritus, at shed frog skin, at the still bodies of frogs who have finally stopped taking their turn in the chord. Water fleas jerk through the two centimeters over the floor tiles. Snails no bigger than a match-head rasp the pale green ceramic. Everything eats the algae, and the algae — a slick, faintly luminous film where the caged bulb touches it — eats the light and the lime leaching from the young concrete and the waste of everything above it. The frogs eat the crickets and the isopods. The crickets eat the algae and the dead frogs. The eggs, laid in gelatinous strings wound around pipe-joints in the warmest mist, unwrinkle in the cold at a rate that would be impossible outside, held as they are in unbroken sound; tadpoles graze, metamorphs haul out onto the tile with tails still attached like commas, and take their places on the second layer of a pipe stack. Exactly as many haul out as go still. A biologist counted through the porthole, once, and counted again the next season, and got the same number to the animal, because the number is not a population. It is a property of the room, like the temperature.

Above them all, in the ceiling seams, are the moths. Pale, mealy-winged, dense as shingles, tucked into every right angle of conduit with their wings folded tent-wise over their backs. They have been waiting for a night that never arrives inside for so long that waiting has become their entire behavior. Now and then one loosens, flutters blindly for three wingbeats in the saturated air, is knocked sideways by the sound-pressure, and resettles an inch away. The frogs ignore them. They are too high, or too dry, or simply not part of the chord. They persist the way the fern persists.

The fern is, in its way, the elder here. One plant, bleached almost to translucence by the dim amber light, has put out three fronds from the crack between two concrete shells and stopped, perfectly, at three. Its white rootlets feel along the grout lines and encounter only grout. It does not grow and it does not die. It photosynthesizes at the rate at which it respires. The moss beside it, black-green cushions clamped to the pipe joints where the misters hit them, does the same: a millimeter of hunger, a millimeter of satisfaction, forever. They give the lower levels just enough green to stand on, and refuse any more, because more would be change, and change has been tuned out of this place.

Take any one inhabitant away in your mind and the picture holds. That is the unsettling recognition that arrives after the pain in your ears flattens into pressure. The frog on the valve is not a character. The cricket mid-step is not going anywhere in particular. There is a larger animal here, and it is made of replacement. When a throat tires, another inflates. When a body stills, an egg takes up the slack downstream. When a cricket is eaten, its grazing is assumed by its dozen identical cousins an hour later. The skin of this animal is copper and tile and concrete shell. Its blood is the recirculated sump water, pumped under the floor and misted from the ceiling and wept from the walls at exactly the rate it is taken up. Its nervous system is the standing wave — those suspended droplets at the nodes are its synapses made visible, holding still because the signal never stops. Its voice is 117 decibels, not as output but as posture, the way a plucked string holds a shape.

It lives by not progressing. Outside, its cousins live by season: thaw, breed in a frantic two-week clamor in meltwater pools, gorge, disperse, burrow, freeze, thaw again. Inside Module 0, season has been collapsed to a point. Breeding and hibernation, hunger and satiety, birth and decomposition are not successive events here but simultaneous textures, all happening at once at low speed in two degrees and permanent wet. It is an ecosystem that has achieved the condition of a single organism: homeostatic, self-repairing, deaf to itself because it *is* the sound it would hear.

So they are, finally, very quiet animals, for all the noise. No frog calls to attract a mate from a distance; the distance does not exist. No cricket flees a predator; the predator is a permanent feature of the ceiling of its world, and also its god of population control, and also, eventually, its grave and its nursery. No moth emerges to find the moon. They have traded the whole vocabulary of urgency — migration, competition, escape, pursuit — for the one verb the room permits: *maintain*. Maintain pitch. Maintain moisture on the skin. Maintain the antenna's wave through air that is nearly water. Maintain three fronds.

If you could stay — if your lips were not going numb, if your plugs were not already failing, if thought itself were not being shaken loose — you would eventually stop seeing frogs and crickets and isopods as separate tenants. You would see the chord wearing them the way it wears the mist droplets: holding thousands of small, cold, wet lives suspended at the nodes of itself, jittering, catching the amber light for a fraction longer than gravity or winter allows, not falling.

And when the hatch seals, it does not miss you. No head turns. The bubble throats keep time. The count remains identical. Up through the yellow steel and the curing concrete, all that escapes of this dense, patient, many-bodied inhabitant is a hum you can feel with a gloved hand on a turbine-house wall, high in silent snow — the mountain, with something in its teeth, singing to itself at the exact note where life stops needing time to go on living.
