At noon it would be easy to mistake this place for crowded.

The caracara is in the middle of the moss then, tall and listening. The hummingbird stitches itself from flower to flower. Finches are working the scree, butterflies are up, bees are loud in the orange bells, and if there is a herder in the stone circle he will be mending the grass roof or turning his face, burned and numb at once, toward the sun.

None of them lives here.

The caracara comes at an hour and leaves at an hour. The finches go down in a loose line before the shadow takes the bowl. The hummingbird is a visitation, a zip and a trembling of green light. The herder keeps his digging stick in the windbreak and his ash swept clean because he knows he will not be staying — he will be gone before the cloud pours over the rim, or he will sleep one night with his back to the stone and be gone at first thaw. Even the swifts, screaming in the bright air above the lip at last light, will not come down into the cold.

She stays.

She is in the black pan at the foot of the largest rosette, the one whose skirt of dead leaves hangs almost to the water. To find her you would have to know not to look for movement. Marbled brown, marbled green, a thumb's length from nose to rump, her back is moss and wet rock at once. Pale flecks stand on her skin like lichen on quartzite. Her sides are the color of peat water. Only her eyes give her away, and only when the sun is directly on the pan — two wet gold beads, lifted just above the surface, patient as bubbles.

By night she is not, to any ordinary sense, alive at all.

The bowl freezes from the bottom of the sky down. First the shadow, blue and complete while the peaks are still rose-gold. Then the frost tide going white across the moss. Then the pans ticking as they skin. She feels it coming hours before a human would, in her feet, in the water cooling against her belly, in the flowers shutting around her. She does not hop for the edge like something fleeing. She goes down.

Under the pan is blackness that never quite hardens: a mouthful of wet peat, the swollen roots of moss, the underside of a cushion plant where a hundred years of slow growth has made a warm dark reservoir. She works herself into it with small, deliberate pushes of her hind legs until only the dome of her back breaks the mud. Her throat stops moving. Her heart, which at noon was a visible flutter under the pale skin, spaces itself out — a beat, a long nothing, a beat. Ice feathers up through the pan above her. Ice feathers in the skin of her own back. The water in her outer flesh turns and she lets it, holding the still, unfrozen center of herself around her heart and her eggs like a cup inside a cup inside the cup of the mountain.

She has done this not for one winter. She has done it ten thousand times. There are no seasons up here to count her life by. She counts in thaws.

And oh, the thaw, when it comes, is not gradual for her. The light hits the rim, spills, strikes the pan. The ice loosens with a tick. Water begins to move again over her back in a cold bright sheet, and with it her heart remembers its quicker rhythm, the frost goes out of her limbs in a deep prickling ache, her throat fills — not with song, not yet, with air, with the first fierce hunger of the day.

Everything in her is timed to the next few hours, because she has only a few.

She feeds as if the afternoon were already ending. Black beetles ticking through the moss stems. Small flies risen off the water. A spider, jeweled and stupid with cold, still slow from the night. She takes them with a tongue faster than the eye that watches her. Her belly, translucent when she is empty, clouds and swells. In the brief summer of noon the pan around her comes alive with algae, a green brightening in the black water, and if she has laid that week — strings of eggs no bigger than wet seeds, tucked under the cushion where the water holds — the tadpoles are already working at it, tails trembling, racing a clock made of ice.

For them the whole of childhood must happen between one hard night and the next warm spell. They feed at noon. They stall at dusk. They go torpid in the peat before they have legs, before they have lungs, suspended and resumed and suspended again, until the day one of them hauls itself onto the moss with a tail still trailing and sits, stunned by air, exactly the color of the place it will never leave.

She sings, if singing is the word, only after the caracara has gone.

*Tok.*

One note. Nothing more. It does not carry — up here no sound does — but it crosses her pan and touches the wet rock, and from another pan twenty paces away, across a stretch of deep moss no frog could cross dry, an answer comes back after a long pause.

*Tok.*

Not a chorus. A privacy. A way of saying where the water is this week, because the water is never in quite the same place twice. A pan cracks and drains. A new hollow fills where melt found a seam in the rock. She moves then, at the wettest hour, when the cloud has been and gone and every surface is jeweled and running, traveling through the moss rather than over it, swimming through its green depth from reservoir to reservoir, feeling for the next black stillness with her feet. Her whole geography is a hand's depth of water held in stone, and she redraws it nightly.

She knows things no visitor stays long enough to learn. She knows the rosette above her pan by the chemistry of its fallen leaves in the water, and she knew, weeks before the spike rose taller than a person, that it was going to flower and die. She laid her eggs in its shade that summer because the dying plant let more light reach the pan, and warmer water means faster children. She knows which cushions are hollow and which are solid to the core. She knows the vibration of a boot set down carelessly — she felt one go through fifty years of growth once, felt the cold water rush into the broken dome, and she did not come up that day at all. She knows the herder's step now, the careful one, stone to stone, never on the green domes. She knows the caracara's stillness most of all, that terrible listening quiet in the center of the moss, and under it she becomes mud, becomes peat, becomes a thing with no heartbeat worth hearing.

She is furred with nothing. She grows no silver coat like the rosettes, builds no skirt of her own dead to stand in. She is too small and too wet for any of that. Her insulation is the place itself, borrowed by the hour — moss over her, peat under her, a thumbnail of water above her that can be ice by night and sky by noon. She survives the equatorial winter that comes every single evening not by resisting it, but by letting it take almost everything. Breath. Movement. The water in her skin. She keeps back one dark warm drop at the center and waits for the sun to come and unlock it.

So if you stay, once, past the hour you were warned to leave — if you sit in the stone circle and watch the frost bloom visibly across the moss and the pans go over with their ticking skins and the flowers shut and the last swift cut away into the bright air — do not think the bowl has emptied.

Under the ice of that black pan, under the rosette's hanging skirt, in the wet dark at the root of the moss, a heart is beating so slowly you would miss it if you held her. Once. Then nothing. Then, in its own good time, once again.

She is what lives here.
