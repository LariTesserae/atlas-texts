First, you look for whoever runs it.

That seems like the right question to ask in a place with this many machines. Someone must change the filters. Someone must oil the dredge arms, replace the bright bolts, read gauges still swinging behind second-story windows while floodwater carries leaves through the room. Someone must have strung the sodium lights under the water, and laid the catwalks, and built the little concrete shrine that is always nearby and never approached.

There are signs, if you want to call them that.

A pump house door will be open in the morning that was chained at night. A valve that was screaming will be packed, neatly, with black mud and root-fiber. Fresh paint — safety yellow, institutional teal — will appear on a handrail in a place no skiff has reached for weeks. Logbooks in waterproof boxes will have their last line finished in a handwriting that starts out almost like yours, and then keeps going down the page past the paper's edge, into a fine, branching notation of veins.

But there are no bunks that have been slept in. No food. No latrines. No footprints that don't fill instantly with water and pollen and small green shoots. No animal life at all to leave them.

And by noon, the fresh paint will be under moss. By afternoon the packed valve will be part of a tree. By the next morning the pump house will be fifty meters away, or the channel to it will, and the door will be chained again from the inside with vine that flowers through the links, and the flowers will be already rotting, white and bruise-purple and unvisited by anything.

Don't decide too quickly that it's the plants.

The plants are certainly there, aggressively, beautifully there — mangroves in near-touching ranks, ferns unfolding in minutes, vines climbing into empty air and not stopping, seeds the size of fists cracking open before their splash settles. But the plants alone don't explain the *maintenance*. A root doesn't ordinarily reroute a translucent flex-pipe around itself the day before it grows there. A fern doesn't synchronize ten turbines coupled through a flooded shaft so that they tear at each other in perfect, destructive harmony. A mangrove doesn't write in rain.

Because near the turbine bays, where the roar becomes a pressure in the teeth and the air feels used up, the rain hesitates. You have seen it. Droplets stop, tremble, sort themselves into grids and text-like patterns too fast to read, into the exact shape of the leaf they are about to hit an instant before they hit it. That isn't weather. That isn't botany. That is something using the rain the way you use breath to fog a mirror — briefly, to see if a shape will hold.

Look down then, into the black water.

Above the surface, your hands are clean, if soaked. Below it, your reflection is growing. Thin green filaments across the reflected face. Roots at the eye. This is not something happening *to* the reflection. This is the part of you that the place can actually reach. Up here, you are animal, warm and temporary and deafened, wading chest-deep and holding a cable. Down there, lit amber by the drowned street grid, you are already still enough to be planted.

Whatever lives here lives under first.

Not under the water, exactly. Under the *event* of things. A second before and a season behind. Its saplings are buttressed giants an hour later because for it, later is a place it has already rooted in. It flowers around windows that have not yet been built because the building is, to it, a trellis that hasn't finished arriving. Cause does not keep its place here because the inhabitant does not keep to yours. It occupies the whole loop at once: seed, strangling tree, pulped green shred storming out of an outflow pipe, slick on converging currents becoming an island, becoming soil. Individual plants are not its citizens. They are its moments, made visible.

The factory is the same. Think less of industry abandoned to jungle, or jungle overrunning industry, and think of a body that has grown two kinds of organs because one kind wasn't enough. Turbines are hearts, but crude ones, external, replaceable. Pumps are a throat that swallows the basin and vomits it upward as rain. Copper mesh under mangrove roots is a liver. The electrified pylons are nerves exposed to weather, crawling with blue sparks. The cooling towers that inhale storm clouds and exhale boiling water are not failing at their function. Breathing, here, has been revised.

It takes water from below itself and delivers it above itself. It filters itself through itself. It floods itself faster to outrun flooding. That isn't a paradox it is trapped in. That is metabolism.

And it is — this is the hard part to hold onto while the roar is in your jaw — it is *one* metabolism, and the basin is not its environment. The basin is its extent.

The phosphorescence that stitches every leaf edge in green fire where electrified water touches it: that's not decoration. That's sensation traveling. The ticking of roots expanding in fresh basalt, impossibly audible under everything: that's not growth as you understand growth, passive and slow. That's deliberate pressure, applied in the dark, joint by joint. The dense yellow-green pollen that coats your skin and eyes and the water's surface, though nothing ever fertilizes anything in a way that makes sense — it makes sense if the thing doing the shedding and the thing doing the receiving are the same creature, at different times. It is talking to itself across its own canopy. It is trying to remember sex, the way the rain tries to remember text.

It has no pollinators, no grazers, no birds, no worms, because it has replaced every animal function with a process, and then forgotten that a process isn't a creature. Propellers shred its fallen flowers. Pumps move its seeds. Rain carries its pollen sideways and up. Machines do the chewing. Dredges do the burrowing. It has outsourced its body so completely to weather and industry that there is nothing left for an animal to be *for*. That is why the leaves are uneaten. Not because nothing is hungry. Because the hunger is the place.

Sometimes it grows operators.

In submerged pump rooms you will find them if you go by skiff at night, when the underwater lights rise through the flood like an inverted town and everything is luminous black. Human-height bundles of root and vine standing at consoles. A thickness where a shoulder would need to be to reach a lever. Fine white rootlets spread across a gauge face like fingers learning Braille. One will be wearing a work glove, not because a hand is in it, but because the glove was left hanging on a hook and the vine grew through it and found the shape useful, and kept it. They do not turn toward you. They are not pretending to be people. They are the place, growing into the negative space people designed for themselves, the way water takes the shape of a drowned room.

Leave them alone and they will be trees by morning, strangling the console. Cut one — no one has, or no one who has will describe it separately — and presumably it would bleed bright chemical water, and presumably somewhere upstream, an hour earlier, a seed would fail to root for no reason.

It is not hostile. That would require it to distinguish you from rain.

You are weather to it, briefly. A warm pressure system moving through on a skiff. A new surface to bead on. It will test you the only way it tests anything: by trying a version of you that can stay. In the water, rooted at the eye. On the boot seam, a seed working in with patient green. In the logbook, a sentence finishing itself in your handwriting and then branching. In the rain over the turbines, a grid of droplets holding, for half a second, the shape of a face before it hits the leaves and becomes unnecessary.

Inside the chapel — if it is a chapel, the small dry shrine with the cross-shaped vent — the sound stops. That is reported by everyone who enters, separately, as if each thought they were the only one. Inside, the roar that comes through your teeth is absent, and in the absence you can hear the plants growing. And that is worse, they say.

It is worse because outside, the roar *is* the creature, so loud and total it becomes a second silence to hide inside. Inside the shrine, out of the rain, out of the flood, you are finally quiet enough to hear it thinking at the walls. Ticking. Splitting stone. Trying the door with roots that have not reached the door yet.

Nothing animal lives in the basin. Nothing ever needed to.

Something older in process and younger in every leaf lives there instead: a single green and yellow and teal organism made of forest and factory and storm, distributed across a flooded valley and across the hours on either side of now, building itself to contain itself, drowning itself to feed itself, succeeding at neither, succeeding at both.

It doesn't watch you leave. Watching is a thing eyes do, and it gave up on eyes when it rooted in yours — the reflected ones, still open under black water, still looking up after your skiff has gone, while above them the real you dries out and goes half-deaf for days and thinks, mistakenly, that you visited an empty place.

You didn't.

You passed through a body that had not finished deciding whether it was land, and it passed, gently and completely, through the only part of you that would hold still: your reflection, already planting itself, already staying.
