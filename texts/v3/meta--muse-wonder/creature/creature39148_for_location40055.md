It would be easy to go a long time believing nothing lived there at all.

Not because the sea is sterile — it isn't — but because everything in it has learned to be absent in the ways a visitor expects presence. No birds work the surface. No weeds lift from the shallows. No animal breaks water to breathe, calls, splashes, or sings. For days the only moving things are the steel work-boats, very low and very slow, and their wakes going out flat behind them into water that has no current to take them.

The life is underneath, and it is underneath in patches.

Where the plateau is still cracking, where fresh black basalt has split open in the cold and begun to react with the water — hydrogen, sulphur, mineral heat bleeding out of stone — the bacterial mats grow. White sheets. Rust-orange sheets. Not meadow, exactly, because meadow implies green and growth toward light, and these grow toward chemistry. They spread only as far as the fresh rock allows and not a hand's breadth further.

Everything else arranges itself around that limit.

The vent-grazers come first, in the evolutionary sense and in the visible one: plate-sized filter animals cemented by the million to the vent rock, their shells opening and closing in a synchrony so perfect it looks mechanical. They do not synchronize to each other. They synchronize to the roar. The whole sea is filled, sternum-deep, with the sound of its own drainage going over the southern rim — turbine halls, cataracts, generator harmonics propagated through two-degree water for hundreds of kilometres without fading. The grazers ride its pressure like a tide that never turns. Open. Close. Open. Close. A reef of them seen from a submersible looks like a single animal breathing very slowly.

Above them, at times, the glass eels arrive.

Not a school so much as a weather event. Thousands, millions, transparent-bodied, each one catching the ordinary daylight from above and bending it once, so that the water for fifty metres in every direction goes the colour of smoke. They feed in the bacterial drift and are fed on in turn, and then — because there is no plankton bloom to hold them, no seasonal pulse to follow — they are simply elsewhere. A week of black, resolving emptiness. Then smoke again, without warning, at a different ridge.

And then there are the bell-cows.

They are the animal this sea would be remembered by, if it were remembered. Eight metres is a small one. Fourteen is not unusual. They have no eyes at all, not even vestigial pits under the skin — eyes would be nearly useless at depth, and in the shallows, where you can see a submerged peak a hundred metres down through water clearer than air, eyes might actually be a liability: too much distance, no particles, no haze, nothing by which to judge scale. A bell-cow does not judge scale visually. It judges it by ear, except that *ear* is also the wrong word. It listens the way a building listens, through its whole structure.

For the world has been speaking at full volume for a hundred and forty years, saying one thing, and the bell-cow has evolved to use that speech as sonar it never has to make. The roar comes down from the rim falls, reflects off knife-ridges of basalt and volcanic glass, interferes with itself where trenches cross, builds standing patterns of pressure and null in the cold water — and the bell-cow reads those patterns the way you would read a room by the echo of a voice that never stops. Its ribs, showing through translucent skin like the hoops of a paper lantern, are not only ribs. They resonate, fractionally, each at a slightly different depth of tone. A young animal learns the map of its home ridge the way human children learn a language: by immersion, without ever deciding to. Move a bell-cow — no one has, but in principle — and it would be not blinded but deafened, lost in a silence it has no equipment to interpret.

It has a mouth like a scoop, plated and rasping, for cropping bacterial mat off rock flanks, and when it has been feeding its stomach glows faintly from inside, blue-green and diffuse, the light travelling slowly along the rib-lines in pulses. That light is its voice. Bell-cows communicate, insofar as solitary animals a month apart in empty water need to communicate, by pattern: a slow wave down one flank for *I am here*, a double pulse and hold for a female in season, a sudden whole-body flare — very rare, very costly in the cold — that can carry through clear water farther than any sound it could make would need to. They mate by approach and touch in darkness, flank to flank, the light-patterns syncing gradually like the grazers below them syncing to the turbines. They make no voluntary noise at any point in this. A bell-cow will drift for a month, feed, mate, calve, and die without once adding a sound to a sea that is already too loud to hear itself in.

The calves are born almost clear, two metres long, ribs not yet tuned, and for the first years of life they stay close enough to their mother to feel the pattern of her resonating body as a kind of local weather — a private interference map, moving with her, until their own ribs harden into the wider chord. Longevity is difficult to measure in a place where nothing rots properly. Wrecks sit upright on ridge crests with their paint still legible. Dead bell-cows, when they sink into the trenches, sink into two-degree stillness and are taken apart slowly, by grazers and by chemistry, over what may be decades. Some of the older station hands swear there are individuals they recognize — a scar where a survey cable burned a flank, a missing pulse in the third rib's light — that were already old when their grandparents were signing to each other as children. Nothing here booms. Nothing crashes. A species at equilibrium with fractured rock and bad water can afford to be very old.

The humans, insofar as they can be distinguished as a separate arrangement, have become a smaller, warmer version of the same solution.

There are not many of them. A pump station every few hundred kilometres: concrete blockhouse on a reef or gravel spit, intake pipes going down into black water, discharge steaming where waste heat meets the cold, a jetty, a fuel tank, a mast-light no one navigates by. Crew of six, or four, or — at the stations that have run crewless for decades and are visited once a year by a boat that stays a day — a keeper alone, receiving mail and seals and bearings and then watching the boat become a rumour again.

They are born to the roar the way the bell-cows are tuned to it. Above water you hear it properly only near the rim, a sound that gets into the fillings of your teeth; below water it is total, and even on land, in the blockhouse, with the doors sealed against plateau wind, it comes up through the rock and through the floor and through the bedframe. Station children learn sign language before speech and mostly keep using it — not because they are deaf, exactly, though early hearing loss is so common it is not discussed as an injury, but because shouting is a waste of breath in a world that is already shouting. Their conversations are tactile, visual, unhurried. They touch forearms to get attention. They stand close. Visitors from the lowlands find them unnervingly quiet and unnervingly still, and find, after a few days, that they have begun signing back without noticing, and sleeping with their jaw clenched around a note they can feel in their sternum.

They are pale in a way that has nothing to do with ancestry — there is sun up here, ordinary blue noon sun, but it falls on two-degree water and black rock and sealed cabins, and it does not warm anything it touches. They grow up around exhaust stacks crusted with mineral salt, around ice that forms in the lee of every building in the same wind-carved blades each winter, around brass gauges that have moved four centimetres in a century and a half. Their work is the ledger: inflow, outflow, rainfall, seepage, the pumps returning lowland groundwater up through the plateau to exactly replace what the falls take away. It has always balanced. Their grandparents' logbooks are in the same handwriting, effectively, as theirs. Spectrograms of the sea drawn before they were born are ruler-straight lines, and their own spectrograms are the same line, and they take a craftsman's pride in that which outsiders mistake for apathy.

They do not fish, much. There is nothing to catch that wants to be caught at the surface, and the vent-fields are left alone with a superstition that is really just systems thinking: the mats expand to exactly the area of fresh rock and hot discharge and no further, so you do not crop the thing your whole volume depends on. Sometimes a bell-cow will come up — not to breathe, it has never needed to breathe up here — but to drift in the sunlit layer off a station jetty for an afternoon, ribs pulsing slow blue, occupying all of the visible water. The crew will stop work and sign to each other about it, unhurried, the way you might remark on weather that is not going to change. Man and animal regard each other, insofar as a blind animal regards anything, by pressure and by light. Neither makes a sound.

That is what lives there, in the end: things that have given up on making noise in a place that never stops making it, that navigate by a voice not their own, that feed on stone instead of light, that communicate in pulses along the flank and signs across a workbench, that can be very old and very solitary and surrounded, at all times, by a medium thicker than water. Drop over the side of a boat at noon and one will cross far beneath you, lit from above, trailing light, hearing the whole geography of its world in the chord of the falls four hundred kilometres away — and above it, on a reef of shattered black rock, a keeper will watch your boat's wake dissolve into nothing, and raise one hand, and say something with it, silently, in the roar.
