At first you would not pick her out as the inhabitant. There is too much else that is alive, and all of it is small, low, busy at its own scale.

The piezo beetles go on clicking on the warm walls. The glass eels go on sliding in the floor channels, silver-gutted, transparent, nosing toward the sump. The vent sparrows press their naked bodies to the cable looms and shiver messages through the copper. Drain flies crawl with their wings furled. Springtails harvest the wet seams. In the valve recesses the hammer frogs wedge themselves and swell and add a note so low you feel it rearrange your ribs.

They all seem separate. Territories made of a degree of heat, a difference in vibration, a pipe that sweats a slightly sweeter water. Until the filters purge.

Then the backwash comes, rich and brown-green and stinking of algae and hot minerals, and everything in the pocket turns — eyes, antennae, damp blind faces, the whole tiled wall of beetles lifting their wing-cases a fraction together. Not only toward the outflow. Toward a point just beside it, in the deepest recess, where the two great membranes flex in the wall and the indicator moss breathes its dull blue light.

That part of the wall moves differently.

The membranes are supposed to move. Everything in there is supposed to tremble, ring, be caught for a trembling instant in the standing roar. But this moves slower. It rises. It settles. In the brownish half-dark, lit amber and green from the valve housings, it has the wet highlight of a pipe housing and the irregular outline of mineral build-up. Ivory and rust-colored deposits have grown down over it in long teeth. Black-green moss has felted itself across it wherever the vibration cancels against its surface. At a glance it is architecture that has gone a little biological with age, the way the whole chamber has.

Get closer — you have perhaps three minutes left before the floor begins to tilt for you — and the architecture opens an eye.

Not well. The eye is filmed, half-lidded, crusted at the rim with polymer salt. It does not need to see. Nothing in there sees much; the sparrows gave it up generations ago. The eye closes again and does not so much look away as cease to participate, and underneath the moss and the mineral, under a plating of beetles that shifts and resettles itself over the curve like a living scale mail, the shape resolves: shoulders, or something that was once shoulders. A vast, flattened back. Limbs folded and folded again into recesses that were not made for limbs but have been worn to them.

She was a hammer frog.

She still is, taxonomically, in the way the fern in the cracked filter cup is still a fern, though it now grows in short armored segments and will never uncurl a proper frond. Once, sixty or seventy years of custodians ago, she was the size of a fist, and she did what the others do — found a warm valve hollow, inflated her sides hard against the metal, and let the recess amplify her sub-bass croak until the whole pipe system took it up. A good place. A place where a small animal could be enormous, acoustically. Where the current brought food to her instead of her having to cross the shredding air.

She never left it. There was no reason to leave it. And the pocket fed her with an almost insulting generosity.

Every purge brought her the tower's waste: dead motes raining down out of the maintenance clouds, beetle larvae, drain-fly eggs, biofilm sloughed from the filters, the mineral-rich sludge the eels graze and cannot finish. Her mouth — wide now, low, lipless, lined with pale mineral ridges where teeth are useless — simply opened with the tide. The water came over her. What was edible stayed. What was not was slowly incorporated.

So she grew along the only lines the room allows. Not up. Nothing tall survives the sound; it tears at the cellular level, it finds veins and seams. She grew low and wide and dense, spreading out of the valve recess and across the sump filter and into the angle where the floor bells down to the drain, following the cancellations, the quiet nodes in the roar where the moss also grows. Her skin thickened in plates, each plate separated by a felted seam, so the vibration passes across her in segments and does not build. Her sides learned to stay partly inflated for weeks at a time, pressing her soft tissues against ceramic-metal and membrane alike, turning her own body into one more baffle. Her bones thickened. Her inner ears, ruined in the first season, calcified into solid conduction rods; she hears now through her sternum, through her kneecaps, through the polished places where her belly never leaves the floor. She hears the way the custodians tap.

And they do tap. Four fingers, a pause, two fingers flat. The sign and the signal are the same thing here. She answers by letting a fraction of air out of her left side. The note is not loud. Loud has no meaning. But it goes down through the piezo-ceramic slab and up through the membranes and changes, for a few seconds, the shape of the standing wave in the chamber. Droplets that had been hanging trembling in midair complete their fall all at once, a small private rain. The beetles lift. The sparrows go still on the cables. Somewhere two decks up, in the inhabited quiet, a turbine's sympathetic hum presumably smooths out — because she is, now, load-bearing.

That is the bargain the whole pocket slowly settled into. The tower's maintenance motes, following an optimization no human wrote, leave her repairs rough. When her skin cracks in the heat, the clouds gather over the crack in the brief shape of a hand, a lens, a wing, and re-knit it with mineral fiber and resin, but they leave the surface pitted. Moss catches the pits. Moss holds water against her. Water cools the plate of beetles living on her back, and the beetles, converting the endless vibration into a trickle of metabolic current, warm her from above in return, clicking faintly as they feed her the surplus energy of the roar. The glass eels graze the biofilm from the seams between her plates where she cannot reach and where, ungrazed, it would stiffen and change her pitch. She, in turn, exhales a slow chemical warmth into the sump water — frog waste, skin secretions, the faint sweet rot of an animal who is also a marsh — and the eels follow that warmth, and the sparrows come down, blind and naked and unafraid, to pick mites from the moss on her shoulders and to raise their litters in the hot, protected fold where her forelimb meets the wall. In the worst pressure cycles, when the turbines peak together and the air in the pocket seems to solidify, everything small shelters on her leeward side, in the acoustic shadow of her body. She is the only large quiet the room has.

She eats sparingly for her size and almost never hunts. A drain fly crawling across her face is taken by a tongue that comes out slow and shocking, pink-gray, adhesive, unhurried — it has nowhere to miss to. Now and then a young hammer frog, ambitious, wedges too close to her recess and croaks too competitively, and the tongue accounts for that too. Mostly she filters. Mostly she waits for the tide table of the purge and opens her mouth and lets the tower feed its own ear.

For that is what she has become, functionally: the thing that hears for the place that exists to swallow hearing. She regulates. When the roar begins to acquire a harmonic the baffles were not tuned for, she swells and absorbs it into the soft places under her ribs. When a coolant leak sings somewhere high in the cable looms, the sparrows shiver it down, body to body, until the message arrives as a disturbance in the beetles on her back, and she shifts one plated foot and taps, and taps again, in a rhythm the custodians — and now the tower — know to read as a location. When she sleeps, which is a matter of her eye filming over more completely and her breathing slowing to four, three rises a minute, the chamber's note goes very slightly flat, and the indicator moss dims, and the whole hidden system aches a little in its teeth until she wakes.

She will not fit through the hatch anymore. She has not fit through it since before most of the custodians were born. Calcium analogues have rooted her, lightly, to the sump grille; her oldest skin has mineralized into the floor at the edges and would tear if she were hauled. It is not clear she experiences this as captivity. The concepts require an outside. She has a territory two meters long and a little more than a meter wide, a greenhouse warmth of thirty-two degrees, water that falls without pause, food that arrives on a schedule older than her own body, and a sound so total that it has replaced silence, weather, and time. Her tadpoles — the few, in the wet years, that the eels do not take — grow up glassy and furtive in the floor channels and most of them are eaten, and the ones that are not crawl away to find valve recesses of their own and begin, fist-sized, to swell and listen.

You back out on your knees, turning sideways at the unmarked oval, with the water beading on your zipper and the chord of the room still ringing in your sinuses. Behind you the beetle wall breathes. The sparrows resume their shivering. The great shape in the recess lets her eye close entirely, adjusts one side against the membrane by a fraction of a centimeter, and the roar — folded, delayed, released out of step with itself — finds its note again and holds it, living, underneath the tower above the clouds.
