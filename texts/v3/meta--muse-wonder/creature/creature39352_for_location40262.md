Nothing lives there in the houses.

That is the first mistake to correct. The houses are too obvious — three and four stories of basalt and mudbrick, ground floors open in deep arches to the street, upper rooms shuttered against a light that never comes. Nothing has ever cooked in them. You can tell by the hearths, which are clean in a way that has nothing to do with abandonment. No ash has compacted in them, no fat has ever entered the stone. The granaries have never held grain; there is no chaff in the corners, no weevil dust, no sweet rot in the wood. The beds built into the walls of the bathhouses have the unworn smoothness of stone that was shaped and then only ever touched by water.

They were not built to be lived *in*. They were built to be lived *around*, the way a lung is built around air, or the way a harbor is built around a tide that has its own business.

What lives there lives in the interval.

You notice it first not as a shape but as a misuse of space. A courtyard widening when you leave it, its drips retreating from one another until the sound suggests a hall much larger than the walls allow. An arcade that takes forty paces on the ebb and two hundred on the flood, both ends visible the whole time, the columns passing and passing without getting any closer together. This is not the city malfunctioning. This is the city making room.

On the ebb, what lives there is compressed. Folded, perhaps. Held in the still places the rules warn you about — in the public basins that keep their reflections a moment too long, in cisterns whose olive-black algae skins crawl a few inches a day and split and begin again, in the perfect, uncracked ceiling shown by a bathhouse pool whose actual ceiling is ruined. Still water keeps things, because still water is where they can wait without being torn apart by the twice-daily pulse.

They prefer stillness the way other creatures prefer shelter.

In the ebb they are almost nothing: a pressure in a flooded market that is not quite accounted for by the depth at your ankles. A warm sphere of water hanging in a doorway at throat height, refusing to fall. A footprint already in the wet stone on the far side of a bridge, going the other way, a little smaller than yours — not child-sized, just *condensed*, as if the person who made it had been pressed by the heat into a slightly denser version of themselves.

Look for them directly and there is only wet stone, empty colonnades, a canal with nothing flicking beneath its mineral skin.

Look down, and the city is inhabited.

It is during the steam, when the pulse comes in from the black marsh and the canals swell step by step up the water-stairs, that they emerge — if emerge is the word for something that was never entirely inside. The reflections light. Amber windows. Pale blue windows. Upper stories above rooflines that, raised, are flat and salt-filmed and dark. A bridge completes itself across a canal that has no bridge, and long before the reflection is steady, the real water below the empty air begins to ripple in a line, footfall by footfall, under a weight crossing from bank to bank.

They live in that second city, which is not a reflection of this one so much as this one is a scaffolding for it. Theirs is dry in places this one is drowned. Theirs is paved in white stone, and sunlit, as you glimpse for half a blink with iron-and-lime water in your eyes in a roofed street where the rain is falling upward into the vault. Theirs has weather in its sky. They keep their windows open to it.

They are not ghosts. Ghosts imply a former, solid tenancy. These have always been the way they are: temporary arrangements of water with opinions about direction.

Seen through the hanging drops — never seen straight, they do not survive being seen straight — they are human in outline the way a reflection in moving water is human. Tall when a courtyard needs height in it, stooped under the bridging rooms when the flood is low. Limbs that end not quite in hands but in a spreading, a branching, like water finding the grooves cut for it in a sluice. No faces that persist. What persists is posture. The angle of a head turned to listen for drip-pitch. The habit of pausing at thresholds that have worn into ramps, as if remembering when they were steps.

They have no eyes for the dark city. In theirs, presumably, they do not need a substitute. Here, they navigate the way the city teaches you to navigate if you stay long enough: by temperature and by sound. They can tell the outer districts by the high, clear ticking of cistern towers, the center by the low tolling of the bathhouse drains. They avoid the seam-streets where the ankle-water turns suddenly, impossibly cold. On the far side of those, the dripping falls in unison and stops when it knows it is being heard, and even they will back out of that other, non-continuous city exactly the way they came, without turning, until the warmth returns — which tells you that as old as they are, there are older arrangements than theirs, less willing to be accommodated.

They do not speak. Water assumes the shape of whatever speaks into it, and they have learned better. They communicate in displacements instead. A bridge delivered back to its starting bank altered by one detail. A stair that was on the left now descending on the right. A district settling in its foundations with a small groan, and a canal half a minute later slapping a wall for no visible reason — an answer, arriving late, because distance here is not a fixed cost. If you live long enough in the steam, you begin to understand that the city's isolated drips are not random. Each one falls alone, considered, into a basin or hollow worn in a stair, with long silences between. It is a language slow enough that a human lifetime might hear one sentence. They have time for it. They are made, mostly, of patience and return.

Their life cycle is the flood pulse. Twice in what would be a day, they are rinsed loose from the cisterns and basins and flooded markets, circulated through the clay pipes that knock softly in the walls as they cool, breathed out through the vents between buildings in a rush of steam. For a little while they are everywhere and populous: a crowd implied by a hundred ripples under bridgeless air, by shutters open only in the canals, by distances stretching to accommodate streets full of traffic that makes no sound the thick air will carry. Then the ebb takes them again and they thin, they condense, they retreat into whatever stillness will keep them intact until the next pulse. Some do not make it into stillness in time. Those are the footprints that stop in the middle of a plaza, the reflection that goes on looking after the head has turned — a remainder, evaporating slowly, trying to finish its gesture before the heat takes it.

That is also how they multiply, if they do. A visitor stays too long in a bathhouse at the center of the third district. A visitor looks. A visitor is kept. At first only in that pool. Then in other still waters nearer the door. Then, one flood later, a set of prints slightly smaller than the visitor's own feet, going the other way across a bridge that should not be crossed on the flood. They are not stealing people. It is simpler and less merciful than that. In a place where water remembers shapes, staying *is* a kind of consent.

And yet they are not the only thing there.

They all — in both cities, the dark one and the lit one — give the central plaza a wide berth, and this is the detail that, given time, stops being architectural and becomes biographical.

The plaza the floods never enter. Black basalt fitted without mortar, dry with an oven-dryness that hurts the teeth in a city where even interiors sweat. Buildings turned blank-backed to it, floodlines climbing their outer walls higher and higher century by century, as if the water were trying to see over. In the middle: nothing. No well, no statue, no drain. A darkness above it more complete than the brown dusk everywhere else, so that sound arrives across those fifty paces late and flattened, as if it had traveled years.

What lives in the plaza is what the water-arrangements were made to live around.

It does not reflect, because nothing about it is wet. It does not ripple, because it does not cross. If the others are the city's circulation, this is its retention — the one thing the twice-daily breathing cannot move. Standing at the edge long enough, you feel it not as a presence but as a pressure exerted by an absence that has mass: the held point in the cycle, the pause when all the water in canals and cisterns and hanging doorway-drops stops for exactly the length of one breath before the next pulse. The stones tick faintly, as if cooling, although they never cool. That ticking is the closest it comes to a voice, and it is not addressed to you.

Perhaps it was the first of them, and it dried out. Perhaps drying out is what, given enough centuries of fidelity to one place, they eventually do — water giving up its arrangements one by one, heat remaining, form remaining, everything that could be rinsed away gone. The floodlines climbing the backs of the surrounding houses would then not be the water trying to see over. They would be the others trying, pulse by pulse, generation by generation, to reach it, and failing by exactly the height of one more dark band carved into a doorpost somewhere else.

Or perhaps it is what the city was built to contain by never quite touching: a dry god, a sealed cistern, a founder who chose the bottom of a hot dark basin precisely because nothing could be seen there — not by the sky, which never clears, and not by visitors, who learn too late that looking down is the only way to look at anything honestly.

But the city goes on indifferent to the interpretation. The pulse comes in. The canals swell. The windows light only where they cannot be entered. Somewhere a reflection finishes a bridge, and the real water beneath empty air dents under the first careful step of someone crossing over to a bank that exists more completely than this one.

They are still crossing.
