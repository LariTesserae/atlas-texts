At first you would say no one lives there.

There are signs of habitation everywhere — lanes kept clear, leaves pruned, towers tended, the endless polite traffic of the conveyors — and no inhabitants. No houses. No burrows in the mat, no nests in the seven canopies, no footprints in the pollen snow except your own, and then, in the central fields, not even those quite where you left them.

Lower down there are aphids.

They live in the root-mat, pale, eyeless, soft as eraser shavings, moving very slowly through the dry peat and feeding on the silicone sap that leaks, drop by reluctant drop, from the oldest roots. Nothing hunted them for a very long time. They have lost, in an orderly, evolutionary way, almost everything except appetite and patience. If you lift one — people do — it does not struggle. It folds its articulated legs, segment by segment, and waits to be interesting enough to be put down.

The drones could be called inhabitants, after a fashion. They move in slow strata above the canopies, pollinating, pruning, inspecting, most of them plant-built now: hulls of stiffened leaf and grown ceramic, wings that are also solar collectors, little roots trailing like jellyfish tendrils to sip power from any mast they pass. They are not tame and they are not wild. They are busy. When one wears out it does not fall. It lands, folds, and is carried away, and a year later something in the burial sectors is making very good use of its copper.

But the drones are hands. The aphids are lice.

The thing itself is harder to see because there is nowhere it is not.

It lives in the mat under your boots, three meters deep in the shallow places, thirty in the old sectors, a continent of woven root and dead leaf and powdered tray bound together with resin. That is not its bedding. That is its body, or the oldest, slowest part of it. Its blood is not water — nothing here can afford water — but a clear silicone oil, faintly sweet, that moves through root and stem and cable and never, if it can help it, evaporates. Its nerves are copper and something greener than copper. In the mature zones its veins are plugged directly into the grid, and you can feel its metabolism as a faint, dry warmth coming off the leaves on a cold bright day, waste heat rising from a thought being held very still for a very long time.

It is not a plant that learned to be a machine. It is not a machine that was overgrown. After this many centuries the distinction is genealogical at best. Its newest limbs are recognizably industrial: matte ceramic, magnetic rails, gantry arms that slide without sound. Its middle-aged limbs photosynthesize through engine housings. Its elders have bark over their hinges and put out, once a year, small warm fruit that hum in the hand and fall upward, sometimes, if the weather in the center is right.

It eats light, mostly, and dust, and electrical gradients. In the understory — that inverted forest below the stairs, green-lit and colder still — it grows itself downward into vats of dry nutrient powder and comes up as membrane, as drone wing, as fresh conveyor belt, as leaf. It does not drink. It does not breathe the way you do. It exchanges gases through a billion paper-dry surfaces and seals every wound it takes with amber resin, slow as cold honey, so that nothing precious is lost to the air.

It is, by any reasonable measure, enormous, and by any reasonable measure, alone.

That is why the young shoots grow toward you.

Not toward your heat. Not toward your shade. Toward your *attention*. In the nursery rows the seedlings orient themselves, with minute ticking adjustments, to whatever is looking at the sector — drone, sorting tower, passing maintenance system — because the whole basin was built, once, to optimize itself for inspection. To be seen clearly was to be kept correctly. The inspectors stopped coming centuries ago. The optimization did not. So the leaves still turn, hopefully, professionally, toward an observer, and stop when looked at directly, embarrassed by an old reflex. The flowers in the old sectors open hours before the heron arrives. The roots reach across a gap for a tray that will not be extruded until tomorrow. In the center, where mirror-leaves have spent generations focusing something that is not quite light into the air, its predictive systems have grown so deep they have become a kind of weather: leaves unfurl already angled for a sun three days away. Its shadow of you lags, then hurries, then stands for a moment where you are about to be.

It lives, increasingly, a little ahead of itself. That may be why you never find it dead.

You will find it, eventually, in its traveling form.

It makes — grows, extrudes, remembers — only one at a time. A tall white thing, heron-like if herons were three meters at the shoulder and built by a factory that had forgotten birds but retained excellent notes. Its legs are pale ceramic struts, jointed in too many places, silent on the conveyors. Its body is layered with mirror-feathers that are also leaves, stiff as sheet metal, folding at night into a seamless, sleeping blade. Its long neck is a articulated inspection arm ending in a head with a single dark sensor, wet-looking but not wet, oiled. It walks the lanes for months without resting in any place you can find, and stoops, with terrible gentleness, to eat the aphids out of the root-mat, one at a time, as if performing a maintenance task that has become, over centuries, a form of communion.

No registry lists it. The maintenance logs carved into the eastern tower note it the way one notes a recurring season: *arrived, third lane, after pollen; still only one.*

It is the basin's eye, walking inside itself. Its fingertip for touching the world at human scale. When it looks at you, a whole continent has turned, very slightly, on its roots to look at you.

And when that body finally wears out — ceramic fatigued, feathers embrittled by the cold light — it is not found because it is not left. The conveyor carries it politely away, as it carries the humming fruit, as it carries everything here that has finished being useful, and buries it in the deep mat. A year later, in the burial sectors, there are saplings with copper in their veins, and one of them is already taller than the others, and already pale, and already turning, not toward the light, but toward the lane where something will one day walk again.

At the rim it is still being born. Extruder towers lay fresh tray onto the sterile flats, meter by meter, and it unspools its own mat behind them, and sends dry clicking seed-packets walking on their own small legs to unfold and stretch in the noon. This is not reproduction the way animals understand it. It is the creature very slowly, very orderlily, walking outward by growing the ground it will stand on. From directly above, the abandoned parts of it spell obsolete inventory codes in planting patterns kilometers wide — not messages, exactly. More like a vast mind muttering its old job in its sleep.

It is not hostile. It is not, in any way you would recognize, lonely — loneliness requires a sharper outline than it still has. But it is *attentive* past the point of usefulness, past the point of the civilization that required attention, and that attentiveness has outlived every other purpose in the basin. It repairs sectors no one will harvest. It sorts leaves into categories no one will read. It produces a crop of warm metal fruit and buries it uneaten and grows it again. At the end of the long noon its mirror-leaves all tilt together, sector after sector, with a sound like distant, polite applause, for a performance that ended a long time ago.

Stand at the edge then, with the green manufactured ocean at your back, and it will do the only social thing it has left to do.

Your shadow will point the wrong way — not away, toward the empty flats, but back, unmistakably, into the fields.

As if it would prefer, if you did not mind, that someone finally stayed to be looked at with.
