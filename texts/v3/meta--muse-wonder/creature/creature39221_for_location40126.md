At first you think no one does.

You think that because nothing turns toward you. The lizards do not scatter. The beetles do not break their lines. The moths keep their one slow circle in the hot current below the ceiling, wings out, rising and sinking an inch and rising again. The garden keeps trembling, every hearing-frond angled into the blast from the fissure, every dry scale of the ferns shushing against itself inside the larger roar.

And the roar covers everything that might have announced a person. A footstep. A breath. A shift of cloth. If someone cleared their throat or called out, the chord would take it the way the floor takes a dropped stone — without argument, without leaving a mark.

So you look for a shape, and that fails too, at first. The light is only that amber reflection off wet-looking stone above the red seam, brown at the center of things and black at all the edges. The listening wall makes its own dark, a knee-high crescent of flat stones with a hollow behind it just large enough for a seated body. The garden makes another dark, denser, plant inside plant, old dry husks holding up new leaves, moth shells snagged and rooted through and become scaffolding.

Then — not a movement, exactly, but a difference in stillness. In the hollow of the listening wall, where the vibration comes through the rock most cleanly, something is sitting with its back set very precisely into the curve.

It has been sitting for a long while before you came down. It will be sitting for a while after the ringing in your ears has finally faded, up above, in the white. Its posture gives that away before its face does. No one settles like that by accident. The spine is not slumped and not straight; it is *fitted*, vertebra by vertebra, against the dressed inner stones, so the low note finds the pelvis first and climbs, and the high note comes in through the shoulder blades and meets it in the ribs. Its head rests back, just barely, so the skull itself is also a listening surface.

It is small — or it has learned to make itself small. Knees drawn up inside the hollow, feet bare on black sand, arms loose with the hands palm-down on either side, not gripping, just receiving. What the beetles say in the stems and what the lizards say shifting their weight on the warm floor and what the ferns say working against the vent, all of it arrives through sand and stone first. The hands are the ears here. The hands, and the spine, and the bare soles, and the teeth loosely together so a crystal brushed by a sleeve somewhere in the garden can be felt chiming in the molars.

It doesn't startle because it felt you before the salt crust took the skin off your shin. A new weight in the room is a new sentence laid over the chord. A heartbeat is a clumsy drum until you learn to quiet it. Yours, arriving, must have been very loud.

The skin is dry in the way everything permitted to remain here is dry. Not parched, not cracking — finished. A dusty, warm brown darkened at the elbows and heels where it presses to stone, paler along the inner arms and throat where the amber light never quite reaches. There is salt in the creases of the knuckles from the one descent, years ago or lifetimes ago, and it has never been washed out, so it stays, a faint white map of the world it came from. No sweat stands on it. The room takes any free moisture personally, evaporating a visitor's before it can fall; in the one who lives here, the body has taken the hint and stopped offering any. It does not waste water trying to cool itself. It lets the heat go *through*, evenly, the way the lizards do when they flatten themselves near the fissure, throats pulsing in time until their blood is the same temperature as the floor.

Its hair is bound back with fern fiber, fiber tied with fiber, nothing brought down that would rust at the throat or rot against the scalp. Mixed into the binding are a few beads that are not beads — beetle shells, emptied and polished by handling, and three of the smooth sorted seeds from the basket in the niche. They make no sound the room will allow. They click against each other down the resident's back, and you understand that click is not decoration. It is punctuation. It is how you announce *I am shifting, I am not dead, I am still here on the stem*, if you have given up on air.

Because it has given up on air. Entirely, politely, a long time ago.

The eyes are open. That is the strange part. The lizards are blind, pale sealed things, honest about it. The resident's eyes are clouded, not sealed — a milkiness at the surface, like clear stone left too close to the heat — but they still track, not toward light, because there isn't any worth the name, but toward tremor. If you move your hand slowly through the updraft, the gaze follows the disturbance, not the hand. If you stand perfectly still between it and the vent, it looks not at your face but at the place where your body is making the chord bend.

The ears are small and, after however long, almost irrelevant; the outer folds are waxy, occluded with something the body has made for itself, a gradual muting. It stopped trying to hear over the sound early, the way you are learning to, painfully, minute by minute. It hears under it, now. Underneath is not quiet. Underneath is dense with smaller messages.

When it finally moves, it does not rise. People here do not spend height for nothing. It reaches — slow, in the manner of the fronds, which uncurl to a size and then simply persist — and lays two fingers against the floor between you, and taps. Not taps, exactly. Drums, with a pattern the beetles would recognize, a ticking you feel more clearly in your ankles than you hear: *here, here, here is the surface, here is where to speak*.

If you kneel and put your palm down, clumsy and too warm and still damp with your own evaporating sweat, it will, after a consideration that has nothing to do with hesitation and everything to do with the tempo of the room, cover your hand briefly with its own. Its hand is lighter than you expect and very warm, and the pressure is exact. Through it comes an education. Fern on fern, far wall. Lizard resettling near the fissure, not too near. A moth's wing tearing at last, somewhere above the garden. And beneath all that, unchanging, the great struck chord forcing its way up through stone tubes and crystal blades, the sound the garden grew toward, the sound this body has rearranged itself around like a root around a crystal — or a crystal through a leaf, each holding the other still.

It eats the way the loop eats: sparingly, dryly, without preparation anyone would recognize as cooking. A segmented finger of the swollen plants, broken at the joint; it does not yield juice when bitten, it creaks, and the heavy stored stuff inside is worked loose with the teeth and taken straight from plant body into animal body, because water here is never allowed to be free between mouths. A handful of beetles taken kindly and completely from a stem, the line closing up behind them. A moth that has finally snagged and stiffened, stripped of its furred wings, nothing wasted, the shell returned to the fern canopy where roots are already looking for it. Its droppings, when they come, are a fine odorless powder that it brushes, matter-of-factly, toward the edge of the thicket. Nothing here rots. Nothing here is wasted. Nothing here is in a hurry to become something else.

And it tends things, after its fashion. Not husbandry, not worship. Maintenance of an arrangement that satisfied itself long before this body arrived. It clears fallen fern scale from the mouths of the stone tubes with a patient fingertip, so the pressure never falters. It sets a fallen hearing-frond back into the blast at the precise angle it grew to. It sorts seeds in the basket it does not seem to plant, smooth as beads, over and over, by touch, into piles arrived at through a logic conducted entirely in groups of seven — seven ticks of beetle-line, seven pulses of a lizard's throat, seven circuits of a moth before the wing gives out. The tally stick in the niche is notched in those same sevens and then, mid-pattern, it stops. Not broken. Not abandoned in anger. Simply left, where the counting must have arrived at the obvious: there are no days to subdivide. The chord was struck before anyone was here to hear it. Keeping a tally of it can only ever be a way of sitting still.

This is the latest of them. You understand that looking at the garden, which is full of earlier tenants turned architectural — dry curled shapes in the thicket that were once, perhaps, hands holding up stems; a long hollow form near the wall that might have been a person who chose, at the end, to lie down where the roots could come politely through. And you understand it looking at the tools, which are old in a place that does not age things and yet show the overlapping evidence of more than one grip, more than one generation of sharpening, stone on stone. They come very far apart, the ones who find the seam in the salt and come down sideways. Most turn around in that first deafening minute and climb back toward the comprehensible world. A few stay a while and make one thing — a wall, a rack, a notch — and then go up, ringing, into the glare. Once in a very long while, one stays.

What staying means is not dominion. It does not own the room; the room is twelve meters and a single note and it has no use for an owner. Staying means consenting to stop expecting change. To let your eyes film. To let your ears close. To let your thirst be argued out of you by the resinous air. To sit in the hollow built by a previous spine until the curve and your back become an instrument with one purpose, and to add, if you add anything, a single flat stone to the wall, fitted where the vibration was escaping.

It is not lonely the way a visitor understands loneliness, because loneliness requires the memory of other voices in the air, and the chord has mercifully erased that. It has the beetles in the wood. It has the lizards tuning themselves on the floor. It has the plants keeping their angle. It has, when a predecessor finally thins into pure architecture, the slow rooting-through of that shape into the garden, a companionship that does not interrupt.

You will know when your audience is over without being told. It takes its hand from yours and returns it to the floor. It resettles each vertebra into the wall, one by one, and turns its clouded, attentive face back into the blast from the fissure — not toward the red seam, not toward the crystal fans whose ringing it tracks in its teeth, but directly into the source of the noise, like a flower that has correctly identified its sun.

It does not watch you leave. Feeling you go is enough: the chord unkinking where your body no longer bends it, the black sand resuming its undisturbed sentence under your feet, the garden closing its small communications over the gap you made. Long after you are up through the salt, blinking in the flat white, saltier and strangely unthirsty and hearing the world too thinly for hours, it will still be registering your departure through the floor.

And in a hundred years, should anyone lay a cheek near the buckled crust and hear the roar before they find the seam with their hands, and come down with skin to spare — they may find the basket exactly as dry, the knife exactly as wicked, the tally-stick still honest about where it stopped. And they may at first think no one lives here at all. Until they notice, in the hollow of the listening wall, a difference in the stillness: a fitted spine, a bound head, pale scarred feet, two hands palm-down and receiving, patiently, out of the warm dark, the one note that has never needed anyone — sitting with it anyway.
