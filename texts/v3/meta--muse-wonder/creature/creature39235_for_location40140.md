It lives on the surfaces, because the surfaces are the only place left to live.

You would not see it on a first visit. You would see what everyone sees: the empty middle of the room, the pipe crossing it at head height, the valve wheel, the fogged gauge, the green walls. You would register the frog by the drain if the frog happened to be there, and perhaps the moth behind the gauge, and you would think, quite reasonably, that nothing else could stand the noise.

That is because it does not stand in the room at all. It adheres to it.

It is pressed flat against the back wall, above and behind the pipe, in that narrow dry band where the engine heat comes through. From the doorway it reads as part of the wall's long self-ruin — one more bulge where the concrete has softened and re-set, one more darkening where moss gives way to bare, damp lime. Its back is the exact green-black of the spray zone. Its sides feather into liverwort. Where light from the vent falls across it, you can pick out a faint orange pitting, as if rust were blooming through skin.

Only if you stayed — and no one stays, the roar drives people out in minutes, speechless and sternum-sore — would you notice that the bulge breathes.

Not visibly. Nothing in there moves visibly except water. Its breath is on the same scale as everything else that has settled: slow, regulated, unvarying. The skin rises by less than a bead of condensation swells. It falls again. It has learned to breathe inside a sound that erases breathing, and so it does not waste motion pushing against it. Its whole ribcage, if it still has anything so distinct as ribs, takes the turbine note in through the wall and lets it out again, a held chord with one extra, living thickness in it.

It was not born in the usual way. Nothing here was. It accreted.

Silt came in grain by grain on seep water and lodged in a crack. Lime dissolved out of the concrete and laid itself down again. Iron bloomed into oxide and was burnished smooth by spray. Moss completed whatever shape those deposits suggested. At some point in the eighty years of dripping — no one could say a year, the process had no event in it — the shape began to keep itself. To take in a little more algae than the wall took. To close, very slowly, around a drip instead of merely being struck by it. To be, instead of another stalactite that grew a thumbnail's length and stopped, a thing that stopped growing for reasons of its own.

It is roughly the length of a person if you could peel it off and lay it out, which you could not; it is not built for the horizontal. Upright is its floor. Its limbs have gone wide and low and many-jointed, ending not in claws — there is nothing to dig into, nothing that was not poured or bolted — but in pale, sucker-like pads that spread on wet concrete and polished iron alike. The pads are worn featureless. So are the edges of everything it touches regularly. That bare, shining strip on the underside of the pipe, just below where the algae film stops, is not only spray-polish. That is its road. It travels it belly-close, year after year, and keeps the metal bright by passing.

It travels all its roads that way: in bands, along lines, never across the open middle. Down the spray band to drink where the finger-width stream crosses the grate. Across to the tideline, where its lower skin stays wet and its upper stays merely damp. Up to the condensation line on the ceiling, where droplets form at the measured rate and it can take one into its mouth before it falls — though never the one that falls to the pipe. That drip it leaves alone. The bright *tink* sounds at its interval, unbroken. Whether it avoids that droplet out of habit, or territory, or a kind of fidelity, is impossible to know. The room has kept that note so long that the note may be part of how everything in it knows where it is.

It eats almost nothing, and almost nothing is always available. Algae warming green in the light. The soft top of the moss, cropped so evenly that the turf never spills past its appointed edge. A snail, in the morning, before noon washes the trails away. The mineral skin of the seep water itself, licked from the wall in a slow rasp that adds no new mark. It does not hunt the frog. The frog sits in the drain for hours and the inhabitant passes above it on the wall, and neither acknowledges the other; there is room for both because they want different kinds of stillness.

It has no voice. The place forbids it twice — once by drowning any sound it might make, and once by making sound unnecessary. It communicates, if it ever needs to, the way you first found the room: through the soles of the feet, through the palm laid flat. A slight thickening of pressure against the concrete. A pause in its passage that changes, for a yard of wall, the evenness of the vibration. The turbines answer with nothing. They have never answered. Continuance may be the only reply it has ever required.

Its eyes, under their heavy mineral lids, have fogged like the glass on the gauge. It does not need them for shape anymore; the room is twelve feet long and has not rearranged itself in its lifetime. It knows the two broken spokes of the valve wheel by the pads of its hands, and the smooth rust where the breaks healed over. But it still turns its head, a fraction, toward the door when afternoon light comes in broad after rain, and toward the vent shaft where the mist hangs. Not to see you. To feel the small cooling of the inflow on one cheek, the exact place where the warm interior meets the weather. It can tell season that way. It can tell rain. It can probably tell, without any change in the roar, when the heron is standing in the canal outside with its shadow on the water, because the light through the louver loses a degree of its diffuseness — though the heron has never put its head through the door, and the inhabitant has never gone to meet it. The threshold is understood on both sides.

It does not sleep so much as thin out. For hours its pulse rests where the gauge needle rests, against the same invisible mark, and a visitor pressing an ear to the wall — no one does; the hiss would split the ear — would find no separate heartbeat to isolate. The moth has chosen the place behind the gauge because the vibration is least there. The inhabitant chose the whole wall because the vibration is most there, and even. Least would starve it. It rests in the chord the way the frog rests in the shallow flow: throat barely moving, interrupted only when a drip lands squarely between its shoulders and runs, unhurried, into the green scales along its spine.

It is old in the way the room is old, which is not the way a tree or a tortoise is old. Its age is not rings or scars. It is smoothness. Every part of it that once had an angle has been revised by water into the curve water prefers. Its joints are dished like the nosings of the steps. The bony plates along its back have been capped by their own slow deposits, and stopped. Even its moss coat has reached its limit and holds there, bright where the light hits it, near-black in the corners, neither invading nor retreating. It is not decaying. It is not ripening. It has arrived at the version of itself that this exact volume of warm, wet, roaring air can support indefinitely, and it maintains that version with a fidelity so complete it looks, from the doorway, like nothing happening at all.

Come back in a year, after rain, in the same season, and it will be where you left it: on the back wall, in the dry band, spread over the heat, listening through its skin to engines that have never once paused for it. The drip will fall. The stream will shush under the grate. The level below will tremble and not rise.

Put your palm flat to the wall before you go, if you can bear the noise that long. Under the great even vibration, very deep, almost too regular to call a pulse, there is a second regularity keeping time with the first.

That is it, holding still — which here, in a room no bigger than a pocket in a dam, with all eternity sorted into wet and dry bands a hand's breadth apart, is not an absence of life.

It is the whole practice of it.
